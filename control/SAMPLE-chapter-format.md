## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
No one came to this land from across the ocean to stay in this age. The people already here had come on foot, long before, and the land was full of hundreds of nations. There is nothing to tell here from the angle of immigration, because immigration had not begun.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- thin era: a little to tell. state=thin. -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Ships from Europe began to reach these shores, but almost no one came to stay. The men on them were soldiers, sailors, and priests who meant to go home again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine" -->
The one lasting exception came near the century's end: in 1565 the Spanish founded St. Augustine, in Florida — the oldest town of European origin in the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s, people from Europe finally came to this land to stay, but only in small numbers and only along the edge of the sea. This is the century when immigration really began.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the English colonies" -->
The English built Jamestown in 1607 and the Pilgrims built Plymouth in 1620. Most who came could not pay for the voyage, so they became indentured servants — bound to work for a set number of years, then free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- story with movie="" means no film; the blockquote then has no Movie line -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="first-jewish-refugees" name="The First Jewish Refugees" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The First Jewish Refugees

> **Who:** The first Jewish community in what is now the United States.
> **When and where:** 1654, arriving at New Amsterdam, now New York City.

In 1654, a ship carried 23 Jewish refugees to New Amsterdam. They had fled Recife, in Brazil, when Portugal took it back and Jews there were no longer safe. The governor, Peter Stuyvesant, wanted to turn them away, but Jewish owners of the Dutch West India Company overruled him, and they were allowed to stay.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="first-jewish-refugees" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, immigration became one of the largest movements of people in the history of the world, and for the first time the country began keeping some groups out by law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Forty-Eighters" -->
Some who came were refugees from failed revolutions in Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-schurz" name="Carl Schurz" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Schurz

> **Who:** A refugee from a failed German revolution who became a Union general, a U.S. senator, and a cabinet member.
> **When and where:** Born in Germany in 1829; came to the United States in 1852.

Carl Schurz fled Germany after the revolutions of 1848 were crushed, arriving in 1852 with no power at all, and rose to become the first German-born U.S. senator.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-schurz" -->
<!-- a new span after a story = the narrator pulling back to the wide view before the next story -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ellis Island opens" -->
To handle the crowds, the government opened a new immigration station in New York Harbor: Ellis Island, on January 1, 1892. The first person through its doors has her own story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="annie-moore" name="Annie Moore" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Annie Moore

> **Who:** The first immigrant ever processed at Ellis Island.
> **When and where:** Arrived January 1, 1892, from County Cork, Ireland.

Annie Moore, seventeen, was first through Ellis Island on New Year's Day 1892. For a hundred years the country told the wrong story about her; in 2006 researchers proved the real Annie never left New York, where she raised her children in a Lower East Side tenement.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="annie-moore" -->
<!-- closing era zoom: end the section back on the wide view -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By the century's end, most newcomers came not from northern Europe but from the south and east — Italians, Poles, Greeks, and Jews fleeing the Russian Empire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1965 the country reopened its doors, and the people who came changed — more now from Asia, Latin America, Africa, and the Caribbean. Many were refugees.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="tung-trinh" name="Tung Trinh and the Bolinao 52" movie="Bolinao 52 (2008)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tung Trinh and the Bolinao 52

> **Who:** A Vietnamese woman who survived a lost refugee boat at sea and settled in California.
> **When and where:** The open sea near the Philippines, 1988.
> **Movie:** Bolinao 52 (2008), a documentary telling the survivors' story.

In 1988, Tung Trinh was one of 110 people who fled Vietnam by boat. The engine died and the boat drifted for 37 days; only 52 survived, rescued at last by Filipino fishermen.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tung-trinh" -->
