## Glossary

- distill: to heat a drink and catch the steam, which cools into a liquor stronger than beer or wine (Before 1500)
- hearth: an old fire pit (Before 1500)
- nicotine: the drug in tobacco, which makes people addicted (Before 1500)
- addiction: a need for a drug so strong that stopping is very hard (Before 1500)
- traditional tobacco: tobacco grown or gathered for ceremony or medicine and offered in prayer, set apart from commercial tobacco, the kind sold in stores (Before 1500)
- Black Drink: a tea made from the leaves of yaupon holly or dahoon holly (Before 1500)
- caffeine: the drug in coffee that keeps people awake (Before 1500)
- cacao: the bean that chocolate is made from (Before 1500)
- peyote: a small cactus whose top holds mescaline, a drug that causes visions (Before 1500)
- datura: a flowering plant that holds the drugs scopolamine and atropine, which cause hallucinations (Before 1500)
- hallucinations: seeing and hearing things that are not there (Before 1500)
- trance: a state like a dream while awake (Before 1500)
- tiswin: the Apache corn beer (Before 1500)
- ambassador: the official who spoke for the king of France in Portugal (The 1500s)
- werowance: a chief of the Algonquian peoples of the Outer Banks (The 1500s)
- uppowoc: the Algonquian name for tobacco (The 1500s)
- olive jars: big Spanish storage jars that carried wine and olive oil by ship (The 1500s)
- bolts: iron shackles (The 1600s)
- provost marshal: the Virginia officer in whose house a drunk lay in bolts (The 1600s)
- stocks: a wooden frame that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place (The 1600s)
- constable: a town officer who kept order (The 1600s)
- ordinary: the old name for a tavern (The 1600s)
- comfit-maker: a maker of sugar-coated sweets (The 1600s)
- brew-house: a building for making beer (The 1600s)
- aqua vitae: a strong liquor like brandy (The 1600s)
- guilder: a Dutch coin (The 1600s)
- alcoholic: a person who cannot stop drinking (The 1600s)
- rum: a strong liquor made from molasses (The 1600s)
- molasses: the thick syrup left over when sugar is made from sugarcane (The 1600s)
- smuggled: brought in secretly without paying the tax (1700 to 1750)
- Parliament: the lawmakers of Britain (1700 to 1750)
- indentured servant: a worker bound to a master for a set number of years (1700 to 1750)
- treats: the liquor and food Virginia candidates gave voters (1700 to 1750)
- punch: a mixed drink made with liquor (1700 to 1750)
- Freemasons: a society of men who met in lodges, with a grand lodge as the head lodge over the others (1700 to 1750)
- privateer: a privately owned ship allowed to attack enemy ships (1700 to 1750)
- Malcontents: Georgia colonists who complained that the rum ban hurt trade (1700 to 1750)
- bumpers: glasses filled to the brim and drunk off at once (1700 to 1750)
- militiamen: part-time citizen soldiers (1750 to 1800)
- cholera: a disease that causes severe diarrhea (1750 to 1800)
- ardent spirits: strong liquor such as rum, gin and whiskey (1750 to 1800)
- still: the equipment used to distill liquor (1750 to 1800)
- tarring and feathering: pouring hot tar on a person's skin and sticking feathers to the tar (1750 to 1800)
- marshal: an officer who carried out the orders of the national courts (1750 to 1800)
- treason: the crime of making war against one's own country (1750 to 1800)
- ransacked: tore through a house and wrecked it (1750 to 1800)
- pardon: freeing a person from their punishment (1750 to 1800)
- House of Burgesses: colonial Virginia's elected lawmaking body (1750 to 1800)
- overseer: the man who ran enslaved people's work (1750 to 1800)
- pledge: a written promise not to drink (1800 to 1850)
- laudanum: a painkiller made of opium dissolved in alcohol (1800 to 1850)
- opium: a drug made from the opium poppy that stops pain and brings sleep (1800 to 1850)
- dram: a small drink of liquor (1800 to 1850)
- temperance: drinking little or no alcohol (1800 to 1850)
- total abstinence: no alcohol at all, not even beer or wine (1800 to 1850)
- license: a paper from the government that allowed a person to sell liquor (1800 to 1850)
- ration: a set share handed out each day (1800 to 1850)
- petition: a paper people sign to ask for something (1800 to 1850)
- delirium tremens: an illness of heavy drinkers who suddenly stop, in which the body shakes, the mind is confused and the person sees things that are not there; it can kill (1800 to 1850)
- morphine: a strong painkilling drug taken out of opium (1800 to 1850)
- spittoon: a bowl for spitting into (1800 to 1850)
- cocaine: a drug made from coca leaves (1850 to 1900)
- state constitution: the state's highest set of laws (1850 to 1900)
- saloon: a bar, mostly for men, that sold drinks by the glass (1850 to 1900)
- search warrant: a paper from a judge that allows a search (1850 to 1900)
- hypodermic syringe: a hollow needle for pushing a drug under the skin (1850 to 1900)
- withdrawal: the sickness that comes when the body loses a drug it depends on (1850 to 1900)
- overdose: too much of a drug at once, enough to make a person very sick or kill them (1850 to 1900)
- Confederacy: the Southern states that broke away to keep slavery (1850 to 1900)
- siege: an attack in which an army surrounds a place to force it to give up (1850 to 1900)
- patent medicines: medicines sold under a brand name whose makers did not have to say what was in them (1850 to 1900)
- opium den: a room where people paid to lie down and smoke opium (1850 to 1900)
- hoax: a lie made up on purpose (1850 to 1900)
- lynching: the killing of people by mobs without any trial (1850 to 1900)
- grog shop: a saloon (1850 to 1900)
- allotments: plots for single families cut from land a Native nation had held together, under the Dawes Act (1850 to 1900)
- Prohibition: the federal ban on making, selling and carrying alcoholic drinks, 1920 to 1933 (1900 to 1950)
- bootleggers: people who made or sold liquor against the law (1900 to 1950)
- alcoholism: the illness of being addicted to alcohol (1900 to 1950)
- cannabis: the plant marijuana comes from (1900 to 1950)
- narcotics: in the 1914 law, drugs made from opium or from coca (1900 to 1950)
- prescription: a doctor's written order for a drug (1900 to 1950)
- heroin: a drug made from morphine (1900 to 1950)
- amendment: a change added to the Constitution (1900 to 1950)
- speakeasy: a hidden bar that sold liquor against the law (1900 to 1950)
- denatured alcohol: factory alcohol with chemicals added so that no one could drink it (1900 to 1950)
- methyl alcohol: wood alcohol, which blinds and kills people who drink it (1900 to 1950)
- medical examiner: the doctor in charge of finding out why people in a city died (1900 to 1950)
- jake leg: the illness from TOCP-poisoned Jamaica ginger, which destroyed nerve cells in the spinal cord and took away the use of the hands and feet (1900 to 1950)
- cirrhosis: damage to the liver from heavy drinking (1900 to 1950)
- marijuana: the dried leaves and flowers of the cannabis plant (1900 to 1950)
- Surgeon General: the nation's top public health doctor (1950 to 2000)
- discriminatory: treating one group of people worse than others (1950 to 2000)
- LSD: a drug that makes people see and hear things that are not there (1950 to 2000)
- psilocybin: a drug from certain mushrooms that causes visions (1950 to 2000)
- Schedule I: the federal list for drugs with a high risk of abuse and no accepted use as medicine (1950 to 2000)
- criminalizing: making something a crime (1950 to 2000)
- intervention: a meeting at which a family asks a person to go into treatment (1950 to 2000)
- involuntary manslaughter: the crime of killing someone without meaning to (1950 to 2000)
- paroled: let out of prison early under watch (1950 to 2000)
- crack: cocaine cooked with water and baking soda and dried into small rocks that can be smoked (1950 to 2000)
- biochemist: a scientist who studies the chemistry of living things (1950 to 2000)
- opioids: strong painkillers made from the opium poppy, or from chemicals that act the same way in the body (1950 to 2000)
- fentanyl: an opioid made in a lab, 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine (2000 to Today)
- naloxone: a drug that can undo an opioid overdose if someone gives it fast enough (2000 to Today)
- cartels: Mexican crime groups that make fentanyl outside the law (2000 to Today)
- bankruptcy: a court process for a business that cannot pay its debts (2000 to Today)
- attorney general: the top lawyer of a state (2000 to Today)
- settlement: a deal that ends lawsuits in return for money (2000 to Today)
- Schedule III: a federal list of drugs that doctors may order as medicine (2000 to Today)
- vaping: using an e-cigarette, which heats a liquid, usually with nicotine, into a mist the user breathes in (2000 to Today)
- secondhand smoke: the smoke other people breathe in (2000 to Today)

## People

- Thomas Hariot: English mathematician who smoked tobacco the Algonquian way at Roanoke and wrote down how the Algonquian people used it (The 1500s)
- Samuel Cole: sweet-maker who kept the earliest licensed tavern known in Boston (The 1600s)
- Robert Coles: Massachusetts colonist made to wear a red letter D for drunkenness (The 1600s)
- Dr. Alexander Hamilton: Annapolis doctor whose 1744 diary records colonial taverns and drinking clubs (1700 to 1750)
- Benjamin Rush: Philadelphia doctor and signer of the Declaration of Independence who wrote that heavy drinking was a disease (1750 to 1800)
- Philip Vigol: one of the first two Americans found guilty of treason, for attacking whiskey-tax officers (1750 to 1800)
- John B. Gough: bookbinder whose drinking cost him his work, who then spent his life asking others to sign the temperance pledge (1800 to 1850)
- John Tackett Goolrick: Confederate soldier whose family's letters record his 30 years of morphine addiction (1850 to 1900)
- John Stith Pemberton: Georgia druggist, addicted to morphine, who invented Coca-Cola (1850 to 1900)
- Carry Nation: Kansas woman who smashed saloons to force officials to enforce the state's liquor ban (1900 to 1950)
- Izzy Einstein: federal Prohibition agent who made arrests in disguise (1900 to 1950)
- Betty Ford: wife of President Gerald Ford, who told the country she was addicted to pills and alcohol and founded a treatment center (1950 to 2000)
- Jeffrey Wigand: tobacco company research chief who told the public his company changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine (1950 to 2000)
- Nan Goldin: photographer who became addicted to OxyContin and led protests against the Sackler family (2000 to Today)

- February 13, 1826: reformers in Boston found the American Temperance Society
- January 17, 1920: national Prohibition begins
- June 17, 1971: President Nixon calls drug abuse "public enemy number one"
- 1996: Purdue Pharma begins selling OxyContin
- June 18, 1643: New Netherland's leaders ban selling liquor to Native people
- 1735: Georgia's Trustees ban rum and brandy, at Tomochichi's request (ended September 29, 1742)
- 1764: the Brown brothers' ship *Sally* carries 17,274 gallons of rum to West Africa to buy people
- July 17, 1794: the attack on John Neville's house at Bower Hill, in the Whiskey Rebellion
- June 2, 1851: Maine lawmakers ban the sale of all alcoholic drinks
- November 1874: women in Cleveland found the Woman's Christian Temperance Union
- June 30, 1906: President Theodore Roosevelt signs the Pure Food and Drug Act
- December 17, 1914: President Wilson signs the Harrison Narcotics Act
- December 5, 1933: national Prohibition ends
- October 27, 1986: President Reagan signs the law setting the same prison term for 5 grams of crack as for 500 grams of powder cocaine

## Questions

- Q: How long have people here used tobacco, and what did they use it for? A: For at least 12,300 years, since the fire at the Wishbone site. Native people offered it in prayer, burning it or smoking it in pipes, and the Algonquian people at Roanoke smoked it to keep the body healthy.
- Q: What happened when the whole country banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933? A: Drinking fell at first to about 30 percent of what it had been, then rose to about 60 to 70 percent. Poisoned factory alcohol killed thousands of drinkers before delegates in Utah ended the ban on December 5, 1933.
- Q: Who has been punished for drugs, and who has not? A: Robert Coles with his red D, the laborer Samuel Caldwell with four years of hard labor, Billie Holiday handcuffed to her hospital bed, and the people sentenced for crack, 91 of every 100 of them Black in 1992, were all punished. Harry Gross never served his jail sentence, and no member of the Sackler family has been charged with a crime.
