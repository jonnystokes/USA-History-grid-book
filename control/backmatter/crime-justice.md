## Glossary

- wampum: strings or belts of beads made from shells (Before 1500)
- clan: a large family group, a Cherokee child belonged to the mother's clan (Before 1500)
- council: a large meeting to decide the nation's business (Before 1500)
- feud: a long run of killings in which each side took revenge for the last death (Before 1500)
- condolence: comfort for people in grief (Before 1500)
- naturalist: a person who studies plants and animals (Before 1500)
- government agent: an official sent to deal with a Native nation (Before 1500)
- alcalde: an official who acted as the judge in a Spanish town (The 1500s)
- appeal: asking a higher judge or official to change a decision (The 1500s)
- Protestants: Christians outside the Catholic Church (The 1500s)
- rape: forcing a person into sex (The 1500s)
- inquiry: an official hearing to find out what had happened (The 1500s)
- friars: Catholic religious brothers (The 1500s)
- just war: a war that is right and fair (The 1500s)
- convent: a house where nuns live (The 1500s)
- martial law: rule by army officers instead of by ordinary courts (The 1600s)
- blasphemy: insulting God (The 1600s)
- treason: words against the king's rule (The 1600s)
- adultery: sex between a married person and someone outside the marriage (The 1600s)
- breaking on the wheel: a way of putting a person to death in which the executioner broke the person's arms and legs with a heavy bar or club and tied the body to a large wooden wheel (The 1600s)
- banish: order a person to leave and never return (The 1600s)
- ducat: a Spanish coin (The 1600s)
- pardon: official forgiveness that frees a person from the punishment (The 1600s)
- sachem: a leader of a Native nation (The 1600s)
- shilling: English money (The 1600s)
- whipping: being struck on the bare back with a whip, often at a post in a public place (The 1600s)
- Sabbath: the day of rest and worship (The 1600s)
- felony: a serious crime (The 1600s)
- stocks: a wooden board with holes that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place (The 1600s)
- pillory: a wooden frame on a post, on a platform in a public place, that locked a person's neck and wrists while the person stood (The 1600s)
- bilboes: iron rings locked around the ankles and joined to a bar, so the person could not walk away (The 1600s)
- branding: pressing a red-hot iron into the skin, which burned a mark into the flesh that stayed for life (The 1600s)
- Quakers: members of a Christian group also called the Society of Friends (The 1600s)
- marshal: the colony's officer who carried out the court's orders (The 1600s)
- enslaved person: a person owned by another person as property and forced to work for no pay (The 1600s)
- correction: a beating (The 1600s)
- witchcraft: using the Devil's power to hurt others (The 1600s)
- magistrate: a local judge (The 1600s)
- Court of Oyer and Terminer: a special court, the words mean "to hear and to decide" (The 1600s)

- pressing: forcing a plea by laying a prisoner under a wooden board and piling heavier and heavier stones on it until the prisoner answered or died (The 1600s)
- warrant: a written order for an arrest (The 1600s), a judge's written order to search a home (1750 to 1800)
- petition: a written request (The 1600s)
- midwife: a woman who helps other women give birth (The 1600s)
- excommunication: putting a person out of the church (The 1600s)
- justices of the peace: local judges who sat on Virginia's county courts (1700 to 1750)
- deed: a paper showing who owns land (1700 to 1750)
- transportation: the punishment of shipping convicts from Britain to the colonies (1700 to 1750)
- Parliament: Britain's lawmaking body (1700 to 1750)
- slave patrol: a group of white men who rode out to watch and control enslaved people (1700 to 1750)

- Negro, Mulatto: old words for a Black person and for a person with one Black and one white parent or ancestor (1700 to 1750)

## People

- Philip Ratcliffe: a servant whipped, his ears cut off, fined and banished for speaking against the Massachusetts government and the Salem church (The 1600s)
- Rebecca Nurse: a member of the Salem church found not guilty of witchcraft, then convicted and hanged (The 1600s)
- Quack and Cuffee: two enslaved men in New York City burned at the stake in the trials of 1741 (1700 to 1750)
- Patrick Lyon: a Philadelphia blacksmith held three months in the Walnut Street Prison for a bank robbery he did not commit (1750 to 1800)
- Charles Williams: the first prisoner at Eastern State Penitentiary (1800 to 1850)
- Allan Pinkerton: a Scottish barrel maker who started a private detective agency in Chicago (1850 to 1900)
- Henry Smith: a Black man tortured and burned to death by a white mob in Paris, Texas, with no trial (1850 to 1900)
- Ed Johnson: a Black man wrongly convicted of rape and lynched after the US Supreme Court ordered him kept safe (1900 to 1950)
- Eliot Ness: the federal agent whose team raided Al Capone's breweries (1900 to 1950)

## Questions

- Q: How did punishment move from the whipping post to the prison cell? A: Colonial judges had people whipped, branded and hanged in public, and had their ears cut off. Pennsylvania's lawmakers turned the Walnut Street Jail into the country's first state prison in 1790, and by 2009 American prisons held about 1.62 million people.
- Q: Who kept order before there were police? A: Among the Native nations, families and clans settled killings, and in the colonies settlers took turns on a night watch while white men in the South rode on slave patrols. Full-time police began work in Boston in 1838 and in New York in 1845, and the US attorney general put 34 federal agents to work in 1908.
- Q: What happened to the people in mobs who killed without a trial? A: In almost every case in this chapter, nobody punished them. No one was charged for the Conestoga killings in 1763, for burning Francis McIntosh in 1836 or for burning Henry Smith in 1893, and no white person went to prison for Tulsa in 1921.
