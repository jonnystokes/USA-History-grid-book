## Glossary

- smallpox: a disease caused by a virus that spread face to face and caused a fever and a rash that turned into blisters and scabs, and in general about 3 of every 10 people who caught it died (Before 1500)
- germ: a living thing too small to see that can cause disease (Before 1500)
- archaeologist: a scientist who learns about the past from what people left behind (Before 1500)
- pristine: untouched and pure (Before 1500)
- bacteria: one kind of germ (Before 1500)
- tuberculosis: a disease caused by bacteria that most often infects the lungs and can spread to the bones, including the spine (Before 1500)
- syphilis: a disease passed during sex, or from a mother to her unborn baby, caused by bacteria (Before 1500)
- yaws and bejel: diseases in the same family as syphilis that spread mainly by touching skin and mouths, not by sex (Before 1500)
- non-venereal syphilis: another name for bejel, syphilis not passed by sex (Before 1500)
- DNA: the set of chemical instructions inside every living cell (Before 1500)
- pinta: a slow skin disease in the syphilis family (Before 1500)
- hepatitis: an infection of the liver, a disease that inflames the liver (Before 1500)
- herpes: a virus that causes sores (Before 1500)
- tularemia: a fever people catch from animals (Before 1500)
- giardia and amoebas: tiny living things that cause stomach infections (Before 1500)
- maize: corn (Before 1500)
- anemia: having too few red blood cells, which leaves a person weak and tired (Before 1500)
- marrow: the soft center of the bone, the tissue that makes blood (Before 1500)
- epidemic: a disease that spreads to many people in one place at the same time (Before 1500)
- immune: able to fight off a disease, so the person will not catch it again (Before 1500)
- genes: the instructions inside every cell that pass from parents to children (Before 1500)
- virgin soil epidemic: an epidemic among people never exposed to the disease, in which everyone in a town can be infected at the same time (Before 1500)
- expedition: a group sent out to explore and conquer (The 1500s)
- bowels: the long tube in the belly that food passes through after the stomach (The 1500s)
- cautery: burning the skin at a painful spot on purpose, as a treatment (The 1500s)
- wiroans: a chief, in the Carolina Algonquian language (The 1500s)
- uppowoc: tobacco (The 1500s)
- paramount chief: a leader over the chiefs of many nations (The 1600s)
- flux, bloody flux: the old word for diarrhea, and diarrhea with blood in it (The 1600s)
- Starving Time: the winter of 1609 to 1610 at Jamestown, when only about 60 of about 240 colonists lived (The 1600s)
- leptospirosis: an infection by bacteria that rats carry in their urine (The 1600s)
- scurvy: a disease caused by too little vitamin C, in which the gums swell and bleed, the teeth loosen, and the person grows too weak to work (The 1600s)
- bloodletting, letting blood: cutting open a vein, most often in the arm, and letting blood run out into a bowl (The 1600s)
- virus: a germ even smaller than bacteria (The 1600s)
- sachem: a leader (The 1600s)
- midwife: a woman who helps other women give birth (The 1600s)
- matron: the woman in charge (The 1600s)
- florins: Dutch coins (The 1600s)
- broadside: a single large printed sheet (The 1600s)
- yellow fever: a disease caused by a virus that the mosquito Aedes aegypti carries from one person to another when it bites (The 1600s)
- inoculation: giving a healthy person a small case of smallpox on purpose, by putting dried pus from a smallpox sore into a cut, so the person would not catch it again (1700 to 1750)
- humor: an old word for a fluid of the body (1700 to 1750)
- selectmen: a town's elected officials (1700 to 1750)
- distemper: an old word for a sickness (1700 to 1750)
- diphtheria: an infection by bacteria that kills the tissue of the throat and nose until the coating of dead tissue stops a child breathing (1700 to 1750)
- borax: a white powder made from a mineral (1700 to 1750)
- Commons House: South Carolina's lawmaking body (1750 to 1800)
- petition: a written request (1750 to 1800)
- almshouse: a home for the poor (1750 to 1800)
- workhouse: a place where poor people lived and were put to work (1750 to 1800)
- contrived: arranged (1750 to 1800)
- disaffected: unfriendly (1750 to 1800)
- extirpate: to wipe out completely (1750 to 1800)
- execrable: hateful (1750 to 1800)
- dissection: cutting open dead bodies to study how the body is built (1750 to 1800)
- Negro: a word for Black people used at the time (1750 to 1800)
- physic: medicine (1750 to 1800)
- militiamen: ordinary citizens called up to serve as soldiers (1750 to 1800)
- purge: a medicine that emptied the bowels (1750 to 1800)
- shock: what happens when too little blood is left in the body, so the person becomes confused, the heart beats fast and the blood pressure drops (1750 to 1800)
- marine hospital: a hospital for sailors (1750 to 1800)
- vaccination: scratching fluid from a cowpox sore into the skin to protect a person against smallpox (1800 to 1850)
- cowpox: a mild disease of cows (1800 to 1850)
- Indian agents: the government officials sent to deal with each Native nation (1800 to 1850)
- criminal negligence: a failure to take care so serious that it should count as a crime (1800 to 1850)
- ingratitude: giving no thanks in return for kindness (1800 to 1850)
- cholera: an infection of the gut caught from food or water that carries its germ, causing diarrhea so heavy that the body loses enough water and salt to kill (1800 to 1850)
- quarantine station: a place where people who may carry a disease are kept apart from others (1800 to 1850)
- anesthesia: the use of a drug so that a patient does not feel pain during an operation (1800 to 1850)
- ether: a drug breathed as a vapor that kept a patient from feeling an operation (1800 to 1850)
- tumor: a lump of tissue that grows where it should not (1800 to 1850)
- inhaler: a breathing device (1800 to 1850)
- vesicovaginal fistula: a hole torn through the wall between the bladder and the vagina by a long, blocked childbirth, so that urine runs out of the woman's body all the time (1800 to 1850)
- bladder: where urine is held (1800 to 1850)
- vagina: the passage a baby is born through (1800 to 1850)
- opium: a drug that dulls pain and brings sleep (1800 to 1850)
- neonatal tetanus: an infection in newborn babies that locks the jaw and other muscles in spasms (1800 to 1850)
- awl: a pointed tool for punching holes (1800 to 1850)
- rectum: the end of the bowel (1800 to 1850)
- indigent: poor (1800 to 1850)
- dysentery: an infection of the gut (1850 to 1900)
- typhoid fever: an infection spread by food and water that has touched human waste (1850 to 1900)
- pneumonia: an infection of the lungs, in which the lungs fill with fluid (1850 to 1900)
- malaria: a fever spread by mosquitoes (1850 to 1900)
- latrine trenches: the ditches soldiers used as toilets (1850 to 1900)
- pestilence: a deadly disease that spreads (1850 to 1900)
- steward: the man in charge of a hospital's supplies (1850 to 1900)
- contraband: goods taken from an enemy in war (1850 to 1900)
- Freedmen's Bureau: a federal office set up to help people freed from slavery (1850 to 1900)
- chloride and lime: chemicals that kill germs (1850 to 1900)
- brothel: a house where men paid women for sex (1850 to 1900)
- artery: a large tube that carries blood from the heart (1850 to 1900)
- spleen: an organ beside the stomach (1850 to 1900)
- Colored: a word then used for Black people (1850 to 1900)
- influenza epidemic: a flu that spread to huge numbers of people at once (1900 to 1950)
- influenza: a disease caused by a virus that infects the nose, throat and lungs (1900 to 1950)
- paralysis: being unable to move the arms, the legs or the muscles used for breathing (1900 to 1950)
- polio: a disease caused by a virus that can attack the nerves in the spine (1900 to 1950)
- vaccine: a medicine that trains the body to fight off a germ before the germ can make a person sick (1900 to 1950)
- antibiotic: a medicine that kills bacteria (1900 to 1950)
- war bonds: bonds people bought to lend money to the government for the war (1900 to 1950)
- radium: a metal that gives off radiation and that bones take up as if it were calcium (1900 to 1950)
- radiation: a stream of energy that damages living cells (1900 to 1950)
- radium jaw: a dentist's name for a jaw bone wasting away from radium (1900 to 1950)
- prevalent: very common (1900 to 1950)
- trachoma: an eye disease that produces blindness (1900 to 1950)
- dementia: the loss of memory and reason (1900 to 1950)
- sharecroppers: people who farmed someone else's land for a share of the crop (1900 to 1950)
- spinal tap: pushing a needle between the bones of the lower back to draw out fluid from around the spinal cord (1900 to 1950)
- draft board: the group that chose men for the army (1900 to 1950)
- diethylene glycol: a chemical used in antifreeze, and a poison (1900 to 1950)
- penicillin: a drug made from a mold that kills many kinds of bacteria, the first antibiotic made in large amounts (1900 to 1950)
- miscarriage: the loss of a baby before it can live outside the womb (1900 to 1950)
- gonorrhea and chancroid: diseases passed during sex (1900 to 1950)
- leprosy: a disease caused by bacteria that damages the skin and nerves (1900 to 1950)
- pellagra: a disease caused by too little niacin, a vitamin, in the diet (1900 to 1950)
- silica: the mineral in sand (1900 to 1950)
- silicosis: a disease in which breathing silica's fine dust scars the lungs until a person cannot breathe (1900 to 1950)
- eugenics: the false idea that a country could improve its people by stopping some people from having children (1900 to 1950)
- sterilization: an operation after which a person can never become pregnant, done by cutting or tying the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, or by removing the womb (1900 to 1950)
- cervix: the opening of the womb (1950 to 2000)
- HeLa: the name of Henrietta Lacks's cells, from the first two letters of her first and last names (1950 to 2000)
- Board of Regents: the New York state board that licenses doctors (1950 to 2000)
- fraud, deceit and unprofessional conduct: legal words for cheating, lying and breaking the rules of one's profession (1950 to 2000)
- probation: a trial period under watch (1950 to 2000)
- iron lung: a metal tube about seven feet long in which a pump changed the air pressure to pull air into a paralyzed patient's lungs (1950 to 2000)
- bellows: a pump with folding sides (1950 to 2000)
- placebo: a shot with no medicine in it (1950 to 2000)
- post-polio syndrome: new muscle pain and weakness 15 to 40 years after polio (1950 to 2000)
- patent: the legal right to be the only one allowed to make and sell an invention (1950 to 2000)
- tracheotomy: an operation that cuts an opening in the front of the neck into the windpipe so that air can get in (1950 to 2000)
- cripple: an old, unkind word for a person who cannot use part of the body (1950 to 2000)
- rejection: the body's defenses attacking an organ from almost anyone else (1950 to 2000)
- technetium-99m: a radioactive chemical that gives off rays a camera outside the body picks up, to make pictures of organs (1950 to 2000)
- fallout: the radioactive dust and ash that falls after a nuclear blast (1950 to 2000)
- thyroid glands: glands that sit in the neck (1950 to 2000)
- plutonium: a radioactive metal (1950 to 2000)
- radon: a radioactive gas that comes off uranium ore (1950 to 2000)
- ventilated: with fresh air pumped in (1950 to 2000)
- surgeon general: the country's top public health doctor (1950 to 2000)
- chronic bronchitis: a long-lasting swelling of the air passages (1950 to 2000)
- emphysema: a lung disease in which the thin walls between the lungs' tiny air sacs break down, so the person cannot get enough air (1950 to 2000)
- settlement: a deal to end lawsuits (1950 to 2000)
- asbestos: a group of minerals that split into thin fibers (1950 to 2000)
- mesothelioma: a cancer of the thin lining of the chest and belly (1950 to 2000)
- health insurance: a plan that pays doctor and hospital bills (1950 to 2000)
- Medicare: health insurance for people 65 and older (1950 to 2000)
- Medicaid: health insurance for people with little money (1950 to 2000)
- Ku Klux Klan: a secret white group that used terror against Black people and their supporters (1950 to 2000)
- stool: solid waste (1950 to 2000)
- CDC: the federal disease agency (1950 to 2000)
- informed consent: the rule that every person in a study must first be told what will be done and what the risks are, and must agree (1950 to 2000)
- tubal ligation: an operation in which a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb (1950 to 2000)
- hysterectomy: an operation in which a surgeon cuts out the womb (1950 to 2000)
- fallopian tubes: the tubes that carry eggs to the womb (1950 to 2000)
- welfare: government money for poor families (1950 to 2000)
- General Accounting Office: the federal office that checks how federal money is spent (1950 to 2000)
- AIDS: a disease caused by HIV, in which other infections and cancers kill the person as the cells that fight infection die (1950 to 2000)
- HIV: a virus that destroys the white blood cells that fight infection, passed through blood, semen and other body fluids (1950 to 2000)

- life expectancy: the average age a newborn would reach if that year's death rates held (2000 to Today)
- genome: the full set of DNA instructions in a person's cells (2000 to Today)

- Absalom Jones and Richard Allen: Black Philadelphians who led volunteers to nurse the sick and bury the dead in 1793 (1750 to 1800)
- Anarcha: enslaved young woman on whom J. Marion Sims operated about thirty times with nothing to stop the pain (1800 to 1850)
- Elizabeth Blackwell: first woman in the United States to earn a medical degree (1800 to 1850)
- Clara Barton: took supplies to Civil War battlefields and founded the American Red Cross (1850 to 1900)
- Hannah Ropes: Civil War head nurse who reported a thieving steward to the Secretary of War and died of typhoid (1850 to 1900)
- Susan La Flesche Picotte: first Native American woman to earn a medical degree, doctor for the Omaha Nation (1850 to 1900)
- Clara Maass: nurse who died after letting infected mosquitoes bite her in a yellow fever experiment (1900 to 1950)
- Josie Mabel Brown: Navy nurse on the 1918 influenza wards at Great Lakes, Illinois (1900 to 1950)
- Charles Pollard: farmer kept in the Tuskegee study for 40 years who brought the men's lawsuit (1900 to 1950)
- Henrietta Lacks: Black woman whose cancer cells doctors took without asking, the first human cells to keep growing in a laboratory (1950 to 2000)
- Jonas Salk: doctor who made the first polio vaccine and did not patent it (1950 to 2000)

## Dates

- 1721: Boston doctors inoculate against smallpox.
- 1900: Army doctors near Havana prove that mosquitoes spread yellow fever.
- 1932: Public Health Service doctors begin the Tuskegee study.
- 1955: The polio vaccine is shown to work and approved for use.
- 2020: COVID-19 reaches the United States.
- 1616 to 1619: A disease kills Native people along the southern New England coast.
- June 24, 1763: Ecuyer and Trent give smallpox blankets to two Delaware men at Fort Pitt.
- August to November 1793: Yellow fever kills 4,041 people in Philadelphia, by Mathew Carey's count.
- July 8, 1800: Benjamin Waterhouse makes the first recorded vaccination in the United States.
- June 18, 1837: The steamboat St. Peters brings smallpox to the Mandan villages.
- October 16, 1846: Ether is given for an operation at Massachusetts General Hospital.
- January 23, 1849: Elizabeth Blackwell earns her medical degree.
- 1918: Influenza kills about 675,000 people in the United States.
- July 30, 1965: President Johnson signs Medicare and Medicaid.
- July 25, 1972: The Tuskegee study story runs in the Washington Star.

## Questions

- Q: Why did new diseases kill so many Native people after 1492? A: The crowd diseases, such as smallpox and measles, had never reached the Americas, so nobody here was immune and a whole town could fall sick at once. Smallpox killed about half of the Cherokee in 1738 and 1739.
- Q: How did Americans learn to stop smallpox, yellow fever and polio? A: Boston doctors tried inoculation in 1721, using a method Onesimus described, and safer vaccination followed in 1800. Army doctors proved in 1900 that mosquitoes spread yellow fever, and after the Salk and Sabin vaccines the last polio case caught inside the country came in 1979.
- Q: Who was used in medical experiments without agreeing, and what changed because of it? A: Enslaved women operated on by J. Marion Sims, the Black men of the Tuskegee study, prisoners and patients in Guatemala, and children at Willowbrook. After the Tuskegee study became public, members of Congress passed the National Research Act in 1974, and a federally paid study on people now needs each person's informed consent.
