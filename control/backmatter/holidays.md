## Glossary

- from time immemorial: since before anyone can remember (Before 1500)
- Faith Keepers: the Onondaga men and women who decide when each ceremony takes place (Before 1500)
- Onondaga: one of the Haudenosaunee nations (Before 1500)
- Thanksgiving Address: the words of thanks, to the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants and more, one at a time, that Haudenosaunee people often use to start and end their meetings, also called the Words That Come Before All Else (Before 1500)
- Cranberry Day: the day each October when the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) greets the sunrise, picks cranberries, shares a meal and sets aside a share for the elders (Before 1500)
- fasted: went without food (Before 1500)
- Green Corn Ceremony: the midsummer ceremony of the Muscogee (Creek) and other nations of the Southeast, held when the first corn was ripe (Before 1500)
- busk: William Bartram's name for the Green Corn Ceremony (Before 1500)
- stomp grounds: the grounds each Creek and Yuchi town keeps for the ceremony (Before 1500)
- Sun-priest: the man in a Pueblo town who watches where the sun comes up over the horizon and sets the planting calendar and the dates of the ceremonies (Before 1500)
- pueblo: the Spanish word for a town, which gave the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest their name (Before 1500)
- Soyal: the Hopi ceremony at the winter solstice that begins their year of ceremonies (Before 1500)
- winter solstice: the shortest day of the year (Before 1500)
- Niman: the Home Dance, the Hopi ceremony in late July (Before 1500)
- First Salmon Feast: the feast the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs and Nez Perce peoples hold each spring before the fishing season opens (Before 1500)
- longhouse: a long building where the community gathers for ceremonies (Before 1500)
- Pleiades: a small group of stars Hawaiians call Makaliʻi (Before 1500)
- Makahiki: the Hawaiian season after the new year began, when chiefs could not make war on each other (Before 1500)
- taro: a plant with a starchy root that Hawaiians cooked and ate (Before 1500)

## People

- William Bradford: governor of Plymouth Colony, who wrote the colony's history, Of Plimoth Plantation (The 1600s)
- Henry Wight: minister and Revolutionary War veteran who started the Fourth of July celebration in Bristol, Rhode Island, which the town still holds (1750 to 1800)
- John Anderson: enslaved man who became Hartford's Black governor in 1776, in a dispute that white officials investigated as a possible plot (1750 to 1800)
- Sarah Josepha Hale: magazine editor who asked governors and presidents for years to make Thanksgiving one national holiday (1800 to 1850)
- Jack Yates: Baptist minister, born enslaved, who led Black Houstonians in buying land for their Juneteenth celebrations (1850 to 1900)
- Anna Jarvis: woman who campaigned to create Mother's Day and then fought the businesses that made money from it (1900 to 1950)
- Frank James (Wamsutta): Wampanoag leader whose speech was stopped in 1970 and who started the National Day of Mourning (1950 to 2000)
- Liz Byrd: Cheyenne schoolteacher and lawmaker who brought the King holiday bill to Wyoming's legislature nine times (1950 to 2000)
- Opal Lee: retired Fort Worth teacher who organized Juneteenth celebrations for about 40 years and campaigned for it to become a national holiday (2000 to Today)

## Places

- Cole's Hill, Plymouth, Massachusetts: the hill above Plymouth Rock where the statue of Massasoit stands, with a plaque from the 1998 settlement, and where the National Day of Mourning is held at noon every Thanksgiving (the "Where to go" place)
- Post Office Square, Plymouth, Massachusetts: a plaque that tells of Metacom, whose head the English put on a pole in that square and left there for 25 years
- Emancipation Park, Houston, Texas: the ten acres Black Houstonians bought for Juneteenth in 1872, the oldest park in Houston, where Juneteenth celebrations are still held
- Arlington National Cemetery, Virginia: where the first national Decoration Day ceremony was held in 1868, on land that had been the home of Robert E. Lee
- Bristol, Rhode Island: the Fourth of July celebration held every year since 1785, which the town calls the oldest in the United States that has never stopped

## Questions

- Q: Who decided when Americans would give thanks, and why did it take until 1941 to fix the date? A: Governors and church elders set the days one at a time, until Lincoln proclaimed a national Thursday in 1863 at Sarah Josepha Hale's request. Every president after him named the day by proclamation, so when Franklin Roosevelt moved it in 1939 to help stores, sixteen states refused by the National Archives' count, and Congress ended the confusion with the 1941 law.
- Q: Which days did Black Americans make their own, from election days to Juneteenth? A: Black governors' election days in New England from about 1749, January 1 from 1808, July 5 in New York in 1827, August 1 after 1834, and Juneteenth from 1866, which Congress made a federal holiday in 2021.
- Q: Why did President Harrison proclaim the first Columbus Day in 1892, and why do some states no longer keep it? A: He proclaimed it for the 400th anniversary of Columbus's landing, a year and a half after a New Orleans mob lynched eleven Italian American men, and some writers say the holiday was partly meant to calm Italy. Since Berkeley in 1992, cities and 17 states have used the day to honor Indigenous peoples instead.
