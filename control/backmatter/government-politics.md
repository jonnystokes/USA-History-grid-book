## Glossary

- Haudenosaunee: a league of Native nations whose homelands lie mainly in what is now New York State and the Great Lakes region, a name that means "people who build a house" (Before 1500)
- Great Law of Peace: the law that joined the Haudenosaunee nations (Before 1500)
- wampum: belts of purple and white shell beads whose patterns recorded the league's laws, the stories told at ceremonies and dealings with other nations, never used as money (Before 1500)
- Grand Council: the league's lawmaking body, with fifty seats (Before 1500)
- hoyaneh: a leader who holds a seat on the Grand Council (Before 1500)
- Keepers of the Central Fire, of the Eastern Door and of the Western Door: the Onondaga, the Mohawk and the Seneca (Before 1500)
- consensus: a way of deciding in which every member must agree, so no law passes while even one member is against it (Before 1500)
- clan: a large group of related families (Before 1500)
- clan mother: a woman the members of a clan choose together, who serves for life, chooses the council members and can remove any hoyaneh (Before 1500)
- resolution: a written statement that lawmakers vote on (Before 1500)
- Iroquois: the more common English name for the Haudenosaunee (Before 1500)
- confederacy: a league of nations or states (Before 1500)
- Constitution: the written plan for how the national government of the United States works (Before 1500)
- anthropologist: a scientist who studies how peoples live (Before 1500)
- pueblos: the Spanish word for towns, the name the Spanish gave the farming towns along the Rio Grande (Before 1500)
- bow priests: the Zuni priests who handled dealings with outsiders (Before 1500)
- archaeologists: the scientists who dig up what people left behind (Before 1500)
- chiefdom: a society led by chiefs and by officials of high rank (Before 1500)
- Cahokia: a city of thousands on the Mississippi River, east of where St. Louis is now, led by chiefs (Before 1500)
- viceroy: the official who ruled in the king's name from Mexico City (The 1500s)

## Questions

- Q: Who decided who could vote, and how did the answer change? A: Each colony's and then each state's lawmakers decided, and the men who wrote the Constitution left it that way. Virginia's lawmakers took the vote from free Black men in 1723, New Jersey's lawmakers took it from women and free Black men in 1807, and Southern delegates took it from Black men again after 1890. Members of Congress took most of that power from the states with four amendments (1870, 1920, 1964, 1971), which state lawmakers approved, and with the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
- Q: When did judges first cancel a law, and what have they done with that power since? A: In 1803, in *Marbury v. Madison*. The justices have used it to allow forced sterilization, separate railroad cars and the wartime camps, and to strike down school segregation, official prayers, poll taxes and unequal districts.
- Q: What happens when people in power break the law? A: A jury, a court or the Senate decides. In 1614 royal judges found Juan de Oñate, a former governor of New Mexico, guilty of 12 charges and ordered him out of New Mexico for life. A jury found a cabinet secretary guilty of taking a bribe in 1929, and a judge sentenced him to a year in prison. A president resigned in 1974 rather than face a vote. Bill Clinton and Donald Trump were impeached, Trump twice, and the senators found neither guilty. A jury found a senator guilty of bribery in 2024, and he went to prison.
