## Glossary

- panic: a time when fear spreads, many people rush to take their money out of the banks at once, and businesses close (Before 1500)
- crash: a sudden fall in prices, of stocks or of homes (Before 1500)
- depression: a long stretch of hard times, lasting years, when businesses close and many people lose their work (Before 1500)
- recession: a shorter stretch of hard times, measured in months, when output falls and people lose their jobs (Before 1500)
- economy: the ways people have of making, sharing and trading the things they need (Before 1500)
- barter: swapping one good for another (Before 1500)
- archaeologist: a scientist who studies the past from the things people left behind (Before 1500)
- surplus: extra food, more than the farmers ate (Before 1500)
- specialist: a person who spends most of their time on one kind of work, such as making tools, instead of growing food (Before 1500)
- Mill Creek chert: a tough stone found only in southern Illinois (Before 1500)
- quarry: an open pit for stone (Before 1500)
- whelk: a large sea snail (Before 1500)
- obsidian: a natural glass that forms in volcanoes (Before 1500)
- indentured servant: a person who signed a contract to work for a master for a set number of years (The 1600s)
- indenture: an indentured servant's contract (The 1600s)
- freedom dues: what a master owed a servant at the end of the term, such as land, tools, clothes or farm animals (The 1600s)
- cash crop: a crop grown to sell rather than to eat (The 1600s)
- Chesapeake colonies: Virginia and Maryland, the two colonies around Chesapeake Bay (The 1600s)
- export trade: the business of sending goods away to be sold in another place (The 1600s)
- headright: Virginia's rule that whoever paid for a person's passage received 50 acres of land for that person (The 1600s)
- passage: the cost of the ocean trip (The 1600s)
- estate: a large holding of land (The 1600s)
- churchwardens: officers of the local church (The 1600s)
- parish: the church district where a person lived (The 1600s)
- correcting: in Virginia's old laws, punishing (The 1600s)
- assembly: in colonial Virginia, the colony's lawmakers (The 1600s)
- caste system: a system that sorts people at birth into ranks they can never leave (The 1600s)
- baptized: having gone through the church ceremony that makes a person a Christian (The 1600s)
- economist: a scholar who studies how people make and spend money (The 1600s)
- mercantilism: the belief that colonies existed to make the home country richer (The 1600s)
- finished goods: things already made (The 1600s)
- Parliament: England's lawmakers (The 1600s)
- enumerated goods: listed goods (The 1600s)
- indigo: a plant used to make blue dye (The 1600s)
- Lowcountry: the low land along the coast of South Carolina (1700 to 1750)
- merchant fleet: all the ships that carry a country's goods for trade (1700 to 1750)
- transportation: sending convicts away across the ocean as a punishment (1700 to 1750)
- felony: a serious crime (1700 to 1750)
- bound laborer: a person who had to work for whoever bought them until their term ended (1700 to 1750)
- pound (£): British money (1700 to 1750)
- molasses: a thick, dark syrup left over when sugar is made (1700 to 1750)
- Middle Passage: the crossing of the Atlantic Ocean on which crews carried captive Africans to be sold (1700 to 1750)
- duty: a tax on goods brought in (1700 to 1750)
- sixpence: six British pennies (1700 to 1750)
- smuggling: bringing goods in secretly (1700 to 1750)
- shackles: metal rings locked around a person's wrists or ankles (1750 to 1800)
- distillery: a place where rum is made (1750 to 1800)
- abolition society: a group working to end slavery (1750 to 1800)
- Continental dollars: the paper money the Continental Congress printed to pay for the Revolution (1750 to 1800)
- not worth a Continental: worth nothing at all (1750 to 1800)
- assumption: the national government taking over the states' war debts (1750 to 1800)
- credit: the trust lenders have that a borrower will pay back what it borrows (1750 to 1800)
- manufacturing: the making of goods in workshops, mills and factories (1750 to 1800)
- tariff: a tax on goods brought in from other countries (1750 to 1800)
- subsidy: money paid to help a business grow (1750 to 1800)
- excise: a tax on goods made and sold inside the country (1750 to 1800)
- tarred and feathered: coated with hot tar with feathers stuck to it (1750 to 1800)
- militia: soldiers drawn from the states (1750 to 1800)
- treason: the crime of making war against your own country (1750 to 1800)
- pardon: setting aside a person's punishment (1750 to 1800)
- market revolution: the change from families growing and making most of what they used to growing crops and making goods to sell, and buying what they needed (1800 to 1850)
- peninsula: a point of land with water on three sides (1800 to 1850)
- geologist: a scientist who studies rocks (1800 to 1850)
- King Cotton: the name for cotton, the most valuable American export, grown by enslaved people (1800 to 1850)
- Upper South: the older slave states farther north, such as Virginia (1800 to 1850)
- Deep South: the cotton states farther south (1800 to 1850)
- capital: money or property that an owner uses to make more money (1800 to 1850)
- census takers: the government workers who count every person in the country (1800 to 1850)
- mortgage: a loan in which the borrower promises property to the lender (1800 to 1850)
- bond: a paper promise to repay a loan with interest (1800 to 1850)
- back a loan or a bond: to promise to pay the lender or the buyer if the borrower cannot (1800 to 1850)
- collateral: property a borrower promises to hand over if the loan is not repaid (1800 to 1850)
- insurance policy: a written deal in which the buyer pays a fee and the company pays out money when something happens, such as a death (1800 to 1850)
- reparations: payments made to make up for a wrong (1800 to 1850)
- negro cloth: a cheap, rough cloth Northern mills made and sold to planters to clothe the people they enslaved, named with the word white Americans of that time used for Black people (1800 to 1850)
- lash: a whip (1800 to 1850)
- loom: a machine that weaves thread into cloth (1800 to 1850)
- call in loans: ask borrowers to pay back money right away (1800 to 1850)
- debtors' prison: a jail where a judge could lock up a borrower who could not pay, with no set date for release (1800 to 1850)
- pauper: a person too poor to feed themselves without help (1800 to 1850)
- bankrupt: unable to pay your debts, so that your lenders can take what you own (1800 to 1850)
- speculate: to buy something in the hope of selling it later at a higher price (1800 to 1850)
- unemployment: the share of people who want work and cannot find it (1800 to 1850)
- bushel: a measure of grain, about 60 pounds of wheat (1800 to 1850)
- creditor: a person who is owed money (1800 to 1850)
- encumber: to crowd or block (1800 to 1850)
- industrial country: a country where much of the work is done in mines, mills and factories (1850 to 1900)
- sharecropper: a member of a family who worked a landowner's land for a share of the crop and stayed in debt to him (1850 to 1900)
- lease: to rent out (1850 to 1900)
- Gilded Age: the name for the years 1850 to 1900, from an 1873 novel by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner (1850 to 1900)
- gilded: covered with a thin layer of gold (1850 to 1900)
- open range: land with no fences, where cattle grazed wherever they wandered (1850 to 1900)
- quarantine: keeping animals apart from others to stop the spread of a disease (1850 to 1900)
- lode: a long vein of metal running through rock (1850 to 1900)
- bank run: a rush by many people to take their money out of banks (1850 to 1900)
- investment bank: a firm that sells bonds and shares to raise money for businesses such as railroads (1850 to 1900)

- National Bureau of Economic Research: the group of economists that sets the dates when hard times begin and end (1850 to 1900)
- grain elevator: a tall building where grain is stored before it is shipped (1850 to 1900)
- husbandry: an old word for farming (1850 to 1900)
- freedpeople: the people freed from slavery (1850 to 1900)

## People

- John Rolfe: the Jamestown colonist whose tobacco shipments to London began Virginia's export trade (The 1600s)
- Eliza Lucas Pinckney: ran her father's three South Carolina plantations from the age of 16 and directed the colony's first successful indigo dye crop (1700 to 1750)
- Alexander Hamilton: wrote the plan to pay the Revolution's debts in full (1750 to 1800)
- Philip Hone: a New York merchant and former mayor whose diary recorded the Panic of 1837 as it happened (1800 to 1850)
- Andrew Carnegie: a Scottish immigrant who started as a mill boy, sold his steel company for 480 million dollars and gave away more than 350 million (1850 to 1900)
- John D. Rockefeller: founder of Standard Oil, which by 1880 controlled about 90 percent of American oil refining (1850 to 1900)
- Henry Ford: the carmaker who roughly doubled his workers' pay in 1914 (1900 to 1950)
- Benjamin Roth: a Youngstown, Ohio, lawyer who kept a diary of the Great Depression for more than ten years (1900 to 1950)
- Frances Perkins: the Secretary of Labor who chaired the committee that planned Social Security, and the first woman in a United States Cabinet (1900 to 1950)
- Gerald Dickey: a Youngstown steelworker who proposed that the workers and the town buy their closed mill and reopen it (1950 to 2000)
- Ed Neufeldt: an Elkhart, Indiana, worker who built RV cabinets for 32 years, lost his job in 2008 and introduced President Obama in 2009 (2000 to Today)

## Questions

- Q: How did slavery make money for people far from any plantation? A: Bankers in London, Amsterdam and New York sold bonds backed by mortgages on enslaved people, Northern companies insured their lives, and Boston mill owners spun the cotton that enslaved people grew. Rhode Island merchants sent out ships that carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery.
- Q: What happened to ordinary families when a boom turned into a crash? A: Judges jailed debtors in 1819, creditors sold enslaved families apart in 1837, one worker in four had no job by 1933, and lenders took 7.7 million to 10 million homes in the crisis that began in 2007. In the 1930s and after 2007, Black families lost jobs and homes at higher rates than white families.
- Q: When did the national government start helping people who lost their jobs? A: In the 1930s. Before 1933 the federal government paid no unemployment insurance or old-age pensions, and members of Congress set up both in the Social Security Act of 1935, though farm and domestic workers had to wait until 1950 and 1954.
