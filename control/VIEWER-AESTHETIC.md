# Visual Aesthetic — "The Lamplit Almanac"

*For the AI building the viewer. This describes the visual STYLE only — the look and feel, not the layout, controls, or behavior. One aesthetic, head to toe.*

## The feeling in one line
Opening a beautifully made, well-loved history almanac by warm lamplight — honest, calm, and quietly full of wonder. Old paper and real ink, but airy and clear enough for a curious young reader. It should feel *trustworthy*, because this is a book that never invents anything.

## Surface and light
The world is made of **warm paper**, not screen-white: a soft ivory/cream with the faintest tooth and grain, the way good book stock catches light. Everything sits under a **warm, low light**, like an afternoon window or a reading lamp — gentle, never harsh, never that cold blue glow. Shadows are soft and shallow, as if pressed lightly into the page. Nothing floats in empty digital black; the darkness, where it appears, is a deep warm ink, not pure black.

## Color
A restrained palette drawn from **real historic inks and natural dyes**, not bright digital primaries:
- **Base:** aged ivory and bone; a second, slightly deeper cream for depth.
- **Ink:** a warm near-black — dark walnut/sepia-charcoal, never a stark #000.
- **Accents (used sparingly, like colored plates in an old book):** indigo blue, iron-oxide brick red, ochre and antique gold, muted forest green, faded slate. These are the colors of dyed cloth, rust, and printer's ink — soft, a little faded, as though time has mellowed them.

Nothing is neon, glossy, or saturated. If a color feels like it came from a marker, it's wrong; if it feels like it came from a dye vat or an inkwell, it's right.

## Type
Typography does the heavy lifting, as it does in a real book. The **history prose is set in a classic, warm serif** — an old-style or transitional face with real character and excellent readability, the kind that has carried books for two centuries. It's generous: comfortable size, open leading, calm measure. Labels, dates, and small text use a **quiet humanist sans-serif** — clean and modern enough to keep a young reader oriented, but never sterile or techy. The pairing says *timeless book, clearly guided*. Small caps, old-style figures, and hairline rules are welcome; loud, condensed, or trendy display type is not.

## Ornament and imagery
The decorative language is **19th-century engraving and woodcut** — the crisp line-art of old almanacs, scientific plates, and botanical cuts — used with restraint and plenty of breathing room. Think fine hairline rules, a simple printer's flourish, the occasional engraved spot illustration or star. A whisper of celestial/almanac motif (a compass rose, a small constellation, a sunburst) can mark the passage of time and add a note of wonder — but sparingly, never busy. Any illustration looks *etched and printed*, monochrome or lightly tinted with the accent palette, never a glossy photo or a cartoon.

## Time, made visible
Because the whole point is moving through five centuries, let **color drift gently with the era** — the deep past a touch more sepia and earthen, the present a touch cooler and cleaner — but keep it one coherent family, a slow seasonal shift, never a costume change. Moving through time should feel like light changing over a long day, not like switching sets.

## The people
When the book zooms all the way in to one real person's story, it should feel **more intimate and warmer** — like a pressed portrait, an engraved plate, or a keepsake tucked into the page. A little hush, a little closeness, a slightly warmer light. These are true stories of real lives, and the look should honor that.

## In three words
**Warm. Printed. Honest.**
