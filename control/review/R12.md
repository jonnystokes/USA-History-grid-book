## Chapter 21: Crime, Police, and Justice

- [2000 to Today] "Of the victims of the crimes" : The sentence gives 76 of every 100 victims behind the executions as white and never says what that shows, so the reader may take it as plain background or form the wrong idea that most murder victims are white : Add the comparison the bank supports (the share of all murder victims who are white) and one plain sentence on what the gap means, or cut the sentence.
- [1850 to 1900] "President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers" : The reason Lincoln looked first for rape and then for massacres is never given, so the cut from 303 to 39 looks arbitrary, and the counts do not settle for a reader (39 approved, one reprieved, 38 hanged, two of them hanged by mistake). The section then ends on a one-line paragraph about the army's chief lawyer with no word on what followed : Add one sentence on why he chose those two kinds of crime, say plainly how many of the 38 were not on his list, and add one line on what the ruling about Sibley changed (or that it changed nothing).
- [1950 to 2000] "Alabama State Trooper James Bonard Fowler" : Six civil-rights cases run together with about twenty-five names and a verdict each. The three 1965 Alabama cases follow the Neshoba paragraph with no label, so they read as more of Neshoba, 1964, and the Beckwith paragraph also arrives under Birmingham with no label. The list ends with no line on what the cases add up to : Give each case a bold run-in label with place and year, keep only the main names, and end with one plain sentence on the pattern.
- [The 1600s] "In March, Tobias, the sachem Tuspaquin" : The Plymouth trial opens with Sassamon, Tobias, Tuspaquin and a son and never says who Tobias or Tuspaquin was, and the pledge sentence can be read more than one way. The next paragraph names a son again, so the reader cannot tell whose son is meant. Six named advisers add six more names that do no work, and the section ends on King Philip's War with no word on what it was or how it links to the trial : Say in one clause who Tobias and Tuspaquin were, make clear who pledged and whose son is meant, replace the six names with a count, and either explain the war in a sentence or end on the hangings.
- [1900 to 1950] "If Mollie's sister Anna, their mother" : The inheritance plan cannot be followed in one read (who dies in what order, whose headrights pass to whom, why that ends with Ernest and then Hale), and the next paragraph adds five more names in one paragraph : Rewrite the plan as two or three short steps in plain order, and give the victims as a short list or with fewer names.
- [1850 to 1900] "It records 4,075 Black people lynched" : The sentence gives 4,075 killed in twelve Southern states from 1877 to 1950 and then more than 4,400 in all, with nothing saying what the second total adds, and this is the chapter's main figure for its question on mobs : Say which killings the larger total includes (other states, other years) or give one total.
- [1950 to 2000] "Officials of the Bureau of Justice" : Three short paragraphs hold about a dozen figures (counts, rates per 100,000, shares), and the era opener and closer already gave the 1950, 1972 and 2000 numbers, so the reader meets them a third time and cannot tell which matter : Keep four counts (1950, 1972, 1990, 2000) and the one gap between Black and white men, as a short list, and say in words what the climb means.
- [2000 to Today] "Courts cleared thousands of people of" : The era opener packs about thirty numbers, years included, into three paragraphs (prison counts, exonerations, police killings, body cameras, murder rates), and the death-penalty section later puts about fifteen into one paragraph, so the reader hits a wall of statistics before any story : Open with the direction (fewer prisoners, the same gap) and two numbers, and leave the rest to the sections that explain them.
- [1850 to 1900] "That year the committee's members hanged" : One paragraph holds six counts written as words (hanged, whipped, sent to Australia, told to leave, handed to the courts, set free) that nothing joins, so the one point, that private citizens ran trials outside the courts, is lost : Keep the hangings and the missing fair trial, and fold the other counts into one plain sentence or cut them.
- [The 1500s] "Two Hopi men were to have" : Hopi men appear for the first time in the list of sentences with no word on who they were or why they were at Acoma, and Vicente de Zaldívar arrives in the paragraph right after Juan de Zaldívar was killed with no word on whether they are related, so a reader may take them for one man : Add a clause saying who the Hopi men were (or that the record does not say), and say how Vicente relates to Juan or mark him as a different man.
- [The 1600s] "Spanish officials later put Juan de" : A section on Oñate's trial in Mexico City sits among English-colony sections with no bridge back to the Acoma trial in the 1500s, so a reader who opens the 1600s alone does not know who he is or what the Acoma charge refers to : Open with one clause naming him as the leader who sentenced the Acoma people in 1599.
- [The 1600s] "The punishment was a whipping for" : The paragraph carries three ideas, the list of whipping crimes, a disagreement between Tarter and the printed 1612 laws over whether adultery brought whipping or death, and the end of army rule in 1619, which is tacked on as a last sentence : Split them so the whipping crimes come first, then one plain line on the adultery disagreement, then the 1619 end on its own line.
- [The 1600s] "English settlers built Salem on Naumkeag," : A paragraph on the Pawtucket and a plague of 1616 to 1619 drops into the middle of the witch-trial summary, after the death count and before the first accusers, with no bridge, so the thread breaks : Move it to the start of the Salem section or tie it to the story in a sentence, so the summary runs straight into the accusers.
- [1750 to 1800] "As many as 200 of the" : The Conestoga story ends on a march to Germantown, a meeting with Franklin and a published list of complaints, with no word on why the men marched or what came of it, and the real point (no one was charged) was made in the sentence just before : Say in one sentence why the men marched and what Franklin's meeting led to, or end on the point that no one was charged.
- [1900 to 1950] "Gunmen killed four lawmen in Kansas" : Two unconnected events sit in two sentences (the 1933 Kansas City killings, the 1934 Dillinger shooting), so a reader can take Dillinger for the killer of the four lawmen, and the Kansas City gunmen are never named or caught : Say who the Kansas City gunmen were or that Dillinger had no part in it, or cut the first sentence.
- [1900 to 1950] "Parden went to Washington and met" : The Ed Johnson story carries eight date mentions in five short paragraphs (January 23, January 24 or 25, February 11, March 10, March 17, a March 18 or 19 disagreement, March 19 again), so the main line, that the Supreme Court ordered him kept safe and the sheriff sent the guards away, gets buried : Keep the month and two or three dates and put the dating disagreement in one short note.
- [1950 to 2000] "James David Martin, 17, killed Michael" : The section never says why a 1989 killing for a pair of shoes belongs in a chapter on justice, so the reader gets a graphic crime story with no point stated, and the last sentence reports a third killing as something three newspapers said, with no outcome : State the point in a sentence if the bank supports one (for example the short sentence and the later killing), and either settle the Allentown claim or cut it.
- [1950 to 2000] "Members of the American Indian Movement" : The section opens on an armed takeover and never says what the movement was or what its members wanted, so the reader gets the shootings and the trial without the reason for any of it : Add one or two sentences on who the movement was and why it took Wounded Knee, and tie the trial back to that.
- [1750 to 1800] "The Fifth forbids forcing anyone to" : The old phrase about being a witness against oneself is not explained, so a reader does not know what is forbidden, and the right to stay silent returns in the Miranda paragraph in 1950 to 2000 with no link back : Add a plain clause (a person cannot be forced to say they did it) and point to it again in the Miranda paragraph.
- [card] "Five minutes?" : The pick is one section of about 470 words, roughly two and a half minutes at the pace the strips use, and it answers only the first of the three questions, so a reader with five minutes leaves with nothing on who kept order or on the mobs : Add a short second pick on mobs (for example the McIntosh section, about 400 words) or change the card to say what the pick covers.
- [all eras] "The newspaper does not name who" : A sentence saying a source does not name who did it, or does not say how, appears about 26 times in the chapter, beside about a dozen sentences saying accounts differ, and each one stops the story until the reader learns to skip them : Keep the rule but state it once per section or in a Not known line as Era 1 does, and keep a sentence only where the missing name is the point.
- [closing] "The chapter begins with families settling" : The last sentence of What changed strings two statements together and ends on a clause that cannot be read in one pass, and it also claims that in every era a fair trial depended on who the person was, though the first era describes no trial : Split it into two sentences, state the claim plainly, and say from when it holds.
- [1700 to 1750] "Both men were tried in 1741" : The box opening repeats what the section just said, that Quack and Cuffee had no lawyer (already in the era opener too, so three times) and were burned, and the confession at the stake is told twice within five lines : Keep the no-lawyer fact in the section and the hook, and say the confession once.
- [1800 to 1850] "The clans agreed to an" : Era 1 says a council ended the clan revenge rules about 1809, and this section says the clans ended only the rule on accidental killings in 1810, so the agent and the scope differ, and the section does not point back to the law of blood : Make the two accounts agree on who ended which rule, and open the 1808 section with a link back to the Cherokee law of blood.
- [card] "Watch who got a trial." : A bare command in the card paragraph reads as performed rather than explained, and the Era 9 closer does the same with a rhetorical question and a teaser saying the next era opens with the answer : State the point as a plain sentence (whether a person got a trial depended on who the person was) and make the Era 9 closer a statement of what the next era shows.

Flow: uneven. The story boxes and the early eras read easily in short plain sentences, but from 1850 on the chapter stacks numbers, names and source notes with no line saying what they add up to.
