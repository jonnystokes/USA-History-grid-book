# R17 reader review: Chapter 28

## Chapter 28: Drugs and Alcohol

- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Treasury Department officials under President Calvin" : This is the section the card sends a reader to with five minutes, but it starts in the middle of the story. It never says that alcohol was banned (the word Prohibition appears once, inside a job title) and bootleggers is not explained here, so a reader who jumps straight in cannot tell why the government would poison alcohol or who was drinking it : Open the section with one plain sentence that a national ban on alcohol was in force and gloss bootleggers in a clause, or change the card to point at this section together with the Prohibition paragraph in the era strip.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "A gang of armed men in" : From here the whiskey-tax section brings in nine named men in about ten short paragraphs (a tax collector, a man serving court papers, an inspector, a marshal, two men who were killed, an army commander, Hamilton, Washington), each for a sentence or two, with nothing holding them together. The event is never called the Whiskey Rebellion, and the Not known line brings in a soldier who was never mentioned at Bower Hill : Group the paragraphs under three plain labels (two tax men tarred and feathered, the fight at Bower Hill, the army), name the rebellion once at the top, and cut or explain the soldier line.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "The CDC's final counts of overdose" : This paragraph and the next give ten figures in six sentences (final and early counts, all drugs and opioids only, 2017 to 2026), and two of them, 79,358 and 79,384, sit in back-to-back sentences and look like a typo. The reader loses the one point, that deaths rose, peaked and fell : Say that in one sentence first, keep three or four numbers, say once what early count and final count mean, and drop or table the rest.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Most of the $5.5 billion will" : The Purdue money cannot be followed: a $5.5 billion fine that mostly will not be paid, $225 million collected, other money to states and towns, $865 million for victims, and in other paragraphs $7.4 billion and $2.9 billion, with no sentence saying which figure is which or how they fit together : Rewrite as one plain sentence on who gets what, and say whether the $2.9 billion is part of the $7.4 billion.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "Judges in Massachusetts punished Robert Coles" : Two story boxes in a row are about Massachusetts colonists named Cole and Coles (Samuel Cole, the tavern keeper, then Robert Coles), and both are tied to 1630, Winthrop's fleet and 1634. Nothing says they are different men, so a reader can merge them into one drunk who kept a tavern : Add a clause to each box that the other man is someone else, or give each heading a tag (the tavern keeper, the man with the red D), and put other material between the boxes.
- [Card] "People in this land have used" : The one-line spine says drugs and alcohol for at least 12,000 years, but the only thing in the chapter that old is tobacco, and the chapter gives no age for alcohol in this land beyond saying it was before Europeans came. The first line every reader sees gives the wrong idea about alcohol : Say tobacco, or plants that change the mind and body, for the 12,000 years, and keep alcohol out of that clause.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Idaho lawmakers in 1887 punished" : This example sits under the bold label about laws that named race, but it is a law that punished white people who kept or used opium dens, which reads as the opposite of the point and leaves the reader to guess why it is here : Add one sentence on why the Idaho law named white people (from the bank, or say the records do not say) and tie it to the 1887 Congress law in the next sentence.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Federal law stopped barring Native people" : This opens by ending a ban on Native people buying alcohol off their nations' land, but no earlier section describes that ban. The earlier sections describe a ban on bringing liquor onto Native land and say Native people themselves could not be charged, so the reader meets what looks like a contradiction and cannot tell which law ended : Say in one sentence what the older law barred and how it differed from the 1834 and 1854 laws, or reword so it ends the ban already described.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Thousands of people were poisoned in" : The jake leg paragraph never says why people drank Jamaica ginger, a medicine, so a reader wonders why anyone would. It also says thousands here (and in the card) and then 30,000 to 50,000 a few lines later : Add a clause on why people drank it (if the bank has it) and say tens of thousands, or give the range first.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Under the law, a person caught" : This tells the 5 grams versus 500 grams fact for the second time in two paragraphs (the era strip told it first), and then the treatment figures, 53 and 38 of every 100, sit beside the sentencing figure with no sentence saying what the comparison shows : Tell the sentencing fact once, and add one sentence on what the treatment numbers are meant to show, or cut them.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Wells quoted words Willard had spoken" : The quote is not about lynching and the text never says what Wells meant by it, so the reader cannot see how it supports the charge of silence. The locust phrase is left unexplained and it is unclear which charge Willard denied : Add one sentence on why Wells quoted it, gloss the locust phrase, and say plainly which charge was denied.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Some officials and newspaper writers in" : The section is titled for the Harrison Act but opens with racist claims about Black and Chinese people and does not reach the act until its fifth paragraph, so the reader does not know why the claims are there until the dispute paragraph : Open with one sentence on what the act did, then give the claims and the dispute as the reason it matters.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "He was drunk again at Roxbury" : The red D scene is the card's opening paragraph told again here almost word for word (red cloth, white background, hung over his clothes, Winthrop's journal line), about 14 minutes of reading later, so a reader going straight through meets the same scene twice : Keep one full telling and give the other a short line, and let the box spend its space on the vote taken away and given back, the church putting him out, and the move to Providence.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "By 1900, about one in every" : The heading promises who was in prison, but the sentence says only that they were found guilty of a crime involving alcohol and Native people, with no word on whether they were sellers or Native drinkers (the earlier text says Native people could not be charged), and the 1910 sentence about lighter punishments has the same gap : Say who the guilty were, or say the records do not say.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "The law set a tax of" : The tax is given as 7 cents a gallon, then large distillers pay as little as 6 and a farmer with a small still pays about 9, so the reader cannot tell what the tax was or why small farmers paid more : Say plainly that the rate depended on how a distiller paid and that small stills paid more per gallon than large ones, with one number each.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "Farmers in western Pennsylvania turned their" : This opening paragraph holds whiskey as money, a 1768 land treaty and who did not sign it, and the next paragraph repeats whiskey as money. Fort Stanwix was also the 1784 treaty two sections earlier, so the reader may think it is the same one : Keep the opening on whiskey, move the land history to its own short paragraph, and say it was an earlier treaty at the same fort.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "President George H. W. Bush held" : The paragraph holds three ideas: the bag on television, the fact that agents lured the seller to the park (left unexplained, so the reader does not see why it matters), and a 2000 spending total that arrives with no bridge : Say in a clause why the lure matters, and give the 2000 budget its own paragraph.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Wheeler and other prohibitionists gained support" : Two links are missing: how the fight for the women's vote gave the ban support, and how anger at Germany did, so the reader is handed two claims with no reason : Add a clause for each link from the bank, or cut the claim.
- [Card] "the businessman whose Jamaica ginger poisoned" : The last paragraph of the card names the Sackler family and Jamaica ginger, which a young reader cannot know yet, and the chapter does not explain them until the 1900 to 1950 and 1950 to 2000 eras : Give each a few plain words in the card, for example the family that owned the company that made OxyContin, and the man whose ginger medicine left people without the use of their hands and feet.
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "On June 1, 1586, men under" : The Hariot box turns from tobacco to the killing of Wingina with no bridge, and nothing says Hariot saw it or why it belongs in a chapter on drugs, so the reader may think the story changed subject : Add one line that links it (Wingina is the leader who let the English stay, already named above) and say what Hariot did or knew, or that the records do not say.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "The same year, members of the" : After the paragraph on the Sally's dead, this jumps to a tax law with no sentence tying it to rum or the slave trade, and the reader is not told why cutting a tax or letting navy officers seize smuggled goods matters : Say in a clause what the law was meant to do about molasses, or give it its own short section.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "In New Netherland, the Dutch colony" : The start of the regular liquor trade is dated about 1650 four times (the 1500s strip, the Spanish wine section, the 1600s strip and the fur section), yet the bans here are dated 1643, 1645 and 1647, before the trade began, so a careful reader sees a contradiction and the date reads like a drumbeat : Give the date once with the hedge that some liquor was sold earlier, and say that the 1643 ban shows it.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "About 5.2 of every 100 middle" : The vaping figures compare different groups, 27.5 of every 100 high school students in 2019 and then 5.2 of every 100 middle and high school students in 2025, so the drop looks bigger than it is : Use the same group in both years or say the groups differ.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Carry Nation smashed saloons in Kansas" : In 13 of the 14 story boxes (every box but Hariot's) the first paragraph says what the Who line has just said, almost word for word, so the reader reads the same sentence twice within five lines : Start the first paragraph with something new, the first scene or fact, and leave the summary to the Who line.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Smokers fell from about 42 of" : The era's one-thing line carries three separate ideas (smoking, the drug budget, crack sentences) in one 43-word sentence and leaves out OxyContin, the era's last section. The question line under it reads as a cliffhanger, and the Next line right below answers it : Pick one idea for the first line, and drop the question or fold it into Next.

Flow: uneven. The people sections (Rush, Gough, Carry Nation, Einstein, Betty Ford, Nan Goldin) are vivid and define their words well, but the whiskey-tax run, the Native-nations liquor laws and the 2000 to Today numbers bury the reader in names and figures, and several paragraphs end on a fact that belongs somewhere else.
