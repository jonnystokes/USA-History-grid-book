## Chapter 4: Immigration to America

- [1950 to 2000] "The Mexican writer Rafael Lemus writes" : The reader is told officials accused Reinaldo Arenas of abusing children and is never told whether that was proved, denied or only claimed. Three versions of the charge follow one another with no verdict, so a 12-year-old can leave thinking the worst may be true. The box also lists his book as published in 1992 before his death in 1990, with no word that it came out after he died : Say what the sources conclude about the accusation, or that the records do not settle it, keep one plain account of the charge, and add that the book was printed after his death.
- [2000 to Today] "Staff of a federal group, the" : The counts of separated children do not add up for the reader. Of 3,924 children taken, more than 600 were reunited and 998 were still apart, which leaves about 2,300 unaccounted for, and the next paragraph adds three more figures. The line above says counts differ because each measures something different but never says what each one measures : Give each count a plain label (taken from 2017 to 2021, still apart in 2023, covered by the lawsuit), say where the other children went, or keep two counts.
- [2000 to Today] "Every year hundreds of migrants die" : Twelve death counts from several agencies fill the section and point different ways. The IOM's 2022 figure is lower than the Border Patrol's, yet the auditors call the Border Patrol's too low and the IOM calls its own a minimum, so the reader cannot tell which number to believe : Say once that the agencies count different things, give one range for 2022 with one line on why it is a floor, and move the yearly figures into a short list.
- [The 1600s] "In November 1637 the colony's lawmakers" : The box never says what Anne Hutchinson did or taught that got her tried and banished. The hook is that the court gave no answer, but the reader has not been told the charge, so the line has nothing to push against and the only reason on the page is the governor's sentence : Add one or two plain sentences on the charge and who objected (Chapter 5 does this for Roger Williams), from the research bank.
- [The 1600s] "She moved to Portsmouth, Rhode Island," : The paragraph swerves into the Dutch governor's night killing of 80 to 120 Munsee people, and the next paragraph says Siwanoy fighters killed her. Nothing says the two events are linked, who the Siwanoy were, or why they attacked her household, so the reader sees two killings and cannot connect them : Add one sentence on how the war reached her family, or say the records do not give the reason, and say who the Siwanoy were.
- [1850 to 1900] "Members of Congress passed the Page" : The chapter makes three first-law claims (the Page Act first to limit immigration, the Exclusion Act first to bar a group by nationality, the 1921 act first with number limits), but the Page Act as told already shut out workers from China and other Asian countries, so the reader cannot see what was new in 1882. The women paragraph then has officers turning women away under the Page Act though the law as told says nothing about women, and ethnicity is glossed in a clause a 10-year-old cannot parse : Say what the Page Act did and did not cover (workers under contract, women said to be prostitutes), say why 1882 differed (every Chinese laborer, whatever his contract), and gloss ethnicity plainly.
- [Closing] "Lawmakers shut out Chinese workers in" : The answer to the second question stops in 1954, but the last era (17 minutes) is about children taken from parents at the border and record detention, so a reader who has just finished it finds an answer that ignores it. The first answer says more people ran from danger than came for land and work, a comparison the chapter never counts, and the third answer skips the step that let people of African birth or descent become citizens : Add one clause on 2018 and 2025 to the second answer, write that others ran from danger in the first, and add the missing step to the third.
- [All eras] "John H. Wise, the official in" : Every story box says its main facts in the Who line, again in the hook, again in the first paragraph, and often once more as the story goes. Wong Kim Ark's refusal at the port appears in all four of those places, and the Annie Moore, Sergey Brin, Jette Bruns and Doukenie Bacos boxes give their headline three times in the first ten lines. A reader with ADHD stops reading the opening paragraph : Let the Who line and the hook carry the headline, and open each first paragraph with what happened first.
- [2000 to Today] "The Census Bureau's American Community Survey" : This section packs 29 numbers and years into 14 sentences (population shares in four different years, five countries, two regions, one region's growth), and its first three sentences repeat the era strip just above almost word for word. The reader cannot hold more than two or three of them, and the answer to the previous closer's question is buried in the second paragraph : Open with Mexico as the biggest source, keep one line on the share being the highest since 1910, add one sentence on Asia and Latin America, and put the rest in a short list.
- [1950 to 2000] "INS officials claimed that as many" : Six different counts (the officials' claim, the INS report, two Texas figures, one for San Antonio, one for the historian) sit in three short paragraphs, and the point, that the real number was far lower, arrives last. The reader finishes holding six numbers and no number to trust : Open with the point (officials claimed more than a million and a historian puts it near 300,000), give the one reason the official figure is too high, and drop the district figures.
- [1900 to 1950] "Laughlin also wrote a model law," : The section is about the men behind the 1924 immigration law, but for four paragraphs it is about forced sterilization of Americans and Nazi Germany, and nothing says why that belongs in an immigration chapter. Carrie Buck appears only as a Virginia woman with no word on who she was or why she was chosen, and Madison Grant, who helped write the 1924 law, comes last : Add one sentence saying the same men and ideas drove the sterilization laws and the immigration law, move Grant ahead of the sterilization paragraphs, and give Buck two lines on who she was.
- [1900 to 1950] "At that time the law let" : The third question's thread breaks here. In 1790 the law allowed only free white people to become citizens, but here it also allows people of African birth or descent, and the chapter never says when or how that changed, here or in the closing answer : Add one sentence where it fits (after the Civil War, with the year, from the bank) and carry it into the closing answer.
- [1900 to 1950] "The ship turned back to Europe" : Eight head-counts in five sentences, and they do not add up. Of 620 who went back to the mainland, 87 moved on, 254 were killed and 278 survived, but 620 minus 87 is 533 and 254 plus 278 is 532. A reader who adds will stop trusting the paragraph : Give the two figures that matter (254 killed, 278 survived) and check the 620 and the 87 against the bank.
- [1850 to 1900] "Revolutionaries murdered the ruler of the" : Six weeks after the tsar's murder a crowd attacks Jews, and the reader is never told why Jews were the target or whether the murder had anything to do with it. The next paragraph says who joined in, not why : Add one sentence on what the sources say led the crowd to turn on Jews, or say the records give no single cause.
- [1850 to 1900] "Italians, Poles, Hungarians and Greeks came" : The heading promises others from southern and eastern Europe, but they get one sentence and no reason for leaving, and the Frank Capra box later gives none. The chapter's first question, why people left home, goes unanswered for the groups the 1924 law was aimed at : Add one or two plain sentences on why these groups came (from the bank), or narrow the heading to the Jews from the Russian Empire.
- [2000 to Today] "The death rate reached about 8.4" : The sentence compares the rate with the previous president's, but the era names no president before Donald Trump's 2025 swearing-in, so DACA, the 2017 decision to end it and the 2018 separations carry no president at all and the reader cannot tell whose term is meant. The rate also has no time unit : Name the president, or whose term, the first time each event appears, and say per year.
- [2000 to Today] "An ICE officer shot Silverio Villegas" : His death is told here among the deaths in custody and again twelve lines later under shootings, with the same date and place, and the shootings paragraph says at least four people were shot but names only three : Tell it once, in the shootings paragraph, name the fourth person or say three are named here, and point back from the custody list.
- [2000 to Today] "Lawmakers ended the old INS and" : Five acronyms (DHS, INS, USCIS, ICE, CBP) in five sentences, and the reader must keep ICE, CBP and USCIS apart for the rest of the era. The memo paragraph later stacks five officials' names, titles and acronyms in four sentences, so who decided what is lost : Say what each agency does in plain words and keep only DHS, ICE and CBP as short forms, and in the memo paragraph state the decision first and name the officials after it.
- [1850 to 1900] "Three years earlier, in 1883, the" : The Statue of Liberty section retells the chapter's opening page almost word for word (the poem, its two lines, nobody reading it in 1886, the 1903 plaque), so a reader who started at the top meets the same paragraph again. The opening's point, that the Exclusion Act came a year before the poem, is not tied back here : Point back to the poem at the start, give only what is new (Lazarus's life, her work with refugees), and add one clause linking 1882 and 1883.
- [1900 to 1950] "Her uncle was a bachelor, a" : An uncle arrives from nowhere because the box never says Doukenie Bacos was traveling to him, and a woman he called her aunt leaves the reader unsure whether the aunt was real. The paragraph before is a pile of record details (full name, ship, two dates, a 1919 mix-up) that holds back the hook : Say whom she was coming to, say what the aunt was, and move the full name and the 1919 note to the end.
- [1750 to 1800] "When the husband of Marie Elisabeth" : A new person arrives with no introduction, since the box has named only the Bérard family. The box also calls him Toussaint throughout and then says he took the surname Toussaint when freed, so the reader thinks it is a slip : Introduce her and her husband when the Bérards first appear, and say he was known as Pierre until he took the surname as a free man.
- [1800 to 1850] "A national ban on bringing enslaved" : The Era 6 strip ends on this sentence with no bridge, after sentences about Germans, the Irish and the mobs of 1834 and 1844, so the strip jumps back 26 years to a new subject. A reader who reads only the strips will not know why it is there : Move it above the Irish and mob sentences with a few words of bridge, or cut it, since the section below tells it.
- [1900 to 1950] "Some detainees killed themselves. The historian" : The second sentence can be read as someone driving them to it, and some then a few disagree. In the same section the paper sons paragraph assumes the reader knows Chinese Americans born here were citizens, which comes from the Wong Kim Ark box and not from this section, so a reader who comes straight to this Five minutes pick cannot follow it : Write the sentence plainly (some detainees killed themselves, most by hanging in the bathrooms, Judy Yung said, with no names or count) and add a clause that people born here were citizens, so a family tie could be claimed.
- [Card] "Chapter 4 of 37" : The card says about 95 minutes, but the ten era strips add to 104 and the chapter runs about 20,500 words, about 103 minutes at the strips' own pace of 200 words a minute. The Era 1 strip says about 3 minutes for about 250 words, which is about a minute : Make the card total match the strips and set Era 1 to about 1 or 2 minutes.
- [Card] "Five minutes? Read" : The pick is the Angel Island section (about 450 words) and its Lee Puey You box (about 200), about 650 words or three minutes at the strips' own pace, and the card does not say the box belongs to it : Say about three minutes and name the box, or point the card at a section that takes five.

Flow: uneven. The early eras and most story boxes read plainly, but the last three eras stack counts, agencies and names faster than a reader can hold them, and nearly every box says its facts two or three times.

## Chapter 5: Migration Across America

- [1900 to 1950] "United States Army officers forced more" : The section never says why the Army removed Japanese Americans (the war with Japan, the reason officials gave, how it was judged later), and it stops at the second set of camps without saying how long people were held or when they went home. The reader gets the how and none of the why or the end : Add one sentence on the reason officials gave and how it was judged later, and one on when the camps closed.
- [1800 to 1850] "This trip was called the" : The chapter names the 1843 Oregon Trail trip the Great Migration, and about 235 lines later uses the same name for the 1910 to 1970 move of 6 million Black Southerners (Chapter 4 also uses it for the Puritans of the 1630s). A reader who learned the first meaning is thrown by the second : Drop the name for 1843, or say it is a different event from the Great Migration of the 1900s.
- [1850 to 1900] "Settlers filled the Great Plains and" : The strip and the closer say soldiers forced the Native nations of the Plains onto reservations, but the era tells only the Navajo, who lived in the Southwest, plus one sentence saying many other nations were forced onto reservations. The Plains nations the horse story introduced never come back : Add one short Plains example from the bank, or change the strip and closer to say what the era shows.
- [1800 to 1850] "More than 500 Chickasaw died of" : The strip, the section opening and the closer all name five nations, but the Chickasaw get one sentence tucked into a paragraph about Creek steamboats and the Seminole get none, so the closer's claim that all five were forced out outruns what the section tells : Add a line each for the Chickasaw and the Seminole, or say the section tells three in full.
- [1850 to 1900] "In 1880 members of a United" : The paragraph gives only what the senators who disagreed with the committee's majority wrote, and never what the majority said, so the reader cannot tell what the committee decided and may take the dissent as its finding : Add the majority's conclusion in one sentence.
- [1750 to 1800] "By the fall of 1755, soldiers" : Two counts look like a contradiction, about 1,100 Acadians shipped by the fall and then 2,242 from one district alone. The paragraph before piles five tallies for the buildings Winslow burned, so the numbers crowd out the scene : Say the 1,100 went to three colonies and the 2,242 went to all places, and give only the 698 total for the buildings.
- [1850 to 1900] "Bryan sold people in family groups" : The sentence says Bryan sold families together as far as the sale's terms allowed and then that the same terms kept husbands and wives apart, but never says what the terms were, so the two halves seem to contradict : Say what the terms were, from the bank, or say plainly that some families were split.
- [1850 to 1900] "More than 1,000 Latter-day Saint emigrants" : The heading asks why the handcart companies were trapped, but the section says only that they left late and an early blizzard struck. The reason they left late appears only in the Levi Savage box below, so a reader who skips the box has no answer : Put the reason in one sentence in the section.
- [2000 to Today] "The couple went to a Red" : The paragraph before says Kimberly's uncle was found dead, and here the couple go to her uncle's trailer, so the reader cannot tell whether it is the same man. The hook says she filmed the flood on a $20 camcorder, and the body never mentions the price or the camera : Write another uncle here and add one sentence on the camcorder to the body.
- [1900 to 1950] "The Black population of Chicago grew" : Growth is given in percent for Chicago, Philadelphia and Detroit and then as about doubled for New York with no years on its figures, so the reader cannot compare the cities. Philadelphia's 500 percent looks out of line beside New York's doubling : Say times as many for each city, give the years for New York, and check Philadelphia's figure against the bank.
- [Card] "Many of the biggest moves across" : The spine is one 51-word sentence. It says the biggest moves were forced and then lists the Great Migration among them though those families went north to get away, it uses lynch mobs before the chapter explains them, and it says slave traders marched more than a million people south where the body says some were sold by traders, some taken along by enslavers and some shipped : Split it into two or three sentences, say forced or driven, and use plain words for lynch mobs or define them.
- [The 1600s] "The Ute, Navajo and Apache got" : The older account spends two paragraphs on a witchcraft arrest, a hanging and a revolt before the horses return, and then names eleven nations in two sentences, so the reader loses the thread between horses spread by trade and the names : Tie the revolt to the horses in one sentence up front (the Spanish fled and left their horses), and name three nations, then say many others.
- [1700 to 1750] "Both groups had come to Pennsylvania" : Chapter 4 says the Germans went to Pennsylvania for cheap land, and here the same Pennsylvania land is too costly for them, with no word that prices rose in between, so a reader who has just read Chapter 4 sees a contradiction : Say that Pennsylvania land had grown costly by then, if the bank supports it, so families moved on.
- [The 1600s] "He led about 100 members of" : The Hooker walk is told five times in about 40 lines (the era strip, the section on early inland moves, the Who line, the hook, and this first paragraph, which repeats the section's sentence almost word for word) : Keep the strip and the box, and cut the section's sentence to a pointer.
- [1800 to 1850] "The trader locked a strong iron" : The box retells the chapter's opening page nearly sentence for sentence (the collar, the padlock, the 100-foot chain, handcuffs in pairs, more than a month to Columbia), so a reader who read the opening meets it again 290 lines later : Point back to the opening and tell only what is new (the second sale into Georgia, the two escapes, the book).
- [All eras] "Ida Mae Brandon Gladney and her" : This first paragraph repeats the Who line and the hook almost word for word, and the same pattern runs through nearly every box in the chapter (the Rebecca Neugin and Brigham Young boxes restate their Who line in the first paragraph, and the Amelia Stewart Knight box ends on its hook again) : Let the Who line and the hook carry the headline, and open each first paragraph with what happened first.
- [1950 to 2000] "Officials of the Bureau of Indian" : Relocation is told as a travel offer (a one-way ticket and a little help), and the reader is never told why the government wanted Native families in cities or what the program was meant to do. The chapter keeps asking who decided people would go, but here the why is missing : Add one sentence on the aim officials gave (from the bank) and one on what families were promised against what they found.
- [1850 to 1900] "Amelia Stewart Knight crossed the Oregon" : Era 7 opens with a strip about reservations and free land and then goes straight into a wagon diary from 1853 with no sentence connecting them, so a reader who has just left the Oregon Trail section of Era 6 thinks the book has gone back. - [1900 to 1950] "Black Georgians had several reasons to" : The paragraph on why people left is about one state without saying Georgia is an example, and the closing answer to the third question leans on that one state's numbers for the whole 6 million : Say Georgia is one example, and add one line on what drew people from other states.
- [1900 to 1950] "Starling organized the pickers to ask" : The pay sentences are muddled. The price at the packing house is said to be ten cents a box for picking, the pickers ask for twenty, and then tangerines sell for $4.40 a box with no word on why that matters, so the reader cannot see the unfairness the story is about : Write three plain facts (pickers got ten cents a box, they asked for twenty, the fruit sold for $4.40 a box).
- [1700 to 1750] "The road followed a Native path" : Eight nation names in five sentences (Shawnee, Iroquois, Delaware, Conoy, Haudenosaunee, Six Nations, Seneca, Catawba), with three names for one league, and then the Lancaster treaty has the Six Nations sell a valley where the Shawnee lived without a word on the Shawnee : Name two peoples, give Iroquois once with its other names, and add one sentence on the Shawnee's part or that the record is silent.
- [1800 to 1850] "Historians disagree about how many of" : Five historians and four different shares fill four sentences, so the reader holds names and percentages and misses the point, which is that the traders' share is argued over, from about 16 percent to about two-thirds : Lead with the range and name two historians at most.
- [The 1500s] "The soldiers captured about 500 people." : Four groups with different punishments are packed into three sentences by sex and age (men over 25, boys and men from 12 to 25, girls and women over 12, children under 12), and the reader has to track the age lines to know who lost a foot, who was enslaved and who was given to the friars : Set the four groups out as a short list, one punishment each.
- [Card] "Chapter 5 of 37" : The card says about 75 minutes, but the strips add to 79 and the chapter runs about 15,600 words, about 78 minutes at the strips' own pace. The Era 1 strip says about 4 minutes for about 450 words, which is about 2 minutes : Make the total match the strips (about 79) and set Era 1 to about 2 minutes.
- [Card] "Five minutes? Read" : The pick is the Indian removal section (about 470 words) and the Rebecca Neugin box (about 180), about 650 words, three minutes at the strips' own pace, and the card does not say the box belongs to it : Say about three minutes and name the box, or point the card at a longer section.

Flow: uneven. The stories carry the chapter well and the Trail of Tears and Great Migration sections read clearly, but several sections hand over strings of counts or nation names with no takeaway, and a few facts are told twice close together.
