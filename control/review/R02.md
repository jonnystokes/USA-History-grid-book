# R02 reader review: Chapters 2 and 3

## Chapter 2: Native Nations

- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "In Minnesota in 1862, United States" : The section on the wars from the nations' side runs about 1,300 words (over six minutes) and moves through nine events with no break: Mankato, Bear River, the Long Walk, Sand Creek, Red Cloud's War, the Marias River, the Little Bighorn, Crazy Horse and Geronimo. Bear River, the Long Walk, Red Cloud's War, the Little Bighorn and Geronimo have no bold label and none of the events has a bridge, so a reader who stops cannot find their place, and Red Cloud's War names the Bozeman Trail without saying what it was : Split it into three or four subheads, give every event a bold run-in label as Sand Creek has, add one bridging line between events, and say in a clause what the Bozeman Trail was.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "President Ford signed the Indian Self-Determination" : The section titled Nations run their own programs holds five topics (the 1975 act, Native children taken from families, the sterilization of Native women, casinos, and museum remains), so the reader goes from forced sterilization straight to gambling with no bridge, and a skimmer looking for the hardest passages will not find them under that title : Give 'Children taken and women sterilized' its own subhead, keep the act, gaming and NAGPRA under a plainer one, and add a one-line bridge.
- [Card] "The Haudenosaunee recorded their first agreement" : The first sentence of the chapter uses Haudenosaunee and Guswenta without saying who these people are, and the explanation comes about three minutes later (line 47). The next paragraph also uses confederacies and allotment before either is explained (allotment is explained at line 606) : Gloss each at first use, for example 'the Haudenosaunee, a confederacy of five nations in what is now New York,' add a short pronunciation, and put a clause after allotment in the preview paragraph.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "The Two Row Wampum, Guswenta, records" : This paragraph is the chapter opening (line 7) repeated almost word for word, including the 1613 date and the hedge about a paper document, about 16 minutes of reading later, so a reader going straight through meets the same story twice : Keep one telling. In the 1600s say it in one sentence with a pointer back, and add only what is new.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Two of the men had been" : The two mistaken hangings are hard to follow. The man who answered also went by the name Caske, yet the text says the name called belonged to another man and never explains how one name could belong to two men, so the reader cannot tell who was hanged in whose place : Rewrite in short steps that say who was called, why the name fitted two men, who answered, and who was hanged.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "Cherokee leaders took their fight with" : This is the Five minutes? section, yet after one short paragraph on the Removal Act the next 220 words (40 percent of the section) are the Worcester v. Georgia case, with six names and places, before soldiers appear at the roundup, and the section then jumps from the 1832 ruling to the 1838 roundup without saying what the soldiers acted on after the Court sided with the Cherokee : Open on the removal, cut the court case to two sentences, and add one bridge sentence on what the officials claimed as their authority in 1838.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "His argument was that no single" : Tecumseh's one idea (the land belongs to all the nations, so no one nation can sell it) appears five times in about 800 words (lines 368, 378, 380, 390 and 400), and the burning of Prophetstown and his death are told twice (lines 382-384 and 402). The box adds only the Creek council at Tuckabatchee : Start the box at Tuckabatchee and cut the two paragraphs that retell the section.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "General Thomas Jesup lured him to" : The Osceola box repeats the section directly above it. The flag-of-truce capture, the date October 21, 1837, the public protest and his death at Fort Moultrie appear in lines 470, 480, 486 and 488, four times in about 20 lines : Cut them from one place and let the box carry only what is new (his birth as Billy Powell and the doctor who kept his head).
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "The Spanish left their horses behind" : More than half of the Po'pay box (246 of 475 words) is about how horses spread and a 2023 radiocarbon study, not about Po'pay, and horses also come up at line 77 and again in Comanche power, so the box loses its person and the horse story is split three ways : Move the horse material into its own subhead (with the line 77 paragraph and Comanche power) and end Po'pay's box on the statue.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Members of the Osage Tribal Council" : Three paragraphs name eleven people (Hale, Anna Brown, Burkhart, Mollie, Lizzie Q. Kyle, Rita and Bill Smith, Nettie Brookshire, Henry Roan, Morrison, Ramsey) tied together by a family tree the reader has to build, so it is hard to keep track of who was killed, who ordered it and who went to prison : Keep Hale, Anna Brown and Burkhart in the prose and put the other victims in a short who-was-who list, or cut the minor names.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "Armed settlers from Paxton Township, Pennsylvania," : The Conestoga killings arrive right after the Royal Proclamation paragraph with no link, and the section Pontiac and the Line now holds four separate stories (Pontiac's war, the smallpox blankets, the Proclamation, Paxton), so the reader cannot say what the section is about : Add one sentence saying why this attack belongs here, and give the blankets and Paxton their own subheads.
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "Estimates of the dead run from" : The Acoma toll is a pile of numbers (a range of 300 to 1,500 plus three more figures in between), and a few paragraphs on, the girls sent to Mexico are about seventy (line 127) and then sixty (line 129) with no note that the two figures differ or who counts which : Give the range once, attribute the figures (the Pueblo council says about 800, the National Park Service about 1,500), and say who counts seventy girls and who counts sixty.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "In Connecticut, English colonists and their" : Neither the Pequot War nor Metacom's war (line 169) is given a cause, so the reader is told who killed whom but not why either war began, and the plain statement that soldiers killed Native people while the coalition killed colonists reads like an even trade : Add one sentence on what started each war, or say plainly that the sources give no single cause.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "Colonial leaders sold hundreds of other" : The order to sell 112 captives is dated August 4, 1675, in a paragraph that follows Metacom's death on August 12, 1676, and the captives are described as having surrendered on promises, which fits the end of the war and not its second month, so it reads like a one-year slip : Check the date against the research bank (it may be 1676).
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "Paquiquineo led an attack on the" : The box says he led the attack that killed the missionaries but gives no reason and never says the records are silent, which is the first thing a reader asks, and the baptism sentence just above (line 91) buries his new name at the end of a long chain of definitions : Add what the sources say about why, or 'the Spanish records do not say why,' and split the baptism sentence in two.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "He outlived the other twenty-eight who" : The hook says Nez outlived the other 28 men who built the code, but the box never says when the others died, so the hook's claim cannot be checked on the page : Add the fact to the box, or change the hook to what the box shows (staff washed his mouth out with soap for speaking the language the Marines later used).
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act" : The Alaska settlement sits under the section Alcatraz and Wounded Knee, 1969 to 1973 with no link, and the paragraph before it joins Nixon ending termination and the Blue Lake law as if they were one event : Give Alaska its own subhead or a bridge, and split the Nixon paragraph so Blue Lake stands alone.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "The occupiers held the village for" : The occupiers are said to be protesting against the tribal chairman at Pine Ridge, but he is never named and no reason is given, so a reader cannot see why Native protesters opposed a Native official : Name him and add one sentence on what the occupiers charged him with.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "In Florida many Seminole people refused" : The last two sentences of the paragraph give the war's cost as ratios (four Seminole people forced out to one killed, three soldiers dead and $32,000) packed into one line, so most readers will skip it : Say it in separate short sentences, or drop the ratios and keep that it was the longest and costliest.
- [Several eras] "Little Turtle was one of the" : Several story boxes restate facts the chapter gave 15 to 70 lines earlier (Little Turtle, lines 340-346 and 358-360. Sequoyah, 406 and 424. Billy Frank Jr., 876-882 and 954. Deb Haaland, 1004, 1026 and 1064-1068. Jessie Little Doe Baird, 1048-1050 and 1084-1088), so the reader gets each fact twice : Start each box where the section stops and say only what is new.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Under the ruling, about 3.25 million" : The only reason given for why the McGirt ruling matters is a one-line remark about federal criminal law that never says what changes, so the case the chapter's opening promised is left unexplained at its payoff : Add one plain sentence on what changed (which courts handle crimes on that land).
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "A protester named Sophia Wilansky was" : The wording that the judges set out her account and accepted it as true for her appeal is court language a young reader cannot decode, four officers are named (three by surname only), a one-sentence line about her running stands alone for effect, and The oil flows paragraph (line 1020) holds six dates : Say that a court summary of her case describes what happened, name only the officer who fired the round, fold the running line into the next sentence, and keep two or three dates for the pipeline.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Osage leaders, led by Chief James" : The paragraph says federal officials forced other nations to take 160 acres a person, but the allotment section (line 608) said only heads of families got 160 acres and single men and children got 80 and 40, so the two passages disagree : Match the earlier numbers (for example up to 160 acres a family).
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Native doctors, lawyers and writers founded" : The era opening and the first section say the same thing in nearly the same words within 12 lines (lines 698 and 710), and the Osage murders and the 1924 and 1934 laws are told in the opening (lines 700-704) and again in full 20 to 50 lines later : Cut the section's repeated first sentence and let the opening keep only a one-line summary of each topic.
- [Era 1, Before 1500] "Nobody knows how many people lived" : Three scholars and four population figures (1.85 million, 18 million, 1 million to 12 million) cover different areas (the lower 48 states against North America north of Mexico), so the reader cannot compare them, and the same 1 to 18 million range is told again in the 1500s (line 63) : Say once 'no one knows, and guesses run from about 1 million to 18 million,' and drop the names.

Flow: uneven. The first five eras and the Acoma, Cherokee and code-talker sections read cleanly in short labeled paragraphs, but the 1850 to 1900 era has a 1,300-word run with no subhead, several story boxes retell the paragraphs above them, and two late sections are grab-bags under headings that do not fit.

## Chapter 3: Land and Environment

- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "By September 23, staff of a" : The last paragraphs of the chapter pile up claim counts, filers' names and court procedure (23 claims, 20 of them filed by one mining company, three sets of plaintiffs, eight groups asking to reopen a case the court had set aside) with the acronyms SUWA and NRDC never spelled out, so the plain point (miners are filing claims while no court has ruled) is lost in the chapter's most recent and hardest passage : Replace lines 2113 to 2119 with three or four plain sentences (claims began within days, several tribes and groups are suing, they argue a president can make a monument but not undo one, no court has ruled) and drop the filers' names.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Members of Congress granted railroad companies" : The section Land taken from Native nations, in acres is a wall of figures, more than twenty in 745 words (129 million to 131 million, 175 million, 51 million, 44 million, 270 million, 10.7 million, 250 tribes, 160 takings, $17.7 million, 2.4 cents and more), often two or three counts for one thing, so the point (the nations' land became railroad, farm and college land) is buried : Keep about five figures with comparisons a reader can picture and cut the alternate counts.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Chapter 1 tells how the Tukudika" : The cross-reference looks wrong. Chapter 2 does not mention the Tukudika, the 1800 to 1850 era promises that the next era will tell how (line 1368), and the very next sentence does the telling, so a reader would hunt for a Chapter 1 account that is not there : Delete the sentence or change it to 'Here is how the Tukudika were taken out.'
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "US Army troops attacked Heavy Runner's" : This contradicts Chapter 2, which gives the Marias River dead as about 177 by the best estimate (37 men, 90 women, 50 children) and 173 by the army's own count, while this says more than 200 : Use Chapter 2's figures or explain why the count differs.
- [Card] "Miantonomi, a leader of the Narragansett" : The chapter's opening story is retold in the Miantonomi box about 100 lines later with the same quote word for word (and touched again at line 1205), and the word scythes, used in the first paragraph, is not explained until the box (line 1233) : Keep the full speech once, cut the repeat, and gloss scythes where it first appears.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "The land north and west of" : The section Whose land the grid covered repeats the seven-nation list from the era opening word for word, then leans on a state history by Knepper with four quoted phrases, each glossed in the same sentence (abide by, duress, coerced, cleared), and a run of survey names and places (Hutchins, Harmar, Hamtramck, Seven Ranges, Geographer's Line), so the point (surveyors measured the land under army guard while the nations rejected the treaty) is hard to find : Paraphrase in plain words, keep one Knepper quote, and cut the survey logistics to two sentences.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Historians disagree about whether the army" : The Disputed paragraph names four historians (Utley, Wooster, Smits, Dobak) with three positions, so the reader cannot tell who says what or what is actually in dispute, and the next fifteen lines add seven more named men with a quote each (Butler, Sherman, Sheridan, Bradley, Hazen, Mooar, Miles) : State the positions in plain words with one name for each, and keep only the strongest three quotes.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "The canal, called Love Canal, covered" : Love Canal (878 words, over four minutes) gives three near-identical tonnage figures (20,000, 21,000 and 21,800) with two end years, then a chain of six names (Young, Curtin, Wilcox, Bartlett, Thiele, Chambers), and the label The second emergency sits on a lawsuit paragraph two paragraphs before the emergency is declared : Give one figure (about 21,000 tons) and say sources differ slightly, keep only the names the reader needs, and move the label to the paragraph that declares the emergency.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Five months before the Cuyahoga fire," : The Santa Barbara oil blowout is dropped into the section The river that burned with no bridge, so a reader following the Cleveland story is suddenly in California, and neither the section title nor the era opening mentions it : Give it its own subhead or add a bridge such as 'The same year, a second disaster hit the coast of California.'
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "When the report came out, the" : This sentence cuts a quote in two to insert a gloss in the middle, so the reader must hold half a quote while reading the definition of communities of color : Split it. Chavis called the pattern environmental racism, meaning communities of color were chosen on purpose for waste sites and polluting factories, and communities of color are places where most people are not white.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "The passenger pigeon died out on" : The end of the passenger pigeon is told four times in this era (line 1617, this section, the Southworth box and its Martha paragraph) and once more in Audubon's box (line 1386), repeating the 1900 shooting and the September 1, 1914 death in near-identical sentences : Fold this section into the box and state each date once.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Members of Congress passed the Dawes" : The allotment paragraphs retell Chapter 2's nearly word for word (138 million to 48 million acres, the Meriam Report's 137 and 47) but give different plot sizes (160 acres for a farm or 320 for grazing, against Chapter 2's 160 for a head of family, 80 for a single man and 40 for a child), so a reader going from one chapter to the next sees two sets of numbers for one law : Use one set of numbers in both chapters and let Chapter 3 point back to Chapter 2 instead of retelling.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Lieutenant Colonel Richard Irving Dodge, an" : Dodge's hide counts use exact seven-digit numbers (1,378,389 hides, 459,463, 3,158,730 bison, 1,215,000) and show the multiplication he guessed at, so the reader loses the point (millions of bison killed in three years) : Round them ('about 1.4 million hides in three years,' 'about three bison killed for each hide shipped') and say what that adds up to.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "The Park Service page does not" : The To Tu Ya paragraph expects the reader to work out that Tenaya's son would be her uncle, so the closing sentence about whether he was the son the guard shot is hard to follow : Say plainly that Tenaya's son would have been her uncle, but the page does not say whether he is the uncle she saw killed.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Justices of the US Supreme Court" : The paragraph labeled The court case turns from the Tukudika to a Bannock hunter in 1896 with no link to Yellowstone until the next paragraph, and the 2019 reversal is about a Crow treaty (a different nation and treaty) without saying so, so a reader may think the same case was reversed : Open with one line saying why a hunting case belongs here, and say the 2019 case involved the Crow treaty.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Pinchot became the first chief of" : The Ballinger dispute packs four new names (Glavis, Ballinger, Dolliver, Taft) and Alaska coal claims into two paragraphs that have nothing to do with the chapter's questions, so the hook (Taft fired him) costs more effort than it repays : Say in one sentence that he fought publicly with the Secretary of the Interior over coal claims and Taft fired him.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Caroline Henderson graduated from Mount Holyoke" : The hook says she kept farming and did not leave, but the box only lists publishing facts (which magazine, which month, who praised her, which press collected the letters) and never says what the dust did to her farm or what her letters say : Add a line or two from the letters or about her farm.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Members of Congress passed a law" : The National Bison Range return is told in nearly the same words at the end of Chapter 2 (18,800 acres, December 2020, the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, 2022), so a reader going from one chapter to the next meets it twice : In Chapter 3 give only what is new (the 1908 law, $1.56 an acre, the 1971 ruling) and point back.
- [Era 1, Before 1500] "Officials of the US Forest Service" : The Before 1500 era jumps to the 1900s (the stopping of fires after about 1940) before the Forest Service has been mentioned, and the same story returns in the sections Every fire put out and Why are the fires bigger?, so a reader taking the era in order meets a flash-forward with no bridge : Shorten it to a one-line pointer to the 1900 to 1950 era, or move the paragraph there.
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "Most of the Native people died" : The era opening says the diseases were carried by European settlers and enslaved Africans, which can read as blaming enslaved people, who were carried across the ocean by Europeans, and the fuller wording saying so comes only 18 lines later (line 1189) : Use the fuller wording at first mention, or say the diseases came from Europe on Europeans' ships.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "US officials gave away the land" : The era closer covers the giveaway and the bison but leaves out the park removals (Yosemite and Yellowstone), which are the third strand of the era, the Five minutes? pick and the first half of the chapter's third question, and its 1872 bullet joins Yellowstone to a railroad hide count as if they were one event : Add the park removals to the one thing and split the 1872 bullet.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "The land grew hotter and drier" : The era closer names climate, fires and animal comebacks but not Cancer Alley and the LaPlace plant, Bears Ears or the land returned to tribes, though the chapter's spine and third question are about who bears the harm : Add a clause for the people and land threads.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "Chapter 2 tells this war from" : The section still retells St. Clair's defeat, Wayne and Greenville that Chapter 2 told in full, spells Little Turtle's Miami name Meshekinoquah where Chapter 2 has Mishikinaakwa, uses Delaware for the nation Chapter 2 mostly calls the Lenape, and introduces Harmar in a tangled sentence (he is the officer who guarded the surveyors and also a brigadier general since 1787, packed into one clause) : Cut the battles to what they meant for the land (acres, the Greenville line), use Chapter 2's spellings and names, and untangle the Harmar sentence.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Some animals came back. Bald eagles" : Eagle counts jump from about 10,000 pairs by 2007 (lines 1872 and 1964) to about 71,400 in the 2020s with no explanation, which looks like a seven-fold rise in about 15 years : Say the later figure comes from a different count, or use one source throughout.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "The historian William Cronon describes these" : This paragraph carries three ideas (Cronon's book, cattle and hogs in planting fields with Miantonomi's complaint, and a last sentence about beavers that arrives from nowhere), and the 1900 to 1950 opening (line 1617) chains four events with and in the same way : Give the beavers their own sentence or move it to The beaver trade, and split both paragraphs.

Flow: uneven. The early eras and the story boxes are short, concrete and often fun to read, but the long 1850 to 1900 and 1950 to 2000 eras and the Bears Ears ending bury the point under counts, names and legal detail, and several stories come back in intros, sections and boxes.
