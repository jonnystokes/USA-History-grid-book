## Chapter 29: Religion

- [Opening] "This chapter follows faith in America" : The roadmap packs eight groups into two sentences and leans on terms the reader has not met yet (Witness, revivals, Latter-day Saints, rations, Sikh, and a phrase about a son asking the Senate to count a crime, which is hard to decode before his story). Rations is defined only in 1850 to 1900 and Sikh is never explained anywhere in the chapter. It also says the Baptists and Methodists became the largest churches in the country with no date and no word Protestant, while 1700 to 1750 says Protestant churches and 1850 to 1900 reports 14 million Catholics. : Cut to one sentence on who was punished and one on who pushed back, drop the names the reader cannot place yet, and add the word Protestant.
- [2000 to Today] "Accusations after 2004. Accusations kept coming" : This paragraph is a bare string of date spans and counts with the point left unsaid (the counts fall from thousands for the 1960s to a few dozen for 2020 to 2022 because they are accusations made later), and the whole section holds more than thirty figures. A reader skims past the human facts and the grand jury's findings around it. The Pew and Gallup section and the church-fire counts in 1950 to 2000 do the same. : State the point in one sentence, keep two or three numbers per paragraph, and drop the rest.
- [1750 to 1800] "Mission San Diego stood on Kumeyaay" : Twelve missions and nine peoples sit in one paragraph with no sentence on what to take from them. The same pile-up recurs in Toypurina's plan (seven village and leader names), the Chumash rising (more than a dozen numbers in three paragraphs), the boarding-school counts in 1850 to 1900, the Kiowa list of eight names in 1900 to 1950, and the six friend-of-the-court groups in 1950 to 2000 (the last two are never placed on a side). : Keep two or three examples inside one sentence (at least nine peoples, among them the Kumeyaay at San Diego and the Tongva at San Gabriel) and trim the others the same way.
- [1900 to 1950] "The 1925 trial at Dayton, Tennessee" : The section never says how the trial ended (verdict, penalty, what happened to Scopes), and the By 1929 line skips it too. After the Bryan and Darrow scene the reader waits for an outcome that never comes. : Add the verdict and result from the research bank in one or two sentences before the evolution-laws section.
- [Card] "Five minutes? Read" : The pick, the Quaker section in The 1600s, is about 630 words, roughly three minutes at the pace the strips use. It answers none of questions 2 and 3 and only part of question 1 (it shows the punishments, not why colonists who left for freedom did them), so a reader on the five-minute path gets one answer out of three. : Name a trail that really is about five minutes and reaches all three questions, for example the Mary Dyer story, the Virginia church tax section and Hiram Price's rules of 1883 (about 1,050 words together).
- [1850 to 1900] "Wovoka's vision. A Northern Paiute man" : The section never says what the Ghost Dance taught or hoped for. Wovoka's lines about the dead and about leaving the whites are left unexplained, then Short Bull speaks of a great change that is never defined, so the reader cannot see why his words or the agent's fear matter. : State the teaching in one plain sentence before the quotes (from the research bank) and say what the great change was.
- [The 1600s] "In 2005 a statue of Po'pay," : The next sentence calls Po'pay the first person in the collection who was born on land that became the United States, which reads as wrong because many others in the collection, George Washington among them, were born on that land. Earlier in the section the knotted cords are never explained (the Spanish torture runners to explain them, then the rising starts three days early), and the 1675 arrests are for ceremonies in the opening but for sorcery here. : Check the claim against its source (perhaps earliest born, or first from New Mexico), say what the cords were for and why capturing runners moved the date, and add one sentence tying sorcery to the ceremonies.
- [1800 to 1850] "A Library of Congress exhibit lists" : The era's overview ends with six society names in one sentence, and only two of them (the Tract Society and the Sunday-School Union) come back later in the era. The board is also named two ways, once here and once in 1850 to 1900. : Say six large societies, among them the Tract Society and the Sunday-School Union, and use one name for the board.
- [1850 to 1900] "Schools for rabbis. Isaac Leeser founded" : Two paragraphs give six institutions and six dates (1867, 1873, 1875, 1886, 1887, 1897) with Talmud and Orthodox glossed in passing and no sentence on what they add up to. The Pittsburgh Platform sentence names a document and says nothing about what it states. : Open with the one idea (Jews built schools to train rabbis, in different branches), keep two or three schools, and say what the Platform said or cut it.
- [The 1500s] "Guale people killed five Franciscans and" : A run of names and dates with no thread. Five friars and four towns arrive in one sentence, then five burned towns on four dates in ten days, and earlier in the era a date run (1568, 1571, 1573, 1574, 1575, 1587). Only Blas Rodríguez and Lucas matter later. : Give the count and one or two names, say that he burned five towns and their corn in ten days, and keep one date for the mission story.
- [The 1500s] "The governor's revenge. Gonzalo Méndez de" : Three near-identical names sit in one era, Menéndez (the 1565 commander), Méndez (the 1597 governor, later named alone as Méndez) and Meléndez (Doña María). A reader will take Méndez for the man who killed the French. : Call the 1597 man Governor Méndez at every mention, or the governor after the first, and keep Menéndez as the Spanish commander of 1565.
- [1900 to 1950] "If you remember one thing: Pentecostal" : Closers labeled one thing carry three things in eras 5 to 9 (27 to 32 words each, joined by commas), so there is no one thing to remember. This one is the hardest to read, because its commas make the place name and the groups that were attacked read as one list. : Give each closer one idea in under 25 words (or call it three things) and here split the Azusa Street clause from the attacks.
- [1700 to 1750] "In 1690: a drawing of New" : The two bullets are about steeples in drawings (the 1771 one is outside 1700 to 1750, and the 1730 drawing the text mentions is never used) and do not back the one thing above them, that the revival split churches into New Lights and Old Lights. They also skip the era's heavy parts (the Apalachee missions, the 1741 hanging, Whitefield, Edwards). : Use bullets that back the line, such as the revival of 1734 to 1735, Whitefield's tour of 1739 to 1741 and Princeton's charter in 1746, and keep the steeples in the body.
- [Closing] "What changed: The chapter begins with" : The line does not match the chapter. Era 1 has no rulers deciding how others worship, and the last two eras show courts not protecting choice (Lyng in 1988 on the Chimney Rock road, Smith in 1990 on peyote, and Landor in 2026 on the shaved dreadlocks). : Rewrite to what the chapter shows, officials in New Mexico and Massachusetts punishing worship and courts later sometimes protecting it and sometimes not.
- [1800 to 1850] "A question for the next era:" : The question is whether Catholics would really build a school system of their own, and this era already answers it twice (in the Bible riots section and in the By 1844 line), and the next era's first lines answer it again. The hook has nothing left to hook. : Ask something the next era leaves open, such as the card's question about how long the Native ceremony ban lasted, or cut the line.
- [2000 to Today] "On Sunday, August 5, 2012, a" : The Saini box retells the Oak Creek shooting in nearly the same words as the section a few lines above (white supremacist, loaded gun, the date, Paramjit Kaur at morning prayers). In that section, six killed is followed by Punjab Singh counted as the seventh person killed, with no word that he died later. : Start the box from Harpreet himself, drop the second telling, and add one clause on when Punjab Singh died.
- [1700 to 1750] "Church members argued over the revival," : Six labels in five sentences (Congregationalists, New Lights, Presbyterians, New Side, Old Lights, Old Side) for the era's key idea and its closer. A reader cannot hold which is which. : Teach one pair (New Lights for the revival, Old Lights against) and put the Presbyterian names in a clause.
- [The 1600s] "Massachusetts officials hanged Mary Dyer on" : The box opens by retelling the hanging the Quaker section just gave, so the June 1, 1660 date appears four times in about 25 lines and the Robinson and Stephenson hanging is told twice. The Seymour box (1900 to 1950) loops the same way (the hallway and the Texas law three times in six lines). : Cut the box's first sentence so the hanging comes once, at the end of her story, and trim the Quaker list to point to the box.
- [1850 to 1900] "Secretary of War Edwin Stanton and" : The Savannah meeting is told three times (the era overview, this section and the Frazier box), and this section is a list of four ministers with ages, years, church sizes and dollar values the reader will not hold. One entry contradicts itself (a man born free because his mother had bought his freedom). : Tell the meeting once, keep two ministers with their church sizes, and fix the Porter line from the research bank.
- [1800 to 1850] "The Illinois Department of Natural Resources" : Two paragraphs inside Joseph Smith's story give competing accounts of how a Meskwaki village was lost (lodge counts and corn payments that differ), then the trouble in Illinois resumes. The reader drops Smith's thread for details that change nothing in it. : One sentence saying accounts differ, with the numbers moved or cut.
- [The 1600s] "Soldiers of the Dutch governor, Willem" : The end of Anne Hutchinson's story brings in a Dutch governor, Munsee people, a war and Siwanoy people in four sentences with no link to her, so the reader cannot tell why her home was attacked. : Add the link (she had moved to land the Dutch claimed, in a war that started that year) and say who the Siwanoy were fighting.
- [1750 to 1800] "St. Thomas. After the walkout from" : Allen and Jones leave St. George's together and set up the First African Church in 1792, then end up leading two different churches in 1794 (Jones's St. Thomas, Episcopal, and Allen's Bethel, Methodist), with no sentence saying they parted or why. Two different men are also named William White (a Black member in Allen's box and the bishop who ordained Jones). : Add one sentence on the split, and tell the reader the two William Whites are different men.
- [The 1600s] "Their leaders had the ears of" : In the overview the aside about Quakers sits inside the sentence, so the verb and the place (Boston) come apart. It is the first paragraph every 1600s reader sees. : Split in two, first who the Quakers were, then that the leaders cut off Quakers' ears and hanged four of them in Boston.
- [The 1600s] "Joining a church. In Massachusetts a" : The lead holds three ideas that do not touch, who could join, a lone sentence about the Bible as the authority for criminal laws, and (in the next paragraph, still under the same lead) the Bay Psalm Book. The reader loses the thread. : Cut the criminal-laws sentence or move it to Reading for God, and give the Psalm Book its own lead.
- [1700 to 1750] "Only graduates of Harvard or Yale" : The paragraph says the Log College students could not become Presbyterian ministers, then says several became revival preachers and three became Princeton trustees, with no word on how the rule was beaten. Three Tennents appear and the text never says Gilbert and William Jr. are two of William's four sons. : Say what happened to the rule, and say two of Tennent's sons, Gilbert and William Jr.

Flow: uneven. The story boxes and hard-history sections are plain and clear, but runs of names, dates and numbers and facts told two or three times break the thread for a reader who loses focus.
