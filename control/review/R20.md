## Chapter 31: News and Communication

- [Era 4, 1700 to 1750] "Not guilty. A jury found Zenger" : The section has just said that under the law of the time truth was no defense, then says a jury freed him and the case changed no law, but never says why the jury freed him. The reader is left with a puzzle at the heart of the card's first question. : Add two plain sentences, if the research bank supports them, on what the jury was told and that the jurors chose to free him although the rule said truth did not count.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "American newspapers took in less than" : The card's third question asks why local papers closed. This section gives the fall in advertising money and the closing adds that readers moved to phones, but nothing says why the advertisers left or where the money went, so the reader gets what happened and not why. : Add one sourced sentence here on where the advertising money went, and carry it into the closing answer.
- [Era 3, the 1600s] "Numb. 1. Benjamin Harris printed Publick" : The first newspaper's story is told four times (chapter opening, Era 3 opening, this section, the Harris story). The opening and this section repeat the page count, the blank page, the four days, the one surviving copy and the single issue almost word for word, about 120 lines apart. : Tell it in full once. Here keep only what the opening leaves out (why the council objected, the license order), and let the Harris story keep only his earlier arrests and later career.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "The Maine. The US battleship Maine" : The section says some Cuba stories turned out false, but its one example has the papers printing a rumor of sabotage and the Navy later saying a mine outside the ship did it. The reader will think the papers were right, or will not know which story was false. : Name one story shown to be false, or say plainly what is known about the cause and which printed claim it contradicts.
- [Era 3, the 1600s, same pattern in Eras 4 to 7] "The Spanish learned of the plan" : This section and the Catua and Omtua story after it both give the capture on August 9 and the early start on August 10. The same doubling happens with the Lexington section and Isaac Bissell's story, the first Black postal workers section and the Nell story, the Zenger section and Anna Zenger's story, and the Franklin pieces. Each fact reaches the reader twice within a page. : In each pair, say the shared facts once, in the shorter piece, and let the story carry only what the section leaves out.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Carr acted after Kimmel made comments" : One sentence packs a talk-show host, a political side, a man accused of a killing and a named activist into a single clause, so a young reader cannot tell what was said, what happened, or why the FCC chair cared. : Split into two or three short sentences saying plainly what the host said and what the chair did, and name the event in a few words.
- [Era 2, the 1500s] "The National Park Service counts 111" : Five numbers from two sources stack up with no total, and the sentence about men spared does not say whose. The section also opens on the killings before saying why they belong in a news chapter (two sides wrote two accounts) and gives the French book only by its title. : Open with the news point, give one clear total, say who was spared, and add a sentence on what the survivor's book said.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "Who was charged. Federal prosecutors and" : Three sources give three different counts of Sedition Act cases and convictions, then the next paragraph lists six people and three newspapers. The numbers do not add up for the reader, and the point (the Act hit Jefferson's supporters most) sits in the first sentence. : Keep that sentence, say in one line that the sources disagree and give the lowest and highest counts, and set the names in their own short line.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Another 1,525 counties had only one" : Era 10 leans on stacks of figures. The news desert paragraphs carry seven figures on counties, people and owners across two years, the Pew paragraph carries seven survey figures, and the Press Freedom Tracker section opens with several yearly totals. A reader skims all of them and keeps none. : State each point in words first (for example, the number of paper owners fell by about half) and keep only the one or two figures that prove it.
- [Chapter closing] "What changed: In 1673 the mail" : The line sets the travel time of the mail in 1673 against how many adults now get news on a device, so the reader cannot see what changed. The opening told the reader to watch speed, and the closing never says how fast news travels now. : Compare like with like, such as two weeks by post rider then and seconds to a phone now, if sourced.
- [Chapter card] "Each new way of carrying news" : The spine says every new way of carrying news brought a struggle over who could carry it and what could be said. The Pony Express, telephone, pictures by wire and rural delivery sections show no such struggle, so the claim fails when the reader checks it. : Change "each" to "many" or "again and again", or add the struggle where it exists.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Federal law had barred anyone but" : The law limited mail carrying to white people who were not enslaved, yet Boston's postmaster hired a Black clerk in 1863, two years before the rule ended, and the story's hook calls the mail whites-only at that time. Nothing says a clerk inside the office was not a carrier, so the reader sees a contradiction or a broken law. : Add a clause that the rule covered carrying the mail and that Nell's job was inside the office, if the records show that.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Rules for the air. The biggest" : One abstract sentence says the big companies' deals might have let them control radio, then the next paragraph jumps to a law and a commission. The reader gets no picture of who would be shut out, what Congress feared, or why it had to hand out spots on the dial. : Add two sourced sentences on what the deals would have let the companies do and why Congress chose to decide who gets which wavelength.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "A forged announcement. Two New York" : The text says the two men did it to move gold prices but never says how a fake call for more soldiers could do that, so the plan looks pointless. : Add one sentence connecting the fake call for more soldiers to gold prices.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "That year he printed an editorial" : The editorial that set off the Wilmington violence is described only as being about how Black and white people related, so the reader cannot tell what Manly wrote or why it enraged white leaders. The Memphis section states plainly what Wells answered. : Say in plain words what the editorial answered and what it said, as the Wells section does.
- [Era 4, 1700 to 1750] "A month in jail. James Franklin" : The item looks like an ordinary notice about a ship chasing pirates. The text says only that officials took offense, never what the item hinted about them, so a mild-looking line leads to a month in jail with no reason the reader can see. : Add one plain sentence saying what the item implied about the officials.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Five journalists for refugee papers. Killers" : The paragraph says five were killed, but the paragraphs after it name four. A reader who counts wonders who the fifth was. : Name the fifth if the sources do, say plainly that the records name only four, or fix the count.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "Prosecutors tried men from the mob" : Eight names run in a row with nothing holding them together, and the facts that matter come after them (the state's top lawyer who joined the prosecution of the press defenders then defended the mob, and nobody was charged with killing Lovejoy). : Lead with those two facts, then put the names in their own short line so a reader can skip it.
- [Era 10, also Eras 9 and 4] "Julian Assange published secret documents about" : Two sentences on the WikiLeaks founder drop into a section on reporters arrested, hit and deported, with no tie to its point. The same loose fact ends the Pentagon Papers section (a 1972 ruling on reporters' sources) and the ads-for-people section (a 1740 South Carolina law on teaching enslaved people to write). : Tie each in a clause, for example Assange to the Espionage Act used on editors and Ellsberg, the ruling to Deep Throat's secret source, the law to who was shut out of writing the news.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "He took no money from the" : The Thomas Paine hook says he took no money from his pamphlet, but the story never says it. It says only that he fell out with the first printer and later wrote that he gave the right to print to every state, so the reader must guess the link. : Add the missing sentence on what Paine did with the money and why he and the printer fell out, or change the hook to what the story shows.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "The penny press, 1833" : This cheerful section sits between the mob attack on Garrison and the Lovejoy story, cutting a run of antislavery press and mob violence in two. A reader going from Garrison to Lovejoy loses the thread and has to pick it up again. : Move it before Freedom's Journal or after Lovejoy so the mob sections run together.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Medgar Evers. Medgar Evers, the Mississippi" : The sentence gives his job, spells out the NAACP, glosses it again, then says what he did on air and whom he answered, so the main verb arrives late. It sits in the section the card sends hurried readers to. : Split in two, who he was (group named once), then what he said on WLBT and when.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Members of Congress set up the" : One section covers five separate things (public broadcasting money, CNN, the fairness rule, talk radio, Fox News) as short paragraphs of dates and names, and no opening line says what ties them together. : Open with one sentence naming the thread (who pays for and who controls what goes on the air), or split into two sections.
- [Era 5, also Era 7] "Seven years later the Federalist members" : The party name is used in the Era 5 opening 134 lines before the text says it is President Adams's party, and the word for the Northern side in the Civil War is never explained though the Southern side's name is. In Era 7's Wilmington count, a word meaning many dozens is also left bare. : Gloss each at first use in a few words.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "If you remember one thing: Television" : The "one thing" is two claims, and the second ends in a three-part run with the same opening word starting each part, which reads as performed. It lists three cases but not the single idea that joins them. : State the single point in plain words and list the three cases on their own line.

Flow: uneven. Short plain sentences, clear section leads and good hooks carry most of the chapter, but facts repeat between sections and their story boxes, a few paragraphs are walls of numbers or names (mostly in Era 10), and several one-line gaps leave the reader without the reason.
