## Chapter 34: Storytelling Evolution

- [The 1600s] "William Darby is one of the" : The Accomack court story is told four times (chapter opener, era lead, the 1665 section and this box), and the box retells the arrest order, the hold-until-security order and the not-guilty verdict that sit in the paragraphs just above it. The Warren box (1750 to 1800) repeats its paragraph's Massachusetts Spy, unsigned plays and governor, and the Brown, Forrest, Serkis and Rubalcaba boxes also restate facts from the text above them. The reader meets the same facts twice in a row and starts to skip : Let the section carry the shared facts and let each box give only what is new about the person. Check every box against the paragraphs above it.
- [2000 to Today] "Reporters at The New York Times" : The Weinstein section runs about 530 words with 17 dates and years, eight named people and several trials, and nothing tells the reader how it answers any of the chapter's three questions. A reader loses the thread somewhere in the run from 2017 to September 2026 : Open with one plain sentence on why this belongs in a chapter about acting, then give the outcomes (charged, convicted, overturned, retried, sentenced) and keep only the two or three dates the reader needs.
- [1850 to 1900] "In July 1890 No Neck told" : The Lakota section jumps around in time (1883, 1885, 1890, 1891, back to 1887, then 1890 again, then 1891), and No Neck's July 1890 statements are split into two places with the deaths between them, so they read like two events. The federal investigation and the ban on new permits have no date. One sentence about the Standing Rock signing also stacks three definitions (agency, Indian agent, reservation) before it reaches its point : Put the section in date order, join the two No Neck passages, date the investigation, and split the Standing Rock sentence in two.
- [1750 to 1800] "Douglass built a theatre in Philadelphia" : Pennsylvania's law against plays (May 1759) is told a few lines above, yet Douglass builds a theatre in Philadelphia in 1759 and the American Company plays the Southwark Theatre there in 1767. The reader is given two facts that seem to cancel each other and no word on how the plays went on : Add one sentence saying how the law was dodged, ended or ignored, or say the records do not explain it.
- [1750 to 1800] "The first plays by American writers" : The era lead puts this after the war, but the Hallam box and the first-American-plays section date the first such play (The Prince of Parthia) to 1767, before the war, and only The Contrast to 1787. The lead points the reader the wrong way : Reword the lead so it gives 1767 for the first American play and 1787 for the Yankee comedy.
- [closing] "banned plays as wasteful or bad" : The first answer says lawmakers up to the Continental Congress banned plays, but the chapter shows Congress only promised to discourage them in 1774 and asked the states to stop them in 1778. The question also asks why, and the answer gives no reason for the arrests of the Black actors or for the Native dance crimes, and it leaves out the Virginia court case the chapter opens with : Use the chapter's own verbs for Congress, add the reasons the chapter gives (or say the records give none), and mention the Accomack case.
- [The 1500s] "Oñate's soldiers acted a second play" : In the last section of the era, the second play (1598) comes after Acoma (1598 to 1599) and Oñate's trial (1612 to 1614), so the story runs backward in time, and a 1614 trial sits in an era labeled the 1500s. Tewa is used with no explanation : Move the second play up beside the first one, or open it with a plain back-in-1598 signal, say what Tewa means in a few words, and mark the trial as later.
- [The 1500s] "About eight months after the play" : The Acoma section is nine paragraphs and 536 words, about 40 percent of the era, and no sentence ties it to acting or storytelling, so a reader asks why a chapter about plays has turned to a massacre, the sentences and a trial : Add one bridge sentence saying why the same expedition's acts at Acoma belong in this chapter, or trim the section to what the chapter needs.
- [The 1600s] "A man named Edward Martin reported" : In the 1665 section, the chapter opens on this case and its first question asks who tried to stop acting and why, but the section never says what Martin complained of or why the king's attorney took the case. The reader is left without the reason the opening set up : Say what the record gives as the complaint, or say plainly that the record does not say.
- [1800 to 1850] "He told General William Hall, 'You" : In the Astor Place section a new general, Hall, arrives with no introduction one paragraph after Sandford is named as the general in charge, and then Sandford, not Hall, answers. The reader cannot tell who commanded the soldiers or who gave the order : Say who Hall was, or drop him if the words were meant for Sandford.
- [1800 to 1850] "Its hero is Metacom, a leader" : Within four sentences the same man is called Metamora (the stage hero), Metacom and King Philip, and nothing says these are one person, so a reader may count three people : Say that the play's hero, Metamora, is based on Metacom (whom the English called King Philip), then keep one name.
- [1900 to 1950] "Later researchers, among them Jefferson Pooley" : The paragraph says researchers checked the panic story but never says what they found. The next paragraph gives a 2 percent telephone count and a hospital check that the reader has to decode, so the point (that the panic was far smaller than the newspapers said, if that is what the researchers concluded) is never written : State the researchers' finding first, then give the evidence.
- [1900 to 1950] "Officials in the state of Ohio" : The bans on The Birth of a Nation come in two paragraphs that list six places, then give separate dates for Chicago, Ohio and Cedar Rapids, then a count of twelve mayors, then say most places let the film play. A reader cannot tell how many places banned it or in what order : Give one count, two or three dated examples in order, and the fact that most places let it play, once.
- [2000 to Today] "The cattleman William Hale ordered the" : Nine people are named in two paragraphs, and the pronouns slip (a mother, a cousin and a sister are each called by her), so a reader cannot tell whose relative is meant. The murders are the heart of the section and the family tree buries them : Tell it through Hale, Burkhart, Mollie and Anna, give the others one line, and use names wherever a pronoun could point two ways.
- [2000 to Today] "By 2019 the members were 32" : The Academy paragraphs stack ten percentages and counts, and 75 percent means men in one sentence and white in another. The next paragraph says 13.5 percent of speaking characters are Asian while Asian people are 6.3 percent of the country, then says 29 films had no Asian speaking character, and never says how these fit together : Say the point first, keep a few numbers, and add one clause explaining how a high share and 29 films with none can both be true.
- [2000 to Today] "They bought about 780 million tickets" : This closes a stack of dates and dollars from 2007 to 2025 with no sentence saying what they add up to, and it says ticket spending rose 1.5 percent while tickets sold fell about 5 percent, with no word on why. The reader cannot tell whether theatres are doing better or worse : Lead the section with its point (fewer people go to theatres), explain the two opposite numbers in one clause if the source does, and cut dates that do not serve the point.
- [1900 to 1950] "FBI agents under J. Edgar Hoover" : The Chaplin box says the FBI investigated him and that his permit to return was canceled, but never says what he was suspected of or what reason was given, so a reader cannot tell why he was shut out : Add the stated reason in plain words, or say the records give none.
- [1900 to 1950] "They billed themselves under a stage" : The text says the pair used a name built from an insult to set themselves apart from white performers in blackface, but never gives or explains the name, so the sentence makes no sense on its own : Say in plain words what the name told audiences, or drop the sentence.
- [1800 to 1850] "No record states the cause of" : The Not known line in the Lane box says no record gives the cause of death and then, in the next sentence, names a probable cause with no source, so the reader cannot tell whose view that is or whether the book is guessing : Name who says so, or cut the second sentence.
- [1700 to 1750] "That play does not survive." : The section above says Aston's play about the colony is lost, but this box calls The Fool's Opera his play and says it was printed in 1730 or 1731. A reader can take that play to mean the printed one and think a printed play is lost : Say plainly that the Charleston play was a different play from the printed Fool's Opera.
- [1700 to 1750] "Williamsburg stands on land that the" : A paragraph about the land's earlier Native peoples drops into the middle of the playhouse story with no label or bridge, and the Charleston paragraph on the Etiwan does the same, so the reader loses the thread twice. Algonquian is never explained : Start each with a short run-in label, as Chapter 35 does in the Hannah Lyman box, or move them to the end of their sections, and explain Algonquian in a few words.
- [Before 1500] "Medicine societies among the Haudenosaunee use" : Haudenosaunee is used in the era lead before it is explained, and medicine society is never explained, since the policy is said to define it only as people who took part in the medicine, which tells a reader who does not know the phrase nothing : Explain both in a few plain words at first use, or say the policy gives no further meaning.
- [1800 to 1850] "Next: After 1850 white actors in" : The Next line at the end of this era says almost the same thing, with the same explanatory clause, as the first sentence of the next era's lead a few lines below, and the same happens at the ends of eras 7, 8 and 9. The reader reads one sentence twice in a row. The chapter-end Next line also says Chapter 35 covers clothes, hair and homes, but that chapter covers clothes, hair and cloth, with some furniture and no homes : Make each Next line name a new fact or a question and let the lead carry the sentence, and change homes to match Chapter 35.
- [card] "Five minutes? Read 'The African Theatre" : The card says about 125 minutes, but the ten era strips add to 131. The section named for the five-minute read is about 450 words, about 2 minutes, and reaches 5 only with the two story boxes after it, which the pointer does not name : Fix the total, and name the boxes or say about 2 minutes.
- [1900 to 1950] "By 1915: Hollywood had its first" : The line gives about 8,000 nickelodeons for 1915, but the text dates that number to 1908, so the snapshot states an older figure as if it were 1915's. In Era 10 the By 2021 line also sits after sections that already run to 2026 and before sections that go back to 2015 and 2017, which jumbles the reader's sense of time : Use a 1915 figure or say by 1908, and move the By 2021 line to where the story reaches 2021.

Flow: uneven. The best stretches (the Accomack court case, the African Theatre, Astor Place, Hattie McDaniel, the strikes) read well, but story boxes often retell the paragraph above them, and several sections (the Lakota tours, the Weinstein trials, the Academy counts) are runs of dates and numbers that a reader will not hold.

## Chapter 35: Styles

- [The 1600s] "Charged with wearing silk, she wore" : The Hannah Lyman box is about 510 words and only about a fifth of it is about Hannah. The rest is a land deed, King Philip's War and counts of people charged (23, 45, 36), and its first paragraph repeats the chapter opener almost word for word, with the hook echoing the opener's last sentence. A reader who came for Hannah loses her : Keep the box on Hannah and her court day, move the war and land lines into the dress-laws section or cut them, and give the box something the opener did not already say.
- [The 1600s] "A first offense brought a warning" : The 1663 scale (a warning, then 20 shillings, then up to 40) does not match the cases that follow, where Hannah is fined 10 shillings and a woman charged before is also fined 10 shillings, the same as under the 1651 law. A reader following the money cannot tell which rule the 1676 court used : Say which rule applied in 1676, or drop the 1663 scale.
- [1750 to 1800] "American cabinetmakers, the skilled makers of" : The Federal style section and the Eames chair box (1950 to 2000) are about furniture, and the Era 5 lead has a sentence on furniture makers, in a chapter whose card, spine and three questions are about clothes and cloth. Skipping the Federal style section, as the card allows, still leaves that lead sentence behind, and the section itself is a string of names with nothing holding them : Say in the card or the sections why furniture belongs here, or cut it, and cut the lead sentence to match.
- [2000 to Today] "He was paid a few cents" : The sentence gives a few cents a piece and, in the same breath, about a dollar for each finished item, and piece is never defined in the chapter (El Monte uses 5 to 7 cents a piece, Los Angeles 5 to 12), so the two figures look like a contradiction. His 15 dollars an hour at Los Angeles Apparel also sits oddly beside the 5.50 dollars an hour in the section above : Define piece once (one sewing step or one garment), and say how his hourly pay fits the 5.50 figure.
- [The 1600s] "Seven married women of Northampton faced" : Two paragraphs reduce to counts of women charged in five towns (7, 9, 9, 7 and 3) and then to the towns themselves being charged, with no point stated, and the Essex County figures just above (18 cases, 12 let go) are more of the same. The numbers wash over the reader : Say what the counts show, keep one or two figures, and cut the rest.
- [1950 to 2000] "The Pawnee Nation once lived in" : A paragraph on the Pawnee's old homeland, attacks by another nation and the 1874 move to Indian Territory sits between the court cases on hair and the Pawnee boys' own story, which waits for the next paragraph. The reader loses the thread of the hair cases : Cut the removal history to one sentence or move it after the boys' story.
- [1900 to 1950] "He held her as his property," : The sentence says the plantation owner held Lowe's great-grandmother as property and that by law she could not refuse him, but does not say what happened. The book says rape plainly elsewhere (Elizabeth Keckley's box), so a young reader may not see that this means rape and that Georgia was born of it : Say it in the same plain words the book uses for Keckley.
- [1900 to 1950] "Annie Turnbo made a hair product" : Annie Turnbo is called Annie Malone in the era lead, and only later in the section is it said she became known as Malone. The Walker box opens on a woman named Sarah Breedlove without saying she is Madam Walker, and both women's products are called Wonderful Hair Grower with no comment on the match. A reader will take these for errors or for different people : Give each woman both names at first mention and add a clause on the shared product name.
- [closing] "Churro wool after 1598, dyes made" : The answer to the third question is a bare list of five materials with dates and says nothing about how each changed what people wore. It names Bakelite, which the chapter shows only in jewelry, telephones, radios and billiard balls : Add a clause on what each material changed and swap Bakelite for a material that was worn.
- [card] "From the first colonial dress laws" : The spine ends on the CROWN Act, a term the chapter does not explain until its last section, and the paragraph after it strings two long lists (eight kinds of clothes, then seven examples of rule makers and cloth makers) that a reader cannot hold at the start. The Five minutes pointer names a section of about 420 words, about 2 minutes : Put a few plain words after CROWN Act in the spine, cut the preview lists to two or three items, and fix or reword the five-minute pointer.
- [2000 to Today] "Guards at a prison in Louisiana" : The Landor paragraph sits under the CROWN Act section, but it is a prison case under a religious-freedom law, and the section says the Act covers employers and public schools. Nothing tells the reader why the paragraph is here, and it may look as if the CROWN Act failed him : Add one sentence saying how his case relates to the Act, or give it its own label.
- [The 1600s] "Most of the Pueblo peoples rose" : This paragraph names Po'pay of San Juan Pueblo, but Chapter 34 names him of Ohkay Owingeh (the same town, which Chapter 34 says the Spanish renamed San Juan), so a reader of both chapters thinks they are two towns : Use one name with the other in brackets at first use in each chapter.
- [1800 to 1850] "He woke up alone in the" : The sentence puts a description of the slave pen right after the US Capitol, so a reader can take the Capitol itself to be the building where traders locked up enslaved people : Say slave pen first, define it, and then say it stood near the Capitol.
- [The 1500s] "The accounts give different amounts." : The paragraph gives five figures in four lines (fourteen rows, 14 arrobas, about 350 pounds, 392 pounds, about 200 pounds) plus an old unit, which a reader cannot hold. The point is only that the soldiers took a huge weight of pearls and the sources disagree : Say that in one sentence with the lowest and highest weights and drop the old unit.
- [1700 to 1750] "Many of those cloths were coarse," : The law's list of cloths is followed by a glossary of eight cloth names where the word coarse turns up in nearly every sentence, so the definitions blur and the reader gets names and no picture : Keep the law's list, say once that all were cheap and rough, and define only two or three.
- [1900 to 1950] "The order banned hoods, capes and" : The paragraph lists nine banned details (hoods, capes, scarves, wide belts and hems, cuffs, pleats, tucks, gathers) with a definition for most, and the section never says plainly why the order was made, although the chapter's first question asks why : Open with the reason in one sentence and keep three examples.
- [1950 to 2000] "James David Martin, 17, killed Michael" : The sneaker section jumps from hip-hop fashion to a murder story with no sentence tying it to the chapter's questions, and the paragraph after it ends on a source calling the reports exaggerated without saying which reports, so the reader cannot tell whether sneaker robberies were common : Add a bridge sentence saying what the case shows about clothes, and say plainly what the police counts show.
- [2000 to Today] "Counts of the dead run from" : The Rana Plaza paragraph gives five numbers for the dead and injured (three of them nearly the same), and the paragraph on the later safety plans gives 15 companies in one sentence and 17 in the next, which a reader tries to reconcile : Give the range once (about 1,100 dead) and one count for the plans.
- [1800 to 1850] "Godey's Lady's Book was a magazine" : The paragraph is nine numbers (years, dollars and copies sold) and never says what the magazine did to fashion : Say what it did, if the sources say, and keep two numbers.
- [Before 1500] "At Cahokia, archaeologists found a copper" : The paragraph spends its length on a workshop's size, a 1956 find, a lost location and a 1999 re-find, none of which bears on what people wore : Keep the one sentence on what the workshop made and cut the rest.
- [The 1500s] "A French group led by René" : The era lead announces Fort Caroline, but no section picks it up and nothing links it to clothing, and the lead also bundles unrelated items (graves, a dagger, sheep). It is a fact that arrives and leaves : Cut it or give it a clothing link.
- [1750 to 1800] "George Washington took the oath as" : In the Washington box, the brown wool suit from Connecticut appears in the era lead, the Who line, the hook and this first sentence, so the reader meets one fact four times : Keep it in the lead and the hook, and open the box's first paragraph with something new.
- [Before 1500] "The art is much older than" : The era ends on two sentences about museum pieces collected in the 1830s, in an era that ends in 1500, so the reader ends the era unsure what is claimed : Say plainly that the museum's pieces are from the 1800s and that quillwork is much older.
- [Before 1500] "Red ochre is a mineral made" : The definition says red ochre can be brown, red or yellow, so a reader asks how a red thing can be yellow : Define ochre as the mineral and say red ochre is the red kind.
- [1850 to 1900] "Davis and Levi Strauss & Co." : The box says they took A. B. Elfelt and others to court over the patent but never says who Elfelt was or what he did, so the sentence has no point for the reader : Say in a clause what Elfelt did, or cut the lawsuit.

Flow: uneven. The people and process stories (Jack, the indigo vats, Douglass, Keckley, El Monte) carry a reader well, but the early eras and the Massachusetts dress-law material are lists of items and counts, and several boxes and sections wander from their point.
