# R01 reader review: front matter and Chapter 1

Method note: word counts are whitespace tokens that hold a letter or digit, markup left out, read at 200 words a minute (the book's own pace). Book-wide figures come from all 37 chapters in build/review. Quotes are the first six words of the passage.

## Front matter

- [How This Book Works] "and the glossary at the back" : The book promises a glossary that lists every explained word with its chapter. The contents list has none, the only back matter in the built book is the sources essay, and this sentence is the one place the word glossary appears, so a reader who looks for it finds nothing : Build the glossary and add it to the contents, or cut the promise until it exists
- [Ten-Minute History, The 1500s] "Columbus's ships came first, to Guanahani" : The paragraph says Spanish soldiers reached what is now the United States and then that Columbus came first, to the Bahamas, so a reader takes away that Columbus reached the United States. Chapter 1 says he crossed four times and never set foot in it : Say plainly that Columbus never reached the mainland, and start the United States story with the first Spanish expedition to Florida that anyone wrote down (Ponce de Leon, 1513, in Chapter 1)
- [Ten-Minute History, 1800 to 1850] "Mexico's leaders gave up more than" : No war is named anywhere in the paragraph, so a reader thinks Mexico chose to hand over California and the rest, while the paragraph's own first line says the country grew by force : Name the war of 1846 to 1848 and who won it in the sentence before the treaty
- [Ten-Minute History, 1750 to 1800] "The first census, in 1790, counted" : The intro says every fact here is told in full, with its source, in a chapter, but no chapter gives the 3,929,214 total, and none gives 16,112,566 Americans serving in the Second World War (the chapter says more than 16 million and names the Defense Department as the source). Census is not explained here either : Use only figures a chapter gives, or add the missing figures and their sources to the chapters, and say in a few words what a census is
- [Ten-Minute History, Before 1500] "The one visit from across the" : Chapter 1 tells a Polynesian crossing to Hawaii between about 1000 and 1200, and Columbus (1492) and Cabot (1497) fall before 1500 by date, so one visit is wrong as written : Say the first known visit from Europe, and drop the word one
- [Ten-Minute History, The 1500s] "Spanish soldiers, and the diseases that" : How This Book Works defines the eras as before 1500 and the 1500s, but this paragraph and Chapter 1's 1500s section put Columbus (1492) and Cabot (1497) in the 1500s with no note, so a reader who reads Before 1500 in every chapter never meets Columbus : Say once in the front matter that the 1500s era starts with Columbus's 1492 voyage, or move 1492 and 1497 to Before 1500
- [How This Book Works] "Each chapter opens with a card:" : One paragraph holds seven ideas (card, era times, By lines, what to remember, story boxes, hard words, glossary), and Before You Start and Four Ways to Read explain the card, the bold lines and the boxes again within 60 lines, so a reader meets the same description three times : Give each feature one line, once, in a short list, and let the other two sections point back
- [How This Book Works] "ends with a short list of" : The two intro sections disagree on how a long era ends (a short list of what to remember, or the one thing to remember) and neither matches the chapters. Of 370 eras, 111 end with a one-thing line, only 46 of those add a dated list, 17 add before-and-after lines that start with a year, 48 add neither, and 109 add a Next line. The same sentence says long eras carry By lines, but 59 of the 111 have none, three of Chapter 1's four among them : Describe the ending once, as it is (one thing, sometimes dates, a Next line), and say By lines come only in the longest eras
- [Before You Start] "and you can skip it." : This says any story box can be skipped, but Chapter 1's card names four other sections as the skippable ones. In Chapter 1 the 35 boxes hold about 62 percent of the text, every section of 1900 to 1950 is a box, and four of the five closing dates (1021, 1806, 1909, 1969) and the answers about Peary and Byrd come only from boxes, so a reader who skips them cannot follow the closing : Say the boxes are the main stories and that the card's skip line is the only skip advice
- [How This Book Works] "A hard word is explained in" : The promise (the first time it appears in a chapter) clashes with Before You Start (each era stands on its own), and Chapter 1 follows neither. Treaty and diplomats are explained twice in back-to-back era openings, scurvy is explained, used twice bare, then explained again, geologist, Great Basin and Inuit are used before they are explained, and census, Soviet and hostage never are : Pick one rule, and if eras stand alone, gloss a word at first use in each era
- [Ten-Minute History, 2000 to today] "Hijackers flew airliners into the World" : The ten paragraphs run 107 to 128 words, but the later ones carry 15 to 17 numbers each (this one 17 in 123 words), so the opening line's thread is gone by the third sentence and a reader skims : Keep three or four events a paragraph, round the counts, and leave exact figures to the chapters
- [Ten-Minute History, 1800 to 1850] "one demographer puts the loss at" : Hard words with no gloss where they first appear (demographer, census, militia, Confederate, electoral votes), though the chapters use plain phrases such as a scientist who studies the size of populations : Use the chapters' plain phrases or add a few words after each
- [Four Ways to Read] "The 37 'five minutes' sections add" : By word count the 37 sections total about 19,200 words, which is 1 hour 36 minutes, not 3 hours. They run 194 to 981 words and average 518 (2.6 minutes). The cards come to 58 minutes, not 50, so the whole short road is about 3 hours 20 to 3 hours 40 minutes, not four and a half hours : Recompute from the final text, or say up to five minutes each
- [Four Ways to Read] "Before 1500: about 2 hours 15" : Some era totals no longer match the strips in the chapters. Adding the 37 strips gives 2 h 37 for Before 1500, 11 h 48 for 1900 to 1950 (not 11 h 30) and 9 h 36 for 2000 to today (not 8 h 45), while the other seven are within 12 minutes. The contents list also gives 280 and 135 minutes for Chapters 25 and 34, where their cards say 275 and 125 : Recompute the ten totals and fix the two contents times
- [Four Ways to Read] "across more than 500 years in" : It promises one chapter in one to two hours, but 11 of the 37 chapters run longer (up to 4 hours 35 minutes), and more than 500 years understates Chapter 1, which starts 21,000 years back : Give the real range (about one to four and a half hours) and say Before 1500 reaches back thousands of years
- [Four Ways to Read] "The 37 cards take about 50" : Every one of the 37 cards (282 to 339 words) puts a story hook of about 85 words and a what-to-watch paragraph of about 105 words, about 190 in all, between the one-line point and the three questions, and the description lists neither. The cards measure 58 minutes, not 50 : Add the two paragraphs to the description, or move the questions up under the one-line point
- [Four Ways to Read] "which answer the three questions and" : Every closing also has four more lines (what changed, what is not known, where to go, what comes next) that the description leaves out, and the 40 minutes counts only the two described parts (the whole closing is about 61 minutes) : Name the other four lines, or say the 40 minutes counts only the answers and the dates
- [A Ten-Minute History] "A Ten-Minute History" : The 1,242 words take about 6 minutes at the book's own 200 words a minute, so the title overstates the time by four minutes : Retitle it, or state the real time
- [Ten-Minute History, 1950 to 2000] "The Soviet Union dissolved on December" : The paragraph never says what the Soviet Union was or that it and the United States were rivals, so the last sentence arrives from nowhere and the Moon landing has no reason : Add one clause on the rivalry earlier in the paragraph
- [Ten-Minute History, The 1600s] "'20. and odd' captive Africans at" : A quote with a period inside looks like a typo and is not explained, in the first place a reader meets the 1619 landing : Write about 20 captive Africans, and give the old wording once with a few words of explanation
- [Ten-Minute History, 1950 to 2000] "Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, stopped" : Does not say why the residents stopped riding the buses, so the reader sees a strike with no cause : Add in a few words that it was a protest against bus segregation
- [Ten-Minute History, Before 1500] "and some researchers question that date" : Says the date is questioned, but Chapter 1 ends the White Sands section with three agreeing tests, the closing repeats that, and nothing there says anyone still doubts : Say the same thing in both places (questioned in 2021, two more tests agreed in 2023)

Flow: uneven. The three intro sections are short and plain but describe the same furniture three times and drift from the chapters, and the Ten-Minute History turns into a run of dates and counts by its fifth paragraph.

## Chapter 1: Exploration and Discovery

- [The 1500s] "killed the people who fought back" : The one-thing line says the armies killed people who fought back, but the section shows both armies killing people who were not fighting: the Tiguex men who had put down their weapons and were tied to stakes to be burned at Arenal, the Turk strangled on Coronado's order, and a man from Hymahi burned to death under questioning. The closer understates the section and gives a wrong idea : Say they killed people who fought back and people who had surrendered
- [1800 to 1850] "By 1840: The last rendezvous had" : Both By lines sit inside sections, not between them. The 1840 line follows Bridger's story, which runs to 1881, so the year goes backward, and the 1810 line sits under the Pikes Peak heading, a section the card says to skip. A reader who skips it loses the first status line (the era is 5,714 words and 2,593 come before the first By line) : Put each By line between sections, in the era's own order, and keep it out of boxes and skippable sections
- [Card] "For the first nine eras, watch" : The same paragraph ends by saying that after 1950 the only places left were the ocean and space, but era 9 is 1950 to 2000, so the two things to watch cover eight eras and the count is off by one, on the first screen of the chapter : Say the first eight eras
- [1850 to 1900] "By 1900 the West was surveyed" : The closer says the census message is why explorers turned north, but the era's own opening says they had already turned north by 1890. The one thing also leaves out the people the explorers met, the card's second thing to watch : Drop the so and put the people into the one thing
- [Era openings] "The explorers of the 1600s went" : The Next line closing an era and the opening of the next era say the same thing in all four eras that have a Next line (2 to 3, 6 to 7, 7 to 8, 9 to 10), so the reader reads the same point twice in a row : Keep the Next line or the opening sentence, and let the opening start with new facts
- [1950 to 2000] "One question this era leaves open:" : The four closers take four shapes (dated bullets, before-and-after lines, One number, one question). This one asks whether anyone would go back and says the next era answers it, so it is not left open, the answer is only that four people flew around the Moon and did not land, and a rhetorical question in the furniture reads as performed : State the fact (no one has walked on the Moon since 1972, and in April 2026 four people flew around it without landing) and use one closer shape
- [Card] "In the South Dakota Cultural Heritage" : The card is 322 words, and about 205 of them (a story and a what-to-watch paragraph) sit between the one-line point and the three questions. The Five minutes pick, Toward the North Pole, stops in 1898, before Peary's 1909 claim that the third question and the closing dates need : Put the questions right under the point, shorten the two paragraphs, and pick a five minutes section that reaches one of the questions
- [Story boxes] "Four men out of an army" : In the Cabeza de Vaca box the first sentence repeats the hook almost word for word. The hook returns within two paragraphs in 13 of the 35 boxes by my count (Smith's scalp, Andrews's eggs, Columbus's lookout, Walsh's cracked window, Apollo 11's one in five), so the reader reads the best line twice in a row : Make the opening sentences add a new fact, or lift the hook out of the body
- [1800 to 1850] "French officials controlled only a small" : The next sentence starts with It and reads as the small part, not the whole of Louisiana, and the long clause defining the land west of the river is hard to follow. The boxes later say Louisiana Purchase, a name this deal paragraph never gives : Make Louisiana the subject, use La Salle's wording (the western part of the Mississippi's river basin), and name it the Louisiana Purchase
- [1800 to 1850] "The doctrine of discovery. American officials" : The idea behind every claim in the chapter (La Salle's claim for France in 1682, the Spanish claim to California in 1769) is first explained here, in the middle of one expedition, in legal wording that never says the people living there did not count : Say in plain words what the rule was and whom it left out, and point back to La Salle's claim
- [The 1500s] "Christopher Columbus crossed the Atlantic four" : The 1500s era opens with Columbus's 1492 voyage and Cabot's of 1497, and the era closer lists 1492 first, with no line saying why 1492 sits in the 1500s while the strips and front matter name the eras Before 1500 and The 1500s : Say once in the era's opening that this era starts with Columbus in 1492
- [The 1500s] "Coronado reported that the people of" : The last paragraphs of the Cabeza de Vaca box stumble. Coronado is named here before the section that introduces him, the phrase about attacking women is vague, and the final paragraph gives two firsts and Arab family roots that the box never supported, since it said only that Estevanico spoke Arabic : Introduce Coronado in a clause, say plainly what the report accuses Estevanico of, and say in one sentence who came first (Juan Garrido, 1513) and what the record shows about Estevanico's roots
- [1950 to 2000] "On October 4, 1957, Soviet engineers" : Soviet appears five times in the era and is never explained, and no line says the Soviet Union and the United States were rivals racing to space, so Sputnik, Gagarin and the Moon program come with no reason for the hurry : Add one clause in the era opening naming the Soviet Union as the rival country
- [1950 to 2000] "In 1969 the people running the" : Says the Tektite project turned her down but not why, and the next paragraph gives her the first all-woman team, so the point of her story is missing : Give the reason the records give, or say the records do not give one
- [1900 to 1950] "It contains navigation readings that were" : The readings were erased with no actor named, while the hook says somebody erased them, so the reader assumes Byrd did : Say who erased them, or say the diary does not say
- [1800 to 1850] "Breckenridge counted 120 to 150 dead," : The counts differ about sevenfold (120 to 150, over 175, and 600 to 700 on land plus 200 or more in the water, while the opening paragraph says at least 120) with no sentence saying the sources disagree. The same pile-up of rival counts sits in the Mabila, Columbus's captives, Sand Creek, White Sands and Walsh paragraphs : Give one line (the sources range from 120 to more than 800), then name each source once
- [2000 to Today] "NASA's rover Curiosity landed in Gale" : Six paragraphs of missions (Curiosity, Perseverance, Ingenuity, New Horizons, the two Voyagers, Webb), each with its own dates, numbers and instrument names and no thread saying what each found or why it matters, so a reader skims : Give each mission one line on what it found, and cut the Voyager power figures and the instrument name
- [1750 to 1800] "Daniel Boone marked the road that" : The first three paragraphs stack names and dates (Walker 1750, Martin 1769, Fort Stanwix 1768, Boone 1767 and 1769) before Boone does anything. La Salle's four ship names and the four marines killed with Cook have the same shape : Start with what Boone did and move the earlier names to where they matter, and keep one ship and one marine name or say the number lost
- [The 1600s] "Four small European settlements stood at" : The era counts four, but St. Augustine (1565), told in the section before, is not among them, and Santa Fe is far from any edge, so a careful reader counts five : Say at least four, or add St. Augustine and drop the edges
- [1700 to 1750] "The Horse People told them that" : A paragraph about 17 destroyed villages has no link to the journey, and the lead plate named in the heading and the card comes only in the last paragraph. The Bering box before it speaks of a second voyage though no first voyage is named : Cut or link the villages paragraph, open the section with the plate, and call 1728 the first voyage
- [1850 to 1900] "Soldiers cut scalps and other parts" : The Beckwourth box never says what Beckwourth did at Sand Creek, so the next line, that the Cheyenne would not trade with him, has no cause. Earlier, a sentence about the father owning the mother hints at rape without saying it, while the Tiguex passage says it plainly : Say what the records say he did at Sand Creek, and use the plain word in the earlier sentence or cut the hint
- [All eras] "A fur trapper who survived a" : Some openings are knots. The Glass box opens with one sentence that stacks a bear attack and the men who left him, the Sacagawea box with an interpreter definition nested inside a clause, and the Henson box with Congress thanking Peary and honors coming more than 25 years later, later than what. Henson's seven voyages over nearly 23 years also run from 1891 to 1909 : Split each into one-fact sentences and fix the 23 years
- [1800 to 1850 and 1850 to 1900] "Smith came back in August 1827" : Some sections jump with no bridge. Smith's box goes from the Mojave crossing to a camp in Oregon a year later and then to a wagon train in Kansas without saying why, and the 1898 islands section starts with McKinley and never says what it has to do with exploring : Add one linking clause to each
- [All eras] "Era 1 of 10" : Era 1 says about 6 minutes (915 words is 4.6) and Era 10 says about 8 (1,296 words is 6.5), while the other eight strips are within half a minute. The strips add to 117 minutes and the text measures about 116, but the card and contents say about 110 : Recompute Era 1, Era 10 and the chapter total
- [Closing] "Peary's North Pole in 1909 and" : The third answer names only the polar claims and the Norse camp, though the chapter also shows other firsts in doubt (Ponce de Leon in Florida, the Ute climb of Pikes Peak), so a reader who read those sections finds the answer thin : Add a clause for them, or reword the question as the two polar claims

Flow: uneven. Most story boxes carry one clear arc with a hook, but name and number piles, rival counts and knotted openings make many of them hard to hold, and the era closers, By lines and Next lines change shape from era to era.
