# R06 reader review: Chapters 10 and 11

## Chapter 10: Energy

- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "From about 1920 to 1925, husbands," : The Osage murders name ten people in three short paragraphs and depend on family links (a sister here, a cousin there, a nephew and his wife) that the reader has to hold in mind. The word their before cousin Henry Roan has no clear owner, and Ramsey is said to admit that Hale hired him to kill Roan while no one is said to know who fired the shot, which reads as a contradiction. : Open with one plain sentence on Hale's plan (kill one family so his nephew inherits their shares), give Mollie's family as a short who's who, keep one killing per sentence, and say why the shooter of Roan is unknown.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Thomas Edison built his Pearl Street" : The Edison box, the section The current war and the Westinghouse and Tesla box tell the same facts three times within about fifty lines. AC is defined twice, Edison's side calling AC deadly appears in both the box and the section, the point that direct current voltage cannot be changed appears twice, a quarter of a square mile appears three times (section, hook, box), and the Tesla patents, the Chicago bid and Niagara to Buffalo are retold in the third block. Neither box says anything about Edison or Tesla as a person. : Tell each fact once, in the section, and let each box tell the person (what Edison and Tesla did and wanted), or cut the repeated lines.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Tribal chairman George Gillette spoke at" : The 1948 contract, the chairman's complaint and the 1950 vote to accept read as if the tribes agreed, yet the section has just said that about 300 families had to leave. The reader cannot tell what the vote decided or whether the tribes had any choice. : Give the steps in order in plain words (what was signed in 1948, what the 1950 vote was about, who forced what) and say what the records do not show.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "From about 1690, whale oil was" : Says whale oil was one of the main lamp fuels for 150 years, but Era 6 says it cost too much for most families and that most homes burned cheaper fuels, and Era 7 says kerosene replaced camphene in most homes. A reader who believes the first line is thrown by the card's first question. : Reword to say richer families burned it, or add that most families used cheaper fuels, so the picture stays the same from the 1600s to the 1860s.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "A fire at the Avondale mine," : The era jumps from the 1896 Niagara story back to 1869 with no bridge. The sections run Drake 1859, Pearl Street 1882, the current war to 1896, Avondale 1869, then Hopewell 1883, so the reader loses the order of the years. : Add a one-line bridge back to coal, or move Avondale ahead of Pearl Street so the years run forward.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "The first generator at Hoover Dam" : The Hoover Dam sentences follow the TVA land-taking paragraph with no lead, no place and no word on who lost what, so they read as part of the TVA story, and Grand Coulee then starts under a new lead. : Give Hoover Dam its own bold lead and one line on where it is and why it is here, or cut it.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Members of the Standing Rock Sioux" : The section jumps from mine deaths and black lung to an oil pipeline with no bridge, and it never says how the pipeline connects to the oil boom earlier in the era or to the card's question about who pays. : Give it its own heading and one sentence tying it to the oil and to that question.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "A small amount of radiation escaped." : The doses (0.08 and 1 millisievert) come with no scale, so the reader cannot tell whether small really is small. The word radiation is explained only in the next section, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission is named here and explained only in Era 10. : Add one everyday comparison for the dose if the research bank has one, explain radiation in this paragraph, and say what the commission is at first use.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "A 2013 study estimated that the" : The Church Rock section ends on a state agency calling the effects limited and a governor refusing a disaster request, with nothing about what the 1,700 Navajo people lost, so a young reader may conclude little harm was done. Three Mile Island was measured in millisieverts above and is measured in curies here. : Say what the records show about harm to the people and their water, or say the records say little, and use one unit or explain the two.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Accounts differ about which company owned" : The Monongah passage gives an owner dispute, a company president's explanation, two expert theories and a runaway coal car in one run, so the reader cannot tell what happened or who was to blame. : Say first that the cause is not settled, give each theory in one short sentence, and name the owner once.
- [Opening] "A tavern keeper named Jesse Fell" : The first paragraph joins an 1808 fireplace and an 1869 mine fire with only a shared town between them, so the reader cannot tell why the two sit together. Fell's story then returns in Era 6 nearly sentence for sentence, with the same quoted phrases. : Add one plain sentence saying how the two events connect, and shorten Fell's second telling or point back to the opening.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800, and Era 7] "Josiah Hornblower was about 24 when" : Two boxes open by retelling the section above them. The Hornblower box restarts with the 1753 crossing, his father and Schuyler, all just told, and the section names Josiah in a source note before it says who he is. The Drake box repeats the first-well and 69.5 feet facts from the paragraph before it, and says he lost his money without saying how. : Start each box where its section stops, introduce Josiah as Jonathan's son at first mention, and say how Drake lost his money or that the records do not say.
- [Era 4, 1700 to 1750] "Dr. Charles Carroll, a surgeon, gave" : Two men named Charles Carroll appear in neighboring sentences and the reader has no way to tell them apart. : Tag the second Charles, or say the surgeon and his cousins without repeating the name.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "William Chadbourne, James Wall and John" : The box carries a person's name, but Chadbourne is named only in the opening paragraphs, the river has three names, and the box ends on James Wall's sworn statement, so the reader is never told what became of Chadbourne or why the mill matters. : Say what the records show about Chadbourne, use one name for the river, and end on the mill.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "The first man put to death" : The Kemmler passage (the chair and the execution) is more than half of the 560-word current war section, with a murder, a trial, a lawyer and two courts, and only one sentence before it ties the chair to AC and DC, so the reader may lose the war. The lawyer and courts sentence names five actors in two lines. : Add one bridge sentence on why Edison's side wanted AC in the chair, and cut the court chain to one sentence.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Westinghouse bid $399,000 to light the" : General Electric appears with no word that it was on Edison's side, so the reader cannot see why the two bids matter. : Say whose side General Electric was on.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "TVA lawyer John Snyder described how" : One paragraph holds a lawyer's quote, a definition of condemn, an economists' estimate, a historian's credit and a verdict on who decided, so the main point (the government set the price and owners had to take it) is buried. : Split it, lead with the plain point, and move the historian credit to the end.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Reporters at BuzzFeed News, for example," : Says some researchers think the official count of 246 deaths is far too low, then names BuzzFeed as the example without giving what BuzzFeed found, so the reader gets a claim and no number. : Give their count in the same sentence or cut the example.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "That was more than 300 times" : The comparison to the Shippingport plant asks the reader to remember a figure from Era 9, and a reader who opened at random has never heard of Shippingport. : Give the plant's power here (60 megawatts) or compare to something from the same era.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Anthony F. Lucas led the drilling." : A run of names with nothing holding them together: Lucas, salt domes, a landowner name, the Texas Company, Texaco and Gulf Oil. The reader is not told why any of them matters. : Keep Lucas and the oil companies that grew from Spindletop, cut the land name, and say what a salt dome has to do with oil.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "At least 11 of these Africans" : Eleven people are taken to Flowerdew in 1619 but the 1624 and 1625 count lists seven, and the reader is left to guess about the other four. : Say that the records do not say what happened to them, or explain the difference.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "American wells produced about 5 billion" : A paragraph of nine figures and years with no picture of a cubic foot or a barrel. The energy independence section does the same, with 101.0 quadrillion Btu against 100.2 and two new units to learn for a gap of 0.8. : Keep the 16 times figure and one record, and say the independence gap in plain words, such as less than 1 percent more than Americans used.
- [Era 1, Before 1500] "Federal officials in charge of forests" : The era is before 1500, but these paragraphs run through the 1900s, 2013, 2014, 2020 and 2021 with no sign that the story has jumped forward. : Add a one-line bridge saying the practice goes on today, or move the modern facts to the era that matches.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Coal passed wood as the country's" : The closer is introduced as one thing but holds three facts, and it leaves out the era's two longest stretches, the current war and Avondale. The Era 8 closer likewise joins two things with and. : Make each closer one idea that matches the longest section of its era.
- [Era 1 strip] "about 4 minutes" : Era 1 says about 4 minutes but has about 380 words, which is two minutes at the rate the other strips use (about 200 words a minute). : Recount the era strips.

Flow: uneven. Short sections, plain definitions and strong human stories carry the reader through the fuels, but several story boxes retell the section above them and the later eras pile up names, units and figures.

## Chapter 11: Transportation

- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "He could not swim, and one" : The hook says Garfield could not swim and fell overboard, but the box says the fourteen-falls claim comes from later campaign biographies and not from the record of his time on the boat, and nothing in it shows that he could not swim or fell even once. The reader leaves believing a story the box itself doubts. : Make the hook say what the record shows (he ran away at 16 and worked six weeks on the Evening Star) and tell the falling story as a tale told later.
- [Opening] "Each new way of moving people" : The spine promises that enslaved people and immigrant workers did much of the building, but none of the three questions asks about the builders, so the closing answers drop that half of the spine, though Eras 6 and 7 spend pages on canal diggers, enslaved railroad crews and Chinese workers. Only the Chinese workers come back in the dates and the Not known line. : Add a question about who built the routes (or fold it into the second) and answer it in the closing.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Nixon later called the meeting for" : The Montgomery bus boycott is named once, in a clause, and never told, though the third question asks how Black travelers fought segregated seating. The reader meets it with no explanation and no pointer to where it is told, and the closing leaves it out. : Tell it in a few sentences or point to the chapter that does, and add it to the third answer.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900, and four other eras] "Frank Sprague built the Richmond Union" : Many story boxes restate the section just above them. Sprague's box repeats the East Cleveland, Baltimore and Montgomery line and the first-successful claim, and its hook (ten cars) is not in the body, which gives 23 cars by May. The canoe in the dark appears three times for Sarah Kemble Knight (Era 4 strip, hook, body). The Miles of Smiles, McLean and Berwick boxes retell the George nickname, the Ideal X and its 58 containers, and the hearing officer's ruling. : Cut each box's first paragraph back to what the section has not said, or say something new about the person.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Black and white riders traveled together" : The Freedom Rides section names seven other people (officials and victims), but the only Black rider named is John Lewis, with no description, and it never says what the riders did after the beatings, so the third question is answered by what was done to them. : Add a sentence or two on what the riders did next, as the research bank allows, and trim some names.
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "The horses came north first, with" : Says the first horses came north with Coronado in 1540, but Chapter 10 has de Soto landing in Florida in 1539 with about 250 horses, so a reader of both chapters sees a contradiction. : Say the first horses in the Southwest, or mention de Soto's.
- [Era 5, 1750 to 1800] "Men writing the Constitution watched his" : The Fitch box repeats the section above it (the Convention watching, the first paying service) and adds only that the service failed and he died in 1798. The Fulton box then retells that Fitch failed and Fulton lasted in its Who line and again in its first lines. : Fold the two new facts into the section, or give Fitch something of his own, and tell the Fitch and Fulton contrast once.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Cornelius Vanderbilt left the largest American" : Three story boxes sit back to back (Hung Wah, Vanderbilt, Twain) with nothing joining them, going from Chinese rail crews to a steamship millionaire to a river pilot, so the reader loses the railroad thread in the longest era. : Put one connecting line before each, or move Twain to the steamboat era and keep Vanderbilt beside the railroad sections.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "US soldiers on horseback led by" : Sand Creek arrives with no bridge and is dated a few months before the Pawnee paragraph that precedes it, which itself comes from nowhere, and the section does not say how a Colorado massacre belongs to a railroad story until the next paragraph. : Put the events in date order and add one line on the link between the fighting and the railroad.
- [Era 3, The 1600s] "Its lower part followed the Pequot" : The first rider's route (New Haven, Hartford, Springfield, Worcester) and the lower part along the Pequot Path from Providence to New London are not one line on a map, so the reader cannot picture the road. : Make clear whether these are two routes or one, and name which path each followed.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "First Grandy paid $600 to his" : Five men's names (James Grandy, Trewitt, Mews, Sawyer, Minner) in two short paragraphs, and the text does not say how Mews's claim led to Sawyer owning him, so the three payments are hard to follow. : Lay out the three payments one per line (who got the money, what happened next) and drop names that do not matter.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "California voters decided the question for" : The section runs vote percentages, a $205.7 million campaign, a union lawsuit, court dates, a Massachusetts deal and two officials' names without saying what the drivers got or why contractor versus employee matters to their pay. : Open with one plain sentence on what was at stake, keep one result per paragraph, and cut Massachusetts to one sentence.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "The Ford Model T went on" : The price falls from $825 to $260 with no reason given, and the chapter never mentions the assembly line, so the reader cannot answer how it got cheaper. : Add one sentence on how Ford built cars faster, or say the records do not say.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Car crashes killed 54,589 people in" : Two sentences of figures with no reason for the peak or for the fall by 1975, so the reader gets numbers and no idea. Chapter 10 tells the 1973 oil cutoff and the 55 mile an hour limit, but this chapter does not connect them. : Say what the records give as the reason, or say they do not say.
- [Era 8, 1900 to 1950] "Justice Stanley Reed wrote that under" : The Supreme Court's reasoning is squeezed into one sentence about travel between states being harder, and the reader cannot see why that means the law could not apply to buses crossing state lines. : Say in two plain sentences what Reed meant, for example that a different seating rule in each state would make trips across state lines too hard.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "American trainmen, the workers who ran" : Two similar rates (about 8.5 and 1.73 in every 1,000) and a twice-the-British-rate claim leave it unclear whether 8.5 is the American or the British figure. : Write it as one sentence saying that in 1889 about 8.5 of every 1,000 American trainmen were killed on the job, twice the British rate, and move the coupling rate to its own line.
- [Era 7, 1850 to 1900] "Crews laid 10 miles and 56" : The Promontory story is told at least four times (chapter opening, Era 7 strip, The railroad across the continent, this paragraph), with the three men's names and the week against four months repeated, and eight Irish workers and eight Chinese workers appear within three sentences. : Keep the full telling in The Chinese workers, the five-minute pick, and cut the others to a clause.
- [Era 1, Before 1500] "On the Great Plains, dogs were" : Repeats Chapter 10's first era (no draft animals, dogs dragging a travois, canoes), with the travois described differently in the two chapters, so a reader who goes from 10 to 11 meets the same page twice. : Keep one telling and point to the other chapter.
- [Era 2, The 1500s] "Juan de Oñate led a Spanish" : Chapter 10 told the same 1598 march and Coronado's 1540 march, with the same place named differently (San Juan de los Caballeros there, San Juan Pueblo here) and the Camino Real described twice, so a reader of both may think there were two colonies. : Use one set of names and say that Chapter 10 tells part of this, or shorten.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "Americans bought about 1.3 million electric" : Chapter 10 told the electric car story too, with the same September 30, 2025 tax credit end but different figures (hybrids or electric about 20 and 22 percent there, electric only 8.1 and 7.8 percent here), so a reader of both will think one is wrong. : Say what each figure counts, or tell the story in one chapter and point to it from the other.
- [Era 9, 1950 to 2000] "Until 1978 the members of a" : The Fares passage uses CAB Sunset Act without tying CAB to the Civil Aeronautics Board, knows two economists only by surname, and puts two percentages side by side (22 percent lower than it would have been, 40 to 45 percent lower in the decades after), one of them an imagined comparison. : Spell out CAB once, give the economists full names or say two economists, and keep the one figure the reader can picture.
- [Closing] "On the Erie Canal a ton" : The answer to the first question stops at 1869, though the chapter spends its second half on container shipping, jets and airline fares, and the What changed line says by 2000 though the chapter runs to today. : Add one later number (the container saving or the jet) to the first answer.
- [Era 10, 2000 to Today] "After the company received an official" : Does not say who gave the order or what it ordered (stop driving, or stop using the name), so the reader has to guess. : Say who ordered what.
- [Era 6, 1800 to 1850] "Ads in Ireland that called for" : Pay is given as dollars a month, then as cents a day in the next sentences, and the hoggees' pay is a third figure, so the reader cannot compare them. : Use one unit throughout, or say that the monthly figure included meals.
- [Card] "about 70 minutes" : The ten strips add up to 77 minutes, and the Era 1 strip says about 4 minutes for about 470 words, which is under 2.5 minutes at the rate the other strips use. : Recount the strips and the chapter total.

Flow: uneven. Sections open on the point and the canal, railroad and bus stories are vivid, but many story boxes retell the section before them, several hooks promise things the box does not give, and the later eras run long lists of dates, names and dollar figures.
