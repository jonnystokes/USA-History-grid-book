## Chapter 20: Government, Law, and Politics

- [card] "What happens to people in power" : The third thing the card tells the reader to watch uses the 1735 Zenger jury as an example of how powerful people who break the law are treated, but Zenger was a printer tried for criticizing the governor, so the first example does not fit the thread and the card promises a pattern the chapter does not show : Swap that example for the governor tried by the king's judges in 1614, which does fit, or reword the thread so the Zenger jury belongs in it.
- [closing] "A jury, a court or the" : The card's third question names a president, a senator and a governor, but the answer gives a cabinet secretary, Nixon, two impeached presidents and a senator and never a governor, so the one governor the chapter puts on trial (Oñate, guilty on 12 of 30 charges in 1614) is left out and the question is only half answered : Add the 1614 trial to the answer in one sentence, and make the card and the answer name the same kinds of people.
- [1850 to 1900] "Under them, slavery ended, everyone born" : The era opener says the amendments made everyone born in the country a citizen, the Wong Kim Ark section says the same, and the 1924 section then says American Indians became citizens that year with no word on why they were outside the rule for 56 years, so the reader meets three claims that do not fit together : Add one plain sentence at the Fourteenth Amendment or at 1924 saying why Native people were not counted before 1924 (if the research bank supports it), and narrow the word everyone in the opener and in the Wong Kim Ark line.
- [1850 to 1900] "Mississippi's voting rules stood until the" : The sentence says the rules lasted until the 1964 amendment, but Era 9 says that amendment ended poll taxes only in federal elections, that state poll taxes fell to a 1966 ruling, and that the 1965 law is what put federal officials over voting rules, so a reader who gets there will think the chapter contradicts itself : Name what each later step ended, or say the rules stood for decades and Era 9 tells how they fell.
- [card] "Five minutes? Read The Zenger trial," : The section named is about 200 words, one minute, and the story that fills five minutes is in the two boxes after it (John Peter Zenger and Andrew Hamilton, about 900 words together), which the card does not name, so a reader who follows the card and stops at the end of the section gets the verdict without the story : Name the section and the two boxes after it, or point the card at the Zenger box.
- [2000 to Today] "About 80 of them were from" : The paragraph says about 140 officers were assaulted, about 80 of them Capitol Police, and then that about 114 Capitol Police officers reported injuries, which is more than 80, and nothing says injuries and assaults were counted differently, so the numbers look wrong : Add one clause on how the two counts differ, or drop one of the figures.
- [2000 to Today] "In all, about 3.6 million American" : The District, Puerto Rico and the island areas come with about ten population figures in three paragraphs, and this total does not match them, since the District plus Puerto Rico plus the island areas come to about 4.3 million, so a reader who adds them finds a gap. The same 3.6 million is repeated in the era closer : Give the island areas as one total, say which places the 3.6 million covers (Puerto Rico and the island areas) or add the District, cut the digits to the two or three that make the point, and make the closer match.
- [1900 to 1950] "Private schools, church schools and, later," : The sentence says homeschooling is legal because of the 1925 Pierce ruling, but a section in Era 9 says homeschooling became legal state by state in the 1980s and early 1990s, so the reader gets two different reasons and dates : Say here that the ruling protects private and church schools and leave homeschooling to the Era 9 section, or say the 1925 ruling is the base the later laws built on.
- [2000 to Today] "The United States Supreme Court's opinion" : The Bush v. Gore section opens inside the dispute, with the Florida court's recount orders and three counts (about 9,000 ballots, 215 votes, 168 votes) before it says what the fight was about, so a reader loses the thread in the first paragraph : Open with one plain sentence on the problem (a vote in Florida so close that hand recounts were ordered), then give the orders in one sentence without the counts.
- [2000 to Today] "A map drawn by race is" : The run-in label promises maps drawn by race, but the paragraph is about the 2013 Shelby County ruling on which places must ask permission before changing voting rules, which is not about maps, and it never says what the ruling changed for those places : Rename the label (for example, asking permission) and add one sentence on what changed once the formula was struck down.
- [1950 to 2000] "In this era the justices and" : The closer says the justices and Congress struck down most of the rules that kept Black Americans from voting, but the era shows poll taxes ending, federal examiners, advance approval of rule changes and equal districts, and never says reading tests or the property and residence rules ended, so the claim runs ahead of what the reader was shown : Narrow the closer to what the era shows, or add the missing step in the body.
- [The 1600s] "In the summer of 1619 Governor" : The 1619 scene (twenty-two men, the Jamestown church, July 30 to August 4, the one building that could hold them) is told in the chapter opening, again here with the same sentence about the church, and a third time in the Pory box with the surviving copy, so the reader hears one story three times : Keep the full telling in the opening or the Pory box and give this section one back-reference sentence.
- [The 1600s] "In 1646 the members of the" : Eight rule changes (1646, 1655, 1656, 1670, 1676, 1677, 1684, 1699) run through two paragraphs, going back and forth with no sentence saying what the pattern is, and the 1699 line barring women reads as if women had been voting until then : Open with the pattern in one sentence, keep the dates that matter, and say plainly whether women had ever voted.
- [1850 to 1900] "On January 31, 1865, the members" : The Lincoln box is about the writ of habeas corpus, and its last paragraph switches to the Thirteenth Amendment with no bridge, so the reader wonders why the subject changed and only the film line in the header hints at the link : Add one bridge sentence saying why the amendment belongs here, or cut the paragraph and let the film line stand alone.
- [1900 to 1950] "George Washington had served two terms," : The Roosevelt box opens on the court fight, then jumps to his four elections, a 1941 order against job discrimination and the camps, each with no bridge, so it reads as four stories under a hook that fits only the first, and the camps, the heaviest part, arrive with no lead-in : Give the camps and the 1941 order short sections of their own, or open each with one bridging sentence, and keep the box on the court fight.
- [1900 to 1950] "In the school year 1919-20, local" : Six figures (four percentages and two district counts) are packed into four sentences and the point, that school money moved from local to state, is never stated, and the last paragraph under the same label jumps to segregated schools with no bridge : State the point first, keep two figures, and move the California segregation paragraph under a label of its own.
- [1950 to 2000] "The NAACP's own history says he" : Marshall's record is given three ways (32 cases and 29 wins by the Fund's history, a 14 plus 18 split by the Times, 14 of 19 as Solicitor General by the Park Service) with a remark that two of the counts differ by one, and the Fund job is told twice in back-to-back paragraphs, so the story stalls in arithmetic : Give one figure (29 wins in 32 cases, as the Who line does), put the source differences in a short Known or Disputed line, and tell the Fund job once.
- [1900 to 1950] "A later House report, House Report" : A report number and a note that the report names two different first bills is a source dispute the reader cannot use, and George Henry White and Representative Dyer arrive with no introduction. The same habit shows in the Jackson box (two sources one vote apart on the House tally) and the 1965 school-funding section (April 11 or April 12) : Keep the main fact, move one-vote and one-day disagreements to a Known or Disputed line or cut them, and give each man a few words of introduction.
- [2000 to Today] "Republicans who acted as electors in" : The paragraph stacks abstract terms (electors, papers presented as certificates, the state's executive) with no who, how many or why, so a reader cannot tell what happened or why it matters : Say in two plain sentences that groups of Republicans signed papers claiming to be a state's electors, that the state's governor had not certified them, and that the papers were left out of the count.
- [The 1600s] "Royal judges in Mexico City tried" : The heading promises what the judges found, but most of the section tells the 1598 and 1599 events and the sentences, and the verdict comes last with only three of the twelve guilty counts named and no word on whether the cut-foot and enslavement sentences were part of the Acoma charge, so the reader cannot tell what the one governor in the chapter was punished for : Put the verdict and penalty first, and say which counts covered Acoma (or that the record does not say).
- [1750 to 1800] "So unmarried women and free Black" : The text says unmarried women could vote under the 1776 rule, then that the 1797 change made it easier for wives and widows, with no explanation of why married women were treated differently, so the reader gets two ideas of who could vote : Add one plain sentence on why married women could not meet the property test at first (what the research bank says) before the 1797 line.
- [1850 to 1900] "Dred Scott, an enslaved man, sued" : The section never says what Scott sued for (his freedom), so the ruling that he had no right to sue lands without its stakes, and the law Congress had passed that the Court struck down is never named : Add one sentence on what he asked for and name the law.
- [card] "The chapter also follows the laws" : The card names votes, judges, lawbreakers and schools as what the chapter follows, but about seven sections cover immigration and citizenship (1790, 1875 and 1882, 1898, 1921 and 1924, 1948, 1965 and 1986, 2002 and 2012) with no hint of why they are here, so they read as strays : Add immigration and citizenship to the card as a fourth thread, or open the first immigration section with a sentence on how it ties to who may vote.
- [1750 to 1800] "The National Constitution Center counts more" : The line says more than two dozen people were found guilty under the Sedition Act, but most other sources put the convictions near ten (about 25 were prosecuted), so the reader may take away the wrong number. Unsure, because I cannot see the book's source file : Check the figure against the Federal Judicial Center's count and change the line to the checked number.
- [The 1600s] "Twenty-two men, six days, and one" : This Pory hook is a triad of fragments, and about half the other hooks (the Washington, Madison, Lincoln, Rainey, Roosevelt and Johnson boxes) use the same two-beat shape of a setup then a twist, which reads as performed and makes the boxes sound alike : Rewrite each hook as one plain sentence that says what the person did and why it matters.

Flow: uneven. Strong signposts, plain definitions and run-in labels carry most sections, but the card and closing do not line up, several passages stack numbers and source disputes a reader cannot hold, and a few boxes change subject with no bridge.
