## Chapter 32: Art

- [Chapter card] "Five minutes? Read Photographs of hunger" : The section the card names is only about 520 words, under three minutes at the pace the era strips use (about 200 words a minute), and it ends on the sentence saying officials hired Lange to photograph the removal. What she photographed and how officials kept the pictures from the public is under the next heading, Dorothea Lange (about 370 words), so a reader who follows the card stops on a cliffhanger after three minutes, not five : Name both headings in the card (together about four and a half minutes), or tell the reader to read on through the Lange box.
- [1850 to 1900] "Ten prisoners died at Fort Marion." : The numbers do not add up. The two graves hold twelve men and that is called two more than the ten who died, but the next sentence says one of the ten, Grey Beard, is buried elsewhere, which leaves three men unexplained, not two. The next paragraph then packs all ten names into one sentence with each nation tacked on after its names, so the end of one list runs into the next man's name : State the count so it adds up (twelve in the graves, nine of the ten among them, three unexplained, or whatever the sources say) and give the ten names as three short sentences, one per nation.
- [1800 to 1850] "The Hudson River School's painters worked" : The paragraph contradicts itself (they worked in the Hudson valley, yet the name comes from shared aims more than from one place) and never says what the aims were. Five paragraphs of Mohican land and removal history then follow, strung on nine dates with no painting in them, before Cole appears, and the Writers paragraph about 14 lines earlier has already told the same move from New York to Wisconsin, with the same agents and treaties : Say in one plain sentence what the painters had in common and cut the name sentence, tell the Mohican removal once with only the dates that matter, and tie each step back to a painting or to Cole.
- [1900 to 1950] "Lange made seven pictures of a" : This is the section the card sends the reader to, yet it never names the photograph or says it became famous (the opening calls it a famous photograph, and the title appears nowhere in the chapter), so the reader cannot connect the two. In the same section the caption word destitute is not explained, the sentence saying the reporter named her can be read as gave her a name, and the Cherokee-ancestry sentences in the Florence Owens Thompson paragraph contradict each other with no sign that the records disagree : Name the picture (its usual title is Migrant Mother, if the bank carries it) and say plainly why it matters, gloss destitute, write printed her name, and cut the ancestry to one sentence saying the records disagree.
- [1950 to 2000] "Robert Mapplethorpe was a photographer who" : The reader is told people fought over this art but never what it looked like. Serrano's picture is described, Mapplethorpe's photographs are not, so the word obscene has nothing under it. The same gap appears in The Dinner Party (the plates show a shape based on the female body, and Alice Walker's complaint says Sojourner Truth was shown differently, with no word on how), in the CIA section (Socialist Realism is named and quoted as stylised and rigid but never explained), and in Gordon Parks's photograph titled American Gothic, a joke that depends on a Grant Wood painting that is never described and sits in a skip-listed section : Add one plain sentence to each saying what the work showed, in the book's usual plain words.
- [1850 to 1900] "Nathaniel Hawthorne's novel The Scarlet Letter" : Three paragraphs are strings of titles, dates and print runs (10,000, 300,000, 1.5 million, 500, 2,915, twelve poems, 65,000, 321 books) with no sentence saying what they add up to, and this section is not on the Can skip list. The same pile-up is in the Era 6 Writers section, Mercy Otis Warren's five play titles in Era 5, Era 9's Photographs and books (four authors, then Ruby Bridges and a Rockwell painting under a heading about photographs and books), and the five owners listed in Dave's box in Era 6 : Pick the one point each run serves (cheap printing reaching millions, Whitman paying to print his own book, one book's sales set against another's), keep only the titles and names that carry it, and give each kept work one plain sentence on what it is.
- [The 1600s] "Oñate sentenced the Acoma men over" : Over twenty-five reads as a count of men, not an age, and it sits in a sentence that also has a foot cut off, a defined term and twenty years. The section also retells Chapter 2 for about 180 words (the rape, Zaldívar's death, the attack) before it reaches the church and the burned masks that are its subject, and its last sentence says the revolt burned the mission churches across the province while San Esteban, built by Acoma workers, still stands : Write men older than twenty-five, cut the retelling to a sentence that points to Chapter 2, and say many of the mission churches.
- [Chapter card] "Chapter 32 of 37 · Art" : The minutes figure on this line is about 150, but the ten era strips add up to 161 (11, 4, 8, 7, 11, 23, 24, 26, 22 and 25) and the chapter runs about 32,000 words, which is about 160 minutes at the pace the other strips use. Era 1's strip is also about two minutes high for its 1,840 words : Change the card to about 160 minutes and Era 1 to about 9.
- [Closing] "Peale was paid for portraits with" : The answer to who got paid is never said in plain words (from the book's own examples, the people who held the artists got the money and the artists did not), and this example is about Peale being paid in enslaved people, Moses Williams's parents, not about an enslaved artist's work being paid for, so it does not answer the question the closing asks : Lead with one plain answer sentence, and drop or reword the Peale clause so it ties to an enslaved artist's work, as the Dave and Barnes examples do.
- [1900 to 1950] "By 1932: Stieglitz had shown that" : The line says Maria Martinez's pots were being sold from her family's shop by 1932, but her box puts Popovi Da's shop after Julian's death in 1943 and gives it no date, so the line has nothing under it. The By 1973 line in Era 9 says New York painters had toured Europe, when the CIA section says the paintings toured and the painters did not know : Make both lines say only what the sections before them said, and date the shop or drop it from the line.
- [1700 to 1750] "John Smibert was the first painter" : The claim that he was the first painter with formal art training in the thirteen colonies contradicts the paragraph above it, where Hesselius is said to have studied art in Sweden and Kühn and Johnston were working years before Smibert arrived in 1729, and the era opener itself says a few painters trained in Europe settled here from 1700 : Say one of the first, or say what makes his training formal.
- [1750 to 1800] "The Barnes family sided with the" : The family sails for England in late 1775 and only Prince's mother, Daphney, is said to be left behind, so the reader does not know where Prince was, and he turns up in the militia two years later with no link. The box also says he was baptized in 1745 and was in his late twenties when bought in 1769, which does not fit unless he was baptized at about four, which the text never says : Say where Prince was when the family left (or that the records do not say) and correct or explain the age.
- [1950 to 2000] "Jackson Pollock painted by walking around" : The floor-and-dripping method is told three times in 15 lines (the section paragraph, the Who line and this opener), then a fourth time in his own words. The same near-repeat is in Stuart's box in Era 5, where the one-dollar-bill engraving is told in the Who line, the first paragraph and again in the third, and in the Whitney protest in Era 9, told in its section and again in Faith Ringgold's box about 28 lines later : Tell each fact once, and let the box add only what the section did not.
- [1800 to 1850] "Smallpox was a disease caused by" : The paragraph opens with a textbook definition and gives no sign it is about the Mandan Catlin painted. The Mandan are not named until the next paragraph and Four Bears's death, the point, comes two paragraphs after that, so the reader is asked to hold a virus in mind without knowing why : Open with the link (five years after Catlin painted the Mandan, smallpox killed almost all of them) and give the definition second.
- [1900 to 1950] "Laura Ingalls Wilder's Little House books," : A new subject arrives from nowhere at the end of the Steinbeck book-ban section. Nothing in the text says her books were banned in 1939, so nothing links them to it, and the closing clause promising the reasons in the last era is a promise, not a bridge : Move the sentence to Era 10, where the reasons are told, or add one sentence saying why she is here.
- [1900 to 1950] "In 1927 she interviewed Kossola, also" : The paragraph is six sentences, five of them about capture and smuggling, with six names, and never says what Hurston did with the interview or why it belongs in her box, so it reads like another story pasted into hers. The box's hook also uses the word royalties, which is not explained until two paragraphs later : Add one sentence on what she wrote about him, or trim the capture story to what the interview meant, and gloss royalties in the hook.
- [1900 to 1950] "The tour was stopped in early" : The same event gets three endings in three sentences (stopped in early April with no one named, cancelled by Congress by the end of April, ended by Marshall in early May), so the reader cannot tell who did it or when. The Smithsonian paragraph in Era 10 does the same with the National Portrait Gallery's director, announced as fired on May 30 and then said to have resigned on June 13 with nothing between : Say who stopped the tour, or say the sources differ, and add one line on what happened between fired and resigned.
- [2000 to Today] "The Pew Research Center found, in" : This section repeats the era opener's smartphone count almost line for line and says nothing about pictures or art, so it reads as a stray statistic. The same thing happens in the Prices section (six sale prices, and the one sentence on where money went follows the Basquiat price so it reads as being about that sale), in the Books paragraph (ISBN numbers, Bowker, a percentage of new titles and audiobook dollars that cannot be parsed), and in the removal counts, which never say what kinds of books were removed or why : Cut the repeat or tie it to who makes pictures, give each run of numbers one plain point, and for the book removals say which books and what the complaints were.
- [2000 to Today] "He got a record on a" : After a paragraph that ends on the buyer, the reader can take he as Beeple. Digital tokens and a record on a computer network are never explained, and the last sentence (both sides owned shares in tokens tied to other works) carries three ideas and no reason it matters. The Thaler paragraph also leans on an 1884 case about a photograph of Oscar Wilde, quoted in legal words, with no plain bridge to why a computer program cannot be an author : Write the buyer, explain a token in one sentence, and restate the 1884 case in plain words (a photograph counts as art because a person made choices, if that is what the case held).
- [1800 to 1850] "put every bit all between" : The book glosses most hard quotes right after them, but not Dave's line about the jar holding 14 (gallons, which the reader is never told), nor his later verse about a jar made of cross, nor the long twisted sentence in Tanner's 1909 quote in Era 7 about whether a teacher would keep him, nor the no-people line from Wilder in Era 10 (what is wrong with it is never said) : Add a plain restatement after each, as the chapter does for patronage and uncultivated.
- [Before 1500] "At 86, he carved an eight-foot" : A living carver born in 1938 arrives at the end of the Before 1500 era right after the Mimbres looting, and the only link is one sentence about coast carvers many paragraphs earlier. The hook is about a pole of Raven and Fog Woman but the box never says who they are, and it calls him a Tlingit of the Raven side with no word on what that side is : Add a one-line bridge (the Northwest Coast carving did not stop in 1500) and one plain sentence on Raven and Fog Woman and the two sides.
- [Before 1500] "A company that sells copies of" : This paragraph is about a company that sells replicas, and its last sentence says the company also hired coal miners, which means the Pocola Mining Company from the paragraph before, so the reader cannot tell which company is meant. The paragraph before it stacks George Evans, a loan, a grandson's tuberculosis and R. W. Wall, and this one adds five more diggers' names, none of whom the reader needs later : Name the Pocola company in that sentence, and keep only the names tied to something they did, with one clause each.
- [1750 to 1800] "A planter paid him for portraits" : The hook ends with the words with two people, which can mean portraits with two people in them, and it leaves out enslaved, which the era opener has. Lower in the box, Moses Williams is said to be not free under the 1780 law that ended slavery slowly, with no reason why, though his parents were freed in 1786 : Write two enslaved people in the hook, and add the one fact that explains why that law did not free Moses (if the bank has it).
- [The 1500s] "That same year the colonists talked" : White's box ends with his getting back in August 1590, and the word back is used for two opposite trips in consecutive sentences, the three-year gap is unexplained, and the last line, the word Croatoan carved on a tree, hangs, since the reader was told the Croatoan were friends of the English and is never told what became of the colonists or that no one knows. Earlier in the era, Le Moyne's lost drawings are told in the opening sentence, again in The attack with a closing twist, again in Drawn again, and again in White's first paragraph : Say he sailed back to Roanoke, add one line on the gap and a Not known line on the colonists (or a pointer to Chapter 2), and tell the Le Moyne loss once.
- [1950 to 2000] "Maya Lin, a student of 21," : Two paragraphs sit under the heading Native artists and Native objects with nothing Native in them, Maya Lin's contest win and Fred Wilson's Mining the Museum, and Wilson's passage ends on a correction about a whipping post with no point stated, so the reader loses the thread : Move them to a heading they fit or add a bridge sentence, and say why the whipping-post correction matters.

Flow: uneven. The story boxes (a hook, then a life told in order) and the strips and closers keep a reader oriented, but runs of titles, dates and prices, three detours (Stockbridge land, smallpox, Kossola) and facts told two or three times within a few lines make a reader lose the thread.
