## Chapter 24: Slavery and Freedom

- [The 1600s] "Slave traders carried Olaudah Equiano across" : The section tells his Middle Passage as plain fact all the way through, and only the last two lines say two records give his birthplace as Carolina, which would mean he never made the crossing he describes. A reader who stops early takes it as proven, and one who reaches the end feels misled. : Open by saying this is what his own book reports, put one plain sentence about the birthplace dispute before the account, and keep the Known and Not known lines.
- [The 1600s] "Anthony Johnson, an African, had arrived" : The John Casor paragraph never says that Johnson claimed Casor as his servant, where Casor had gone, or why Johnson sued a white neighbor, so the reader cannot tell who held Casor or what the court is deciding until the ruling in the next paragraph. It sits on the card's five-minute path. : Add the missing link in one or two sentences (who claimed Casor, why Parker is in the case, what Johnson asked for) before the ruling.
- [1900 to 1950] "An all-white jury in Mississippi had" : A murder appeal sits in the section on voting tests and never says how the two connect, so the reader cannot see why Henry Williams is here. The understanding-clause paragraph before it is also hard, because it says a man had to be able to read and then that a man who could not read could still register, and gives the point (one officer decides who passes) only afterward. : Add one sentence linking the jury to the voter lists (if the research bank gives it), and open the understanding-clause paragraph with what the rule was for.
- [1850 to 1900] "The 14th Amendment was part of" : The 14th Amendment is named three times in this era (this By 1868 line, the Revels story, the Black Codes paragraph) and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 once, and nothing says plainly what either did (only the Revels story hints it was about citizenship), though the chapter's third question depends on them. The first real explanation comes in Era 9. : Give one plain sentence at first use (who it made citizens, what equal protection means) and let Era 9 point back to it.
- [1900 to 1950] "A prisoner named James Knox, from" : The paragraphs give three causes for Knox's death (a reported suicide by poison, an autopsy finding of heart failure with poison added after death, then fatal injuries from whipping and hot water) and never say how they fit together or which one the sources trust, so the reader leaves not knowing how he died. : Say in two plain sentences what the autopsy found and what the later accounts add, and mark which part is disputed.
- [1750 to 1800] "British soldiers fired on a crowd" : The heading promises two promises of freedom, but the section opens on the Boston Massacre and Crispus Attucks with no link to any promise, names only Dunmore's promise, and gives the Patriot promise as one clause in the last sentence, so the reader looks for a second promise and does not find it. : Bridge or move the Attucks paragraph, and give the Patriot promise its own bold label and two sentences (who promised what to whom).
- [1700 to 1750] "A string of fires began in" : The section gives counts and three judges' names but never says what the records show about whether a plot existed, or what the offer of a reward to the main witness means, so its point is missing. Only one of the three judges appears again. : State the point in the first sentence, say plainly what the offer to Mary Burton means, and cut the two judges who do not return.
- [1750 to 1800] "Twelve years later she was a" : A kidnapped seven-year-old becomes a published poet in one sentence, with nothing on how she learned to read and write, so the reader gets the gap and not the story the box is about. : Add one or two sentences on how she learned (as the research bank supports) before the 1773 book.
- [1800 to 1850] "Solomon Northup picked cotton in Louisiana" : Northup arrives with no introduction. Nothing says he was born free and kidnapped until his own box, about 13 minutes of reading later, and his book title is cited as if the reader knew it. In the box, four names in one short paragraph and a sale under another name that the text never explains are hard to follow. : At first mention add a clause (born free, kidnapped in 1841, story told below). In the box keep the buyers who matter and say who gave him the second name.
- [1950 to 2000] "Alex Haley published Roots in 1976" : The 1976 book, the January 1977 miniseries and the rise in family searches are told in the era opener, again in this section, and a third time in the Alex Haley box, and the note that parts of Haley's work were challenged appears three times (this section, the box's Who line, The challenges), all in an era of about four minutes. : Keep the facts in the box, cut this section to one bridge sentence or fold it into the opener, and say the challenge once.
- [1800 to 1850] "In 1850 the members of Congress" : The Next line at the end of this era and the first lines of the Era 7 opener, two lines later, say the same two things in almost the same words: the 1850 law that forced people in free states to help catch escapees, and slavery ending fifteen years later. : Give the Next line a different teaser or open the Era 7 text on the Fugitive Slave Act.
- [card] "Five minutes? Read" : The five-minute pick is a 437-word section, about two minutes at the strips' own pace, and it touches only the first of the three questions. The Can skip list is about 340 words, and skipping Churches and hush harbors removes the only definitions of hush harbors and AME, both used again in Era 7, so the card's promise that the chapter still makes sense without them does not fully hold. The ten strips add up to 108 minutes, not the 105 on the card. : Build the five-minute path from sections that touch all three questions, move the two definitions out of a skippable section, and make the minutes agree.
- [card] "Slavery here was built on purpose" : The spine is one 41-word sentence with four ideas. The card's second paragraph of prose is 115 words, the longest in the chapter, and holds the scope, two things to watch, a list of three rules and a hand-off. It uses repair with no gloss until Era 10, and says Chapter 25 takes over the rights campaigns after 1877, while Eras 8 and 9 here cover Jim Crow, the stolen vote, Brown and the 1964 and 1965 laws. : Cut the spine to two short sentences, split the paragraph, say what repair means, and reword the hand-off to match what this chapter covers.
- [1850 to 1900] "The 13th Amendment became part of" : This one-sentence paragraph sits after Juneteenth, apart from the paragraph that opens the 13th Amendment, so it reads as part of Juneteenth and the dates jump back. The Five Tribes paragraph that follows has no bridge from Era 6, which showed the Choctaw and Chickasaw driven off their land and now shows them as enslavers, and the heading does not name it. : Move the sentence up into the 13th Amendment paragraph, add a bridge sentence to the Five Tribes paragraph, and add it to the heading.
- [1850 to 1900] "The crossing took 45 days." : The hook promises the voyage, but the box never mentions it and 45 days appears nowhere else. Later the survivors ask Meaher for land, he refuses, and then they buy land from him, which reads as a contradiction because the text does not say they asked for it as a gift. : Add a sentence on the crossing or change the hook, and say they asked him to give them land.
- [1850 to 1900] "Delegates met at the Mechanics' Institute" : The second sentence, about reopening a meeting that was rewriting the state constitution, cannot be followed: what meeting, who ended it, and why the mayor called it unlawful. The totals then do not add (34 Black and 3 white dead make 37, not 38, and 119 and 17 wounded make 136, not 146) and the text does not say the counts come from different sources. The By 1868 line says nobody was punished in either city, but only the Memphis text says so. : Give the setup in two plain sentences, show the two counts as two sources or give one, and support or soften the By 1868 claim.
- [1800 to 1850] "South Carolina's lawmakers had banned teaching" : Six sentences carry eight dates and six states, and the heading says reading while the first two laws ban writing, so the reader never learns when reading became the crime. The next paragraph names Nat Turner's revolt about six minutes of reading before his box, and the new laws after Turner are told again in his box and in the By 1840 line. : Say in one sentence when reading itself became the crime, keep three laws, and give Turner a clause at first mention.
- [1850 to 1900] "Within a year of the war's" : President Lincoln signs the bank law in March 1865, then President Andrew Johnson reverses Sherman's order that fall, and nothing says Lincoln was killed in April, so a reader who opens the chapter at this section is lost. : Add one clause at Johnson's first appearance.
- [1800 to 1850] "Douglass wrote in his Narrative (1845)" : The Narrative line about hearing Auld forbid his lessons is quoted with no word on what Douglass took from it, so the reader cannot see why a ban was a lesson against slavery. The Who line says he learned to read on his own, but the story gives him Sophia Auld's alphabet and bread-paid lessons, and it jumps from age twelve to the 1838 escape. : Add one plain sentence on what the ban taught him, change the Who line to say he learned in secret, and add a bridge to the escape.
- [1800 to 1850] "Nat Turner was an enslaved preacher" : The box gives counts (at least 55 white people killed, more than 100 Black people killed, 19 hanged) but no scene, plan or reason, so the reader learns nothing about what Turner did or why, in a chapter whose second question is how enslaved people fought back. The Movie line says the 2016 film differs from a 1915 film of the same title without saying what that film was. : Add two or three sentences on what he did and what drove it (as the research bank supports), and either say in a clause what the 1915 film was or cut the line.
- [1750 to 1800] "Historians disagree about the right word" : This paragraph lands in the middle of the Paris account, between Madison Hemings's statement and the promise about her children, so the thread breaks. The 2026 DNA sentence says descendants carried more Jefferson DNA than expected but never says what was expected or why that settles who the father was, and the text says she was never legally freed and then that the 1833 census lists her as free, with no line saying she lived free in practice. : Move the disagreement after the DNA paragraphs, say what the study found, and add a clause for the 1833 line.
- [1800 to 1850] "Her son Peter was five years" : Four men appear in two sentences in a chain of sales (Dumont, Eleazar Gedney, Solomon Gedney and a brother-in-law in Alabama), and the box never says when Peter was sold compared with her walking away, so the reader cannot follow who did what. : Name only Dumont and the man who sent Peter to Alabama, and say when the sale happened.
- [1850 to 1900] "Harper's Weekly printed pictures made from" : The magazine is said to have used another name, and later accounts are said to say its story mixed up three men, which leaves the reader unable to tell who Peter was, which name the records use, or what was confused. : Say plainly which name the army records give and what the magazine got wrong, or cut the two sentences.
- [Before 1500] "Native nations took captives in war" : The opener says most nations let a captive become family and that the Northwest Coast bought and sold people whose children were slaves, and the two sections then say it again nearly sentence for sentence, so about a third of the era's 234 words repeat. : Cut the opener to one sentence or let the sections carry only new detail.
- [1850 to 1900] "After 1900 sheriffs, judges and mine" : Lynched is first used in this Next line and again in the Era 8 opener with no meaning given, and is defined only in the Lynching section further down. : Gloss the word where it first appears.

Flow: uneven. The story boxes and the early eras read easily in short plain sentences, but from 1750 on many people, cases and terms arrive with no setup or are explained later or never, and the same facts are told in the opener, the section and the box.
