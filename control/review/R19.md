## Chapter 30: School and Education

- [Closing, R19 l.2072] "Horace Mann argued for free schools" : The first answer gives the year Mann began arguing, not a date school became free, and the chapter never says when free schooling became general, so half of the first question goes unanswered. The second answer leaves out Native children (the card's own spine and the boarding-school sections) and says families got in through the courts, though the chapter shows courts upholding exclusion in the Sarah Roberts, Plessy and Martha Lum cases : Add one plain sentence on when free schooling spread (or say no single date exists), put Native children and the 1975 law letting nations run their own schools into the second answer, and say the courts both kept children out and let them in.
- [1850 to 1900, R19 l.780] "The mayor read out the Virginia" : The mayor gives the Virginia law's top penalty as a $100 fine and six months in jail, but the section on Virginia's 1831 law (l.634) says a white teacher was fined $50 and jailed up to two months, so one law has two sets of numbers : Make the numbers agree, or say which law each set comes from.
- [1850 to 1900, R19 l.726] "Alvord's count for the half year" : Seven lines pack about 18 numbers (4,027 schools, 8,907 teachers, 210,018 pupils, 554 and 635 buildings, three summer months, four school subjects) plus a note on double counting, so a reader skips the run and misses the point (freed people paid for 1,094 schools, and about one million pupils were taught in five years) : Keep three numbers (1,094, 210,018, one million), say in one sentence what they show, and cut the month and subject counts.
- [1950 to 2000, R19 l.1577] "In much of the Southwest, school" : This is the section the card sends a short-on-time reader to, and its first three paragraphs are survey percentages (17 numbers: a third of 5,800 schools, 15 in 100, two-thirds in Texas, 5 in 10 poor barrios, then 48, 12, 7 and 3 in 100 on enforcement) before any child speaks, while the strongest part, the seventh graders' own essays, comes after : Open with the detention slip and the children's words, and shrink the survey to two sentences.
- [1750 to 1800, R19 l.404] "Ann Wager taught every class the" : The Williamsburg Bray School is told six times (opener, era intro, school section, curriculum paragraph, and twice in the Ann Wager box), five of them in under 80 lines, each time with the point that the London charity wanted slavery taught as God's will, and the box retells the section above it point for point (taught every class, died in 1774 and the school closed, spelling and prayers) : Say the God's-will point once in the section, cut it from the era intro and the box, and let the box keep only what the section lacks (her 20 pounds a year, rent, widowhood, Isaac Bee).
- [1700 to 1750, R19 l.231] "In 1774 Lewis Burwell advertised for" : Isaac Bee is told three times (opener, here, the Wager box) with the forge-a-pass line quoted word for word twice, and here he arrives in the wrong era before the reader knows his school. The example also fits badly, since the ad says he would get someone else to write the pass, and Isaac could read, which this 1740 law did not ban : In the 1740 section say only that enslavers feared forged passes, and keep Isaac's story in the Wager box with a pointer back to the opening page.
- [1850 to 1900, R19 l.918] "Officials of the US Department of" : The 2024 Interior count (417 schools, 973 deaths, 18,624 names, 65 burial sites) is told here, in the 1900 to 1950 section, in the 2000 to Today section on the investigation, in that era's closer and in the closing Not known line, and the 2024 national monument proclamation is told here and again in 2000 to Today, so a start-to-finish reader meets the same numbers five times : Tell the count and the proclamation in full once (where the investigation is told) and give the other places a short pointer.
- [1850 to 1900 and 2000 to Today, R19 l.828] "By 1899: Southern lawmakers had written" : By-lines are the place-markers for a reader returning after a break, but this one comes before the 1884 line (l.882), the 2023 line (l.1849) comes before the 2022 line (l.1911), and the 1948 line (l.1237) comes before sections dated 1928 to 1944, so the dates run backward : Order them by date, each at the end of the stretch it sums up.
- [1950 to 2000, R19 l.1774] "One question left open: Would the" : The closer says the next era answers whether schools stayed desegregated, but the answer never appears in words. It sits in the third of five paragraphs of percentages in the 2000 to Today separation section, which uses four different yardsticks, and the 43 percent figure first appears in this closer, not in the Era 9 body. Era 10's closer then says arguments over whether children could go to school faded, which its own separation and discipline sections do not show. The Era 6 closer's bullets (deaf school, North Carolina lashes, teachers' school) also skip the free-school half of its one-thing sentence : Open that section with a plain sentence (mostly no, from about 43 percent in the mid-1980s to about 16 in 2021), keep one yardstick, put the 43 in the Era 9 body, and match the bullets and the Era 10 line to what each era shows.
- [1900 to 1950, R19 l.989] "The federal government's own figures show" : More than 50 numbers in about 280 words (graduates, enrollment, the Depression, the war dip, girls and boys, school-term days that repeat the 144 and 99 of the previous era), then 10 more in the one-room counts with seven-digit bus figures, though the era intro and closer already carry the one number that matters. The same wall appears in the literacy counts (1700 to 1750, l.267) and the 1838 wage paragraph (1800 to 1850, l.532) : Keep three or four figures and the reason (jobs vanished in the Depression), and say in a sentence what the buses did.
- [2000 to Today, R19 l.1899] "The same survey shows which children" : More than 50 numbers in 11 lines, all in one pattern (X of every 100 students, Y of every 100 hit, suspended, expelled, arrested), and only the girls sentence says what the pattern means, so the reader cannot tell which comparison to hold. The same wall is in the college counts (1950 to 2000, l.1503), the disabled-child counts (l.1649), the homeschool counts (l.1833) and the separation counts (l.1859) : State the finding once in words (Black boys and disabled children were punished out of proportion), give three pairs as examples, and cut the rest or set them in a short list.
- [The 1600s, R19 l.149] "When one classmate did not know" : The paragraph never says why Cheever beat Barnard for another boy's failure (the warning is two paragraphs up), and its label calls that classmate another boy, so a reader thinks there are two boys and loses who beat whom : Tell it in order. Cheever had warned that he would beat Barnard whenever a classmate failed. A classmate failed and Barnard was beaten. The classmate then skipped his lessons on purpose and Barnard was beaten for days. Barnard beat him after school and he never came back.
- [1850 to 1900, R19 l.920] "They flogged children, beating them with" : The paragraph glosses flogging as beating with a whip or a rod, lists whipping as a separate act, then says the report does not say what tools staff used, so the reader cannot tell what the difference is or what the report says. The whole list is then repeated almost word for word in 2000 to Today (l.2017) : Define flogging as beating without naming tools, or say the report does not define it, and keep the list in one place.
- [1950 to 2000, R19 l.1365] "Of the Southern cities counted, the" : A list of seven city percentages ends on Kansas City, Missouri as the lowest of the Southern cities, which reads as a mistake and as a wall. Two other Era 9 figures look wrong at a glance: 13,249 Catholic schools in one year and a high point of 13,292 the next (l.1631), and the By 1990 line (l.1657) says more than 1,000 magnet schools when the section says about 1,000 in 1981 and more than 2,400 by 1991 : Cut the list to two cities and one sentence, explain or drop Kansas City, drop the second Catholic figure, and fix the By 1990 line.
- [The 1600s and later, R19 l.193] "Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck was the first Native" : The story boxes retell the section above them. His 1665 degree and being the only Native student to finish come in the Harvard section, the Who line, the body and the last line (four tellings in 25 lines). The Horace Mann box repeats the 1837 job and the eleven years (l.480), the John Dewey box repeats the 1896 to 1904 school and The School and Society (l.1115), and the Peter Mills box repeats the whole case from the section on seven children, which also lists seven names before the box picks one (l.1641) : Start each box where its section stops (his letter and early death, Mann's boyhood, Dewey's own career, Mills's day in court), cut the retold facts, and trim the seven names to two or three.
- [1900 to 1950, R19 l.1205] "Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote" : The opinion is quoted in long split pieces with hard words (degenerate, imbecility, manifestly unfit) and the first quote gets no plain restatement (only the vaccination quote is explained), and the line about three generations is explained only by saying the third was Vivian, so the first two are never named : Add one plain sentence after the first quote (the state could stop people it judged unfit from having children) and name the three generations (Carrie's mother, Carrie, Vivian) if the research bank has them.
- [1800 to 1850, R19 l.632] "Georgia's lawmakers passed their law in" : After Virginia and North Carolina get their own paragraphs, three states are packed into one run of years and amounts, Virginia's 1831 penalties come back a second time under the other states, South Carolina gets one sentence that says nothing about what changed, and the Walker and Turner paragraphs ask the reader to sort eight laws before or after two events : Give each state one line (year, who was punished, how), keep Virginia in one place, say how South Carolina's law widened, and swap the chronology for one sentence (most of the laws followed Walker's Appeal and Turner's revolt).
- [Several eras, R19 l.526] "Her book about Bridgman, built mostly" : A handful of sentences need a second read. Here the pronoun could mean Mary Swift or Laura Bridgman and the teachers' journals have no owner. Others: the missions sentence that lists Florida, then Virginia, then New Mexico out of order (The 1500s, l.51), Webster's spelling changes given with no example (1750 to 1800, l.446), the Hartford seminary sources, where two sources have near-identical names (1800 to 1850, l.550), the blind school set up in 1829 but opened in 1832 in a father's house with no father named (l.590), the Cumming ruling, where the parents' request to cut off money to the white high schools is never stated (1850 to 1900, l.794), and the Mendez hook, where took means accepted (1900 to 1950, l.1315) : Rewrite each in plain order with one example and name the people, for instance Mary Swift's book, drawn from journals kept by Bridgman's teachers.
- [1900 to 1950, R19 l.1045] "A court fight over religious teaching" : The heading calls it a school prayer case but the text never says what the religious teaching was, and the section ends on a legal timing point (federal courts applying the ban on an official religion to the states, forty-five years later) that a 12-year-old cannot unpack : Say plainly what Freeman objected to (if the research bank has it) and either explain the last sentence in a clause or cut it.
- [1900 to 1950 and later, R19 l.1067] "Members of a joint committee of" : Paragraphs arrive with no link to the story around them. The Carlisle hearing detours into laundry, flour, football profits ($223,789.83) and double-charged train tickets before Julia Hardin speaks, and Pop Warner's departure appears with no reason (l.1067-1089). The New London school explosion has no tie to the chapter's threads and an ambiguous count of the dead (l.1289). Medgar Evers arrives in the G.I. Bill section as a stranger (l.1341). The Sputnik law splits the Little Rock and Virginia school-closing story (1950 to 2000, l.1393), and a paragraph on tablets and home internet ends the school-year section though it belongs with the 2020 closings (2000 to Today, l.1887) : Shrink the hearing's money to one clause of cause, give New London and Evers a one-line link or cut them, move the Sputnik law next to the 1965 federal money section, and move the tablets paragraph into the 2020 section.
- [The 1600s and 1800 to 1850, R19 l.570] "The word dunce comes from the" : Two word-history tangents pull away from the beatings. The dunce's cap passage goes to a thinker who died in 1308 and a debunked hat story after an English novel, with no American evidence (1800 to 1850, l.566-570), and the spare-the-rod paragraph goes to an Old English monk, a comic poem and three possible dates, 1662, 1663 and 1664 (The 1600s, l.155-157) : Cut each to one or two sentences, keeping only that the saying is not in the Bible and that the dunce's cap was a pointed paper hat used to shame a pupil.
- [The 1500s, R19 l.55] "Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and about" : Under a heading about mission teaching, the first paragraph is the 1565 founding of St. Augustine (a count of colonists, women, a chief named Seloy, a fort in his council house) and says nothing about teaching. The Paquiquineo box then takes about 520 of the era's 890 words and gives its reason only in its last line (the one person of the 1500s whose schooling by Europeans is recorded), after travel stops, three family names the record cannot choose between, and two killings : Open the Florida part with the teaching (Pareja's pupils learning to read in under six months), cut the founding to a sentence, and move the why-this-story line up under the hook.
- [Several eras, R19 l.800] "College leaders began to admit women" : Runs of names and dates with nothing holding them together, where the reader needs one idea. Here, five women and schools (Iowa 1860, Wilkinson 1873, Stockwell, Spencer, Cornell 1872) then four women's colleges with opening dates and class sizes (l.800-802). The same in Boston's land paragraph (The 1600s, l.103), the Primer's print history (l.123), the 1879 Carlisle approval by four named officials (1850 to 1900, l.892), normal schools state by state (l.866), Prince Edward's local committees and votes (1950 to 2000, l.1407), the Minnesota charter law's failed votes and first schools (l.1633), and the Mamie Garvin Fields box, which reads as a list of school names and years (1900 to 1950, l.1021) : For each, keep the one fact that matters, say what it shows, and fold the rest into a clause or cut it.
- [Before 1500, R19 l.33] "No writing system was in use" : The next sentence says the first writing system made for a language here was the Cherokee syllabary of 1821, but a few lines later Pareja's pupils write letters in their own language (The 1500s, l.59) and the 1600s sections have Timucua and Massachusett books, so the reader meets a contradiction in the first pages. The dispute paragraph on the Great Law's date also overlaps itself (oral tradition says 1000 to 1400, some say the late 1100s, written records say 1450) : Say invented by a Native person, or say Latin letters were later used to write Native languages, and give the Great Law one range (late 1100s to about 1450).
- [Card and later, R19 l.5] "School in America grew from a" : The spine is one 40-word sentence with two ideas, the first thing every reader meets, and a few lines further on sound staged rather than explained: a twist ending on who was not charged at the Scopes trial (1900 to 1950, l.1191), a stray short sentence about stones thrown at a bishop's house (1800 to 1850, l.604), the irony line that ends the Bray curriculum paragraph (1750 to 1800, l.386), the narrator's aside inside the history about the next era (1950 to 2000, l.1745), and the same three-part line (which school, what was taught, how learning was measured) in Era 9's Next, Era 10's intro and Era 10's closer : Split the spine in two, end each paragraph on its last fact, and keep the three-part line once.

Flow: uneven. Concrete stories with plain glosses carry the early eras and the Ruby Bridges, Mendez and Josephine Foster sections, but from 1850 on long runs of figures, facts retold across eras and in the boxes, and a few tangents make a reader lose the thread.
