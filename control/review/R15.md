## Chapter 25: Rights and Movements

- [2000 to Today] "Federal immigration agents shot and killed" : The DREAM Act and DACA story ends and, with no linking sentence, the killing of two U.S. citizens by federal agents in Minneapolis begins, so the reader cannot tell why it sits under immigration or what it has to do with a rights movement. ICE is never spelled out, and the era opener names the two victims without saying who they were : Give the killings their own short section, or add one sentence on the link (an immigration operation, and any protest that followed, or say the record is silent). Spell out ICE and put one plain clause about the two victims in the opener.
- [1950 to 2000] "Members of Congress then passed three" : The opener counts three laws, but this era later tells of at least three more federal laws (1973, 1974, 1975). The Civil Rights Act of 1964, the law the March on Washington led to, is touched in passing three times (Alice Paul, NOW, Bostock) and never told. The era closer then credits laws for what courts did to segregated schools and buses : Add a short paragraph on the 1964 act (what it banned, who signed it), change the opener to three of the laws, and make the closer say rulings and laws.
- [1950 to 2000] "The march's sponsors were all men." : Ten men and five groups are named in one paragraph, then the section ends without saying what the 250,000 people heard or did. King's speech appears nowhere in the chapter, so the reader learns who organized the march and who was left out but never what happened that day : Cut the list to Randolph, Rustin, King and Lewis, and add two or three sentences on the day itself (the speeches, the place, what came of it).
- [The 1600s] "Armed Dutch settlers at Corlaer's Hook," : The telling of Anne Hutchinson's death swerves into a Dutch killing of the Munsee, with new place and group names, and never says how that war led to the Siwanoy killing her, so the reader cannot see why the massacre is there : Keep one sentence on the war, say how it reached her family (or that the record does not say), and drop the extra names.
- [1850 to 1900] "More than 150 armed white men" : The Colfax killings are followed at once by President Hayes pulling soldiers out of a Louisiana state house in 1877, with no word that soldiers were in the South to protect Black citizens or what their leaving meant, so the second sentence looks unrelated : Add one or two plain sentences on why troops were there and what followed when they left.
- [2000 to Today] "The justices of the Supreme Court" : This opens the ending of the right to an abortion, but no earlier era tells how that right was won in 1973 (the word first appears in this era), so the reader gets the loss without the win, and the closing answer to the third question leans on it : Add a short 1973 paragraph in the 1950 to 2000 era, or one plain sentence here on how the right began.
- [1850 to 1900] "Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote that" : The Plessy ruling, the era's turning point, is given as two long legal quotations with no plain version, so the reader cannot say what the Court decided : Put the decision in one plain sentence first (separate cars were legal if called equal, and any sense of insult was the passengers' own doing), then keep Harlan's line.
- [1750 to 1800] "Her last sentence used the colonists'" : After two sentences about Blackstone, the pronoun and the phrase for her last sentence have no clear owner (it means Abigail's March letter), so the reader loses who is speaking and which sentence is meant : Name Abigail and the March letter in that sentence.
- [2000 to Today] "She described a round district that" : Justice Kagan's imagined district is written as fact, in the present tense, right beside Louisiana's real numbers, so the reader may think it describes Louisiana : Say she asked readers to imagine it, and keep the verbs conditional.
- [The 1500s] "Las Casas watched Spanish soldiers kill" : The opening line says he watched the killing at Caonao, but the account says he had stepped away to another part of town just before, so the hook and the story disagree : Match the hook to the account (he came back to the dead) or add what he saw.
- [1950 to 2000] "In the first Levittown, in New" : The lease is said to bar everyone who was not white except white servants, which makes no sense because white people were already allowed (it probably meant servants of other races). The section also never says Levittown was a new suburb of houses sold by one builder : Check the source and fix the clause, and open the section with one plain line on what Levittown was.
- [1900 to 1950] "The 2001 Oklahoma state commission put" : One paragraph stacks four numbers for the size of the mob from four sources, and a pronoun points back across a paragraph break, so the reader gets figures but no takeaway : Replace with one sentence giving the range, keep the note that the largest figure is thought to be a misprint, and name the sources only if needed.
- [1800 to 1850] "After 1850, people refused their rights" : This Next line can be read as people refusing rights, and the 1850 to 1900 closer repeats the same wording, so the reader has to stop and re-read at each era change : Write people who were denied their rights in both places.
- [1950 to 2000] "In the same years, farmworkers organized" : The farmworker story stops after a walkout and a vote to join, then two paragraphs give the accusations against Chavez, so the reader gets the accusations without learning what the strike won. The Moratorium deaths just above bury a three-or-four-dead dispute in names and sources : Add two sentences on what the farmworkers won before the accusations, and give the Moratorium toll as three or four with one line on the dispute.
- [1950 to 2000] "Califano signed the rules." : The result of the 1977 sit-in is a four-word sentence at the end of a paragraph about who brought meals, and the opener never says the protest worked. Judy Heumann's box then retells the occupation and the union money almost as told twenty lines earlier : Put the outcome in its own sentence right after the sit-in's length, and trim the box to what only she did.
- [2000 to Today] "In 2016, they said, the" : Three legal terms (seizure, clearly established, qualified immunity) are chained in one paragraph, so the reader cannot tell why no one was held responsible for Wilansky's injury : Open with one plain sentence on the result (the officers could not be sued because the right was not yet clear), then explain the terms one at a time or cut two.
- [1700 to 1750] "Quakers changed their rules about slavery" : Several sections run past their strip's dates, so a reader going by era or returning after a break looks in the wrong place. This one covers 1754 to 1778 under a 1700 to 1750 strip, the Tulsa payments story runs to 2026 under 1900 to 1950, and the anti-lynching heading that says thirty years covers 1900 to 1922 : Add a cue in each era opener where a section runs ahead, or move the late parts, and correct that heading.
- [1950 to 2000] "Black and white students used their" : The card's Five minutes pick is about 600 words, three minutes at the strips' own pace, and it ends on a head count of Freedom Riders with no word on what the rides changed, though the sit-in half does give a result : Add one sentence on what the Freedom Rides won, or point the card at a section that takes five minutes and lands.
- [1850 to 1900] "She believed she was 105." : The story ends on a twist after saying she died at about 86, with no word on why the two ages differ (the 1800 to 1850 era gives 1797 as her birth year), so the reader is left puzzled : Explain in one clause that she did not know her birth year, or cut the sentence.
- [The 1600s] "The Quakers put the paper aside" : The card's opening story (forgotten, found in 1844, lost again, found in 2005, now at Haverford) is retold here almost sentence for sentence about 170 lines later : Keep one full version, and here say only that the paper was forgotten and found again.
- [2000 to Today] "Tarana Burke built her work with" : Her use of the words before 2017 is told three times in ten lines (the last paragraph of the Me Too section, the hook, and this first sentence) : Cut the last paragraph of the Me Too section or this sentence.
- [1900 to 1950] "Fifteen years later, five families in" : Ten proper names (five families, four districts, one lawyer) sit in two sentences, then three lawyers and five groups in the next paragraph, so the reader cannot hold who sued whom : Say five families sued four districts, name only Mendez and Marshall, and say several groups joined.
- [1850 to 1900] "Colleges for women opened in these" : Four colleges with dates and student counts fill four sentences, then Spelman, three universities, two doctors and three lawyers follow as first-woman lines, with no takeaway until Bradwell : Keep two or three examples, say once what the run of firsts shows, then go to Bradwell and Lockwood.
- [1800 to 1850] "The committee's members refused each time" : The Sarah Roberts story ends with the father suing the city in 1848, and the result is never told here or in the 1855 section, so the reader is left hanging : Add what happened to the suit, or say the records do not say, before the 1855 section.
- [Card] "Most of the rights Americans have" : The spine is one 45-word sentence carrying three ideas (rights won after being refused, the cost, rights taken away again) and is the first line every reader meets. The card total is about 275 minutes while the era strips add to 281 : Split the spine into two or three short sentences, one idea each, and make the total match the strips.

Flow: uneven. The short early eras and most story boxes read easily, but from 1850 on many sections pile up names, counts and source disputes, and a few jump subject with no bridge.
