## Chapter 22: War and the Military

- [Card] "Far more people have died in" : The one-line spine is 43 words with a colon and three ideas, and its first clause (more people than battle deaths) is hard to parse on a first read, so the sickness point and the point about soldiers killing defenseless people arrive welded to a puzzle : Drop the first clause and say it in two short sentences, one on sickness killing more soldiers than weapons until World War II and one on soldiers killing defenseless people.
- [Card] "This chapter follows the wars fought" : The card and the spine promise wars on this land, but much of the chapter is wars fought elsewhere (Mexico, Cuba, the Philippines, France, the Pacific, Korea, Vietnam, the Middle East), so the promise is wrong for roughly half the chapter and nothing warns the reader when the map changes : Say wars Americans fought, at home and abroad, in the spine and the intro, and add one line at 1898 that the wars now move overseas.
- [Card] "A small photograph on glass, of" : The hook tells the whole Humiston story, reveal included, then the next paragraph moves on with no word on why it opens this chapter. The 1850 to 1900 box retells it beat for beat, with the same count of other unknown graves, about 600 lines later, so a reader meets the same story twice : Cut the card to two sentences that tie the photograph to one of the three things the chapter watches, and let the box carry the full telling.
- [1850 to 1900] "Counting the dead. Union records list" : Fourteen lines after the era's first count (about 620,000, then 750,000, then 650,000 to 850,000) comes a second count with three new totals that add up to about 498,000, and the word incomplete appears three times in four sentences. The pronoun in the first count's comparison with World War II could also mean either figure. The reader cannot tell which number to keep : One counting paragraph that leads with the plain range, then says the record counts are partial, with the word incomplete used once.
- [1750 to 1800] "Enslaved men could not serve in" : Three lines earlier the chapter says about 7,000 of the Continental Army were Black, so this reads as a contradiction and the reader cannot see how Black men served : Say in one sentence how Black men got into the army despite the militia ban, or say the ban covered only the militia.
- [1850 to 1900] "Other counts put the Black dead" : The paragraph before says the fort held 262 Black soldiers, yet the higher counts reach near 300 dead, more than were there, so the reader assumes a mistake : Say who else the higher counts include, or give one count.
- [The 1600s] "The colonists let the survivors go" : The next two sentences say very few survived the Deer Island camp, then that about half of some 900 died, with no word that the two sources disagree, so the reader cannot tell what happened : Say plainly that the counts differ and by how much, or give one and note the other.
- [1850 to 1900] "That same year Lee commanded the" : The nearest year before it is 1866, when Norris gave his account, but the raid was in 1859, the year of the escape, so the reader gets the wrong date. John Brown's raid at Harpers Ferry is never explained anywhere in the chapter : Write the year, 1859, and add a few plain words on what the raid was.
- [The 1500s] "The battle at Mabila began when" : This comes after de Soto's death in 1542 and goes back to 1540 with no bridge, and it never says the army is de Soto's (it is called only the expedition), so the reader cannot place it : Open with the year and de Soto's army, or move it before the account of his death.
- [1700 to 1750] "Soon after the march began, at" : The box never says Eunice Mather Williams is his wife, and his daughter is also named Eunice, so the reader cannot tell who died at the river and who stayed in Canada : Name her as his wife at first mention and give the daughter her age (seven) when she is named.
- [1850 to 1900] "The draft began in New York" : The paragraph jumps from the draft to mobs killing Black New Yorkers, with no sentence on why the draft angered the crowd or why the mob turned on Black people, so the link is missing : Add one or two plain sentences on what set off the riot and why Black New Yorkers were the target.
- [1900 to 1950] "Scientists and engineers of the Manhattan" : Hiroshima and Nagasaki are told without ever saying what an atomic bomb is. The only plain explanation is the line on nuclear weapons in the next era, after the reader has met both cities : Give a one-sentence definition at first use (the era opener) and say how it differs from other bombs.
- [1900 to 1950] "More than a million American soldiers" : The Meuse-Argonne arrives with no word on who the United States was fighting or why it joined, and Germany first appears as the enemy a few paragraphs later. World War II has the same gap, with Germany first named at D-Day : Add two plain sentences at the era's opening naming the sides and what drew the United States in.
- [1850 to 1900] "The Buffalo Soldiers. Members of Congress" : The section on the army in the West has no lead sentence. Four separate stories follow (Buffalo Soldiers, Little Bighorn, Nez Perce, Wounded Knee) with nothing joining them, and the Little Bighorn paragraph has no bold lead and no reason for the fight : Open with two sentences on what these wars were about and give the Custer paragraph a lead and a why.
- [1800 to 1850] "More than 1,500 US soldiers had" : The Seminole paragraph ends on a four-part ratio (four forced out, one killed, three soldiers dead, $32,000) that a reader cannot hold or check against the 1,500 deaths just given : Cut the ratio and keep the total deaths and the $20 million.
- [1900 to 1950] "Isaac Woodard. Sergeant Isaac Woodard, of" : The G.I. Bill loans end on a number, then a bus beating in South Carolina begins with no link. It is not a war event, so the reader cannot see why it is in this chapter : Add one sentence that Black veterans also met violence at home, or say what the two have in common.
- [1950 to 2000] "American scientists and military officers tested" : The hydrogen bomb tests arrive right after Korea with no bridge and no stated tie to the chapter's three questions, and the section ends on a physicist's remark without saying what followed for the people harmed : Add a lead sentence on why the tests belong here and one on the aftermath.
- [2000 to Today] "Abbey Gate. The last American plane" : The paragraph opens with the final plane leaving Kabul, then goes back four days to the bombing. Nothing says why troops were leaving or who ISIS-K is, so a reader dropped in at 2021 cannot follow : Put the events in order and add one bridging sentence on the 2021 withdrawal.
- [1850 to 1900] "Sickness in the camps. Measles spread" : The chapter's main point gets three sentences, filed under the heading about amputations, and gives 79,462 typhoid cases instead of deaths, so a reader hunting for what killed soldiers does not find it : Give it its own heading and tie it to the 224,097 Union deaths from other causes given earlier.
- [The 1600s] "The swamp fort. About 1,000 English" : The English attack a Narragansett winter village with no reason given, though the chapter showed the Narragansett fighting beside the English at Mystic, so the reader loses who is fighting whom : Add one plain sentence on why the English attacked them.
- [1750 to 1800] "General John Burgoyne surrendered his army" : Saratoga and Yorktown, the war's turning points, are names, ranks and numbers with no sentence on what either led to, so they read as a list : Add one clause each on what the surrender meant and cut a rank or two.
- [2000 to Today] "The Wounded Knee medals. Soldiers of" : It sits under the heading about who may serve, along with a Department of War renaming, and neither is about who may serve. It also retells the Wounded Knee numbers from the 1850 to 1900 era in nearly the same words : Give both their own headings and cut the recap to one sentence plus the new facts.
- [Card] "For most of this history, typhoid" : Typhoid, dysentery and drafted appear in the opening paragraph with no plain meaning, and are explained 350 lines later : Gloss each in a few words at first use (a sickness from dirty water, a gut infection, ordered into the army by law).
- [Card] "Read The Mexican War, 1846 to" : The named section is about 520 words, under three minutes, and it covers sickness and punishment but not who was made to fight, one of the card's three questions : Name a section (or a section plus its box) that touches all three, or say which question the five minutes answers.
- [Closing] "The fighters changed from farmers called" : The closer gives the militia's service as a matter of weeks, which the chapter never says, and its own examples run far longer (a year in the Revolution, seven years in the Seminole war) : Drop the time span or match what the chapter shows.

Flow: uneven. The stories, headings and bold leads carry a reader well, but stacked figures with two or three source names in a paragraph, and a few missing bridges and definitions, slow the long middle and late eras.

## Chapter 23: America and the World

- [The 1500s] "Fort Caroline, the French fort, stood" : This section retells Chapter 22's Matanzas story in full, in the very next chapter, with the same killings, the same sixteen spared and Menéndez's own words. It also gives 132 men and says some accounts have everyone killed, where Chapter 22 says 132 people and about 50 women and children spared, so the two read as conflicting : Keep the king's contract and who was not asked, cut the killings to two sentences that point back, and make the counts match.
- [1900 to 1950] "American soldiers in the Philippines tortured" : The era retells Chapter 22's Igbaras, Samar, Glenn, Waller and Smith story with the same orders, the same 400 to 500 houses and the same eleven guides, and the water cure is now explained three times across the two chapters : Keep what is new (the Senate hearing, Roosevelt's letter, the Insular tie), cut the repeated details, and explain the water cure once with a pointer.
- [1850 to 1900] "Much of the civilian toll came" : Says cholera alone killed over 200,000, more than the State Department's whole civilian figure given just above, and this chapter lists estimates up to a million, while Chapter 22 gives 200,000 as the top figure three times, including its Not known line : Give one range in both chapters and say plainly that the higher counts are disputed.
- [1700 to 1750] "Almost 3,000 New England troops under" : Louisbourg is retold from Chapter 22 (the same 561 burials, the same swap for Madras) with a different sailing date (April 4 here, March 24 there) and different troop numbers, which reads as an error : Reconcile the date and numbers and trim the retelling.
- [1950 to 2000] "Castle Bravo. The largest test was" : Retells Chapter 22's Castle Bravo (Rongelap fallout like snow, 23 fishermen, 57 thyroid cancers) and disagrees with it: nearly three times what the designers expected there, about two and a half times here : Pick one figure, shorten the retelling and point back.
- [2000 to Today] "Strikes at sea. American forces began" : The boat strikes (down to the two Trinidad men and the lawsuit), the Maduro raid, the Iran counts, the Costs of War totals and the Lancet survey repeat Chapter 22's last era almost word for word. The chapters also differ on the date of the Iraq invasion (March 19 there, March 20 here) and on whether Maduro was charged before or after the raid : Tell each once, point from the other chapter, and make the dates and order agree.
- [1750 to 1800] "Adams bought a new coat to" : The hook promises a coat story, but the box never mentions a coat. It gives the date of the meeting and a letter : Tell the coat story or change the hook to something the box delivers.
- [Closing] "Its leaders took land across the" : The question asks how a weak country came to rule islands and overthrow governments. The chapter shows weakness into the 1820s and rule by 1898 but never says what changed, and the answer lists what was taken, not how : Add the missing middle in an era opener (what made the country strong enough) and make the answer say how.
- [Card] "Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1887 to" : The named section is about 290 words, under two minutes, and ends at the overthrow, so it never reaches the 1897 petition and the 1898 resolution that the card opens with : Point to the Hawaii run through the petition section, or bring the payoff into the pick.
- [Closing] "Only the people of the Northern" : The first answer drops the era's limit to today's five territories, so a reader who remembers Hawaii's 1959 vote for statehood sees a conflict : Add today's territories to the sentence, or mention the Hawaii and Alaska votes.
- [Before 1500] "Polynesian voyagers were the first people" : Three date ranges in three sentences (1000 to 1200, then 300 to 750, then a later range called narrower, 1200 to 1290), and the last cannot narrow the first, so the reader cannot tell what scientists now believe : Lead with the current best range, then say older estimates were earlier and some newer ones later.
- [1750 to 1800] "Members of the Confederation Congress, meeting" : The 1783 treaty runs six dates across two paragraphs, and the Franklin box and the Morocco treaty paragraph show the same date runs, with the point (the treaty took effect in 1784) never stated. The line about French diplomats signing their own first version is unclear : Say signed 1783, in force 1784, and cut the middle dates.
- [1750 to 1800] "Accounts of the cost differ too." : Four dollar figures from two sources, none given as the answer, so the reader leaves not knowing what the ransom cost : Lead with about a million dollars in all and drop the rest.
- [1800 to 1850] "Instead, American officials agreed to take" : The sentence says the United States took on a $5 million bill for damage that American citizens did when they rebelled, and the reader cannot tell who was owed the money or why the United States paid : Say in plain words whose claims the United States took on and against whom.
- [1800 to 1850] "When senators voted to approve the" : Articles IX, X and XI, the Protocol of Querétaro and the 1851 land law arrive as a run of legal details, and the reader never learns what the cut paragraphs of Article IX said : Tell each loss in one plain sentence (what was promised, what the Senate cut, what happened to the land) and drop article numbers where possible.
- [1800 to 1850] "Texas. Members of the House and" : A joint resolution is used with no meaning here and explained about 120 lines later for Hawaii, and Texas arrives with no background : Explain the term at Texas, say Texas had left Mexico, and note that Hawaii was taken the same way.
- [1900 to 1950] "American officials backed Panama's break from" : The heading says a Frenchman signed for Panama, but the section never says how that happened or why it matters, so the reader misses the point about who was in the room : Add one plain sentence on how a Frenchman came to sign and who was left out.
- [1900 to 1950] "Hawaii and the Virgin Islands. Hawaii's" : Under the heading on who became a citizen and when, Hawaii gets a government but no word on whether Hawaiians became citizens, though every other place gets a date : Add one sentence on Hawaii's citizenship and its date.
- [1900 to 1950] "Some women were told, falsely, that" : The word falsely seems to cover the claim that employers would not hire unsterilized women, yet the next sentence says employers did favor sterilized women, so the paragraph contradicts itself : Attach falsely to the claim about undoing the operation only and state the hiring fact once.
- [1950 to 2000] "The Cold War was a long" : It defines the war as a rivalry without saying over what, never explains the Soviet Union, and repeats two of the era opener's sentences nearly word for word six lines earlier : Add one plain sentence on what the two sides disagreed about and who the Soviet Union was, and cut the repeat.
- [1750 to 1800] "He later gave his own reason" : The reason sentence carries three ideas (Spain's new peace, a British consul, a pass), and the reader cannot tell what the consul did or why it mattered. The beating is also told twice with different details : Split into two sentences, say what the consul told the Algerians, and describe the beating once.
- [1850 to 1900] "Liliʻuokalani yielded under protest on" : The box repeats, twelve lines after the section, that she yielded under protest and that Cleveland could not restore her, and the overthrow is summarized in nearly the same words several more times in the chapter : Cut the box's first paragraph to a link and keep the 1895 trial.
- [1950 to 2000] "The capital, the canal and an" : Three unrelated items sit under one heading, and the paragraphs on Washington, D.C. have no link to the territories or the wider world, so the reader cannot see why they are here : Add one sentence tying D.C. to people who cannot vote for president, or move it to the territories section.
- [1900 to 1950] "Albizu Campos could be drafted but" : The hook says he could be drafted and could not vote for president, but the box mentions neither a draft nor a vote : Tell both in the body or change the hook.
- [1950 to 2000] "CIA Director Allen Dulles oversaw the" : The sentence uses CIA, then spells it out, and reads as if the agency itself was being planned : Introduce the CIA first, then say Dulles oversaw the plan and Roosevelt ran it in Iran.

Flow: good. Short sections open on their point, the thread of who was in the room holds across eras, and most terms are defined where used, but the date-heavy treaty passages in 1750 to 1800 and the stories retold from Chapter 22 slow it down.
