# Hard Subjects Policy

**Binding on every research and writing agent. Read this alongside `control/AGENT-BRIEF.md`.**
Records Jon's rulings of 2026-09-06. Full context: `control/DECISIONS.md`, Part 1.
This file is prose, not a grid file — it carries no `hb-` markers and the grid validator does not run on it.

---

## 1. The reader premise — everything below follows from this

The reader of this book does not know this history. They are 8 to 15 years old, and for most of
what is in these chapters this will be the first account they ever read. They cannot see an
omission. They cannot reconstruct what a milder word replaced. They have nothing to check the
sentence against.

So softening does not protect that reader. Whatever the book says happened is what happened, as
far as they will ever know. A softened sentence does not leave a gap that gets filled in later —
**it silently becomes their understanding of the event.** The choice is never between a hard
version and a gentle version. It is between an accurate account and a false one. Softening works
only for a reader who already knows the facts and can restore what was left out. This book has no
such reader.

That is why `AGENT-BRIEF.md` §6 — nothing improper gets filtered out — is a rule about accuracy,
not about toughness.

## 2. The seven forbidden moves

Each one produces a passage in which no single word is a lie and the whole is false. All seven are
forbidden. Each **after** is real text from this book at the file and line named; each **before** is a constructed contrast, not something the book says.

**1 · Euphemism** — a mild word standing in for the act.
- Before: "Students who broke the rules were disciplined."
- After: "the switch cut from a tree and the hickory stick kept in the corner; the ruler across the knuckles" (`outlines/education.md:112`).

**2 · Minimization** — a quantity written smaller or vaguer than the record supports.
- Before: "Some children died at the schools."
- After: "at least 973 children died at them; at least 74 burial sites beside them — and the report itself says the count is low" (`outlines/native-nations.md:183`).

**3 · Downplaying** — the fact is present but framed as incidental, typical, or the person's own doing.
- Before: "Like many veterans, Ira Hayes had trouble adjusting, and he died young at 32."
- After: "paraded, toasted, and left to struggle, he died of exposure after a night of drinking" (`outlines/native-nations.md:238`).

**4 · Semantic abstraction** — a category noun standing in for the acts it covers.
- Before: "The Klan engaged in racial violence across the South."
- After: "whipping, arson, and murder campaigns; the Colfax Massacre (April 13, 1873) killed between 62 and 153 Black men, most after surrender" (`outlines/slavery-freedom.md:262`).

**5 · Sanitization** — the body removed from an account of bodily harm.
- Before: "The women who painted the dials became ill, and some died."
- After: "The radium settled in their bones like calcium; jaws crumbled ('radium jaw'), and women in their twenties began dying" (`outlines/elements.md:140`).

**6 · Gist extraction** — the point kept, the specifics that make it checkable dropped. MECHANICAL.
- Before: "Allotment drastically reduced the land Native nations held."
- After: "from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934 — about 90 million acres, two-thirds of what was left, gone" (`outlines/native-nations.md:180`).

**7 · Lossy summarization** — a passage compressed until the named agent, the date, or the mechanism falls out. MECHANICAL.
- Before: "The government made Native children attend boarding schools."
- After: "an 1891 law let the government compel attendance; an 1893 law let it withhold rations, clothing, and annuity payments from parents who refused; some agents sent reservation police for the children" (`outlines/native-nations.md:183`).

**About the two marked MECHANICAL:** nobody decides to do 6 or 7. They enter on their own when an
agent is compressing under context pressure — long chapter, thinning budget, a passage that has to
fit a smaller cell. They feel like good editing while you do them. They are the most likely way
this policy gets broken, and the only defence is to check your own draft against the source for
them specifically. See §7.

## 3. The standard: `outlines/native-nations.md:183` is the FLOOR

Jon's ruling on DECISION 1: that passage is the book's standard, **not its upper limit.** No
chapter may go softer than it. Read the whole line before writing hard material of your own.

It quotes Pratt's stated purpose in his own recorded words, gives the two laws that compelled
attendance, then states what was done to the children: "punishments documented by the later federal
investigation include beatings, solitary confinement, withheld food, mouths washed with soap."
Then the 2024 federal count — 417 schools, at least 973 children dead, at least 74 burial sites,
with the report's own statement that the count is low. Then it closes on what the children did:
kept their languages in secret, and made the friendships that seeded the next century's organizing.

**The pattern to copy, in order:**

1. **Named source** — whose investigation, whose count, whose recorded words.
2. **Documented specifics, including bodily harm where a source states it.** Not the category, the acts.
3. **Numbers carrying their own uncertainty** — "at least", "between 62 and 153", "the range is disputed; state it". Never resolve a source dispute silently (`AGENT-BRIEF.md` §3).
4. **No adjectives telling the reader how to feel.** The facts carry it.
5. **Close on what people did, not on the wound — where the record shows one** — survival, refusal, organizing, the court case, the language kept. That is the last thing the reader is left holding, and it is also true.

**Where the record shows no such close, do not supply one.** An invented act of
survival, resistance or repair is still an invented fact, and ruling C outranks this
pattern. Some events end in nothing but the harm; end there and let it stand.

A chapter that tells Native children's deaths at this depth while flinching from lynching or
Tuskegee is not being kind. It is being inconsistent about whose suffering gets stated plainly.

## 3b. WHY we refuse to soften — and the clinical-word rule (Jon, 2026-09-07)

The reason is not neutrality. Jon's words:

> The whole point of reading negative parts of our history is to understand terrible acts
> and become emotional about it so that we remember it and do our best to not let these
> things happen again. If we sanitize the writing, then we will not understand exactly
> what happened, and we won't be as emotional about it, which makes it harder to remember.

So the target is not a reader who has been *informed* that something bad happened. It is a
reader who **understands what was done and is moved by it**, because that is what makes it
stick and what makes them refuse it later. A sentence that leaves the reader unmoved by an
atrocity has failed, however accurate its dates.

This does not license adjectives, staged scenes, or dwelling — §5 still binds. The feeling
comes from the facts being stated plainly and completely, not from the writer performing.

### The clinical-word rule

**A clinical, legal, or institutional word that stands in for something done to a human body
must be defined in plain language at first use, and the method must be stated.**

Words like *sterilization*, *lobotomy*, *flogging*, *branding*, *forced feeding*, *shackling*
are containers. A child does not know what is inside them, and a reader who does not know
what was done cannot understand or remember it. That is sanitization by vocabulary — forbidden
move 1 wearing a lab coat.

For each such word, give three things:
1. **What it is, in plain words** — what was actually done.
2. **What it did to the person** — the effect on their body or life.
3. **By what method** — if a drug, what it did biologically; if an instrument or an
   operation, a short factual description of what was cut, removed, or destroyed.

**Worked example — sterilization**, which must be handled this way everywhere it appears in
this book: say that it means the person can no longer become pregnant, permanently, and say
by what method it was done. If the record shows the tubes carrying eggs to the womb were cut
or tied, say so. If the womb itself was removed, say so.

**Honest and non-sexual, and not lurid.** Describe the body as a doctor's report would, in
words a child can follow. Do not eroticize, do not linger, do not use euphemism, and do not
reach for horror — one plain sentence of what was done is stronger than a paragraph of
adjectives, and it is the only version the reader will trust.

**Where the record does not say which method was used, say that it does not say.** Never
guess a procedure. An unknown method stated as unknown is honest; an invented one is the
worst failure in this project.

## 4. The naming rule — never invent a name

- **Never invent a name, a person, or a composite.** No representative child, no typical worker, no figure assembled out of several real ones. Use what the record gives.
- **A named account → name the person and say plainly what happened to them.**
- **Documented but unnamed → tell it unnamed.** "This happened to children at these schools" is honest. An invented name is not. An honest unnamed account beats an invented name every time.
- **Unnamed material lives in `hb-zoom` prose.** `hb-story` blocks are for named, real, documented people only.
- Living people: only from already-published, on-the-record accounts they chose to make public. Never approach anyone. Never use a private story.

## 5. What the style guide already forbids — in both directions

`control/writing-style-guide.md` binds outlines as well as prose (`AGENT-BRIEF.md` §9). It rules
out softening **and** it rules out luridness. Being plain is not the same as being graphic, and the
ban on softening is not licence for adjectives, staged scenes, or dwelling.

**Against softening:** §1.5 bans hedged predicates ("may have contributed"). §2.1 requires claims
that can be checked, so "many Americans" fails wherever a number exists. §1.9 requires a reference
point for every comparative. §3.6 requires you to say so when the evidence is mixed, instead of
smoothing it.

**Against dramatizing:** §1.7 bans evaluative adjectives — describe what happened, do not tell the
reader how to feel about it. §1.8 bans promotional language ("remarkable", "horrifying"). §1.10
bans staged scenes and "imagine yourself" openings. §1.3 bans the proverb-shaped moral. §1.2 bans
balanced-clause decoration, including decoration built out of a death.

**The hinge is §2.2:** choose the word that matches the event, not the word that sounds more
dramatic or more restrained than the evidence supports. Both errors are the same error.

**One trap.** §3.3 (information density) says a sentence that can be removed without loss should be
removed. That licenses cutting *sentences that add nothing*. It never licenses cutting a fact
because the fact was hard to fit. Cutting facts under §3.3 is move 6 or 7 in a respectable coat.

State it once, at full accuracy, and move on. Do not repeat the harm for effect and do not linger.

## 6b. Two checks that caught real defects in the finished prose

- **Personification (style guide §1.1) is the rule most often broken while FIXING something
  else.** A verifier flagged four agentless passives at the boarding-school arrival ("the
  child's hair was cut"). The repair replaced them with *"The school cut the child's hair"* —
  which is still §1.1: an institution acting. The correct version names the people: *"Staff
  cut the child's hair."* Laws, acts, treaties, schools, agencies and nations do not do
  things. People in them do. The same pass fixed *"The treaty recognized"* and *"The Act set"*.
- **A law cannot kill anyone.** An era summary read "a law written in Washington was used to
  take that money and to kill more than sixty of the people who owned it." That both
  personified the law and hid the killers. It now names the 1921 guardianship law, the white
  guardians appointed by local courts, and the fact that guardians and husbands stole.

## 7. Self-check — run this on your own draft before you report

1. Did I write "was treated harshly", "conditions were difficult", "was mistreated", or "suffered" anywhere a source states what was actually done? Replace the phrase with the act.
2. Did I drop a number because it was hard to state precisely? Put it back with its uncertainty attached — "at least", "between X and Y", "the count is low", "the range is disputed".
3. Did I compress a passage and lose the named agent, the date, or the mechanism? Compare against the research bank line by line. This is the failure that arrives without a decision being made.
4. Is every person in my draft a real, documented individual from the record — no composite, no representative figure, no invented name? Is every unnamed account in `hb-zoom` prose rather than in an `hb-story` block?
5. Did I add anything the source does not contain — an evaluative adjective, a staged moment, a sentence telling the reader how to feel, a second pass over the same wound? (§1.7, §1.8, §1.10)
6. **Did a law, act, treaty, school, agency, company or nation DO something in my draft?**
   They cannot. Style guide §1.1. Name the people who acted. This is the rule most often
   broken while repairing a passive — check every fix you made, not just the first draft.
7. **Did I use a clinical or institutional word for something done to a body** —
   sterilization, lobotomy, flogging, branding, restraint — **without saying in plain words
   what it was, what it did to the person, and by what method?** See §3b. If the record does
   not give the method, say that it does not.
8. **The reader test.** Would someone who knows nothing else about this event come away knowing what happened, and does the passage end on what people did rather than on the wound? If they would come away with a milder event than the record describes, the draft fails, however accurate each single word is.
