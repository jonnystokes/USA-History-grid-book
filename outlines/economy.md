## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The people of this land made their livings by farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering, and they traded what they made and grew across long distances. There was no money here. Goods moved through barter and gift exchange between people and between nations, and some farming towns grew large enough to support full-time craftworkers and traders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia and the trade routes" -->
- Cahokia, near modern St. Louis (peak about 1050–1150), was the largest city north of Mexico. Corn surpluses fed workers who did not farm — toolmakers, builders, traders (Illinois State Museum; Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site).
- Cahokia controlled the making and movement of hoe blades of Mill Creek chert, quarried in southern Illinois and found more than 400 miles away, from Louisiana to Minnesota (Mill Creek chert studies; Illinois State Museum).
- Sea shells from the Gulf coast, copper from Lake Superior, and obsidian in the West moved along rivers and trails as goods. Wampum as record and exchange is `money`'s subject.
- No named individual is documented from this angle; the spans carry the era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="economy" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
European crowns sent expeditions to this coast looking for gold and silver, and in the land that became the United States they found none. The trades that lasted were plainer ones: fish dried on the northern coasts, and furs bought from Native trappers. There was no colonial economy yet, because there were almost no colonists.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold hunts that failed, trades that lasted" -->
- Spanish silver came from mines in Mexico and South America — not from this land. The expeditions that searched here (Coronado, de Soto — `exploration`'s stories) went home without treasure.
- July 1534, Chaleur Bay, north of this land on the Atlantic coast: Mi'kmaq traders held beaver furs up on sticks to Jacques Cartier's crew and traded them for knives and iron goods — the first recorded fur trading between Europeans and Native people on the coast. The fur trade reached the future United States in the next century (Canadian Museum of History).
- European fishing fleets worked the cod waters off the northeast every season; fish, not gold, was the first steady European business in these waters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonies each built an economy around what the place could sell: tobacco in the Chesapeake, furs along the northern rivers, fish, timber, and ships in New England. The rules were written in London. Under the idea called mercantilism, colonies existed to supply raw goods to the home country and to buy its finished goods back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco saves Virginia" -->
- Virginia found no gold and nearly starved. Its cash crop turned out to be a plant: exports of tobacco grew from about 2,300 pounds in 1615–16 to about 20,000 pounds by 1617, about 1.5 million pounds by 1630, and about 22 million pounds a year by 1700 (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Tobacco also served as money in the Chesapeake — `money` tells that side. Shared with: `money` (commodity money) · `food-farming` (the crop in the fields) · `slavery-freedom` (who was forced to grow it as the century went on).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-rolfe-economy" name="John Rolfe" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Rolfe
> **Who:** the Jamestown settler whose tobacco experiments gave Virginia its cash crop. · **When and where:** Jamestown, Virginia, 1612–1614.
By July 1612 Rolfe was growing tobacco from imported West Indies seed, milder than the local plant, and he worked out how to cure the leaf for shipping. His first shipments reached London in 1614. Within a generation tobacco paid for the colony (Encyclopedia Virginia; Historic Jamestowne). His marriage to Pocahontas is told in `native-nations`; no documentary film about him exists.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-rolfe-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Furs, and rules from London" -->
- One Dutch ship, the *Arms of Amsterdam*, carried 7,246 beaver skins to Amsterdam in a single 1626 cargo — recorded in the same letter that reports the purchase of Manhattan. Beaver felt hats in Europe drove the demand (New York State Library, Schaghen letter). The trading companies themselves are `big-business`'s story.
- Navigation Acts: from 1651, colonial trade had to travel in English or colonial ships; from 1660, "enumerated" goods — tobacco, sugar, cotton, indigo — could be shipped only to England or its colonies (Britannica).
- New England ships ran Atlantic circuits: goods and rum to West Africa, enslaved people across the ocean, sugar and molasses back north. The buying and selling of human beings was part of how these colonies made money — said plainly. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (lead — the Middle Passage and the system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Farming was most colonists' living, but the colonies also built ships, traded across the Atlantic, and sold tobacco, rice, indigo, wheat, and fish. Economic historians Peter Lindert and Jeffrey Williamson calculated in 2016 that free colonists had higher average incomes than the people of England by 1774. Much of that wealth, especially in the South, was produced by enslaved people who were paid nothing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the colonies sold" -->
- Tobacco from the Chesapeake; rice and indigo from the South Carolina Lowcountry, grown and processed by enslaved workers; wheat and flour from the middle colonies; fish, rum, and ships from New England (research bank; colonial-economy scholarship).
- Shipbuilding: colonial timber made ships cheaper than English-built ones, and by the Revolution about one-third of the British merchant fleet was American-built (Encyclopedia.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="London's rules tighten" -->
- Molasses Act, 1733: a sixpence-a-gallon duty on molasses from non-British islands, widely dodged by smuggling (Encyclopedia.com).
- Iron Act, 1750: colonial raw iron could enter Britain duty-free, but building new colonial mills to finish iron into goods was banned. Colonists built them anyway (Britannica).
- The point of the rules, stated simply: Britain wanted the colonies' raw materials and the colonies' shopping, and wanted the profitable finishing work kept at home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney
> **Who:** the sixteen-year-old who ran three plantations and built South Carolina's second cash crop. · **When and where:** near Charleston, South Carolina, 1739–1744.
Left in charge of her father's three plantations at 16, she spent five years testing indigo, the plant behind blue dye. The 1744 crop produced 17 pounds of good dye, and she shared seed with neighboring planters. Exports went from about 5,000 pounds in 1745–46 to about 130,000 pounds by 1748, and indigo became second only to rice in the colony's trade. The dye was made by enslaved workers, and skilled dye-makers brought from the Caribbean carried much of the knowledge — the profit went to planter families like hers (South Carolina Encyclopedia; Britannica).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Independence was won on borrowed money. The paper dollars Congress printed to pay its soldiers lost almost all their value, and the new government began life owing about 79 million dollars. Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury, built the plan that paid the debts and established the country's credit.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The war's bill" -->
- Congress could not tax, so it printed Continental dollars from 1775. By the end of 1777 it took about 4 paper dollars to equal 1 silver dollar; by 1781, about 100 — "not worth a Continental" (Harvard American Currency collection; NY Fed history). The currency itself is `money`'s lead; the debt is ours.
- Hamilton's count in January 1790: about 54 million dollars of national debt, plus about 25 million owed by the states (First Report on Public Credit).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The whiskey tax" -->
- 1791: a federal excise tax on distilled spirits — 7 cents a gallon — to help carry the war debt. Frontier farmers moved their corn to market as whiskey, and the tax fell hard on them.
- 1794: western Pennsylvania farmers attacked tax collectors; Washington called up about 13,000 militia and marched west; the rebellion dissolved without a battle (Mount Vernon; TTB). Shared with: `drugs-alcohol` (whiskey as drink and money, and the rebellion's people) · `government-politics` (federal power proven).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton
> **Who:** the first Secretary of the Treasury, who made the new country's debts into the foundation of its credit. · **When and where:** New York and Philadelphia, 1789–1795.
> **Movie:** Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)
His plan, laid out in the First Report on Public Credit (January 1790): pay the war debts in full, and have the federal government take over the states' debts too, so every creditor would trust the new government. The assumption plan was losing in Congress until a June 1790 dinner with Jefferson and Madison produced the trade — the debt plan passed, and the permanent capital went south to the Potomac. His December 1791 Report on Manufactures argued the country should grow its own industry with tariffs and subsidies; Congress never voted on it, but the argument ran through the next two centuries (National Archives; Gilder Lehrman). His arrival as an immigrant is `immigration`'s story; the Mint and the national bank are `money`'s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1800 most American families grew and made most of what they used. By 1850 far more of them raised crops and made goods to sell, and bought what they needed with the money — historians call the change the market revolution. Canals and steamboats cut the cost of moving goods to a tenth of what wagons charged, cotton grown by enslaved people became the country's largest export, and a panic in 1837 was followed by six years of closed banks, failed businesses, and lost work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From household to market" -->
- After the Erie Canal opened in 1825, freight from Buffalo to New York City fell from about 100 dollars a ton by road to about 10 dollars by canal, and the trip dropped from about twenty days toward ten (EBSCO; Britannica). The canal itself is `transportation`'s; the price collapse is what changed farming.
- The first big factories: New England textile mills hired farm daughters for cash wages — money entering households that had rarely handled it. The mills' working day and the mill girls' strikes are `work-workers`' (their Harriet Hanson Robinson story).
- California gold (found January 1848): about 80,000 people went in 1849 and about 300,000 by 1855. Most miners found little; the merchants who sold them food, tools, and clothes made the steadier money. Shared with: `migration` (lead — the crowds) · `elements` (the metal).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="King Cotton" -->
- Cotton was 51.6 percent of the value of all U.S. exports in 1840, and stayed above half nearly every year until the Civil War. Between 1820 and 1860 about 80 percent of the world's cotton came from the United States (OpenStax U.S. History).
- The crop was planted, picked, and processed by enslaved people. The country's biggest business ran on forced, unpaid labor — said plainly, in the same sentence. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (lead — the system and the people) · `food-farming` (cotton crowding out food crops).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837" -->
- A panic, explained simply: too many people trying to get their money out, or sell what they own, at the same time — and finding that nobody is buying.
- Years of borrowed money had gone into western land and cotton. When cotton prices fell and British lenders pulled back, New York banks stopped paying out gold and silver on May 10, 1837 (the bank side is `money`'s). Of about 850 banks in the country, roughly 4 in 10 closed entirely or partly failed, and hard times lasted until about 1843 (Lumen/US history surveys).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-hone-economy" name="Philip Hone" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Hone
> **Who:** a New York merchant whose diary recorded the Panic of 1837 day by day. · **When and where:** New York City; diary kept 1828–1851.
A carpenter's son who got rich in the auction business and served one term as mayor (1826–27), Hone wrote in his diary nearly every day for 23 years. In the spring of 1837 he recorded the city's business stopping around him: "No goods are selling, no business stirring, no boxes encumber the sidewalks of Pearl Street." His own fortune shrank in the crisis years. The diary, published after his death, is one of the fullest firsthand records of the era's money life (The Diary of Philip Hone, 1889 edition).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-hone-economy" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900 the United States became an industrial country. Railroad track grew from about 9,000 miles to about 193,000. By 1890 American mills made more steel than Britain's, and by 1900 more than Britain's and Germany's combined. The same half century brought panics in 1873 and 1893 that each turned into years of failed businesses and men out of work, and it made the largest private fortunes the country had seen. Mark Twain named the era in an 1873 novel: the Gilded Age — a thin layer of gold over the problems underneath.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the takeoff was made of" -->
- Steel rails fell from about 140 dollars a ton in 1872 to about 20 dollars by 1900 (steel-industry histories); cheap steel built the railroads, and the railroads carried everything else.
- The transcontinental railroad (completed 1869) cut a coast-to-coast trip from months to about a week and tied western farms, mines, and ranches into national markets. Shared with: `transportation` (lead — the line) · `big-business` (the land grants) · `migration`, `immigration`, `native-nations`, `work-workers` (their angles).
- Cattle: Texas longhorns worth about 5 dollars a head at home brought up to about 40 dollars at the Kansas railheads; over 5 million head walked north 1866–1884 (Texas State Historical Association). The Homestead Act (1862) turned about 270 million acres — near a tenth of the country — into private farms (NPS). Copper from Michigan's Keweenaw, silver from the Comstock Lode (1859), and aluminum falling from about 12 dollars a pound to under 1 dollar by the mid-1890s each built new industries (NPS Keweenaw; ACS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The panics" -->
- 1857: the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company failed on August 24 after its New York manager's embezzlement, and the telegraph spread the bank runs in days (Ohio Memory).
- 1873: the investment bank Jay Cooke and Company failed on September 18, loaded with railroad bonds nobody would buy. The New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days — its first closure ever — about 18,000 businesses failed, and the downturn ran 65 months, the longest on record until the 1930s (PBS American Experience; Federal Reserve History; NBER dating). Shared with: `money` (the bank runs).
- 1893: about 500 banks and 15,000 businesses failed. Nobody counted the unemployed then; later estimates for the worst of it run from about 17 to 25 percent — state the range (Encyclopedia.com).
- 1894: Jacob Coxey led unemployed men from Massillon, Ohio to Washington — about 100 leaving on March 25, about 500 arriving May 1 — to ask for a 500-million-dollar public-works jobs program. Coxey was arrested for walking on the Capitol grass and jailed 20 days. It was the first sizable protest march on Washington, and the jobs program he asked for did not exist until the 1930s (Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farm anger" -->
- Farmers paid whatever the one railroad in town charged to move their crop. The Grange, founded 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley, grew to about 800,000 members by the mid-1870s and won state laws capping railroad and grain-elevator rates; the Supreme Court upheld them in Munn v. Illinois (1877) (Britannica). Shared with: `food-farming` (farm life) · `big-business` (regulation of companies) · `money` (the gold-versus-silver fight, their lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-carnegie-economy" name="Andrew Carnegie" movie="The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Carnegie
> **Who:** the immigrant bobbin boy who made steel cheap, became one of the richest men alive, and gave most of it away. · **When and where:** Scotland and Pittsburgh; working from 1848, steel from the 1870s, sold out 1901.
> **Movie:** The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)
His family left Scotland broke in 1848; at about 13 he went to work in a Pittsburgh-area cotton mill changing bobbins for 1.20 dollars a week. Telegraph messenger, then railroad manager, then steel: his mills drove the price of steel rails from about 140 dollars a ton down toward 20. In 1901 he sold Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan's group for 480 million dollars and spent the rest of his life giving the money away — more than 350 million dollars, including about 2,500 public libraries (Carnegie Corporation; Britannica). The shadow is part of the story: in 1892 his company broke the steelworkers' union at Homestead, and ten men died in the battle there. The strike is told in `work-workers`; the company's side of that fight in `big-business`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-economy" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-economy" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Rockefellers (American Experience, 2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller
> **Who:** the bookkeeper who organized oil refining into a national business and became the richest man in the country. · **When and where:** Cleveland and New York; Standard Oil founded 1870.
> **Movie:** The Rockefellers (American Experience, 2000) — about the family across four generations, not him alone.
Standard Oil controlled about 90 percent of American oil refining by 1880. The scale had a price the country argued about for forty years — and an effect every family saw at home: kerosene, the lamp fuel of ordinary houses, fell from about 26 cents a gallon in 1870 to about 7 cents by 1890 (industry price series; FEE). He later gave large sums to universities and medical research. How he built the monopoly, the reporter who exposed it, and the 1911 breakup are `big-business`'s story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-economy" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Assembly lines cut the price of cars and much else, and in the 1920s ordinary families bought automobiles and radios, many on installment payments. In October 1929 stock prices collapsed. By 1933 about one worker in four had no job — the Great Depression, the hardest times in the country's history. The federal government answered by hiring millions of people directly, and production for the Second World War ended the mass unemployment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-economy" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford
> **Who:** the carmaker whose high wages and falling prices turned workers into customers. · **When and where:** Highland Park, Michigan; the five-dollar day announced January 5, 1914.
> **Movie:** Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013)
In 1913 the new assembly line made Ford's factory jobs so relentless that workers quit at a rate of 370 percent a year. On January 5, 1914 Ford announced five dollars for an eight-hour day — about double the going pay. The conditions attached are part of the story: the full rate was "profit-sharing," paid only after six months, and only to workers whose homes passed inspection by Ford's Sociological Department — agents checked for drinking, savings habits, and how families lived. The economics worked: turnover fell, and the Model T's price kept falling — 825 dollars at its 1908 launch, 260 dollars by 1925 — until Ford workers could buy Fords (The Henry Ford; EBSCO). Told honestly, the man had ugly chapters: his newspaper spread antisemitic articles through the 1920s, and his company fought unions with violence into the 1930s — the union fight is `work-workers`'; the assembly line as invention is `technology`'s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The crash and the Depression" -->
- The Dow Jones stock average rose from 63 (August 1921) to 381 (September 3, 1929), then fell to 41 by July 1932 — down 89 percent (Federal Reserve History).
- A depression, explained simply: a downturn so deep and long that closed businesses close other businesses, because nobody has money to buy anything.
- By 1933: about 25 percent unemployment — one worker in four, by the estimates made afterward, since no official monthly count existed yet; national output down about 30 percent; about 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks had failed — the bank runs are `money`'s telling (FDR Library; Britannica). Breadlines in every city; shantytowns called Hoovervilles, named for the president.
- Summer 1932, the Bonus Army: World War I veterans — estimates run from 10,000 to more than 20,000, many with wives and children — camped in Washington asking early payment of a war bonus not due until 1945. On July 28 police shots killed veterans William Hushka and Eric Carlson; President Hoover then ordered the Army to clear the camps, and General MacArthur's troops used tanks and tear gas and burned the shacks. Veterans finally got the bonus in 1936 (Britannica; National Archives). Shared with: `war` (veterans and the country's debt to them).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-roth-economy" name="Benjamin Roth" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Roth
> **Who:** a Youngstown, Ohio lawyer who wrote down what the Depression did to his city, week by week, for more than a decade. · **When and where:** Youngstown, Ohio; diary begun June 1931.
Roth was a working lawyer, not a rich man, when the steel town's banks began to close. He started a diary to make sense of it: which banks froze accounts, what a house or a law client's fee was worth now, who was out of work, what people said and feared. He kept writing into the 1940s. Published in 2009 as The Great Depression: A Diary, it is one of the clearest ordinary-eye-level records of the economy failing and healing (PublicAffairs, eds. Ledbetter and Roth).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-roth-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New Deal changes government's job" -->
- Before 1933 the federal government paid no unemployment insurance and no old-age pensions. After the New Deal it did — the change stated that plainly.
- The CCC (from March 1933) put about 3 million young men to work planting and building at 30 dollars a month, about 25 of it sent home. The WPA (1935–1943) employed about 8.5 million people and built about 650,000 miles of roads, 125,000 public buildings, and 75,000 bridges (Britannica). Shared with: `work-workers` (the jobs as jobs) · `government-politics` (the programs and the fights over them).
- Farm payments began: the AAA (May 1933) paid farmers to grow less — about 10 million acres of cotton plowed under and 6 million pigs killed while people stood in breadlines; the contradiction produced food-relief programs, and paying farmers became permanent policy (Britannica; USDA). Shared with: `food-farming` (the farm and the table, their lead on the families).
- Social Security Act, signed August 14, 1935: old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, funded by payroll taxes (SSA).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frances-perkins-economy" name="Frances Perkins" movie="Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frances Perkins
> **Who:** the Labor Secretary who led the design of Social Security. · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1933–1945; the Committee on Economic Security, 1934–35.
> **Movie:** Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)
The first woman in a U.S. Cabinet, Perkins chaired the Committee on Economic Security that Roosevelt created in June 1934. Its plan — old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, paid for by workers and employers — went to Congress in January 1935 and became the Social Security Act that August (SSA History; FDR Library). What put her on this road — watching the Triangle fire in 1911 — and her factory and wage laws are told in `work-workers`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frances-perkins-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War production ends the Depression" -->
- Unemployment fell from 14.6 percent (1939–40) to 1.2 percent (1944), the lowest ever recorded (EH.net).
- The country built about 300,000 military aircraft between January 1940 and August 1945, and by 1945 the United States made about half of all the manufactured goods in the world (PBS; National WWII Museum). The battles are `war`'s; the factories' workers are `work-workers`'.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For about twenty-five years after the war, pay rose across the whole country: the inflation-adjusted income of the typical family roughly doubled between 1947 and 1973, and the gains reached poor, middle, and rich households alike. Then came a decade in which prices and unemployment rose together, and after 1979 the steel mills and factories of the industrial Midwest began closing for good. By the century's end, four American jobs in five were in services — stores, offices, hospitals, schools — and the economy was in the longest growth stretch yet recorded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boom, and where it came from" -->
- Median family income about doubled in real terms, 1947–1973 (Census data; Brookings). Economists Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo showed the gap between high and low wages narrowed sharply in the 1940s and stayed narrow — they named it the Great Compression (NBER).
- The G.I. Bill: by 1956, 7.8 million of 16 million returning World War II veterans had used its education and training benefits, and the VA backed about 2.4 million home loans by 1952 (VA). Shared with: `education` (the classrooms) · `home-family` (the houses) · `war` (the veterans).
- The Employment Act of 1946 declared maximum employment a federal responsibility and created the Council of Economic Advisers — the ideas of the British economist John Maynard Keynes, that government spending should fight slumps, written into American law in softened form (Federal Reserve History).
- Container shipping (from 1956) cut the cost of loading ocean freight from about 5.86 dollars a ton to about 16 cents, by economist Marc Levinson's calculation — near-free shipping later moved much manufacturing overseas. Shared with: `transportation` (lead — McLean, the ship, the box).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stagflation: the 1970s squeeze" -->
- Until the 1970s most economists taught that prices and joblessness rose at different times, not together. Then both rose at once — the word coined for it was stagflation.
- Consumer prices rose about 11 percent in 1974; unemployment hit 9 percent in May 1975; prices rose more than 13 percent a year in 1979–80; unemployment reached 10.8 percent at the end of 1982, the highest since the Depression (BLS). The 1973 oil embargo that quadrupled oil prices is `energy`'s story; the Federal Reserve's high-interest-rate cure is `money`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Rust Belt" -->
- Manufacturing was about 3 in 10 American jobs in the early 1950s; by 2005 about 1 in 9, and about 1 in 12 by the 2020s (BLS). Factory employment peaked in June 1979 at 19.6 million and fell for forty years.
- Youngstown, Ohio, September 19, 1977 — "Black Monday": Youngstown Sheet and Tube announced the Campbell Works closure, ending 5,000 jobs at once. Within about five years the surrounding Mahoning Valley lost about 50,000 jobs in steel and related work (WKBN; Belt Magazine — journalism, labeled). Shared with: `work-workers` (the workers' daily loss, their target story) · `big-business` (the corporate decisions).
- The 1980s farm crisis: Midwest farmland values fell up to 60 percent, 1981–86; farm debt hit about 215 billion dollars by 1984; about 300,000 farms defaulted and about 300 farm banks failed; Farm Aid's first concert (September 22, 1985) raised over 7 million dollars (MNopedia; Farm Aid). Shared with: `food-farming` (lead — the families and the tables).

### Gerald Dickey
> **Who:** the Youngstown steelworker who proposed that the workers and the town buy their mill. · **When and where:** Youngstown, Ohio, 1977–1979.
Dickey was recording secretary of Steelworkers Local 1462 when Black Monday ended 5,000 jobs. His answer: the workers and the community should buy the Campbell Works and run it themselves. Clergy formed the Ecumenical Coalition of the Mahoning Valley around the plan and commissioned a study that said a modernized, worker-community-owned mill could run. Washington signaled support, then denied the roughly 100 million dollars in federal loan guarantees the plan needed, and the mill never reopened (United Steelworkers history; Ohio History Connection, Gerald Dickey collection; Staughton Lynd, The Fight Against Shutdowns). The campaign failed; the idea of worker ownership it spread did not.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gerald-dickey-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1980s and 1990s, both sides plainly" -->
- Tax cuts: the top income-tax rate fell from 70 percent to 50 percent in 1981, and to 28 percent by the 1986 reform. Inflation fell from 13.5 percent (1980) to the 3-to-4 percent range by the mid-1980s. The national debt roughly tripled during the 1980s. State all three facts; the argument over them continues (EBSCO; Brookings; Treasury data).
- The 1990s: growth ran from March 1991 to March 2001 — 120 months, the longest on record until then (NBER); unemployment touched 3.9 percent in 2000; the federal budget ran surpluses in 1998–2001; technology stocks rose about sixfold from 1995 to March 2000. The bust lands in the next era.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most Americans now work in service jobs, much of what they buy is made overseas, and a growing share of work runs through apps and software. Since 2000 the country has been through a technology-stock bust, a housing crash that became the worst downturn since the 1930s, the longest expansion on record, a two-month collapse when the COVID-19 pandemic arrived, and the fastest price rises in forty years. This section is current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The dot-com bust" -->
- The NASDAQ stock index peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000 and fell about 78 percent by October 2002; the recession that followed (March–November 2001) was short (Britannica; NBER).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008, explained simply" -->
- Home prices rose for years, and lenders wrote mortgages that many borrowers could not repay. Banks bundled those mortgages into investments sold as safe. When home prices fell, the investments failed, and the firms holding them began to fall.
- Lehman Brothers failed September 15, 2008 — the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history, about 619 billion dollars in debts (EBSCO; Britannica). Congress then authorized 700 billion dollars to steady the banks; about 443 billion was actually spent, and most of it was later repaid (Treasury; CBO).
- The Great Recession (December 2007 – June 2009, NBER): about 8.7 million jobs lost; unemployment peaked at 10 percent in October 2009 (BLS). Shared with: `money` (banks, mortgages, the Fed) · `home-family` (the foreclosed households — their lead, about 3.8 million foreclosures 2007–2010) · `big-business` (the bailed-out firms). Topic documentary: Inside Job (2010).

### Ed Neufeldt
> **Who:** the laid-off RV plant worker who introduced the president in the town the recession hit hardest (journalism-sourced). · **When and where:** Elkhart, Indiana, 2008–2009.
Elkhart called itself the RV capital of the world, and when buyers stopped buying, the county's unemployment rate went from about 5 percent to nearly 20 percent in about a year. Neufeldt had built cabinets at Monaco Coach for 32 years when the plant closed in fall 2008. On February 9, 2009, President Obama's first trip as president brought him to Elkhart to argue for the stimulus bill, and Neufeldt introduced him. Reporters followed Neufeldt for years: three part-time jobs, including one at a bakery over an hour away, before factory work returned (ProPublica; NPR; NBC News — journalism, labeled).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ed-neufeldt-economy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The long recovery and the gig shift" -->
- The recovery was slow, then long: June 2009 to February 2020, 128 months — the longest expansion in records going back to 1854 (NBER).
- By 2021, 16 percent of U.S. adults had earned money through an online gig platform (Pew Research). The app job itself — pay set by an algorithm, no boss to face — is `work-workers`' telling; ours is the shape of the whole: services above 8 in 10 jobs, manufacturing about 1 in 12 (BLS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2020, and the prices after" -->
- The pandemic recession lasted two months, February to April 2020 — the shortest on record (NBER). Unemployment hit 14.8 percent in April 2020; output fell in the spring at the fastest rate ever measured and rebounded at the fastest rate that summer (BLS; BEA). Congress passed about 2 trillion dollars in relief in the CARES Act (March 27, 2020).
- Consumer prices rose 9.1 percent in the year to June 2022, the largest rise since November 1981 (BLS). Economists disagree about the causes — pandemic supply-chain failures, federal relief spending, and energy prices after Russia invaded Ukraine are each blamed in different measures; state the disagreement. Price rises eased to near 3 percent by mid-2023 (BLS; recent coverage is journalism, labeled).
- Wealth, stated plainly with numbers: at the end of 2024 the top 10 percent of households held 67 percent of all household wealth; the bottom half of households together held 2.5 percent (Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts).
