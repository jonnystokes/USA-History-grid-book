## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Hundreds of separate nations lived on this land, and each one had a religious life of its own. There was no single Native religion then, and there is none now. People worshipped in a particular place, at a particular time of year, among the people they lived with. Nobody here wrote in an alphabet, so nothing from before 1500 survives in writing. What is known comes from what people built and buried, and from the traditions the nations have kept and still keep.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What is known, and how" -->
Archaeologists dig up buildings, graves, and objects. They can measure which way a doorway faces and where the sun comes up in line with it. That evidence is solid, and it has a hard limit. A wall lined up with the midwinter sunrise proves the alignment and nothing else. It does not tell anyone what the people who built it believed, or what they said while they stood there.

The nations themselves hold oral traditions that reach back past 1500, and each nation decides for itself how much of that is anyone else's business. Written descriptions of Native religion begin after 1500. Europeans wrote them, and many of the writers were missionaries whose work was to end the ceremonies they were describing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Places built to line up with the sky" -->
In Chaco Canyon, in what is now northwestern New Mexico, builders put up Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida, and Peñasco Blanco in the middle and late 800s. They lined many of these great houses up with the sun, the moon, and the compass directions, and they cut roads out from the canyon to more than 150 great houses across the region. Pueblo people today say Chaco was a gathering place where many peoples and clans came together to share ceremonies and knowledge, and that it stands on their clans' sacred migration paths.

At Cahokia, on the Illinois side of the Mississippi across from what is now St Louis, people built about 120 mounds between roughly 800 and 1400. Ten to twenty thousand people lived there at its height. The largest mound, called Monks Mound now, went up in fourteen stages and rises in four terraces to about 100 feet. Beside it stood a ring of upright wooden posts, rebuilt about five times and ranging from about 240 to 480 feet across. The posts marked where the sun came up at midsummer, at midwinter, and at the two equinoxes — the two days a year when daylight and darkness are the same length. The equinox post lines up with the front of Monks Mound.

Between about 1 and 400 CE, people in the Ohio valley built walls of earth in circles, squares, and octagons, some of them large enough to hold a crowd. Mica, obsidian, copper, and shark's teeth turn up inside them, carried from as far as the Rocky Mountains and the Atlantic coast. Archaeologists call these builders the Hopewell culture. That is not the name of any nation. It is the surname of Mordecai C. Hopewell, who owned the Ohio farm where Warren K. Moorehead dug out twenty-eight mounds in the 1890s. The people who built the earthworks belonged to several different nations.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first organized religion Europeans brought to this land arrived with Spanish soldiers, and the priests and the soldiers worked together. Franciscan and Jesuit friars built missions among the Timucua and Guale of Florida and Georgia, and from 1598 among the Pueblo nations of New Mexico. A mission was a church, but it was not only a church. Native people who joined one were baptized, taught Catholic doctrine, counted as subjects of Spain, and required to send men to work for the Spanish. Nearly everything written about any of this was written by the missionaries themselves, so this whole era is known from one side.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The missions in Florida and Georgia" -->
Missionary work in Florida began in 1568. Jesuits went first, converted almost nobody, and gave up on Florida in 1571. The first Franciscans reached St Augustine in 1573. After a fresh group of friars arrived from Spain in 1587 the mission field grew fast. San Pedro de Mocama was the first lasting mission in what is now Georgia, and by the end of 1595 there were missions in another Mocama town and in no fewer than five main Guale towns.

A mission town with a priest living in it was called a *doctrina*, from the Spanish word for teaching. The friar there baptized people, said Mass, and drilled the catechism. Spanish officials used the same towns to get workers. Under a system called the *repartimiento*, a set number of unmarried Native men from mission towns had to leave home each year and work at St Augustine. Teaching doctrine was the mission's main instrument, which made the first European schooling in this land a form of religious instruction with conversion as its purpose (`education` covers it as schooling).

Not every missionary agreed that soldiers belonged in this work. Luis Cáncer de Barbastro, a Dominican who had come from Spain to the Caribbean in 1518, spent his career arguing that Native people should be converted without force, and he got a decree from King Charles V to try it in Florida. He sailed from Havana in early June 1549. On 26 June 1549 he went ashore to greet a group of Tocobaga people near Tampa Bay, and they killed him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Five friars killed in Guale, 1597 — and what is not known" -->
In the autumn of 1597, Guale people killed five Franciscan friars and destroyed their missions. The Spanish abandoned the northern missions until 1604.

Who killed the friars, and why, has never been established. J. Michael Francis and Kathleen Kole translated the earliest surviving Spanish documents and published them in 2011. They wrote that the two questions that have dominated the argument — who killed the five friars and why — are ones "neither of which can be answered with any certainty." Older tellings name a young man from Tolomato called Juanillo and say a friar had rebuked him in public over having more than one wife. This book does not repeat that, because the documents do not carry it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
In early May 1598, Juan de Oñate claimed the province of New Mexico for Spain at the Rio Grande ford by what is now El Paso. Franciscan friars travelled with his column. That autumn he went to every pueblo, had translators demand allegiance to the Spanish government, and gave each pueblo a missionary. That is where the Franciscan mission field in New Mexico starts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Doña María Meléndez
> **Who:** The Timucua chief of Nombre de Dios, the town beside St Augustine, and a Catholic · **When and where:** Late 1500s and early 1600s — Spanish Florida

Nombre de Dios was a Timucua town before any Spaniard preached in it. Doña María Meléndez was its *cacica*, its chief. Timucua towns were led by women as well as men, and the Spanish wrote her title in the feminine form.

Her mother ruled the town before her and was one of the first Timucua people to become Christian. Doña María was a Christian too. She married a Spanish soldier called Clemente de Vernal, and he lived with her and their children at Nombre de Dios rather than in the Spanish town.

Franciscans set up their first mission with a resident priest at Nombre de Dios after 1587. The Florida Museum of Natural History's account of the mission says the friars' success there was owed largely to her.

By 1606 she ruled the Timucua towns along the coast from St Augustine north to about Cumberland Island in what is now Georgia. The National Park Service says that was possibly through Spanish help. The record does not settle it.

Everything known about her was written down by Spaniards. Nothing survives in her own words, and nothing in the record says why she became Christian.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Several colonies in this land were founded as religious projects, and most of them did not extend their own freedom to anybody else. English Puritans crossed the ocean from 1630 onward so they could worship as they believed they should. Once they were running New England, they hanged four Quakers on Boston Common for coming back after being banished. Maryland's assembly passed the first law here protecting freedom of worship, and the same law made denying the Trinity a crime punished by death. Two colonies did the opposite and said so in writing: Roger Williams's Rhode Island and William Penn's Pennsylvania.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who the Puritans were, and what they left" -->
The Puritans were English Protestants who wanted the Church of England stripped of what they saw as leftovers of Roman Catholicism. In the 1620s the men who ran the English church and government refused. They put Puritan ministers out of their posts and threatened them, in the language of the time, with "extirpation from the earth."

What they did to ordinary Puritans was physical. In 1630 one man was sentenced to prison for life, had his property taken, his nose slit, one ear cut off, and his forehead branded with the letters S.S. Branding means pressing hot metal against skin to burn a permanent mark into it. The letters stood for "sower of sedition" — a man who spreads rebellion. From 1630 onward as many as 20,000 Puritans left England for America.

The Pilgrims who landed at Plymouth in 1620 were not Puritans, though the two are often mixed up. The Pilgrims had given up on the Church of England and separated from it altogether. The Puritans thought it was a real church that needed fixing.

In the Massachusetts churches a person could not simply join. You had to have had a conversion experience and be able to satisfy the other members that you had.

The first book printed in British North America came out of this. Richard Mather and two other ministers put the Psalms into verse so congregations could sing them, and the *Bay Psalm Book* was printed at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640. Eleven copies still exist.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four Quakers hanged in Boston" -->
Quakers, who call themselves the Religious Society of Friends, formed in England around the preacher George Fox in 1652. They taught that the light of Christ is inside every person, which meant a person needed no minister to reach God. Other English Protestants called that dangerous heresy. By 1680 English jailers had held 10,000 Quakers, and 243 of them had died in the King's prisons of torture and mistreatment.

Massachusetts treated them much the same way. In 1656 and 1657 the men of the General Court outlawed what they called "the cursed sect of heretics commonly called Quakers." They fined ship captains who carried Quakers and colonists who owned Quaker books. In October 1658 they made it a capital crime — a crime punished by death — for a banished Quaker to come back into the colony. Between 1659 and 1661 four Quakers were hanged on Boston Common under that law.

Three of the four can be named from the records used for this book. William Robinson, a merchant from London, and Marmaduke Stephenson, a ploughman from Yorkshire, were hanged together. Mary Dyer was brought to the gallows with them and made to watch them die. The rope was put around her neck, and then she was reprieved and put out of the colony. She came back. She was hanged on Boston Common on 1 June 1660. The fourth name is not printed here, because it could not be confirmed from a source good enough to use.

Mary Dyer's first trouble with Massachusetts was for supporting Anne Hutchinson, and it drove her family to Rhode Island in 1638. She became a Quaker in England in the 1650s, came back to New England, and was arrested and banished three times before the time that killed her.

Charles II ordered the executions stopped in 1661, after Quakers in England pressed him to. By 1677 Friends could hold meetings openly in Massachusetts. Virginia's assembly had passed its own anti-Quaker laws from 1659, including a death penalty, and nobody was executed under them. Thomas Jefferson wrote about that later and refused to give Virginia any credit for it. If no one was hanged there, he said, "it was not owing to the moderation of the church, or the spirit of the legislature."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maryland, 1649 — the first toleration law, and what it left out" -->
Cecil Calvert, the second Lord Baltimore, was a Catholic, and Maryland was the one English colony where Catholics could worship openly. He sent the Maryland assembly a set of proposals about religion. The freemen met at St Mary's City from 2 April 1649, rewrote part of what he sent, and on 21 April voted it into law as An Act Concerning Religion. People have called it the Toleration Act ever since.

Its protection was real and it was narrow. No person "professing to believe in Jesus Christ," the act says, shall "be in any ways troubled, molested, or discountenanced for or in respect of his or her religion, nor in the free exercise thereof." That covered Catholics and Protestants and nobody else.

The same act opens by ordering death for other beliefs. Anyone who "shall from henceforth Blaspheme God that is Curse him or deny our Saviour Jesus Christ to be the Son of God or shall deny the holy Trinity ... shall be punished with death & Confiscation or forfeiture of all his or her lands and Goods to the Lord Proprietary." A Jew, a Muslim, or a Christian who did not accept the Trinity could be hanged under the law that is remembered for toleration.

A third part of it went after insults. Calling a neighbour a heretic, an idolater, a Puritan, a Presbyterian, or a Popish priest, or any similar name "in a reproachful manner relating to matters of Religion," cost a fine of ten shillings, half to the person insulted. Anyone who could not pay was "publickly whipt" and held in jail until they apologised in public in front of a magistrate.

Maryland Puritans took control and swept the act away in 1654, putting Catholics, Jews, Quakers and other dissenters under legal penalties. In 1661, after the king was back on the English throne, Marylanders repealed the Puritan laws and the act was in force again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two colonies that let people alone, and the first Jewish congregation" -->
Roger Williams founded Providence in 1636 as, in his words, "a shelter for persons distressed for conscience." In colonial Rhode Island, Anglicans, Puritans, Quakers, Catholics, Jews and people of no religion lived as neighbours with the same rights. His story is below.

William Penn was a Quaker. In 1681 he turned a debt Charles II owed his father into a charter for the province of Pennsylvania, and by 1685 as many as 8,000 Quakers had crossed to it. Penn's Frame of Government of 1682 promised that everyone who believed in "One Almighty and eternal God" would "in no wayes be molested or prejudiced for their Religious Perswasion or Practice in matters of Faith and Worship." Then it added the part that went further than any other colony: nobody in Pennsylvania could "be compelled at any time to frequent or maintain any Religious Worship, Place or Ministry whatever." You did not have to attend a church, and you did not have to pay for one.

That is why German families came. The first group reached Philadelphia from Krefeld in 1683, and they were Mennonites and perhaps some Dutch Quakers. Mennonites, Dunkers, Schwenkfelders, Moravians and German Baptists followed, all of them leaving religious punishment behind in Europe. People described the province as an asylum for banished sects.

Twenty-three Jewish refugees from Dutch Brazil reached New Amsterdam in 1654, driven out when the Portuguese retook the colony and brought the Inquisition with them; the Inquisition had burned a Brazilian Jew at the stake in 1647. Within a year the group was holding religious services in the city, the first Jewish worship in what is now the United States. By 1658 Jews had settled at Newport, Rhode Island, for the same reason. `immigration` leads on how they got here and on the governor who tried to expel them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading for God" -->
New England is where children's schooling first became a legal duty in this land, and the reason written into the law was religious. In 1642 the men of the Massachusetts General Court ordered parents and masters to teach children and apprentices enough "learning as may enable them perfectly to read the English tongue." Five years later they passed a second law because the first was widely ignored. Its opening line says it is "one of the chief projects of that old deluder Satan to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures." People have called it the Old Deluder Satan act ever since. The point of teaching a child to read was so that the child could read the Bible without a minister in between.

Harvard was founded in 1636 for the same reason, at the other end of the same system. The colonists' own pamphlet of 1643, *New Englands First Fruits*, gives the purpose: "to advance Learning and perpetuate it to Posterity; dreading to leave an illiterate Ministery to the Churches, when our present Ministers shall lie in the Dust." They were afraid of running out of trained ministers when the ones they had died.

The schoolbook that taught New England children to read was *The New England Primer*, made by a printer called Benjamin Harris. Nobody knows exactly when it first came off the press. The American Antiquarian Society dates it between 1687 and 1690, and says the only evidence is booksellers' advertisements, because the printing records are gone. The first advertisement Harris placed for it was in 1690, and no copy printed before 1727 survives anywhere.

The Primer taught more than letters. One of its pages showed the burning alive of John Rogers at Smithfield in England on 4 February 1555, the first Protestant executed under the Catholic queen Mary, with his wife and ten children drawn in beside the fire. A long verse beside the picture, written as his dying advice to his children, told them to "Keep always God before your Eyes" and to "Abhor the arrant Whore of Rome, and all her Blasphemies" — that is, to hate the Catholic Church. The Library of Congress says generations of New England children read it, and that it helped fuel the anti-Catholic feeling that lasted in that region well into the 1800s.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico — punished for their own ceremonies" -->
In the pueblos of New Mexico the Spanish colonists wanted Pueblo religion gone. For eighty years Franciscan friars pressed for the complete destruction of the kivas — the round rooms, partly underground, where Pueblo people hold ceremonies — and for an end to the masked dances the friars called idolatry. The Architect of the Capitol's account of these years puts it plainly: Pueblo people "found practicing their religion were tortured and sometimes executed."

In 1675 Spanish officials convicted forty-seven Pueblo leaders of sorcery. Some of the forty-seven were executed. One of the men who was not was Po'pay, of Ohkay Owingeh, a farmer and a member of a medicine society whose work was healing and the spiritual life of his people. He was flogged: whipped in public, on the bare back, until it bled.

Five years later, on 10 August 1680, the Pueblo nations rose against the Spanish and drove them out of New Mexico for twelve years. Po'pay led it. `native-nations` tells the rising itself; what belongs here is that it began over the right to hold a ceremony.

New Mexico gave Po'pay's statue to the National Statuary Hall Collection in the United States Capitol in 2005. He is the first person in that collection born on land that became the United States. No picture or written description of him survives, so the face is the sculptor's own work. Cliff Fragua, of Jemez Pueblo, carved him, and wrote of the statue: "On his back are the scars that remain from the whipping he received for his participation and faith in the Pueblo ceremonies and religion."

The Indian Pueblo Cultural Center, which the Pueblo nations run themselves, describes those eighty years this way: "the Pueblo people suffered violence, cruelty, and hunger; exploitation of labor and resources; harassment and persecution for practicing long-held religious traditions that are interwoven in daily life."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Roger Williams
> **Who:** A minister thrown out of Massachusetts who founded a colony where the government was not allowed to touch religion · **When and where:** Born London about 1603; Massachusetts 1631; founded Providence, Rhode Island, 1636; died Providence 1683

He was the son of a London merchant, studied at Cambridge, and was ordained a minister in the Church of England. He disliked its hierarchy, its wealth, its political power, and its ornament. He reached Massachusetts Bay in 1631, one year after the Puritans settled it.

In Massachusetts, religious rules were the law and the government enforced them. Williams preached that it should not. He said a government has no business enforcing anyone's religion, and that a person's spiritual life is separate from civic life. He called it soul liberty. The National Park Service calls what he preached "new and dangerous ideas," which is how Massachusetts took it.

The court ordered him out of the colony. Rather than be shipped back to England, where he might have been executed, he walked out into the winter. Wampanoag people, whose language he had learned and with whom he had traded, took him in and kept him alive. With help from Wampanoag and Narragansett people he travelled by canoe to a freshwater spring at a place the Narragansett called Moshassuck, and he settled there with their permission in 1636. He named it Providence. In 1638 the Narragansett sachems Canonicus and Miantonomi signed a deed that formally established the settlement and kept Narragansett trading rights inside it.

He wanted the place to be "a shelter for persons distressed for conscience," and it was. Anglicans, Puritans, Quakers, Catholics, Jews and people who believed nothing lived in Rhode Island as neighbours with the same rights. He thought plenty of them were wrong. That was the point: being wrong was not the government's business.

In 1644 he published *The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution*, an argument against forcing anyone to worship. "God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any civill state," he wrote, and forced uniformity is "the greatest occasion of civill Warre, ravishing of conscience ... and destruction of millions of souls." He put it more bluntly elsewhere: "forced worship stinks in God's nostrils." The year before, in 1643, he published *A Key into the Language of America*, a book about the Narragansett language and the people who spoke it.

Parliament granted Rhode Island a patent in 1644. Williams went back to England again in 1663, and Charles II signed a charter that recognised the colony's "livlie experiment." He died in Providence in 1683 and is buried there at Prospect Terrace. Congress made his home ground a national memorial in 1965.

### Anne Hutchinson
> **Who:** A Boston woman who held her own religious meetings, taught her own reading of scripture, and was banished and then thrown out of her church for it · **When and where:** Born Alford, England, about 1591; Boston from 1634; banished November 1637; excommunicated March 1638; killed 1643

She crossed on the *Griffin* in the autumn of 1634 with her husband William and their children, following their minister John Cotton to Boston. `immigration` tells the crossing.

From 1636 she held a meeting in her own house once a week to go back over the Sunday sermon. It started as a meeting for women, and men began coming too, including men who mattered in the colony. In Massachusetts the minister's teaching in the meeting house was the authorised version. A second meeting in a private house, led by a woman, was a rival service.

What she taught is the part that got her tried. She said that God's grace alone decides who is saved, and that good behaviour has nothing to do with it. She also said she could tell which ministers and which neighbours had received that grace and which had not. That second claim was the unanswerable one, because it put her in judgement over the colony's ministers. Her opponents labelled her teaching antinomian, a word meaning "against the law," by which they meant it let a saved person ignore the rules. That was their name for it, not hers.

At her trial she said God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation," and would curse the Puritans if they harmed her. The court called that blasphemy.

There were two proceedings, five months apart. The General Court tried her at Newtown, now Cambridge, in November 1637 and banished her. Then the church at Boston tried her separately and excommunicated her in March 1638 — put her out of the congregation. Its charge included that she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer; and a Magistrate than a Subject." `rights-movements` carries the trial itself, in the words of the record.

In the spring of 1638 she went to Aquidneck Island in what became Rhode Island and founded the town of Portsmouth. After her husband died she moved in 1642 onto Dutch land on Long Island Sound. In 1643 she and several of her children were killed there in a Siwanoy attack. One daughter survived.

A colony that its people had crossed an ocean to build, so that they could worship as they believed they should, put her out of it for worshipping as she believed she should.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A wave of revival preaching crossed the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s and made something new: a religious event that people in different colonies took part in at the same time. Historians call it the Great Awakening. It was not a revival of churchgoing, because churchgoing was already high — between 1700 and 1740 an estimated 75 to 80 percent of colonists went to church, and new churches were going up fast. What the revival preachers wanted was a moment they called the new birth, a conversion a person could feel and name. Churches split over whether that moment was real religion or a stirred-up crowd.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Lights and Old Lights" -->
Preachers who backed the revival were called New Lights among the Congregationalists and the New Side among the Presbyterians. The ones who thought the whole thing was noise and hysteria were the Old Lights and the Old Side. The split ran through congregations and families, and it lasted.

The revival had burned out in New England by the mid-1740s, and preachers carried it south, where it set off revivals that went on into the 1800s. The churches that backed it — Presbyterians, Baptists and Methodists — were the biggest Protestant groups in the country by the early 1800s. The ones that opposed it or were split by it, including the Anglicans, the Quakers and the Congregationalists, fell behind.

Something almost opposite was happening at the same time among a small number of wealthy and educated colonists. Deists kept the idea of a creator and dropped the rest, including the divinity of Jesus. Thomas Jefferson and John Adams were among them. There were never many deists, and the revival churches swamped them in the next century.

You could see the growth from a boat. Three drawings of the New York skyline from 1690, 1730 and 1771 show what happened: nothing much in 1690, and eighteen church steeples by 1771.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The revival builds a college" -->
Presbyterian ministers in the colonies had to be Harvard or Yale graduates. That rule was the problem, because it shut out the men trained by William Tennent.

Tennent was an Irish-born, Edinburgh-educated Presbyterian minister who ran a school at Neshaminy, in Bucks County, Pennsylvania, from 1726 until he died in 1745. People called it the Log College, because it was a log house about twenty feet long and nearly as wide. He drilled his pupils in ancient languages and the Bible, and several of them, his four sons among them, became revival preachers. None of them could be ordained.

So the New Lights built a college of their own. They planned it in early 1746, and on 22 October 1746 the acting governor of New Jersey, John Hamilton, granted a charter for the College of New Jersey. That is Princeton, and its own historians call its founding a direct outgrowth of the Great Awakening. Six months after the charter, three Log College graduates — Samuel Blair, Gilbert Tennent, and William Tennent Jr. — became trustees, along with Samuel Finley and Richard Treat.

Only that one college was founded inside this half-century. The other colleges that came out of the same religious argument belong to the next section: Rhode Island College in 1764, Queen's College in 1766, Dartmouth in 1769. `education` leads on all of them as colleges.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Preaching to enslaved people" -->
Revival preachers took their message to enslaved people, and enslavers pushed back. George Whitefield printed an open letter in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* in 1740 addressed to enslavers in Maryland, Virginia and the Carolinas. He set out their objection in order to attack it: that "teaching them Christianity would make them proud, and consequently unwilling to submit to Slavery." He answered that this was a dreadful reflection on their own religion. He did not say slavery was wrong, and he owned enslaved people himself.

The record of who converted in these years is mostly held in the papers of the people who held them. One name that survives is Leah, an enslaved woman in Jonathan Edwards's household at Northampton, who became a full member of the Northampton church during the revival. The independent Black churches come later, after 1750.

### George Whitefield
> **Who:** An English preacher whose colonial tours were the peak of the Great Awakening, and who campaigned to have slavery legalised in Georgia · **When and where:** Born Gloucester, England, 16 December 1714; seven visits to America from 1738; died Newburyport, Massachusetts, 30 September 1770

He was ordained in the Church of England and spent his career at odds with it. He first reached Georgia in May 1738. He came back in November 1739, landed at Philadelphia, and got to Savannah in January 1740. His tour of the colonies from 1739 to 1741 was the high point of the Great Awakening in America. One sermon in Boston drew as many as 30,000 people.

Many churches would not let him in, so he preached outdoors and carried a folding pulpit with him. He is thought to have preached about two thousand sermons from it. The first recorded time he used it was at Moorfields in England on 9 April 1742, in front of a crowd people estimated at twenty or thirty thousand. Some of them threw stones, rotten eggs and pieces of dead cat at him. He kept going.

What he preached was the new birth: the conversion a person feels happening. He admitted in a printed sermon of 1739 that "how this glorious Change is wrought in the Soul cannot easily be explained." The Library of Congress lists what made him work on a crowd: a big voice, a performer's delivery, simple language, and advertising he placed in newspapers ahead of himself.

He had enemies. A London engraving of 1763 called him Dr. Squintum, mocking his crossed eye, and accused him of chasing women and of getting rich off collections.

In 1740 he founded an orphanage called Bethesda near Savannah, in a colony where slavery was against the law. He decided he could not run it without enslaved workers. In 1741 he wrote that farming more land than his hired servants and older boys could manage was "impracticable without a few negroes." In 1747 he wrote to a donor that friends in South Carolina were paying for "a plantation and slaves in this province which I purpose to devote to the support of Bethesda," and that "One negroe has been given me — Some more I purpose to purchase this week."

From 1747 he campaigned to have Georgia's ban on slavery lifted. He told the colony's trustees that "Georgia never can be a flourishing province, unless negroes are employed," and that if the law stood he could not "cultivate the plantation in any manner." The trustees gave way, and slavery became legal in Georgia in the early 1750s; sources put it at 1751 or at 1752, when Georgia became a royal colony. When Whitefield died in 1770 he left 4,000 acres of Georgia land and fifty enslaved people to the Countess of Huntingdon.

His death at Newburyport made an impression across the colonies. One of the poems written for him came from a seventeen-year-old enslaved girl in Boston, Phillis Wheatley, and it was printed that year. `art` and `slavery-freedom` tell her story.

### Jonathan Edwards
> **Who:** The minister of the Northampton revival and the Awakening's leading thinker, and a man who bought and held enslaved people for most of his adult life · **When and where:** Born East Windsor, Connecticut, 5 October 1703; Northampton, Massachusetts; died Princeton, New Jersey, 22 March 1758

He was the fifth of eleven children of a Connecticut minister, went to Yale at thirteen, and was top of the class of 1720. In 1726 he joined his grandfather Solomon Stoddard at the church in Northampton, Massachusetts, the largest and most important church outside Boston, and took it over from him. He married Sarah Pierpont in 1727 and they raised ten children.

In 1734 and 1735 his own congregation went through a wave of conversions, and he wrote a book describing exactly what he had watched happen — who was converted, how old they were, what they said, how the town changed. Revival preachers in America and Britain used it as a model for what to look for.

His most famous sermon is "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," preached at Enfield, Connecticut, on 8 July 1741. He told the people in front of him that everything they trusted to keep them safe "would have no more influence to uphold you and keep you out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock." Then he told them what was holding them up instead: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire." He preached that God decides everything, that people are sinful by nature, that hell is real, and that a person has to be born again.

He was also the Awakening's best defender in argument. Critics said the conversions were an illusion or the devil's work, and Edwards wrote book after book trying to set out how you tell a real change of heart from a false one.

Edwards bought and held enslaved people. Yale's own research project on slavery lays out the record. The first was a teenaged girl named Venus, whom he bought at Newport in 1731; the last was a child named Titus, in 1756. The others known by name are Leah, Rose, and a married couple, Joseph and Sue. His wife Sarah asked family members and other ministers to find enslaved people for the household. He defended slavery as something the Bible allowed, and argued it was a way to convert the people held in it. Late in his life he turned against the slave trade, writing that it was unjust to take away the freedom of people born free. His son Jonathan Edwards Jr. and his student Samuel Hopkins went further after his death and called for ending slavery altogether.

In 1750 his own congregation dismissed him, after he tried to tighten the rules for who could take communion. He spent 1751 to 1757 at Stockbridge, on the western edge of Massachusetts, preaching to a small English congregation and working as a missionary to about 150 Mahican and Mohawk families.

In late 1757 he agreed to become president of the College of New Jersey. He had been there a few months when he was inoculated against smallpox. Inoculation then meant deliberately giving a person a small dose of live smallpox so that they would have a mild case and be safe afterwards. A few of the people inoculated died of it. Edwards was one of them, on 22 March 1758.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before the Revolution, Congregational churches in New England and Anglican churches in the South were paid out of public money. People were taxed for a minister whether they went to hear him or not. Between 1776 and 1833 the states took that apart, one at a time, and the new federal government never built anything like it. That is the change in these years.

Churchgoing did not fall off. What changed was the number of different churches. There were now too many kinds of churchgoer for any one church to be everybody's, and Baptists and other people outside the paid churches had spent years being taxed for ministers they never listened to. They organised against it and won.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the fight over the church tax in Virginia" -->
Virginia had the Church of England. Colonists were required to attend its services and to pay taxes that kept its ministers. The men on the parish vestries also ran poor relief and looked after orphans, so the parish was the local government as well as the church.

In 1779 the delegates in the Virginia assembly stopped the tax that paid Church of England ministers. Then they argued for six years about what should replace it. Patrick Henry's answer, put in on 24 December 1784, was a religious tax that let each taxpayer send his own share to whichever church he chose. George Washington wrote to George Mason that he did not object in principle to "making people pay towards the support of that which they profess," though he thought the bill would cause trouble. Richard Henry Lee backed it, writing that "the experience of all times shows religion to be the guardian of morals." John Marshall, later Chief Justice of the United States, voted for it too.

James Madison got the vote put off to the following autumn and spent the year organising against it. In June 1785 he published a long argument against religious taxes called the *Memorial and Remonstrance*, without his name on it. Baptists and most Presbyterians were on his side. By the autumn he had the votes, and Henry's bill died.

In its place the delegates took up a bill Thomas Jefferson had written back in 1777. They passed it on 16 January 1786 and it became law three days later: the Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom. "No man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place, or ministry whatsoever." Jefferson said the protection was meant to cover "the Jew and the Gentile, the Mahometan, the Hindoo, and Infidel of every denomination."

Virginia was ahead of most of the country, not typical of it. Massachusetts, Connecticut and New Hampshire were collecting religious taxes at the same time, and Maryland and Georgia passed such taxes without ever collecting them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="no religious test, and the First Amendment" -->
The Constitution written in Philadelphia in 1787 says almost nothing about religion. The one line is in Article VI: "no religious Test shall ever be required as Qualification" for a federal job. Charles Pinckney of South Carolina proposed it on 20 August 1787 and the convention adopted it ten days later, partly to head off complaints that the new government would keep people out of office over their religion.

Benjamin Franklin, then eighty-one, asked on 28 June 1787 that the convention open each day with prayers. He told the delegates that "the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this Truth — that God governs in the Affairs of Men." They voted him down. The reason given was that there was no money to pay a local minister to come and do it.

The silence in the Constitution worried people on both sides. Some thought it should have said more about God; one complaint was that it contained "no recognition of his mercies to us . . . or even of his existence." Others were afraid it would let the new government set up a national church. Madison's own voters in Virginia included a large number of Baptists who wanted the question settled. On 28 September 1789 the members of both houses voted to send twelve amendments to the states, and by December 1791 enough state legislatures had approved ten of them. The first begins: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof."

That bound Congress. It did not yet bind the states, and the states went on doing as they liked for another forty years. `government-politics` covers the court cases built on this amendment much later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the last church tax" -->
Massachusetts went on taxing people for churches for forty-two years after the First Amendment was ratified. Article III of its 1780 constitution required the legislature to make every town and parish pay for "public Protestant teachers of piety, religion and morality," and let the legislature order people to go and listen to them. A man's church tax went to his own denomination's minister if his town had one. If it did not, the money went to the parish minister anyway.

Article III had been the hardest part of writing that constitution. Phillips Payson, a Congregational minister at Chelsea, made the case for it in a sermon in 1778: "let the restraints of religion once be broken down . . . and one might well defy all human wisdom and power to support and preserve order and government in the state." Isaac Backus, who led the New England Baptists, answered him that year in a pamphlet, arguing that state money corrupts churches and that the best thing a government can do for religion is leave it alone. The Baptists lost. There was real doubt that Article III had won the two thirds of the vote it needed, and in 1780 the state's officials declared it adopted anyway.

Article III stood fifty-three years. In 1833 an amendment replaced it. Churches could still choose their own ministers and agree to pay them, but they had to raise the money themselves, and any member could leave by handing the society's clerk a written notice. Massachusetts was the last state to stop taxing people for a church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first Black churches" -->
In these years Black Americans founded churches of their own, and the men who founded them had been born into slavery.

George Liele was born enslaved in Virginia in 1750. He was ordained a Baptist minister in 1775 and preached at plantations up and down the Savannah River. In 1778 he gathered a congregation of Black Baptists in Savannah. During the Revolution he told his followers to side with the British, who were promising freedom to enslaved people who came over. When the British left Savannah in 1782 he went with them, and ended up in Jamaica.

Andrew Bryan, who had been baptised by Liele, kept the Savannah congregation going. White Savannahians had him and his brother Sampson arrested, jailed, and whipped for preaching, because they were afraid that any gathering of enslaved people was the start of a revolt. Neither Georgia's state historical marker nor the New Georgia Encyclopedia says how many lashes he was given. Bryan kept preaching. On 20 January 1788, in a barn at Brampton plantation, a white minister named Abraham Marshall and a Black minister named Jesse Peter baptised more than forty of Bryan's followers, made them a church, and ordained Bryan as their minister. He later bought his own freedom and his family's, and bought the land for the church building. He died in Savannah in 1812.

In Philadelphia the break came in the middle of a Sunday service. Richard Allen and Absalom Jones, both born enslaved and both free by the 1780s, were members at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church. The white members there moved the Black members out of the seats they usually sat in, put them around the wall, and then sent them up to the gallery. One Sunday, while they were kneeling to pray, a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones and pulled him up off his knees. Everyone Black in the building walked out together. They had helped pay for that gallery.

The sources do not agree on when this happened. The traditional date is 1787. The historian Richard S. Newman argues for 1792 or 1793, which would make it a planned protest rather than a sudden one, and the Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia puts it "in the early 1790s." Allen's own account of the walkout gives no date at all.

The walkout produced two churches rather than one. Jones took most of the group into the Episcopal church, and their congregation was received as the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas on 17 October 1794. Allen stayed Methodist and opened Bethel Church at Sixth and Lombard in July 1794, in an old blacksmith's shop hauled onto the lot he had bought in 1791.

### Richard Allen
> **Who:** Born enslaved, bought his freedom, walked out of a white church and built the first Black denomination in the United States · **When and where:** Born Philadelphia, 14 February 1760; Dover, Delaware, then Philadelphia; died Philadelphia, 26 March 1831

He was born enslaved to Benjamin Chew, a Philadelphia lawyer, and sold with his family to Stokely Sturgis, a farmer near Dover, Delaware. He became a Methodist and started preaching as a teenager while he was still enslaved. In 1780 he bought his freedom for two thousand dollars, working on his own time over about five years to earn it. The dollars were Continental currency, which by 1780 had lost most of its value.

He came to Philadelphia in February 1786. In his own words: "I preached in the commons, in Southwark, [in] Northern Liberties, and wherever I could find an opening. I frequently preached twice a day, at 5 o'clock in the morning and in the evening, and it was not uncommon for me to preach from four to five times a day. I established prayer meetings; I raised a society, in 1786, of forty-two members."

Three men joined him in wanting a church for Black Philadelphians: Absalom Jones, William White and Dorus Ginnings. The elder at St. George's forbade their prayer meetings. Allen wrote that the Black members of the church "were considered as a nuisance."

Then came the Sunday in the gallery. Allen wrote down what was said. A trustee took hold of Absalom Jones while he was kneeling and told him, "You must get up — you must not kneel here." Jones answered, "Wait until prayer is over, and I will get up and trouble you no more." The trustee said, "No, you must get up now, or I will call for aid and force you away," and signalled a second trustee, who went to pull William White up. "By this time, prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church." Allen added that the Black members had paid toward finishing St. George's, "in building the gallery and laying new floors," and were turned out of it as soon as it was comfortable.

They rented a storeroom and were threatened with being "read publicly out of meeting" if they kept worshipping there. Benjamin Rush and Robert Ralston gave money. Allen and Jones collected $360 on their first day out with the subscription paper, which Allen said was "the greatest day's collection that we met with."

He bought the lot at Sixth and Lombard in 1791 and opened Bethel Church there in July 1794. He became the first Black man ordained a Methodist minister, in 1799. Seventeen years later he brought African Methodist congregations from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland together at Philadelphia to form the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and they elected him its first bishop. He died at his home on 26 March 1831 and is buried in the basement of Mother Bethel.

### Absalom Jones
> **Who:** Born enslaved, taught himself to read, and became the first Black priest in the Episcopal Church in the United States · **When and where:** Born Delaware, 6 November 1746; Philadelphia from 1762; died 1818

He was born enslaved in Delaware. He taught himself to read, using the New Testament as one of the books he learned from. When he was sixteen his mother, his sister and his five brothers were sold away. The man who owned him brought him to Philadelphia, where he went to a Quaker night school for Black people.

He bought his freedom in 1784. In 1787 he and Richard Allen started the Free African Society, which worked against slavery and supported widows, orphans and the poor.

After the walkout from St. George's, Jones led most of the group into the Episcopal church. Their congregation was received into the Diocese of Pennsylvania on 17 October 1794 as the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. He was ordained a deacon in 1795. In 1802, at fifty-six, he was ordained a priest by Bishop William White — the first priest of African descent in the United States. Delaware's state historical marker gives 1804 for that; the Episcopal Church's own archives give 1802.

He also started a day school, a Female Benevolent Society and an African Friendly Society, and in 1800 he petitioned Congress about slavery. `rights-movements` covers that petition. He died in 1818.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Once the states stopped paying ministers, churches had to go out and get their own members. That is what these fifty years are: a competition for souls, run by preachers who travelled. Revivals came so thick that the magazines that tracked them gave up counting. One of them wrote in 1827 that revivals "are becoming too numerous in our country to admit of being generally mentioned in our Record."

The kind of Christianity that won is called evangelicalism. It taught that a person is converted in a moment they can feel and name, and that a converted person then goes and does something about the world. Out of that second half came thousands of societies for handing out Bibles, teaching children, sending missionaries, and getting people to stop drinking. The largest of them, in 1826 and 1827, were the American Education Society, the American Board of Foreign Missions, the American Bible Society, the American Sunday-School Union, the American Tract Society and the American Home Missionary Society.

In these same fifty years Americans killed each other in the street over which Bible a school should read, and a governor ordered a whole church driven out of his state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="camp meetings" -->
In 1800 two revivals started at once, at opposite ends of the country: a quiet one in New England and a loud one in Kentucky. The Kentucky revival produced the camp meeting.

Presbyterian ministers organised the first ones, copying the long outdoor communion gatherings of the Church of Scotland. Families loaded a wagon with food and bedding, drove to a field or the edge of a wood, pitched a tent and stayed several days. The biggest was at Cane Ridge, near Lexington, in the summer of 1801, organised by a Presbyterian minister named Barton Stone.

The Presbyterians and Baptists who invented camp meetings soon wanted nothing to do with them, because of how people behaved at them. The Methodists took them over and carried them east, and for decades a camp meeting was a Methodist thing.

Benjamin Latrobe, an architect, drew the plan of a Methodist camp meeting in Fairfax County, Virginia, on 8 August 1809. His sketch shows the men's seats separated from the women's, the tents for Black worshippers separated from the tents for white ones, and at the side of the camp a boarded pen filled with straw, where, he wrote, "the converted were thrown that they might kick about without injuring themselves."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Sunday school was a reading school" -->
Sunday schools began in England in the 1780s and reached Philadelphia in 1791, when a group of pastors and merchants founded the First Day Society. They paid local schoolmasters to teach boys and girls, and grown men and women who could not read, to read and write. The textbook was the Bible. Sunday was the day poor working children were not at work, and there were no free public schools yet, so for a lot of children this was the only school there was.

In 1817 a group of Philadelphia businessmen started the Sunday and Adult School Union and staffed it with volunteers instead of paid schoolmasters. Within a year they had opened forty-three Sunday schools in the city and were teaching 5,970 pupils. It became the American Sunday School Union, sent missionaries out to start Sunday schools in towns, in the countryside, and on Native reservations, and turned into one of the biggest publishers of children's books in the country. The American Tract Society, started in 1825, handed out an estimated 35 million books and tracts in its first ten years.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="whose Bible - Philadelphia, 1842 to 1844" -->
The men who ran the new public schools called them nonsectarian, and then opened the day with Protestant hymns and readings from the King James Bible. Catholic families said that was a Protestant school paid for with everyone's taxes.

In 1842 Bishop Francis Patrick Kenrick wrote to the board that ran Philadelphia's public schools. He asked two things: that Catholic children be allowed to read the Douay Bible, the translation Catholics use, and that they not be made to take part in Protestant religious exercises. Early in 1843 the men on the board answered with what looked like a compromise. Children could be excused from the King James Bible and could use any Bible "without note or comment." The Douay Bible has notes and comments, so that rule shut out the one Bible the Catholic families had asked for.

In February 1844 a Catholic school director in Kensington named Hugh Clark suggested pausing Bible reading until the board could write a rule both sides could live with. A rumour went round that Clark had ordered a teacher, Miss Bedford, to stop reading the Protestant Bible. Testimony afterwards showed the rumour was false. It was repeated anyway.

Nativists — people who wanted the country kept for native-born Protestants — held a rally on 3 May 1844 and marched into Kensington. On 6 May a nativist named George Shiffler was shot dead. By the end of that day a second man, apparently a bystander, was dead too, and several more nativists were wounded, two of them fatally. On 8 May crowds burned St. Michael's church at Second and Master Streets, St. Augustine's at Fourth and Vine, and a Catholic seminary.

It started again on 5 July at the Church of St. Philip de Neri in Southwark. Militia under General George Cadwalader fired on the crowd with cannon. By the morning of 7 July four militiamen and probably a dozen rioters were dead, and many more were wounded. No source gives a reliable total for the two riots together.

After 1844, Catholic bishops stopped trying to fix the public schools and began building a second school system of their own. `education` leads on the schools themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a new religion, and two states that drove it out" -->
Joseph Smith grew up in the part of western New York people called the Burned Over District, because so many revivals had swept through it. In 1830 he published a book at Palmyra, the Book of Mormon. He said he had translated it from gold plates, in a language he called reformed Egyptian, and eleven men signed statements saying they had seen and handled the plates. The book tells of Israelites led out of Jerusalem to the Americas about 600 years before Christ. His followers understood their church as the earliest Christianity brought back.

They were driven from New York to Ohio, from Ohio to Missouri, and from Missouri to Illinois. Missouri's legislators had made a whole county, Caldwell, in 1836, and told them to live in it. More converts kept arriving and they settled past its borders. After an election riot in 1838 and a Mormon attack on a militia unit they had taken for a mob, Governor Lilburn Boggs issued an order on 27 October 1838. It said the Latter-day Saints must be driven out of the state or, in the order's own word, exterminated. Three days later, on 30 October 1838, a mob attacked the settlement at Haun's Mill and killed eighteen men and boys. Over the next year about eight thousand church members left Missouri for Illinois, most of them with their property gone. Boggs's order stayed on the books until Governor Bond cancelled it on 25 June 1976.

In Illinois they built the town of Nauvoo. Their neighbours resented the way Mormon voters all voted together, and resented their business dealings. They were angry at the news that some church leaders had more than one wife. That news split the church itself as well. Smith ordered the press of an opposition newspaper destroyed and was jailed at Carthage for it. On 27 June 1844 a mob broke into the jail and murdered him and his brother Hyrum. Accounts differ on the details; the Library of Congress says he was shot, and that one man in the mob tried to behead him and was stopped. Vigilantes then attacked Mormon farms around Nauvoo.

Brigham Young planned an evacuation of about 15,000 people. The wagons began crossing the Mississippi into Iowa on 4 February 1846. `migration` follows them west.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the religion enslaved people were given, and the one they kept" -->
In the South, enslaved people were mostly kept out of ordinary church attendance. Charles Colcock Jones, a Presbyterian minister in Liberty County, Georgia, wrote in 1842 that they "were not permitted, on working days, to attend the public administration of the word in company with their masters," and that where they did attend, part of the white church was set aside for them.

Jones spent his career persuading planters to have Christianity taught to the people they held. His book *The Religious Instruction of the Negroes in the United States*, finished on 4 July 1842, tells ministers which verses to use. They are the verses about obedience: Ephesians 6:5, Colossians 3:22, and 1 Timothy 6:1 — "let as many servants as are under the yoke count their masters worthy of all honor." A minister's job, he wrote, was to teach enslaved people "the duties of their station."

Frederick Douglass wrote down what that looked like from the other side. In August 1832 the man who held him, Thomas Auld, was converted at a Methodist camp meeting in Talbot County, Maryland. Douglass hoped it would free him, or at least make Auld kinder. "I was disappointed in both these respects," he wrote. "I believe him to have been a much worse man after his conversion than before. Prior to his conversion, he relied upon his own depravity to shield and sustain him in his savage barbarity; but after his conversion, he found religious sanction and support for his slaveholding cruelty." Auld became a class-leader and housed the visiting preachers. Douglass's line about that: "while he starved us, he stuffed them."

A young white man named Wilson started a Sunday school at St. Michael's to teach enslaved people to read the New Testament. They met three times. Then two class-leaders from Auld's own church, West and Fairbanks, came with sticks and other missiles, drove them off, and told them not to meet again.

Douglass also wrote down what Auld did to a young woman named Henny, whose hands had been burned useless in a fire when she was a child. Auld tied her up and whipped her bare shoulders with a heavy cowskin until the blood ran, and while he did it he quoted scripture: "He that knoweth his master's will, and doeth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes." He left her tied four or five hours at a time, whipped her before breakfast, went to his store, and whipped her again at dinner in the places already raw. Then he put her out to fend for herself.

Not every minister was Auld's kind. Douglass names one, George Cookman, whom he says the enslaved people loved and believed was working to free them.

Out of sight of all this, enslaved people held their own worship. `slavery-freedom` leads on that, on the laws that made it a crime, and on Nat Turner and Denmark Vesey.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the churches split before the country did" -->
American Protestants split their own churches over slavery years before the states seceded.

In 1844 the Methodists' General Conference voted to suspend Bishop James Osgood Andrew of Georgia from his office for as long as he held enslaved people, whom he had got through his marriage. Within days the delegates adopted a Plan of Separation letting the conferences in slave states leave. In 1845 those delegates met at Louisville, Kentucky, and made the Methodist Episcopal Church, South.

The Baptists split the same year. Georgia Baptists put forward a slaveholder, James E. Reeve, to be a missionary; Northern Baptists refused to appoint him. From 8 to 12 May 1845, Baptists from the South met at Augusta, Georgia, and formed the Southern Baptist Convention, with William Bullein Johnson as its first president.

Meanwhile the African Methodist Episcopal Church, which started with 8 ministers and 5 churches, had 176 ministers, 296 churches and 17,375 members by 1846. Women preached in it early and met resistance for it; one of them, Juliann Jane Tillman, was drawn from life for a Philadelphia engraving in 1844.

The Shakers, who had come from England with Mother Ann Lee in 1774, sent people from New York to Kentucky in these years to look at the revivals, and made a modest number of converts. They danced in worship, often in circles inside circles, and got their nickname from the shaking.

### Charles Grandison Finney
> **Who:** A lawyer who quit the law the morning after his conversion and became the most influential revival preacher in the country · **When and where:** Born Warren, Connecticut, 29 August 1792; upstate New York, then Oberlin, Ohio; died 1875

He wrote his own opening line: "I was born in Warren, Litchfield county, Connecticut, August 29, 1792. When I was about two years old, my father removed to Oneida county, New York, which was, at that time, to a great extent, a wilderness." His parents did not belong to a church. He said there were almost no religious books where he grew up, and that he seldom heard a sermon.

He was training to be a lawyer at Adams, in Jefferson County, New York, when he was converted in 1821. The next morning a client came to the office to ask if he was ready for a ten o'clock hearing. Finney's answer: "Deacon B, I have a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead His cause, and I cannot plead yours."

He preached across upstate New York and then, from September 1830, spent six months in Rochester, preaching every day of the week and three times on Sunday and spending most of each day praying with people one at a time. Rochester is where he began using the anxious seat: a bench at the front where anyone worried about their soul was asked to come and sit, in front of everybody. He explained why in his memoirs. Well-off people, he found, were "too proud to take any position that would reveal them to others as anxious for their souls," so he wanted "something that would commit them publicly to the service of Christ."

His methods were called the New Measures, and other ministers attacked them. So did his teaching, because he said a person could decide to be converted rather than wait for God to do it. His *Lectures on Revivals*, published in 1835, became the handbook other revival preachers worked from.

That same year Oberlin College in Ohio was close to collapse. A New York businessman and abolitionist, Arthur Tappan, rescued it on two conditions: that Finney be made professor of theology, and that Black students be admitted on the same terms as white ones. Finney took the job and also became pastor of the Oberlin church. He was president of the college from 1851 to 1866 and stayed on as its pastor until 1872. He went on lecturing there until he died in 1875. He was not Oberlin's first president, though he is often called it. That was Asa Mahan.

What he taught tied the revivals to the reform societies. The proof that a person had really been converted, he said, was "a person's benevolence toward others."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-grandison-finney" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-smith" name="Joseph Smith" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Smith
> **Who:** Founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, driven out of four states and killed by a mob · **When and where:** 1805-1844; western New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Nauvoo, Illinois; killed at Carthage, Illinois, 27 June 1844

He grew up in the stretch of western New York people called the Burned Over District, because so many revivals had passed through it that there was said to be nothing left to burn. Nearly every new church of that time was a branch off an older one. His was not. The Library of Congress calls it *sui generis* — the only one of its kind.

In 1830 a printer at Palmyra, New York, published the Book of Mormon for him. Smith said an angel had shown him gold plates buried in a hillside, written in a language he called reformed Egyptian, and that he had translated them. Eleven men signed statements that they had seen and handled the plates. The book tells of a family led out of Jerusalem to the Americas about 600 years before Christ, and of Christ appearing to their descendants here. Smith's followers understood the church he organised as the first Christianity restored after centuries of loss.

People drove them out wherever they settled. From New York they went to Ohio, from Ohio to Missouri, and from Missouri, under a governor's written order, to Illinois. There they built Nauvoo on the Mississippi and made it one of the largest towns in the state.

Nauvoo ended in the same way. Neighbours resented the Mormons voting together and Smith's run for president, and were angry at the news that some leaders had more than one wife — news that split the church from the inside as well. When a newspaper in Nauvoo printed attacks on him, Smith ordered its press destroyed. He was jailed at Carthage for it. On 27 June 1844 a mob broke into the jail and killed him and his brother Hyrum. Accounts of the details differ. The Library of Congress says he was shot, and that one man in the mob meant to cut off his head and was stopped.

Brigham Young took over and began moving about 15,000 people west. The wagons started across the Mississippi on 4 February 1846.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Immigration made the country far more Catholic and far more Jewish than it had ever been. Protestants who had thought of the United States as theirs fought that in schools, in state constitutions and in the street.

At the same time, about four million people who had been held as property were free, and the churches they had already built became the only institutions they owned outright. When the government wanted to know what those four million people wanted, it sent for their ministers.

On the reservations, officials in Washington wrote down a list of religious ceremonies and made them crimes. That list stayed in force until 1978.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the ministers who spoke for the freedpeople" -->
On 12 January 1865, in a house in Savannah, the Secretary of War, Edwin Stanton, and General William Sherman sat down with twenty Black men and asked them what freedom should look like. Thirteen of the twenty were ordained or licensed ministers. The rest were deacons, class-leaders and church officers.

The church was the one institution the freedpeople had built, paid for and run themselves, so its ministers were the people the government sent for.

Somebody wrote down each man's details, and they show what those congregations already were. William J. Campbell, 51, born in Savannah and freed by a will in 1849, was pastor of the 1st Baptist Church, about 1,800 members, with church property worth $18,000. John Cox, 58, had bought his own freedom in 1849 for $1,100 and led the 2d African Baptist Church, 1,222 members. James Porter, 39, free-born because his mother bought his freedom, was lay-reader at St. Stephen's Protestant Episcopal Colored Church, about 200 members. Jacob Godfrey, 57, had been a class-leader and steward in the Methodist church since 1836, and until the Union army arrived he had been held as property by James E. Godfrey, a Methodist preacher serving in the Confederate army.

The twenty chose Garrison Frazier to answer for all of them.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a second school system" -->
Catholic families had asked the public school boards to stop making their children read a Protestant Bible. The boards refused, so the families built schools of their own.

It started before the Civil War. Bishop John Neumann began building parish schools in Philadelphia in the 1850s. The bishops of the United States made it national policy at the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore, which met from 9 November to 7 December 1884. There they declared that every parish that could afford one should run its own school. Their pastoral letter put it this way: "A Catholic congregation without a Catholic school is like a family without a mother."

In 1834 there had been fewer than a dozen parish schools in the whole country for about half a million Catholics. Fifty years later the bishops were telling every parish to build one and to make it as good as the best schools in the country.

Protestants objected to the money. In 1875 Congressman James G. Blaine of Maine, at President Grant's suggestion, proposed an amendment to the Constitution: no money raised by taxation for public schools "shall ever be under the control of any religious sect; nor shall any money so raised or lands so devoted be divided between religious sect or denominations." Enough House members voted for it to pass. In the Senate it fell four votes short. Legislators in thirty-seven states then wrote their own version into their state constitutions, where most of them still are.

The word the amendment used was "sectarian." It meant Catholic, and nobody at the time pretended otherwise. Historians of these amendments describe the years they were written as a time of open hostility to the Catholic Church and to Catholics generally. Blaine ran for president in 1884. A supporter at one of his events called the Democrats the party of "rum, Romanism and rebellion," and Blaine did not disown it. Historians think it cost him Irish Catholic votes and the election.

`education` leads on the schools themselves; `government-politics` on the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="synagogues, yeshivas, and the door shut at the hotel" -->
There were about 2,500 Jews in the United States in 1800. By 1860 there were somewhere between 125,000 and 200,000, and by 1880 between 230,000 and 300,000. Then, in 1881, Czar Alexander II was assassinated, and the pogroms that followed in Russia — massacres, often with the government's help — started a mass migration of East European Jews to America.

American Jews spent these years building the institutions a religion needs. Isaac Leeser founded Maimonides College in Philadelphia in 1867, the first place in America to train rabbis. Isaac Mayer Wise brought 34 congregations together as the Union of American Hebrew Congregations in 1873 and founded Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati in 1875. Etz Chaim, the first yeshiva for Talmudic study in the country, opened in New York in 1886, the Jewish Theological Seminary in 1887, and the Rabbi Isaac Elchanan Theological Seminary in 1897.

They also divided. Wise wanted one American Judaism. At a banquet in 1883 for Hebrew Union College's first graduating class, the food served was not kosher. Traditional guests got up and left, and the argument that followed produced the seminaries that trained the rabbis who were not Reform. In 1885 the Pittsburgh Platform set out what Reform Judaism held.

Hostility followed them. In 1862 General Ulysses S. Grant issued an order expelling Jewish civilians from Kentucky, Tennessee and Mississippi. In 1877 a New York banker named Joseph Seligman was refused a room at the Grand Union Hotel in Saratoga because he was Jewish, and the Library of Congress marks that as the point where social antisemitism in America became open. New Hampshire granted Jews political equality the same year — 1877 — which is how late some states were.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the year a ceremony became a crime" -->
On 30 March 1883 the Office of Indian Affairs published a document called the Rules Governing the Court of Indian Offenses. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Hiram Price, issued it after a letter from the Secretary of the Interior, Henry M. Teller, who had called Native dances heathenish and objected to the work of medicine men.

Price named specific ceremonies as crimes. "The 'sun-dance,' the 'scalp-dance,' the 'war-dance,' and all other so-called feasts assimilating thereto, shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" For a first offence, agents withheld the person's rations for up to ten days. For a second, fifteen to thirty days without rations, or up to thirty days in the agency jail.

Rations were the food the government had agreed by treaty to hand out to people whose hunting country it had taken. So the penalty for dancing was that a family did not eat, for as long as a month.

Another rule covered healers: "The usual practices of so-called 'medicine-men' shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" A medicine man could be jailed for at least ten days, and held until he gave evidence that he had stopped.

These rules stayed enforceable until Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1978. That is ninety-five years.

In 1889 a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka had a vision during an eclipse of the sun on 1 January. Accounts of what he taught differ. In one account he saw the settlers gone, the buffalo back, the dead returned and everyone living in peace, and he told people to keep peace with white Americans and dance a circle dance. The version that spread on the Plains was different: that if the dance were done right, white people would be destroyed. The dance spread across hungry reservations, agents and newspapers called it the start of a war, and troops were sent. `native-nations` leads on what followed at Standing Rock in December 1890 and at Wounded Knee on 29 December 1890.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the churches and the boarding schools" -->
Federal officials did not run the Indian boarding schools alone. They handed a large share of them to churches, and they paid the churches to do it.

Investigators at the Department of the Interior, in a report published in 2024, counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools. Of those, 210 were operated by a religious institution or organisation, and at least 59 different religious bodies took federal money to run or support them. That count leaves out the schools churches ran with no federal money at all.

The breakdown, in their figures: 132 Protestant, 77 Catholic, 5 other. The largest single groups were Presbyterian with 31 schools, the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions with 20, Methodists with 15, Quakers with 13 listed under that name and 8 more listed as the Religious Society of Friends, Episcopalians with 12, and Jesuits with 10. Dozens of Catholic teaching orders appear on the list — Sisters of Mercy, Sisters of Providence, Sisters of St. Joseph, Sisters of St. Francis, Ursulines, Grey Nuns.

The money went back further than the schools. Members of Congress had been paying for this kind of work since 1819. In the Civilization Fund Act of 3 March that year they voted $10,000 a year to introduce, in the law's own words, "the habits and arts of civilization" among Indian nations, and most of it went to churches. They stopped the yearly payment in 1873. Between 1871 and 1969, the same investigators estimate, more than $23.3 billion in today's money was made available for the boarding-school system and the policies around it.

Government reports say plainly how unusual that handover was. A Senate report describes the reservations as "distributed among the major religious denominations, which, in an unprecedented delegation of power by the U.S. Government to church bodies, were given the right to nominate new agents, and direct educational and other activities on the reservations." The Commissioner of Indian Affairs wrote in 1872 that the agencies had been apportioned among the denominations so that each could "assume charge of the intellectual and moral education of the Indians thus brought within the reach of their influence." A Senate committee also found that the "military was frequently called in to reinforce the missionaries' orders."

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a temple in a gold-mining town" -->
Chinese miners and labourers brought their own religion with them. In Weaverville, in Trinity County, California, a Taoist temple burned down and the community built another. Work started in February 1874 and it was dedicated that April. They called it the Temple of the Forest Beneath the Clouds.

It is the oldest continuously used Chinese temple in California and is still a place of worship. In 1956 Moon Lim Lee, whose ancestors built it, and his wife Dorothy gave it to the state, and it is now a California State Historic Park.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Garrison Frazier
> **Who:** A Baptist minister, born enslaved, chosen by twenty Black church leaders to tell the Secretary of War what freedom meant · **When and where:** Born Granville County, North Carolina, about 1798; Savannah, Georgia, 12 January 1865

He was 67 years old at the meeting, and the minutes record the rest: born in Granville County, North Carolina; bought his own freedom and his wife's eight years earlier for $1,000 in gold and silver; an ordained Baptist minister for thirty-five years. His health had got bad enough that he no longer had a congregation of his own.

Twenty Black church leaders met Edwin Stanton and William Sherman in Savannah on 12 January 1865. They picked Frazier to answer the questions for all of them, and someone took down what he said.

Asked what slavery was, he said it was "receiving by irresistible power the work of another man, and not by his consent."

Asked what freedom was, he said it was "taking us from under the yoke of bondage, and placing us where we could reap the fruit of our own labor."

Asked how his people could best take care of themselves, he said: "land, and turn it and till it by our own labor — that is, by the labor of the women and children and old men."

Asked whether Black people would rather live among white people or by themselves, he said, "I would prefer to live by ourselves, for there is a prejudice against us in the South that will take years to get over." Every other man in the room was then asked the same question one at a time. All of them agreed with him.

Sherman left the room during the meeting so the men could be asked what they thought of him.

Four days later Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15, setting aside coastal land for freed families. `slavery-freedom` tells what happened to that land.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two things happened to American religion in these fifty years. It got louder, through radio and through a new Pentecostal worship that started in a Los Angeles stable in 1906 and spread around the world. It also got angrier with itself. Protestants split into two camps over how to read the Bible and argued it out in a Tennessee courtroom. Oregon voters tried to shut every Catholic school in their state. Meanwhile federal agents were still punishing Native people for holding their own ceremonies, and would go on doing it for decades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Azusa Street, 1906" -->
In April 1906 a new kind of Christian worship started in a house and then a shed in Los Angeles, and it spread from there around the world. It is called Pentecostal. The name comes from a day in the Bible when the first Christians were said to be filled with the Holy Spirit and to speak in languages they had never learned.

Pentecostals hold that this still happens. Speaking in tongues means speaking sounds you have not learned and do not understand, and they take it as proof that God's Spirit has filled a person. Meetings had no set order of service. People shouted, wept, sang, fell to the floor, and spoke in tongues, and anyone in the room might get up and preach.

The first tongues-speaking came at a house at 214 North Bonnie Brae Street on 9 April 1906. Within a week the meetings moved to 312 Azusa Street, a building about forty feet by sixty. It had been an African Methodist Episcopal church, founded by Biddy Mason, who had been enslaved and won her freedom in a California court. By 1906 the building was a tenement and a livery stable. The people at the first meetings were Black washerwomen, maids and labourers. White visitors came, and then came in greater numbers. It was one of the first congregations in the country where Black and white people worshipped together under a Black pastor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-j-seymour" name="William J. Seymour" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William J. Seymour
> **Who:** The Black preacher who led the Azusa Street revival, the start of Pentecostal Christianity.
> **When and where:** Born 2 May 1870 at Centerville, Louisiana; Los Angeles from early 1906; died 28 September 1922.

His parents, Simon and Phyllis Seymour, had both been enslaved.

In 1905 he was in Houston and wanted to study at a Bible school run by a preacher named Charles Fox Parham. Texas law kept Black and white students apart. Parham let him attend on one condition: he had to sit out in the hallway and listen to the lectures through the doorway. What he learned there was that speaking in tongues is the proof that God's Spirit has filled a person.

He came to Los Angeles in early 1906, invited by Julia Hutchins to help pastor a Holiness church. He preached what he had learned in the hallway. The congregation locked the door against him. He kept meeting in a private house, and on 9 April 1906 the tongues-speaking began there.

From September 1906 the mission printed its own newspaper, *The Apostolic Faith*. It went out free, and at its height it reached about fifty thousand subscribers around the world. Seymour ran the mission until his death in 1922, and it kept going until 1931. The building was pulled down. A plaque now marks the spot, and it calls the place the cradle of the worldwide Pentecostal movement.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-j-seymour" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christians arguing with Christians about Genesis" -->
The Scopes trial of 1925 is usually told as religion against science. It was not. It was one group of Christians against another, arguing about how to read the first book of the Bible, with a public school as the ground they fought on.

One side held that every word of the Bible is exactly true. The other held that the Bible is true about God without being a science book. Both sides put their argument into the trial record at Dayton, Tennessee. On 20 July 1925 the prosecutor William Jennings Bryan took the witness stand himself and said, "I accept the Bible absolutely." Clarence Darrow, for the defence, said his own purpose was "preventing bigots and ignoramuses from controlling the education of the United States." Bryan had written a closing speech he never got to give, because both sides dropped their closing speeches; it was printed afterwards. In it he asked, "Can the fathers and mothers of Tennessee be blamed for trying to protect their children from such a tragedy?" The tragedy he meant was a child losing faith in God.

The defence's answer came from Christians too. Kirtley Mather, who ran the geology department at Harvard and had trained at a Baptist college, put a written statement in the record: "Evolution is not a power, nor a force; it is a process, a method. God is a power, a force." `government-politics` tells the Butler Act and the verdict; `education` tells what a school may teach.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the states that made evolution illegal to teach" -->
Tennessee was not alone, and its law was not the last. Oklahoma passed one and repealed it in 1926. Mississippi passed one in the 1920s and still had it in 1968. Arkansas voters passed theirs at the ballot box in 1928: a teacher who taught "that mankind ascended or descended from a lower order of animals" was guilty of a crime, fined up to five hundred dollars, and had to leave the job. The legislatures of Florida and Texas passed resolutions against teaching evolution rather than laws. Between 1921 and 1929 bills of this kind were introduced in twenty states.

Tennessee repealed its law in 1967. Arkansas never prosecuted anybody under its law in the forty years it stood, and a Little Rock biology teacher had it struck down in 1968.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the year Oregon voted to close the Catholic schools" -->
On 7 November 1922 the voters of Oregon passed a law ordering every child aged eight to sixteen into a public school. The Ku Klux Klan had put it on the ballot through the Scottish Rite Masons, and the schools it was meant to close were the Catholic ones. It passed 115,506 to 103,685. The penalty fell on parents: a fine of $5 to $100, or two to thirty days in the county jail, for each day a child stayed away.

The Sisters of the Holy Names of Jesus and Mary had run schools in Oregon since 1880, teaching Catholic religion alongside the ordinary subjects. They sued, and so did a boys' military academy that was not religious at all. On 1 June 1925, in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, the Supreme Court struck the law down before it took effect. The National Catholic Welfare Conference paid for the appeal. Every church school in the country still stands on that ruling. `education` tells it as school law and `government-politics` as constitutional law; here it is a state where a majority voted to shut one religion's schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="aimee-semple-mcpherson" name="Aimee Semple McPherson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aimee Semple McPherson
> **Who:** The Pentecostal preacher who built the Angelus Temple in Los Angeles and preached to the country by radio.
> **When and where:** Born 1890 near Ingersoll, in Canada; Los Angeles from 1918; died 1944.

Her mother belonged to the Salvation Army and her father led a Methodist choir. As a small girl she sang and prayed in Salvation Army services. She was the only Salvationist in her school, and the other children taunted her for it. In high school her teachers taught evolution and she began to doubt what she had been raised on. She started going to a Pentecostal church instead.

She married a Pentecostal minister, Robert Semple, and went with him as a missionary to China, where he died. Her first child was born a month later. She married again, to Harold McPherson, and lived in Providence, Rhode Island. In 1916 she left and went preaching across the country.

In 1918 she settled in Los Angeles. By 1923 her preaching had paid for the Angelus Temple on Glendale Boulevard, a church with an auditorium that seats 5,300 people. She put radio towers on the roof and preached to people at home. She used theatre to hold a crowd. Once she rode a motorcycle onto the stage dressed as a policewoman and preached on the subject "Stop! You are breaking God's law!" In 1926 she opened a Bible college; by 1944 it had trained more than three thousand ministers and missionaries.

Her congregation kept a store of food at the Temple, brought in an item at a time. During the Great Depression the Temple gave out more than a million and a half meals.

She held separate meetings for Black people in Florida, because, as she put it, "the dear colored people did not feel free to attend the white meetings." In Oakland, California, she preached to the Ku Klux Klan.

On 18 May 1926 she walked into the sea at Ocean Park, near Santa Monica, and did not come out. Police, lifeguards, lifeboats, motorboats and an aeroplane searched for her, and within two days her friends had given up hope she was alive. Five weeks later, on 23 June, she came out of the desert near Douglas, Arizona, and was carried into a hospital. She said two men and a woman had taken her at the beach and held her in Mexico for half a million dollars in ransom. Posses searched Sonora for the shack she described and found nothing. California charged her over the story, and on 10 January 1927 the district attorney dropped the case. Nobody ever established what happened in those five weeks.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aimee-semple-mcpherson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the government was still punishing Native worship" -->
The 1883 rules that made the Sun Dance an offence, and took away a family's food as the penalty, were still being enforced in the 1920s. Agents also found other ways to punish people for worshipping.

At the Kiowa Agency in Oklahoma, Superintendent C. V. Stinchecum held back people's own trust money until they signed a paper promising not to dance. In the autumn of 1916 he withheld payments from about ninety people, and in January 1917 he sent Washington a list of 109 names. Humming Bird, Kiowa Bill, Henry Poolaw, George Mopope, White Horse, Silver Horn, Max Frizzlehead and Kiowa Jim Tongkeamha were among them. In 1921, after a Fourth of July dance where eleven horses were given away, he cited nine Kiowa men for "the reckless giving away of property" and held their money too. By November only three had signed.

Washington backed the agents up. On 26 April 1921 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Charles Burke, sent out Circular 1665, which let any superintendent shut down a dance he judged immoral. Burke followed it with a supplement in February 1923 and a message to all Indians the same month.

It changed in 1933, when John Collier became Commissioner. Collier sent every agency a circular titled "Indian Religious Freedom and Indian Culture." It said that "no interference with Indian religious life or ceremonial expression will hereafter be tolerated." That was the first time the federal government had ever written down that Native religion was to be protected. It did not settle it. The 1883 rules stayed legally enforceable until 1978, and a 1940 wildlife law made holding an eagle feather a federal crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-black-elk" name="Nicholas Black Elk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Black Elk
> **Who:** An Oglala Lakota holy man who was also a Catholic catechist, a teacher of the faith.
> **When and where:** Born December 1863 on the Little Powder River; Pine Ridge, South Dakota; died August 1950.

In his ninth year he had a vision. He met a council of six grandfathers who stood for the four directions and for the earth and the sky, and they gave him the power to heal and a pipe of red pipestone.

He was twelve at the Battle of the Little Bighorn on 25 June 1876. He took his first scalp there, and he gathered guns, ammunition and clothing from dead and dying soldiers.

In 1886 he joined Buffalo Bill's Wild West and performed at Madison Square Garden. The next year the show sailed to England for Queen Victoria's Golden Jubilee. He got separated from it, travelled around Europe on his own, found Buffalo Bill again and came home.

He was back at Pine Ridge in 1889 and danced the Ghost Dance. In December 1890 he heard cannon and rifle fire from Wounded Knee, rode towards it, helped the survivors and wept for the dead.

His wife Katie War Bonnet died in 1903. In 1904 he was baptised a Catholic and took the name Nicholas. For the rest of his life he worked as a catechist, teaching the Catholic faith and preparing people for baptism, and he remained a holy man among the Lakota. He did not treat the two as a choice.

In 1930 the poet John Neihardt interviewed him, and the book *Black Elk Speaks* came out in 1932. He died in August 1950. Near the end he told his daughter Lucy, "The only thing I really believe is the pipe religion." In 2017 the Catholic diocese of Rapid City asked Rome to make him a saint.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For fifty years about seven Americans in ten told pollsters they belonged to a church, a synagogue or a mosque, and the figure barely moved. Congress put God into the Pledge of Allegiance and made "In God we trust" the national motto. Black congregations in the South housed the civil rights movement, and men bombed their buildings and killed their members for it. Families went to court over what a public school day should contain, and the rulings went both ways. In 1978 Congress passed a law saying that Native Americans were allowed to practise their own religions, which had not been safely true for ninety-five years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="God in the country's official words" -->
On 14 June 1954 Congress amended the Pledge of Allegiance to the flag. Two words went in, and the pledge became "one Nation under God, indivisible, with liberty and justice for all." On 30 July 1956 Congress passed another joint resolution, three lines long: "That the national motto of the United States is hereby declared to be 'In God we trust.'" The words had been on coins since 1864, which `money` tells; 1956 is the year they became the country's official motto.

The polling company Gallup has asked Americans since 1937 whether they belong to a church, synagogue or mosque. In 1937, 73 per cent said they did. The number stayed near 70 per cent for the next sixty years and was still 70 per cent in 1999. Gallup is a polling company and not a government count, and it asks people what they say they are.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Billy Graham and the crusades" -->
Billy Graham was born in Charlotte, North Carolina in 1918 on a dairy farm, was ordained by the Southern Baptists in 1939, and worked for Youth for Christ from 1944 to 1948 speaking at rallies. In 1949 he held a campaign in Los Angeles that was booked for three weeks and ran for seven. After that he built an organisation around the work: the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association, a film company, a weekly radio programme called *Hour of Decision*, and a daily newspaper column. His New York crusade in 1957 ran every night for sixteen weeks in Madison Square Garden.

On 18 July 1957 the man who gave the opening prayer at that crusade was Martin Luther King Jr. Graham had telephoned him, and the evangelist Leighton Ford sent the invitation by telegram on 5 July. King asked in the prayer for help "for a warless world and for a brotherhood that transcends race or color," and then led eighteen thousand people in the Lord's Prayer. Six weeks later King wrote to Graham: "You have courageously brought the Christian gospel to bear on the question of race." The next summer he wrote again to object, because Graham had shared a platform with the governor of Texas, and King called that an "endorsement of racial segregation and discrimination."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a church that was bombed three times" -->
In June 1956 an Alabama court banned the NAACP from operating anywhere in the state. Ministers in Birmingham answered by founding something a court could not ban. About a thousand people met at Sardis Baptist Church on 5 June 1956 and started the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights. Fred Shuttlesworth, who had been pastor of Bethel Baptist Church since March 1953, said officials could outlaw an organisation but not a movement, and that putting "Christian" in the name gave it a foundation and set it apart from the groups being called Communist. Bethel Baptist Church was the movement's headquarters from 1956 to 1961. Its meetings were on Monday nights, and the National Park Service describes them as "emotional testimony, soul-lifting music, impassioned preaching, and fundraising." By 1958 those weekly meetings were also being held in 55 other working-class churches around the city.

The church was bombed three times. On Christmas night 1956 twelve sticks of dynamite went off in the gap between the church and the parsonage, destroying the parsonage. Shuttlesworth was inside talking to a deacon, his family was watching television, and he came out with a head injury. The bomb was set the night before the movement had said it would ride the segregated buses. Two hundred and fifty people rode them the next day and twenty-two were arrested. A second bomb came on 29 June 1958 and a third in 1962. The 1958 bombing was not solved until 1980, when J. B. Stoner was convicted of it.

After the first bomb the congregation began guarding its own church. `rights-movements` tells the Birmingham campaign, the children's march and the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church.

Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act on 11 August 1978. Its findings say what the country had been doing. "The lack of a clear, comprehensive, and consistent Federal policy has often resulted in the abridgment of religious freedom for traditional American Indians." Such laws "often deny American Indians access to sacred sites required in their religions, including cemeteries," and "at times prohibit the use and possession of sacred objects necessary to the exercise of religious rites and ceremonies." And: "traditional American Indian ceremonies have been intruded upon, interfered with, and in a few instances banned." In the same resolution the members of Congress declared it the policy of the United States to protect "their inherent right of freedom to believe, express, and exercise the traditional religions of the American Indian, Eskimo, Aleut, and Native Hawaiians." That is ninety-five years after the 1883 rules that made the Sun Dance a punishable offence, which this chapter tells in 1850 to 1900.

It was a statement of policy, and it did not decide cases. In 1990, in *Employment Division v. Smith*, the Supreme Court held that Oregon could refuse unemployment pay to two men fired for taking peyote at a ceremony of their Native American Church. Congress answered on 6 October 1994 with an amendment to the 1978 Act. The members wrote into it that 28 states protected the ceremony and 22 did not, and they made the use of peyote by an Indian for traditional ceremonial purposes lawful everywhere in the country. `native-nations` tells what the long ban did to the nations themselves.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="whose Bible was it" -->
For most of American history a public school day opened with a prayer or a Bible reading. In 1962 the Supreme Court stopped official school prayer, in *Engel v. Vitale*. In 1963 it stopped official Bible reading, in *Abington School District v. Schempp*. `education` tells what changed in the school day and `government-politics` tells the rulings as law. The question underneath both was whose religion the school day belonged to.

The parents who brought the 1962 case were not one faith. Ten children's parents sued. The New York court had already found that the school prayer did not fit the beliefs of Jews, Unitarians or the Ethical Culture society, and one of the parents was an agnostic.

The family who brought the 1963 case went to church every week. Edward and Sidney Schempp and their children Roger, Donna and Ellory belonged to the Unitarian church in Germantown, Philadelphia. Pennsylvania law made every school read ten Bible verses each morning, and a child could be excused if a parent wrote in. Edward Schempp would not ask. He testified that his children would be called "odd balls" by teachers and classmates every day of the school year; that other children lumped every religious difference together as atheism, and in 1962 atheism meant communist and un-American; and that a child excused from the reading had to stand in the corridor outside the homeroom, which looked like a punishment for bad behaviour.

Two scholars testified about the book itself. Solomon Grayzel explained that the Jewish scriptures have no New Testament, that from a Jewish point of view calling Jesus the son of God is close to blasphemy, and that reading those passages to children without explanation had done them harm. Luther Weigle, testifying for the school district, said the Bible was non-sectarian, meaning it belonged to no one denomination. Asked again, he said he meant non-sectarian among Christians.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the day after, and the years after" -->
A congressman filed a constitutional amendment to undo the prayer ruling the day after it came down, and others were being drafted. Representative Taylor of North Carolina was first; the newspapers noted that Billy Graham was one of his constituents. Senator Beall of Maryland had one ready for the Senate. Representative Chelf of Kentucky said that if he could not get an amendment he would try to stop Supreme Court justices serving for life. None of the amendments passed.

Schools worked out other things to do. Some had already been settled. In 1948, in a case brought by a mother named Vashti McCollum, the Court stopped religious teachers coming into public school buildings in Champaign, Illinois, to teach religion for half an hour a week. In 1952 it allowed the opposite arrangement in New York City, where children left the building and went to a church or synagogue for the lesson. The line was physical: religion class inside the school was out, children released to go elsewhere was in.

After 1963 came the moment of silence. Alabama passed a law in 1978 for one minute of silence for meditation. In 1981 it added the words "or voluntary prayer," and in 1982 it let teachers lead willing pupils in a prayer. In 1985 the Court struck down the 1981 law because its purpose was to promote religion, and left the plain silence alone.

In 1992 the Court stopped a school inviting clergy to pray at graduation. Rabbi Leslie Gutterman of Temple Beth El had prayed at Deborah Weisman's middle school graduation in Providence, Rhode Island. The principal had handed him a pamphlet telling him to keep it non-sectarian, and the Court said that made the prayer the school's.

One thing the 1963 ruling did not touch, and said so plainly: teaching *about* religion. Justice Clark wrote that a person's education is not complete without studying the history of religion, and that the Bible is worth studying for its literature and its history, as long as it is taught as a subject and not as a service.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="objecting to the curriculum" -->
Arkansas had made it a crime to teach that people descended from animals, and the law was still there in 1965. That autumn the Little Rock schools handed their biology teachers a new textbook with a chapter on Darwin's theory in it. Susan Epperson, who taught tenth-grade biology at Central High School, sued instead of choosing between the book and the law, and a parent named H. H. Blanchard joined her. On 12 November 1968, in *Epperson v. Arkansas*, the Supreme Court struck the law down and said what it had been for: "fundamentalist sectarian conviction was and is the law's reason for existence." The same ruling said the First Amendment requires neutrality "between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." No justice voted to keep the law, and they did not agree on the reasoning. Justice Black wrote separately that he could not be sure what the voters of 1928 had been thinking.

Louisiana's legislators tried another way. Their law said a school that taught evolution had to teach creation science as well, and a school that taught creation science had to teach evolution as well. The people who sued to stop it were Louisiana parents, Louisiana teachers and religious leaders. Religious organisations filed briefs on both sides: the Catholic League for Religious and Civil Rights and the Christian Legal Society asked the Court to keep the law, the New York Committee for Public Education and Religious Liberty asked it to strike the law down, and separate briefs came from the Rabbinical Alliance of America and from a group headed by the Reverend Bill McLean. On 19 June 1987, by seven votes to two, the Court struck it down in *Edwards v. Aguillard*, holding that "the preeminent purpose of the Louisiana Legislature was clearly to advance the religious viewpoint that a supernatural being created humankind."

The largest fight over books was not in a court. In April 1974 the school board of Kanawha County, West Virginia, which had 46,000 pupils in 124 schools, voted to adopt 325 language-arts textbooks. A board member named Alice Moore, the wife of a minister, said some of them were against religion and against the country. Twenty-seven ministers condemned the books and petitions came in with 12,000 signatures, and in June the board voted three to two to buy most of them anyway. Another minister, Jim Lewis of Charleston, was the books' loudest defender, so there were clergy on both sides in one county. When school opened on 3 September attendance dropped by a fifth, 3,500 coal miners walked out in sympathy with the boycott, and people fired guns, threw firebombs and set off dynamite at schools. Eleven people were arrested and the schools closed for four days from 12 September. The protests ran on into 1977 and drew in the John Birch Society and the Ku Klux Klan, and the Reverend Marvin Horan was tried for plotting to bomb schools. No source found for this book gives a count of the injured, so this book does not give one.

Some families objected to the school itself. In *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, decided 15 May 1972, Old Order Amish and Conservative Amish Mennonite fathers were convicted of keeping their children out of school after the eighth grade, and the Supreme Court ruled that Wisconsin could not make them send them. The belief underneath it was one line from Paul's letter to the Romans, "be not conformed to this world," which the Amish take as a rule for daily life. They did not object to the first eight grades, because a child needs to read the Bible, farm and deal with people outside the community. They objected to the high school as the world. `education` tells what changed in the school day and `government-politics` tells all of these rulings as law. Religious families were also one of the two roots of modern homeschooling, with `home-family` leading on the household side.

### Billy Graham
> **Who:** A Southern Baptist preacher who filled stadiums for fifty years and became the best-known Christian evangelist in the country · **When and where:** Born Charlotte, North Carolina, 7 November 1918; died Montreat, North Carolina, 21 February 2018

He grew up on a dairy farm outside Charlotte and decided in 1934, after hearing a travelling preacher there, to give his life to God. He was ordained by the Southern Baptists in 1939, took a theology degree at the Florida Bible Institute in 1940 and a degree at Wheaton College in Illinois in 1943, and married Ruth Bell that August.

From 1944 to 1948 he travelled the country speaking at rallies for Youth for Christ. Then, in 1949, he preached a campaign in Los Angeles that had been booked for three weeks and ran for seven. He built an organisation around the work and kept building it: the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association, a film company, a radio programme, a newspaper column. In 1954 he preached in London for twelve weeks. In 1957 he preached in Madison Square Garden in New York every night for sixteen weeks.

On 18 July 1957 he had Martin Luther King Jr. give the opening prayer at that crusade. King asked for "a warless world and for a brotherhood that transcends race or color" and led eighteen thousand people in the Lord's Prayer. On 31 August King wrote to him, "You have courageously brought the Christian gospel to bear on the question of race." The following July King wrote again to object that Graham had appeared on a platform with the governor of Texas, which King called an "endorsement of racial segregation and discrimination." Graham held integrated crusades in Birmingham, Alabama at Easter 1964.

In 1996 Congress voted Billy and Ruth Graham a gold medal, and it was handed to them in the Capitol Rotunda on 2 May. The law's findings claim his crusades had reached a hundred million people in person and two billion on television. He stepped back from his association in 2000, preached a last crusade in Queens, New York in 2005, and died at ninety-nine. On 28 February 2018 his body lay in honour in the Rotunda of the Capitol, only the fourth private citizen to be given that.

### The Revis family of Bethel Baptist Church
> **Who:** A church deacon and his two daughters, whose front porch became the guard post for a congregation that kept being bombed · **When and where:** Collegeville, Birmingham, Alabama, 1956 to the early 1960s

James Revis was a deacon of Bethel Baptist Church and sat on the executive committee of the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights. His house stood across the street from the church.

After the bomb of Christmas night 1956 destroyed the pastor's house, members of the church and the movement volunteered to watch the buildings. In 1957 the Revis family closed in their own front porch to make a room for the guards to sit in. His daughter Robbie Revis Smith described it: "with our house sitting on the corner, it gave a very good view several ways that they could see because they had the glass out there and they put a little gas heater out there so they wouldn't be too cold sitting up. They did this year round." Her sister Laverne Revis Martin said their parents made the room "just so the guards would be able to watch."

The volunteers called themselves the Civil Rights Guards. They worked in two shifts, six in the evening to midnight and midnight to six in the morning, and they were armed. Women of the movement took a weekly shift too, among them a florist named Desta Brooks and a schoolteacher named Minnie Eaton.

At about half past one in the morning on Sunday 29 June 1958, Laverne was coming home from work when she saw a bomb smoking beside the church. She told the guards. They carried it into the street, where it went off, blew a hole in the road and broke the church windows without bringing the building down.

Robbie Revis Smith, on what the porch meant to the neighbourhood: "So it was kind of a good feeling coming home because you never came home without a person out there watching you, so you didn't have to worry about that."

### Jonas and Frieda Yoder
> **Who:** An Old Order Amish father who would not send his daughter to high school, and the daughter, who said in court that the decision was her own religion too · **When and where:** Green County, Wisconsin, 1968 to 1972

Wisconsin required every child to be in school until sixteen. Frieda Yoder was fifteen and had finished the eighth grade of the public school, and her father Jonas did not send her on to the high school.

The reason was one line of scripture. Amish belief takes Paul's instruction in his letter to the Romans, "be not conformed to this world," as a rule for ordinary life, and treats the high school as the world. The Amish did not object to the first eight grades. A child needed to read the Bible, farm, and deal with people outside the community.

Jonas Yoder was charged, tried and convicted along with Wallace Miller and Adin Yutzy in the Green County Court. The Supreme Court decided for the three fathers on 15 May 1972.

Frieda was the only child in the case who was called to give evidence. She testified that her own religious beliefs were why she had stopped going to school after the eighth grade. Justice William O. Douglas agreed with the ruling as it applied to her father and disagreed as it applied to the other two men, because nobody had asked Barbara Miller or Vernon Yutzy what they believed. He wrote that the Court had never squarely faced the question of whose religion decides a child's schooling, the parents' or the child's.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Fewer Americans belong to a congregation than at any time since anyone started counting, and the group that has grown fastest is people who say they have no religion at all. The religions practised here are more varied than they have ever been. Two arguments this chapter has followed since the 1600s were still running in these years: what a public school day may contain, and whether a person is safe in the building where they worship. A third became public knowledge. Catholic bishops had known for decades that priests were sexually abusing children, and had moved those priests to new parishes instead of reporting them to the police. Every number in this section carries the year it describes and the year it was published, because a number is no use unless you know which year it belongs to. The newest figures here describe 2022 to 2025 and were published between 2023 and 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="how many people say they have no religion" -->
The Pew Research Center is a polling organisation. Between 17 July 2023 and 4 March 2024 it asked 36,908 American adults about their religion, and published the results on 26 February 2025. Sixty-two per cent said they were Christian, against 71 per cent in a comparable study in 2014 and 78 per cent in 2007. Twenty-nine per cent said they had no religion, against 23 per cent in 2014 and 16 per cent in 2007. Those are the people usually called the "nones," which covers atheists, agnostics and people who answer "nothing in particular." Of the rest, 1.7 per cent were Jewish, 1.2 per cent Muslim, 1.1 per cent Buddhist and 0.9 per cent Hindu. Pew's own headline was that the fall in the Christian share had slowed and might have stopped, because it had been roughly steady for four or five years.

Gallup, another polling company, has asked since 1937 whether people belong to a church, synagogue or mosque. Seventy per cent said yes in 1999. Fifty per cent said yes in 2018. Forty-seven per cent said yes in 2020, the first time the answer had been under half. Gallup published that in March 2021. Broken down by age group over 2018 to 2020, 66 per cent of people born before 1946 belonged to a congregation and 36 per cent of people born between 1981 and 1996 did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="faith and school now" -->
Maine pays the fees for children who live in towns with no secondary school of their own, and since 1981 it had paid only where the school was nonsectarian, meaning it belonged to no religion. David and Amy Carson of Glenburn sent their daughter to Bangor Christian Schools, founded in 1970 by Bangor Baptist Church, and paid the fees themselves. Troy and Angela Nelson of Palermo sent their son to Temple Academy and could not afford to send their daughter there too. In *Carson v. Makin*, decided 21 June 2022 by six votes to three, the Supreme Court held that Maine could not refuse to pay because a school was religious. The two schools' own descriptions of what they do are in the record. Bangor Christian's stated objectives include "lead[ing] each unsaved student to trust Christ as his/her personal savior," and the school says there is no way to separate its religious teaching from its academic teaching. Temple Academy says the Bible is used in every subject it teaches, including mathematics. `government-politics` tells the four rulings that moved public money towards religious schools as law, and `education` tells vouchers and savings accounts as school choice.

Joseph Kennedy coached football at Bremerton High School in Washington State from 2008. After each game, once the players and coaches had shaken hands, he knelt at the halfway line and prayed for about thirty seconds. Players began asking whether they could join him and he told them, "This is a free country. You can do what you want." Most of the team joined him after some games. Nobody complained for more than seven years. In September 2015 the school district told him to stop, and after he prayed following the games of 16, 23 and 26 October it suspended him on full pay and barred him from the football programme. In *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District*, decided 27 June 2022, the Supreme Court ruled for him by six votes to three. The nine justices read the same record and did not describe the same events. Justice Neil Gorsuch, for the majority, wrote that he had "knelt at midfield after games to offer a quiet personal prayer." Justice Sonia Sotomayor, dissenting, wrote that "to the degree the Court portrays petitioner Joseph Kennedy's prayers as private and quiet, it misconstrues the facts," and that he had led student athletes in prayer at the same place for years.

In 1980, in *Stone v. Graham*, the Supreme Court struck down a Kentucky law requiring the Ten Commandments to be posted in every public classroom. In 2024 Louisiana's legislators passed a law requiring the same thing, on a poster or framed document at least eleven inches by fourteen, with the text as the central focus and printed large, and the law sets out the exact wording that must be used. Parents sued, and the case is named for one of them, the Reverend Darcy Roake. The objections in the record are religious objections. A Jewish parent swore that the law "misappropriates a Jewish text, ripping it from its Jewish context," alters its meaning, and then requires the altered text to be shown to non-Jews, "in violation of core Jewish tenets that oppose proselytizing." Another said Louisiana's version leaves out the deliverance of the Jews from slavery in Egypt, which is a key piece of the Jewish story. A Presbyterian minister who is also a parent objected to the state putting its stamp of approval on one particular version. A federal judge stopped the law, a three-judge appeals panel agreed on 20 June 2025, and then on 20 February 2026 the full Fifth Circuit lifted the order. That court said plainly that it was not deciding whether the law is constitutional, only that the question was not ready to be decided, and three judges dissented. As of September 2026 no court has answered it. Texas has a similar law and a similar case, in which a rabbi, Mara Nathan, is one of the people suing. This is the same argument the Schempp family made in 1963, about whose version of a holy book a school hands to children.

The second school system this chapter describes in 1850 to 1900 was staffed by members of religious orders working for very little, and there are far fewer of them now. The Center for Applied Research in the Apostolate at Georgetown University, which draws its figures from the Catholic Church's own directory, counted 178,740 religious sisters in the United States in 1965 and 33,135 as of 1 January 2025. Catholic elementary schools went from 9,366 to 4,606 over the same sixty years, and Catholic secondary schools from 1,986 to 1,178. `education` tells the enrolment figures.

Some families teach their children at home for religious reasons, and the federal government asks them why. In the 2022–23 school year, 53 per cent of parents homeschooling a child aged 5 to 17 said a desire to provide religious instruction was an important reason and 11 per cent said it was the most important one. More of them named a concern about the environment of other schools, at 83 per cent important and 28 per cent most important. Those figures come from the National Center for Education Statistics and were published in September 2024. `education` tells how many children are homeschooled, and `home-family` tells homeschooling as household life.

Sexual abuse means an adult using a child's body for sex. In 2002 the Catholic bishops of the United States asked John Jay College of Criminal Justice to find out how much of it their own priests had done, and the college published the answer in February 2004, from surveys covering 98 per cent of the country's diocesan priests. It found allegations against 4,392 priests for the years 1950 to 2002 that had not been withdrawn and were not known to be false, about four per cent of all the priests who had served in those years, and 10,667 people who said they had been abused. Half of them were between eleven and fourteen when it happened. Sixteen per cent were between eight and ten, and nearly six per cent were under seven. Eighty-one per cent were boys. The commonest acts recorded were touching over and under the child's clothes, oral sex performed by the priest, and rape or attempted rape, which was alleged in 22.4 per cent of cases. Most of the priests accused had done more than one of these things to the same child. It happened most often in the priest's own house or the parish house, and next most often inside the church.

Only 384 of those 4,392 priests were ever criminally charged. Fifteen per cent were reported to the police by a victim, and the report says a much smaller number were reported by their own diocese. Before 1993 only a third of the cases were known to church officials at all.

In August 2018 a grand jury in Pennsylvania published what it had found in half a million pages of the internal documents of six Catholic dioceses. It counted credible allegations against more than three hundred priests and more than a thousand children it could identify from the church's own records, and said it believed the real number was in the thousands. Its own summary: "Most of the victims were boys; but there were girls too. Some were teens; many were pre-pubescent." Some were raped. "But all of them were brushed aside, in every part of the state, by church leaders who preferred to protect the abusers and their institution above all." The grand jury then set out what it called a playbook for concealing the truth, and its first item was language. Never write "rape" in a diocesan document; write "inappropriate contact" or "boundary issues." Do not let trained investigators ask the questions. When a priest has to be removed, tell his parish he is on sick leave, or say nothing. Keep paying his housing. If people find out, move him somewhere nobody knows. "Finally and above all, don't tell the police." Because of that concealment, the grand jury wrote, almost every case it found was too old to prosecute.

The FBI counts crimes it classes as motivated by hatred. The United Methodist Church argued for decades about gay members and gay ministers, and in these years it split. A special conference in 2019 wrote a rule letting a congregation leave the denomination and keep its buildings for reasons of conscience over that argument. The rule ran out on 31 December 2023. By then more than 7,600 congregations in the United States had used it, about a quarter of the denomination's American churches. On 1 May 2024, at the General Conference in Charlotte, North Carolina, the delegates who remained voted 692 to 51 to remove the ban on ordaining ministers described in their rule book since 1984 as "self-avowed practicing homosexuals," and removed the ban on holding same-sex weddings as well.

When buildings closed in 2020, services went onto screens, and many stayed there. Pew asked 11,377 adults about it between 16 and 27 November 2022 and published the answers in June 2023. Sixteen per cent were going to services in person only, ten per cent were watching on a screen only, and seventeen per cent were doing both, which comes to 43 per cent doing one or the other at least monthly and 57 per cent doing neither. Of the people doing both, 76 per cent said they would rather be in the room. The commonest reason given for watching on a screen was that it was convenient.

### Joseph Kennedy
> **Who:** A high-school football coach who lost his job over a prayer at the halfway line, and won his case in the Supreme Court · **When and where:** Bremerton, Washington, 2008 to 2022

He had spent nearly twenty years in the Marine Corps before he began coaching football at Bremerton High School in 2008. At the end of each game, once the players and coaches had shaken hands, he took a knee at the 50-yard line and prayed for about thirty seconds, giving thanks for what the players had done and for being part of their lives.

At first he prayed alone. Then players asked whether they could join him, and he said, "This is a free country. You can do what you want." After some games most of the team knelt with him, and sometimes players from the other team were asked along. He started adding a short talk to the prayer when other people were there. Nobody complained for more than seven years.

The district superintendent found out in September 2015, when an employee of another school praised the practice to Bremerton's principal. On 17 September the district wrote to him. On 16 October it told him not to do anything that a reasonable onlooker could take as the school endorsing prayer while he was on duty. He prayed after the games of 16, 23 and 26 October. On the 16th, most of his players were singing the school fight song; players from the other team and people from the crowd came and knelt with him before he finished. On the 23rd nobody joined him. On the 26th other adults gathered round him. After that game the district put him on paid leave and barred him from the football programme.

On 27 June 2022 the Supreme Court ruled for him by six votes to three. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that the First Amendment protects a person's own religious observance from being punished by the government.

The dissent describes a different scene. Justice Sonia Sotomayor wrote that the case was about "whether a public school must permit a school official to kneel, bow his head, and say a prayer at the center of a school event," and that the majority "misconstrues the facts" in calling the prayers private and quiet, because the record showed him inviting others and leading student athletes in prayer in the same place for years.

### Harpreet Singh Saini
> **Who:** An eighteen-year-old whose mother was shot dead at morning prayers, and who asked the United States Senate to start counting people like her · **When and where:** Oak Creek, Wisconsin, and Washington, D.C., August and September 2012

His parents brought him and his brother Kamaljit from India to the United States in 2004, when he was ten, so that the boys could get an education.

On Sunday 5 August 2012 a white supremacist carrying a loaded gun walked into their gurdwara at Oak Creek. He killed Harpreet's mother, Paramjit Kaur, while she sat for morning prayers, and five men, all of whom wore turbans.

Two days later the brothers ate the last rotis their mother had made. She had made them from scratch the night before she died. Harpreet said that with the last bite came the understanding that it was the last food made by her hands they would ever eat.

He had just started his first day of college, and she was not there to see him off.

On 19 September 2012, forty-five days after she was killed, he gave evidence to a subcommittee of the United States Senate. He said: "I want to tell the gunman who took her from me: You may have been full of hate, but my mother was full of love."

He told the senators what his mother's faith required of her. "Like all Sikhs, she was bound to live in Chardi Kala — a state of high spirits and optimism. She was also taught as a Sikh to neither have fear of anyone nor strike fear in anyone."

Then he asked them for one thing. "I came here today to ask the government to give my mother the dignity of being a statistic. The FBI does not track hate crimes against Sikhs. My mother and those shot that day will not even count on a federal form. We cannot solve a problem we refuse to recognize."
