## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first people to explore this land walked or paddled into it from Asia during the last ice age. Human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, are at least 21,000 years old and probably about 23,000. Scientists argued over that date, and a second study in 2023 used two other methods and got the same answer. By 1500 hundreds of Native nations lived across the continent. Estimates of how many people lived north of Mexico run from about 1 million to about 18 million, and nobody has settled the number.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade routes and Pacific voyaging" -->
- Native traders moved Lake Superior copper, Gulf of Mexico shells and western obsidian hundreds of miles. Tools from Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, turn up more than 400 miles away. The trade itself belongs to `economy` and `marketplace`.
- Polynesian sailors found and settled the Hawaiian Islands between about AD 1000 and 1200 (the archaeologist Patrick Kirch's estimate). Some scientists put the first landing closer to 1200 to 1290.
- Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations themselves, lead) · `america-world` (Hawaii's later story).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="leif-erikson" name="Leif Erikson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Leif Erikson
> **Who:** a Norse sailor from Greenland, son of Erik the Red, who led one of the first European voyages to North America. · **When and where:** about 1000 to 1021, Newfoundland, Canada.
- Two Icelandic sagas tell of Leif reaching a land he called Vinland. The sagas describe trade with the people the Norse called Skraelings, and fights with them that led the Norse to leave.
- In 1960 a local man, George Decker, led Helge Ingstad to old mounds at L'Anse aux Meadows. Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad dug there from 1961 to 1968 and found a Norse camp.
- In 2021 scientists dated wood cut with metal blades at the camp to the year 1021. It is the only exact date for Europeans in the Americas before 1492.
- Historians disagree on how long the Norse stayed. Estimates run from a few years to about twenty. Five earlier Native groups had lived at the same spot, the oldest about 6,000 years before.
- The camp proves the Norse reached Newfoundland. It does not prove this camp was Leif's own.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s European sailors mapped the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, and a few Spanish armies marched inland. Every place they reached was the home of a Native nation. The explorers took people captive, seized food and fought the towns that refused them. By 1600 the only lasting European town in what is now the United States was St. Augustine, Florida.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sailing the coasts, 1492 to 1535" -->
- Columbus reached the island of Guanahani in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492 and never set foot in what is now the United States. The name "America" comes from the Italian navigator Amerigo Vespucci. A German mapmaker, Martin Waldseemuller, put it on a world map in 1507.
- John Cabot, an Italian sailing for England's King Henry VII, landed once on the coast of Newfoundland or nearby on 24 June 1497. His ship, the *Matthew*, carried 18 to 20 men. Historians still argue about where he landed.
- Juan Ponce de Leon led the first recorded European expedition to Florida in 1513. His 65 people included Juan Garrido, a free African man. The Calusa of southwest Florida drove his ships off, and his men captured eight Calusa. When he came back to build a colony in 1521, Calusa warriors wounded him, and he died of the wound in Cuba.
- Giovanni da Verrazzano, sailing for France in 1524, mapped the coast from the Carolinas to Maine. About 30 Lenape canoes met his ship in New York Bay. Farther south his men took an eight-year-old boy from an old woman to carry back to France. Verrazzano wrote this down himself in his letter to the French king.
- Jacques Cartier sailed the St. Lawrence River in present-day Canada from 1534 to 1542. He kidnapped the Iroquoian leader Donnacona, his two sons and seven others. None of them came home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Armies inland: de Soto and Coronado, 1539 to 1542" -->
- Hernando de Soto landed in Florida in May 1539 with 620 men and 220 horses and marched through the Southeast for three years. His men ate the stored corn of the Apalachee and other towns and seized dozens of men, women and children to carry their loads. He held the Coosa leader hostage to pass through Coosa land.
- At Mabila, in present-day Alabama, on 18 October 1540, de Soto's men fought for about nine hours and burned the town with people inside. The National Park Service gives about 2,000 Mabila dead and over 200 Spanish. Other accounts give about 20 Spanish dead. One of the expedition's own writers recorded: "We killed them all either with fire or the sword."
- De Soto died of fever beside the Mississippi River on 21 May 1542. His men sank his body in the river so the nations they had fought would not learn he could die.
- Francisco Vazquez de Coronado left Mexico in February 1540 with about 400 European soldiers and 1,300 to 2,000 Native allies from Mexico, looking for cities of gold. When the Zuni town of Hawikuh refused to let them in, his men attacked it. Hopi guides led Garcia Lopez de Cardenas and his men to the South Rim of the Grand Canyon, and they became the first Europeans to see it.
- Along the Rio Grande in the winter of 1540 to 1541, Coronado's men took the Tiguex pueblo of Coofor and forced its people out. The fighting began after a Spanish soldier assaulted a Tiguex woman. Hundreds of Tiguex were killed, dozens of captives were executed at Arenal, and women and children were made slaves.
- In 1541 a Native guide the Spanish called "the Turk", probably Wichita or Pawnee, led the army onto the Plains to a place called Quivira in present-day Kansas. Coronado found grass houses and no gold. He had the Turk garroted, which means strangled with a tightened cord or collar.
- Spanish officials later charged Coronado and Cardenas with crimes against Native people on the march. A court of Coronado's friends cleared him. A court in Spain convicted Cardenas.
- Shared with: `war` (Mabila and the Tiguex War, lead on the fighting) · `native-nations` (what the entradas did to the nations) · `food-farming` (de Soto's pigs and the seized corn).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, 1565" -->
Pedro Menendez de Aviles and about 800 colonists settled at the Timucua town of Seloy in September 1565 and used Seloy's council house as their first fort. Within months the Timucua were attacking the Spaniards to drive them away. In 1566 Timucua warriors shot flaming arrows at the fort, and the Spaniards moved across the bay to Anastasia Island. The town's growth belongs to `city-building`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="christopher-columbus" name="Christopher Columbus" movie="1492: Conquest of Paradise (1992)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Christopher Columbus
> **Who:** an Italian sea captain from Genoa who sailed west for Spain looking for a sea route to Asia. · **When and where:** 1492 to 1504, the Bahamas, the Caribbean islands, and the coasts of Central and South America.
> **Movie:** 1492: Conquest of Paradise (1992)
- Four voyages, 1492 to 1504. He landed first on Guanahani, home of the Lucayan Taino, and named it San Salvador.
- On his first voyage he took Taino people captive and brought them to Spain to show the king and queen.
- As governor of Hispaniola his soldiers made war on the Taino until they agreed to pay a tribute of gold. Every Taino aged 14 and older had to bring in a small measure of gold dust every six months. The priest Bartolome de las Casas wrote that people who failed had their hands cut off. Historians dispute how often that was done.
- In February 1495 Columbus rounded up about 1,500 Taino and shipped about 500 of them to Spain to be sold as slaves. About 200 died on the voyage.
- In 1500 a royal investigator, Francisco de Bobadilla, arrested him and sent him to Spain in chains. Bobadilla's report records that Columbus had a man's ears and nose cut off for stealing corn and then sold him as a slave. The report was found again in a Spanish archive in 2005.
- He died in Valladolid, Spain, on 20 May 1506. He never said in public that he had found a new continent.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="christopher-columbus" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="cabeza-de-vaca-estevanico" name="Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico
> **Who:** two of the four survivors of a Spanish expedition that crossed the Southwest on foot, one a Spanish officer and one an enslaved African man. · **When and where:** 1528 to 1539, Florida, Texas, the Southwest and northern Mexico.
- Panfilo de Narvaez landed near Tampa Bay in April 1528 with 300 men. Attacks, hunger and disease wore them down. The survivors built rafts, and about 80 men washed ashore on or near Galveston Island in November 1528.
- Estevanico (Esteban) was born about 1500 in Azemmour, Morocco. In 1522 he was sold as a slave to the Spanish nobleman Andres Dorantes, who brought him on the expedition.
- On the Texas coast Native people held the survivors as slaves for years. In 1534 the last four escaped: Alvar Nunez Cabeza de Vaca, Dorantes, Alonso del Castillo Maldonado and Estevanico. They walked west working as healers and reached Mexico City in July 1536. Cabeza de Vaca published an account of the journey in 1542.
- In 1539 the Spanish viceroy sent Estevanico north as guide for a friar, Marcos de Niza. He went ahead with Native guides from Sonora and was killed at the Zuni town of Hawikuh. The surviving accounts of his death disagree about why.
- He was not the first African to explore this land. Juan Garrido sailed with Ponce de Leon in 1513.
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (Estevanico as an enslaved man, separate slug).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s English, Dutch, French and Spanish colonists built small towns on the Atlantic coast and along the Rio Grande. Jamestown began in 1607 and Santa Fe about 1610. Almost everything inland was still Native land that no European had mapped. The explorers of this century travelled by river, in ships and canoes, and Native guides and hosts told them where the rivers went.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Champlain and the lake, 1609" -->
Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608. In the summer of 1609 he went south with Wendat, Algonquin and Montagnais warriors to the lake that now carries his name, between New York and Vermont. At Ticonderoga in late July they fought a party of Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) men, and Champlain shot and killed two of them. Shared with: `america-world` (New France).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-hudson" name="Henry Hudson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Hudson
> **Who:** an English sea captain hired by the Dutch East India Company to find a sea route to Asia. · **When and where:** 1609 to 1611, the Hudson River in New York, then Hudson Bay in Canada.
- In September 1609 his ship, the *Half Moon*, sailed up the river the Lenape called Muhheakantuck. It now carries his name. Verrazzano had seen its mouth in 1524.
- His mate Robert Juet kept a journal of the voyage. On 6 September men in two canoes attacked the ship's small boat, and an arrow killed a sailor named John Colman. On 9 September the crew seized two Native men and dressed them in red coats to keep them aboard.
- On the way back down the river, on 1 and 2 October, the crew killed about 10 to 12 Native men, by Juet's own count. A man who climbed in a cabin window and took a pillow and two shirts was shot dead. The cook cut off a swimmer's hand with a sword, and the man drowned. The crew fired muskets and a small cannon at canoes and at about a hundred men on the shore near Manhattan.
- In June 1611, after a winter trapped by ice in James Bay, most of his crew mutinied. They put Hudson, his teenage son John and seven sick or loyal men into a small open boat and sailed away. No European saw them again. No mutineer was punished.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-hudson" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="marquette-and-jolliet" name="Marquette and Jolliet" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Marquette and Jolliet
> **Who:** Jacques Marquette, a French Jesuit priest, and Louis Jolliet, a fur trader born near Quebec. · **When and where:** May to autumn 1673, from St. Ignace, Michigan, down the Mississippi River to the mouth of the Arkansas River.
- Illinois people had told Marquette about the great river and invited him to their towns. The two men set out on 17 May 1673 with two canoes and five paddlers of French and Native ancestry.
- Michigamea warriors nearly attacked them, and the town's elders stopped the attack and fed them. The Quapaw hosted them for two nights near the mouth of the Arkansas River.
- They learned that the river ran to the Gulf of Mexico and not the Pacific. On 17 July they turned back, about 435 miles from the Gulf. They feared the Spanish, and they were told that the nations farther south were well armed.
- Jolliet's canoe overturned in the Lachine Rapids near Montreal and his notes were lost, so Marquette's account is the main record. Marquette died of dysentery in 1675.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marquette-and-jolliet" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-de-la-salle" name="Robert de La Salle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert de La Salle
> **Who:** a French fur trader and explorer who claimed the Mississippi valley for France. · **When and where:** 1682 to 1687, the lower Mississippi River and the Texas coast.
- In early 1682 he led a party by canoe from the mouth of the Illinois River down the Mississippi to the Gulf of Mexico. He was the first European known to follow the river to the sea.
- On 9 April 1682 he claimed the whole river basin for King Louis XIV and named it Louisiana. The Native nations living there were not asked.
- In 1684 he sailed from France with four ships and 300 colonists to settle at the river's mouth. The ships missed it and landed at Matagorda Bay, Texas, on Karankawa land. Pirates took one ship, one sank in the bay, and the last, *La Belle*, ran aground.
- On 19 March 1687, near present-day Navasota, Texas, one of his own men, Pierre Duhaut, killed him from an ambush. Another man in the party then shot Duhaut.
- In 1688 Karankawa warriors killed the 20 adults left at the colony and took five children captive. Archaeologists found the wreck of *La Belle* in Matagorda Bay in 1995.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-de-la-salle" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these fifty years English colonists stayed mostly east of the Appalachian Mountains. The two journeys into country no European had mapped came from outside the English colonies. French traders crossed the northern Great Plains, and a Russian navy expedition reached the coast of Alaska.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The La Verendrye brothers on the northern Plains, 1742 to 1743" -->
- In April 1742 two sons of the French fur trader Pierre La Verendrye and two other Frenchmen left a fort in present-day Manitoba. Two Mandan guides led them west from the Mandan towns on the Missouri River in July.
- For months they travelled with Plains nations that the French called the Horse People, the Bow People and the Little Cherry People. Historians still argue about which modern nations these were. The brothers may have been the first Europeans to see the Rocky Mountains, in what is probably Wyoming. Their journal is hard to follow, and nobody knows their route for sure.
- On 30 March 1743 they buried a lead plate with their names scratched on it beside the Missouri River. It was dug up at Pierre, South Dakota, in 1913 and is kept in the state's Cultural Heritage Center.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="vitus-bering" name="Vitus Bering" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Vitus Bering
> **Who:** a Danish-born officer in the Russian navy who led Russia's first voyages to Alaska. · **When and where:** 1728 and 1741, the Bering Strait and the coast of Alaska.
- In August 1728 he sailed north through the strait between Asia and North America that now carries his name.
- In 1741 he commanded the *St. Peter*. On 16 July the crew sighted Mount Saint Elias on the Alaska coast and landed briefly near Kayak Island. The naturalist Georg Steller, on board, described new animals, among them the bird now called Steller's jay.
- His second ship, under Aleksei Chirikov, reached southeast Alaska. Chirikov sent two boats ashore to look for a harbour, and neither came back. He sailed home without them.
- On the way back the *St. Peter* was wrecked on an island near Kamchatka. Bering died there on 19 December 1741, and about 28 of his men died on the voyage. Scurvy was blamed at the time. Scurvy is a disease caused by a severe lack of vitamin C, and it causes weakness, gum disease, wounds that will not heal and bleeding under the skin. Scientists who studied his bones in 1991 concluded that his own death was more likely caused by heart failure.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="vitus-bering" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The 1783 peace treaty with Britain set the new United States' western border at the Mississippi River. Native nations lived on almost all of that land, and nobody invited them to the treaty talks. Spain claimed California and sent soldiers there in 1769 to hold it. Colonists from Virginia and North Carolina pushed through the Appalachian Mountains into Kentucky, which the Shawnee used as a hunting ground. On the Pacific, Spanish soldiers, a British navy captain and an American fur trader reached places Europeans had never mapped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pacific: California, Hawaii and the Columbia" -->
- In early November 1769 Sergeant Jose Francisco Ortega and his scouts, part of Gaspar de Portola's Spanish expedition, crossed Montara Mountain, south of present-day San Francisco, and found a huge bay blocking their way. They were the first Europeans known to see San Francisco Bay. Rumsen people near Monterey later brought the starving soldiers ground seeds to eat.
- The British captain James Cook reached Kauai on 18 January 1778, the first European known to see the Hawaiian Islands. One of his crew shot and killed a Hawaiian man there. Cook's crews also brought diseases Hawaiians had never had.
- On 14 February 1779 at Kealakekua Bay, Cook tried to take the high chief Kalaniopuu hostage to force the return of a stolen boat. In the fight that followed, Cook shot a man dead, and Hawaiians killed Cook and four marines. Seventeen Hawaiians were killed.
- On 11 May 1792 the Boston fur trader Robert Gray sailed his ship *Columbia Rediviva* into the mouth of a great river and named it the Columbia. That same year Gray's crew fired on Chinook people at Grays Harbor, in present-day Washington, and killed 20. He also had an empty village of about 200 houses burned on Vancouver Island. His own officer wrote that Gray "had let his passions go too far."
- Shared with: `america-world` (Hawaii and the Oregon claim) · `land-environment` (the sea otter trade).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-boone" name="Daniel Boone" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Boone
> **Who:** a hunter from North Carolina who marked the first road for colonists through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky. · **When and where:** 1767 to 1778, the Appalachian Mountains and Kentucky.
- Native nations used the Cumberland Gap long before colonists did. A Virginia doctor, Thomas Walker, told colonists about it in 1750.
- Boone first went into Kentucky to hunt in 1767. In December 1769 a Shawnee party caught him, took all his furs and told him never to come back. The Shawnee counted Kentucky as their hunting ground and had never agreed to give it up.
- In 1773 he led his family and about 50 others toward Kentucky. On 9 October a band of Delaware, Shawnee and Cherokee men attacked a small group that had gone back for supplies. They tortured and killed Boone's oldest son, James, and Henry Russell, the son of another settler. An enslaved man named Adam hid in driftwood and survived. The families turned back.
- In March 1775 Boone gathered Cherokee leaders at Sycamore Shoals, where the judge Richard Henderson bought the Cherokee claim to Kentucky for his planned colony. Boone then cut the Wilderness Road through the gap. An enslaved man named Sam, who was in Boone's work party, was killed with his owner when Native men attacked their camp. Boone built Boonesborough on the Kentucky River.
- Shawnee warriors captured Boone in 1778, and he lived among them for months before he escaped. The settlers who followed his road belong to `migration`.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 doubles the country, and the U.S. government's maps of it are nearly blank — though Native nations lived across all of it and knew it well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mountain men, the sea expedition, and gold" -->
Mountain men, recruited from Ashley's 1822 newspaper advertisement, trap beaver and in the process find the passes and trails later travelers used; they meet each year at the rendezvous. Exploring also goes to sea with Wilkes's U.S. Exploring Expedition, 1838–1842 (six ships, about 350 men, Pacific islands, the Northwest coast, and the recognition of Antarctica as a continent; the specimens help start the Smithsonian). Gold found in 1848 sends searchers over country no U.S. map had covered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lewis-and-clark" name="Meriwether Lewis and William Clark" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Meriwether Lewis and William Clark
1804–1806 — about four dozen people, roughly 8,000 miles from St. Louis to the Pacific and back; Jefferson's orders; the winter at Fort Mandan where Charbonneau and Sacagawea were hired and Jean Baptiste was born; no easy water route; Fort Clatsop in 1805; the winter-camp vote that included York and Sacagawea; one death from illness (Floyd) and one deadly clash on the return.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lewis-and-clark" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sacagawea" name="Sacagawea" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sacagawea
A young Shoshone woman who interpreted and guided while carrying an infant the whole way; her brother Cameahwait supplied horses.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sacagawea" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="york" name="York" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### York
Clark's enslaved companion on the whole journey; voted at Fort Clatsop.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="york" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-colter" name="John Colter" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Colter
Left the expedition in 1806 to trap, saw the Yellowstone thermal country about 1807–08, and escaped a Blackfeet capture on foot.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-colter" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="hugh-glass" name="Hugh Glass" movie="The Revenant (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Hugh Glass
> **Movie:** The Revenant (2015)
1823 grizzly mauling, left behind, crawled and walked about 200 miles to Fort Kiowa.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hugh-glass" -->

### Zebulon Pike
1806–07, sighted the peak that carries his name, failed to climb it, was arrested and released by the Spanish.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zebulon-pike" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jedediah-smith" name="Jedediah Smith" movie="" kind="famous" status="candidate" -->
### Jedediah Smith
1824, rediscovered South Pass with Crow help; mauled by a grizzly and sewn back together; killed in 1831 on the Santa Fe Trail.

1824, first white man to see the Great Salt Lake; Fort Bridger 1843.

Army mapmaker, surveys of 1842–46 with Kit Carson, reports written with Jessie Benton Frémont that Congress printed by the thousand.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The country now reaches both oceans, but the West is still thinly mapped by newcomers. In 1890 the Census reports no continuous frontier line left, and the era of land exploration closes; the next unknowns are the poles, the sky, and the deep ocean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great surveys and 1898" -->
Four great surveys follow the Civil War — King, Hayden, Wheeler, and Powell. Hayden's 1871 Yellowstone party carried William Henry Jackson's photographs and Thomas Moran's paintings back east, and Yellowstone became a national park in 1872. Alaska bought from Russia in 1867 and mapped in earnest during the gold rushes of the late 1890s. In 1898 Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines come under U.S. control and Hawaii is annexed; surveying and mapping follow (**territories thread**).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-wesley-powell" name="John Wesley Powell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Wesley Powell
A one-armed Civil War veteran who ran the Colorado River through the Grand Canyon in 1869 and later led the USGS (**disability thread**).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-wesley-powell" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jim-beckwourth" name="Jim Beckwourth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jim Beckwourth
A Black mountain man born enslaved and later freed, a Crow war leader, who found the Sierra pass that bears his name in 1850; the town that promised him payment reneged, and as a Black man he could not sue; his was the first published mountain-man life story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jim-beckwourth" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="exploration" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The continental map is filled in. Explorers turn to the poles, the sky, and far continents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poles and record flights" -->
Polar claims and disputes; long-distance flight records that were journeys into the unknown as much as transport.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The surface of the earth is largely mapped. What is left unknown is the deep ocean and space.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The space race and the deep ocean" -->
Sputnik in 1957; NASA created in 1958; Shepard the first American in space in 1961 and Glenn the first American in orbit in 1962; Kennedy's Moon goal; Apollo landings through 1972 and none since; the shuttle era opens crews to women and to Black astronauts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The land is mapped from satellites and a phone shows streets worldwide. The frontiers left are space and the deep ocean, and robots do much of the going.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Robots to Mars and the deep sea" -->
Mars rovers — Curiosity in 2012, Perseverance with the Ingenuity helicopter in 2021. New Horizons past Pluto in 2015 and Arrokoth in 2019. Voyager 1 and 2, launched 1977, now in interstellar space. The James Webb Space Telescope launched in 2021 and returning light from early galaxies. Seabed 2030's effort to map the entire ocean floor — parts of Mars are mapped in finer detail than parts of the deep seafloor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-cameron" name="James Cameron" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### James Cameron
2012 solo dive to the Challenger Deep, the third person ever that deep and the first alone.
