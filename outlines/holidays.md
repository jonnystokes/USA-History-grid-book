## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The nations here marked the year with ceremonies. Each one fell at a set point in the year: when a star rose, when the sun reached the end of its path along the horizon, when the corn ripened, or when the salmon came back up the river. Many of them gave thanks for food as it came in: the maple sap, the strawberries, the first ripe corn, the first salmon. Nobody here wrote in an alphabet before 1500, so the written descriptions come from later. Outsiders wrote some of them in the 1700s. The nations wrote others themselves, and many of the ceremonies are still held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Giving thanks through the year in the Northeast" -->
- The Onondaga Nation, one of the Haudenosaunee nations of what is now New York, lists seven ceremonies in its year: Midwinter, Maple Sap, Planting, Bean, Strawberry, Green Corn and Harvest (Onondaga Nation website).
- Men and women called Faith Keepers decide when each ceremony happens. They go by the seasons and the moon, so the dates move from year to year. Midwinter lasts 21 days, with thanks given for different things on each day (Onondaga Nation).
- Haudenosaunee people open and close their gatherings with the Thanksgiving Address, which they call the Words That Come Before All Else. The speaker thanks the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the food plants, the medicine plants and more, one by one. Each part ends with the words "Now our minds are one" (Mohawk version, English translation published 1993, Six Nations Indian Museum and the Tracking Project, via NMAI).
- Nancy Eldredge, a Nauset Wampanoag writer, wrote in 1998 that Wampanoag people held thanksgiving ceremonies in early spring for a good growing season and in the fall for the harvest. They also gave thanks for the strawberries and the cranberries as they ripened, through the thirteen moons of the year (Plimoth Patuxet Museums).
- The Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), on Martha's Vineyard, still holds Cranberry Day each October. The day opens with a greeting at sunrise, and people pick the cranberries, share a meal and set aside a portion for the elders (Aquinnah Wampanoag tribal website).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Green Corn Ceremony in the Southeast" -->
- The Muscogee (Creek) and other nations of the Southeast held the Green Corn Ceremony in midsummer, when the first corn was ripe. It lasted four or eight days. Men fasted for about a day and a half to two days (Eric Bowne, Encyclopedia of Alabama).
- People put out every fire in the town and lit a new one in the town square. Each household then relit its own fire from it. The towns forgave every crime except murder (Encyclopedia of Alabama). `crime-justice` tells the forgiveness as law.
- English traders called the ceremony the busk, from the Creek word *puskita*, which means a fast (Britannica) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- William Bartram, a naturalist from Philadelphia, traveled through Creek towns in the 1770s and published his account in 1791. He wrote that the Creek had a feast for almost every month of the year, and that the busk, in August, was the main one. Each town held its own busk when its own corn was ripe (Bartram, *Travels*, pages 509 to 510, Documenting the American South).
- Bartram wrote that families first made new clothes, pots and pans. They swept their houses and the whole town, piled up worn-out clothes and the old grain, and burned the pile. After three days of fasting, on the fourth morning, the high priest made a new fire by rubbing dry wood together, and every house took its fire from it. The women then brought in the new corn, and the town danced in the square at night (Bartram).
- The written accounts come from the 1700s: James Adair, William Bartram and Benjamin Hawkins (Encyclopedia of Alabama). Creek and Yuchi towns in Oklahoma still hold the ceremony at their stomp grounds (Encyclopedia of Alabama).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Watching the sun in the Southwest" -->
- In the Pueblo towns of the Southwest, a Sun-priest watches where the sun comes up on the horizon each morning. His sightings set the planting calendar and the dates of the ceremonies (High Altitude Observatory, National Center for Atmospheric Research).
- Among the Hopi of northern Arizona, the ceremonial year opens with the Soyal ceremony at the winter solstice in December, the shortest day of the year. A ceremony follows in each village about once a month until the Niman, or Home Dance, in late July (High Altitude Observatory).
- `religion` tells the buildings lined up with the sun, at Chaco Canyon and Cahokia. This chapter tells the calendar.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The First Salmon Feast on the Columbia River" -->
- The Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs and Nez Perce peoples of the Columbia River hold a First Salmon Feast each spring before the fishing season opens (Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission).
- Their tradition says that when the Creator was about to bring people onto the earth, Salmon offered his body to feed them. At the feast people bless and drink water first, then eat the salmon and the other foods (CRITFC).
- The feast at the Celilo Longhouse in Oregon is held on the second weekend of April (CRITFC).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Makahiki in Hawaii" -->
- In Hawaii, which became part of the United States much later, the new year began when the Pleiades, a small group of stars the Hawaiians call Makaliʻi, rose at sunset in late October or early November (Kamehameha Schools, Kaʻiwakīloumoku).
- The Hawaiian historian David Malo, born about 1793, wrote that four months were set aside for the Makahiki season (Malo, *Hawaiian Antiquities*, quoted by Images of Old Hawaiʻi).
- Priests carried an image of the god Lono around each island. At the edge of each land division, the local chief handed over the taxes for the king: pigs, taro, sweet potatoes, feathers, kapa cloth and mats (Malo). Chiefs were forbidden to make war during the season (Kamehameha Schools).
- People held races, boxing and wrestling matches, and games of skill during Makahiki (Kamehameha Schools).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish and French colonists brought the Christian church year to a few places on the coast of Florida and along the Rio Grande. That year was built around Sundays, Easter, Christmas and the feast days of saints. The colonists named places after those days, and they held church services of thanks when they landed. Several towns today call one of those services the "first Thanksgiving." None of them became a yearly holiday. Across almost all the rest of the land, the Native nations kept their own ceremonies, and Europeans saw very few of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Places named for holy days" -->
- Juan Ponce de León reached the coast of what is now Florida in early April 1513, during the Easter season. He named the land *la Florida* after *Pascua Florida*, the Spanish name for the Easter feast (Wikipedia and University of South Florida FCIT) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Pedro Menéndez de Avilés sighted the Florida coast on 28 August 1565, the feast day of Saint Augustine. He named his town St. Augustine after the saint (Florida Museum of Natural History timeline).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Services of thanks on the Florida coast, 1564 and 1565" -->
- French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline near what is now Jacksonville in June 1564. Their leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on 30 June 1564 he had them gather on the beach and sing and pray to thank God for their safe arrival (Laudonnière, published 1586, quoted by the National Huguenot Society via Heidelblog). Timucua people lived on that land.
- On 8 September 1565, Menéndez and about 800 Spanish settlers came ashore at Seloy, a Timucua town. Father Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales said a Mass of thanksgiving. Afterward, the priest wrote, Menéndez "had the Indians fed and dined himself" (National Park Service, Castillo de San Marcos, and Jacksonville History Center).
- The Park Service calls this Mass and meal "the first community act of religion and thanksgiving in the first permanent European settlement," 56 years before Plymouth. It also says the day "did not become the origin of a national annual tradition" (NPS, quoting historian Michael Gannon).
- Two weeks later, on 20 September 1565, Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline and killed most of the men there. At an inlet south of St. Augustine they then killed French survivors who had surrendered: 111 in late September and 134 in October, by the Park Service's count. The Spanish named the inlet Matanzas, which means "slaughters" (NPS, Fort Matanzas). In a letter of 15 October 1565, Menéndez told King Philip II that he killed them because they "spread the Lutheran sect," meaning the Protestant faith, and because they had settled "without permission of Your Majesty" (Menéndez, letter, Early Visions of Florida). `war` tells the killings in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A thanksgiving on the Rio Grande, 1598" -->
- Juan de Oñate led about 500 colonists, with about 7,000 cattle and other animals, north across the Chihuahuan Desert to colonize New Mexico. The crossing took about 50 days. They reached the Rio Grande near what is now San Elizario, Texas (Texas Almanac, adapted by Mike Kingston).
- On 30 April 1598, Oñate ordered a day of thanksgiving. Franciscan friars said Mass. The colonists ate game they had hunted and fish that local Native people brought them. Oñate then read out a claim to all the land along the river for King Philip II of Spain (Texas Almanac). `storytelling-evolution` tells the play performed that day.
- Some Texans claim this day as the first Thanksgiving. Others made the same claim for a feast held by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's men in Palo Duro Canyon in 1541, and historians later questioned that one (Texas Almanac).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A harvest dance at Secotan, 1585" -->
- In July 1585 the English artist John White visited Secotan, an Algonquian town in what is now North Carolina, and painted a large ceremony there. Men and women from other towns danced around a ring of carved wooden posts. Thomas Hariot, who traveled with White, wrote that each visitor had marks on his back to show which town he came from (Encyclopedia Virginia, "A Festive Dance," British Museum painting).
- Encyclopedia Virginia states that the ceremony was "perhaps connected to the corn harvest." The surviving records do not say what the ceremony was called or what it meant to the people of Secotan.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists in the 1600s did not agree about holidays. In Virginia they kept Christmas as people did in England. In New England the Puritan leaders kept Sundays and nothing else on a fixed date. They called fast days for prayer in times of drought or war, and days of thanksgiving, spent in church, after rain, a harvest or a victory. In 1659 the men of the Massachusetts Bay General Court made keeping Christmas a crime. The Plymouth harvest meal of 1621, which Americans now call the first Thanksgiving, was a three-day feast with about ninety Wampanoag men. Nobody called it the first Thanksgiving until 1841.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and thanksgiving in Virginia" -->
- In the winter of 1608 to 1609, bad weather kept John Smith and his men at Kecoughtan, a Powhatan town where Hampton, Virginia, is now, for six or seven days over Christmas. Smith wrote that they "were never more merry," and that the people of Kecoughtan fed them oysters, fish, meat, wild birds and bread (Smith, *Generall Historie*, 1624, Book 3).
- On 4 December 1619 the ship *Margaret* landed 35 settlers and Captain John Woodlief at Berkeley Hundred on the James River. The land belonged to the Weyanock people, part of the Powhatan alliance (Wikipedia, "Weyanoke people") (unconfirmed: search summary only). The London investors who sent the settlers had ordered that the day of arrival "shall be yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of Thanksgiving to Almighty God" (Berkeley Plantation). The settlers held the service.
- On 22 March 1622, Powhatan men attacked the English settlements along the James River. They killed eleven people at Berkeley (Berkeley Plantation). Virginians began holding the Berkeley thanksgiving again in 1958 (Berkeley Plantation).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The harvest meal at Plymouth, 1621" -->
- The Plymouth colonists built their town in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. Most of the Patuxet people had died in an epidemic between 1616 and 1619 (Marr and Cathey, via `health` bank). Of the 102 people who came on the *Mayflower*, 53 were alive by the fall of 1621: 24 men, 5 women and 24 children and teenagers (Plimoth Patuxet Museums).
- Two people who were there wrote about the harvest that fall. Edward Winslow wrote a letter in December 1621, printed in London in 1622 in a book now called *Mourt's Relation*. William Bradford, the governor, wrote a history of the colony years later (Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- Winslow wrote that after the harvest, Governor Bradford sent four men hunting birds "so we might after a special manner rejoice together." Massasoit, the leader the English called the Wampanoag's "greatest king," came "with some ninety men." The English "entertained and feasted" them for three days. The Wampanoag men went out, killed five deer, and gave them to the governor and other leaders (Winslow, in *Mourt's Relation*, 1622, Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- Massasoit's name was Ousamequin. Plimoth Patuxet Museums states that the Wampanoag understood an alliance as a spoken agreement sealed by giving gifts, and that the three days may have been a show of strength and a renewal of the alliance the two sides had made that year.
- Neither Winslow nor Bradford called the meal a thanksgiving. To the colonists, a thanksgiving was a day of prayer in church. In 1841 a Boston minister, Alexander Young, printed Winslow's letter with a note: "This was the first Thanksgiving, the harvest festival of New England." Plimoth Patuxet Museums names that note as the first time anyone called the 1621 meal the first Thanksgiving. New Englanders had already credited the Pilgrims with starting their yearly thanksgiving. The *Salem Gazette* of 28 November 1816 called it the festival that "our Pilgrim forefathers" began (Jane Hampton Cook, GenealogyBank, 2020). `how-we-know` tells how the story grew after 1841.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### William Bradford
> **Who:** Governor of Plymouth Colony, who wrote the colony's history, *Of Plimoth Plantation*. · **When and where:** Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts, 1621 to 1623.
- In the fall of 1621 Bradford, as governor, sent four men to hunt birds for the harvest meal. The Wampanoag men who came with Massasoit gave him and other leaders five deer (Winslow, in *Mourt's Relation*, Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- On Christmas Day 1621, Bradford called the men of Plymouth out to work. Most of the men who had just arrived on the ship *Fortune* said that working on Christmas went against their consciences, and Bradford let them stay behind (Bradford, *Of Plimoth Plantation*).
- At noon he found them playing games in the street. Some were throwing a heavy bar, and some were playing stool-ball, a bat-and-ball game. Bradford took their equipment away. He told them they could keep the day by praying at home, but that nobody would play in the streets while others worked (Bradford).
- In the summer of 1623 a drought was killing Plymouth's corn. Bradford wrote that the colonists held a day of humiliation, which meant fasting and praying. When rain came and the harvest was good, they held a day of thanksgiving (Bradford).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-bradford-holidays" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fast days and thanksgiving days in New England" -->
- The Puritan leaders of New England called a fast or a thanksgiving when something happened, and each was a day of church services. At Plymouth in 1623, the colonists fasted during a drought and held a thanksgiving after the rain came (Bradford).
- In Massachusetts Bay, the General Court and the church elders set these days. In September 1638 the Court asked the elders of each church to keep a day of thanksgiving on the last Thursday of the eighth month (October, because their year began in March) for the ships that had arrived safely and for the harvest (*Records of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 1).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving days after the Pequot War, 1637" -->
- On 26 May 1637, Connecticut colonists under Captain John Mason, with Mohegan and Narragansett allies, set fire to the fortified Pequot town at Mystic and shot the people who tried to escape. Between 400 and 700 Pequot were killed, most of them old men, women and children, by the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation's own history (`war` bank). `war` and `native-nations` tell the war.
- In early June 1637, the Massachusetts Bay Court set 15 June as a day of thanksgiving in the churches (*Records*, vol. 1). John Winthrop, the governor, wrote in his journal that a day of thanksgiving was kept in all the churches "for the victory obtained against the Pequods" (Winthrop's journal, quoted in search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- In August 1637 the Court asked the magistrates to work out a day of thanksgiving with the church elders for the soldiers' return, with the soldiers to be feasted by their towns. It then ordered that 12 October 1637 be kept as a day of thanksgiving to God for "subdewing the Pecoits, bringing the soldiers in safety," for the end of a church meeting, and for good news from Germany (*Records of the Governor and Company of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 1, 1853).
- Some writers say the 1637 thanksgiving was the first official Thanksgiving Day and the start of the modern holiday. William B. Newell, a Penobscot man and a former head of anthropology at the University of Connecticut, said the first Thanksgiving Day was proclaimed in 1637 to mark the killing of 700 Pequot men, women and children who had gathered for their Green Corn ceremony (quoted on many websites) (unconfirmed: search summary only). The records of the attack do not mention a ceremony. The historian Jeremy Bangs disputes Newell's claim (History News Network, 2005). The court records show that Massachusetts Bay held thanksgiving days for the victory over the Pequot. They also show that the colonists held many other thanksgiving days, before 1637 and after it, for ships, harvests and rain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas made a crime, 1659 to 1681" -->
- On 11 May 1659 the General Court of Massachusetts Bay passed a law that anyone found "observing any such day as Christmas or the like, either by forbearing of labor, feasting, or any other way" would pay a fine of five shillings (Mass Moments, Mass Humanities).
- The Puritans said nothing in the Bible told Christians to keep Christmas. The minister Increase Mather called Christmas "a profane and superstitious custom" (Mass Moments).
- King Charles II's officials in London pressed Massachusetts to bring its laws in line with England's. In 1681 the Court repealed the Christmas law (*Records of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 5, 1681 entries, page 339). Most people in Massachusetts still did not celebrate Christmas for many years after that (Mass Moments).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ceremonies banned in New Mexico" -->
- From 1656 to 1665, the Franciscan leader in New Mexico, Alonso de Posada, forbade the Pueblo people's own ceremonies. He ordered the friars to go into the kivas, the Pueblos' underground rooms for worship, and to burn the masks and destroy the sacred objects there (Dennis Zotigh, NMAI, via `religion` bank).
- In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested for "sorcery." Three were hanged. A fourth killed himself. The rest were whipped in public and put in prison, until Pueblo men came to Santa Fe and forced Treviño to free them. One of the men freed was Po'pay, who led the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (Zotigh, Newberry Library, via `religion` bank). `religion` and `native-nations` tell the revolt.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1700 to 1750 the colonies still had no holiday in common. Each colony kept its own days, and many of them came from Britain. New England governors printed orders for days of thanksgiving and days of fasting, and nobody was allowed to work on those days. Boys and young men in Boston marched through the streets on Pope's Night every 5 November. In New York, enslaved and free Black people gathered for Pinkster, a spring holiday brought by the Dutch. In New England, Black communities began choosing their own governors and kings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and fast days by the governor's order" -->
- A New England governor, with the advice of his council, named each thanksgiving and each fast day. Printers published his order as a large sheet called a broadside, and the order called on people to go to worship (Library of Congress, Printed Ephemera Collection).
- On 14 October 1721, Governor Gurdon Saltonstall of Connecticut set Wednesday, 8 November, as a thanksgiving. His order named the colony's escape from the smallpox then spreading in Boston, the peace, and that year's harvest (Library of Congress, Saltonstall proclamation, 1721).
- Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts ordered a thanksgiving for Thursday, 14 August 1746, because the Duke of Cumberland's army had defeated a rebellion in Scotland. The order ended: "all servile Labour is prohibited on said Day" (Library of Congress, Shirley proclamation, 1746). Servile labor meant ordinary work.
- Massachusetts governors also ordered fast days, such as the public fasts proclaimed by Jonathan Belcher in 1734 and by Spencer Phips in 1745 (Library of Congress).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night in Boston" -->
- In the early hours of 5 November 1605, guards in London found Guy Fawkes under the Parliament building with fuses and matches. He and a group of other English Catholics had planned to kill King James I when Parliament opened. Parliament then passed a law for a public thanksgiving every year on 5 November (Royal Museums Greenwich). In New England the day became Pope's Night, and crowds burned figures of the Pope (Old North Church).
- In Boston, young men and older boys built wagons with moving figures of the Pope, the Devil and other enemies of the king. They dressed up, paraded through town, and asked homeowners for money. The night ended with a meal outdoors and a bonfire (J. L. Bell, *Boston 1775*).
- Boston had two gangs, from the North End and the South End. Each built its own Pope wagon and tried to capture the other's. The winners burned the captured figures on Copp's Hill or on the Common (J. L. Bell). The fights between the gangs began in the 1740s (Wikipedia, "Pope Night") (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The printer Isaiah Thomas, born in 1749, remembered the crowds blowing on conch shells, beating on the sides of houses with clubs, and shouting "North end forever" or "South end forever." As a boy he got too close to a wagon and was knocked out by a brick (Isaiah Thomas, *History of Printing in America*, second edition, quoted by J. L. Bell).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster in New York" -->
- Pinkster is the Dutch name for Pentecost, a Christian feast held seven weeks after Easter. Dutch settlers brought it to New Netherland in the 1620s (Historic Hudson Valley).
- The oldest known written account of Pinkster, in the *New-York Weekly Journal* of March 1737, describes a gathering in the fields outside New York City and African-style musical instruments (Historic Hudson Valley).
- Enslavers gave enslaved men and women several days off at Pinkster. Many walked from farms into the city to see family members who lived apart from them. They sold berries, herbs, sassafras bark, drinks and oysters, and spent the money at the celebration (Historic Hudson Valley).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black governors and kings in New England" -->
- Black communities in New England held their own elections for a governor or a king. In Connecticut and Rhode Island, where white men elected their governor, Black men elected a Black governor. In Massachusetts and New Hampshire, where the king of England appointed the governor, Black men elected a king (New England Historical Society).
- The first known name comes from New London, Connecticut, in 1749. A man named Hercules served as governor there, and he is known from his wife Florio's gravestone (Connecticut State Library). Black election days in Massachusetts may have begun earlier, in 1741 (Wikipedia, "Negro Election Day") (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- In Connecticut the Black election came the day after the white men's election, and the celebration went on for a week. People dressed in their best clothes, some borrowed from the people who enslaved them. The enslaver of the man elected was expected to pay for food, decorations and liquor (Connecticut State Library).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans began celebrating their independence on its first anniversary, 4 July 1777, and within a few years they also celebrated George Washington's birthday. Neither was a national holiday yet. In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official holiday. Congress, and then President Washington, asked Americans to keep days of thanksgiving on dates they chose, but nobody set a yearly date. Older customs changed too. Boston's leaders turned Pope's Night into a protest against British taxes, and General Washington banned it in his army. In the South, enslaved people got a few days off at Christmas, while those who worked in the house served their enslavers' guests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night, 1764 to 1775" -->
- On Pope's Night, 5 November 1764, one of the Pope wagons in Boston ran over and killed a boy named Brown. Sources give his age as five to nine. The surviving records do not give his first name or say who was driving. The death happened early in the day, and the parades and the fight between the gangs went on that night (J. L. Bell, *Boston 1775*).
- In August 1765, crowds in Boston protesting the Stamp Act, a British tax on printed paper, used the Pope's Night customs: figures hanged and burned, marches and bonfires. They broke into three or four large houses and smashed the furniture. Ebenezer Mackintosh, a shoemaker and captain of the South End gang, led crowds at the protests (J. L. Bell).
- On 5 November 1765, Mackintosh and Henry Swift, captain of the North End gang, agreed to march together instead of fighting. Wealthy merchants paid for a dinner for them (J. L. Bell).
- On 5 November 1775, General George Washington ordered his soldiers not to keep "that ridiculous and childish Custom of burning the Effigy of the pope." He wrote that the Americans were asking Catholic Canadians to join them, and that insulting their religion at such a time was wrong (Washington, General Orders, 5 November 1775, Founders Online). Pope's Night ended in Boston during the Revolution (Old North Church).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Fourth of July, 1777" -->
- On 2 July 1776, the Continental Congress voted for independence. The next day John Adams wrote to his wife Abigail that the day "ought to be solemnized with Pomp and Parade, with Shews, Games, Sports, Guns, Bells, Bonfires and Illuminations from one End of this Continent to the other." Congress formally adopted the Declaration of Independence on 4 July, and Americans celebrate that date (Massachusetts Historical Society. National Park Service).
- In July 1776, crowds greeted the public readings of the Declaration with parades, bonfires and gunfire. The first planned Fourth of July celebrations came a year later, in Philadelphia and in Boston (Mount Vernon).
- In Philadelphia on 4 July 1777, the armed ships in the Delaware River were decorated with flags. At one o'clock each ship fired thirteen cannon, one for each state. That night bells rang, fireworks went up on the city's common, and people lit their windows (*Pennsylvania Evening Post*, 5 July 1777, quoted by Mount Vernon).
- In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official state holiday (Mount Vernon).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Henry Wight
> **Who:** A Congregational minister and Revolutionary War veteran who started a Fourth of July celebration that has been held every year since. · **When and where:** Bristol, Rhode Island, 1785 to the 1820s.
- Henry Wight was born in Medfield, Massachusetts, on 26 May 1752. He left his studies at Harvard to serve in the Revolutionary War. One account says he served as a soldier, and another says he served as a surgeon (New England Historical Society. Rev War Bristol).
- In January 1785 the First Congregational Church of Bristol, Rhode Island, which had 36 members, hired him as its minister (New England Historical Society).
- Bristol stands on Mount Hope, Montaup in the Pokanoket language, where the Pokanoket Wampanoag lived and where Metacom had his base in King Philip's War. Metacom was killed there on 12 August 1676, in an ambush led by the English captain Benjamin Church. After the war four Boston merchants bought the land, as part of Plymouth Colony, and started the town (Wikipedia, "Bristol, Rhode Island"). `native-nations` tells the war.
- Bristol people had already fired cannon on the Fourth. In 1777 they fired 13 cannon at dawn, one for each state (New England Historical Society).
- In 1785, his first year in Bristol, Wight led the town's first Patriotic Exercises on the Fourth of July. He gave a speech that honored the veterans and celebrated the new country's freedoms (New England Historical Society. Bristol Fourth of July Committee).
- He was Bristol's minister for 43 years and spoke at the Fourth of July for about 40 of them (search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only). He died in 1837 (Rev War Bristol).
- Bristol has held the celebration every year since 1785. The town calls it the oldest continuous Fourth of July celebration in the United States. Its parade probably grew out of the walk to the Patriotic Exercises in the early 1800s (Bristol Fourth of July Committee. Town of Bristol).

- After the American army forced a British army to surrender at Saratoga, New York, in October 1777, the Continental Congress set 18 December 1777 as a day of thanksgiving for the whole country. Samuel Adams, Richard Henry Lee and Daniel Roberdeau wrote the order (search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only). The date is from US House of Representatives History, Art and Archives.
- Congress also named days of fasting in 1775, 1776, 1778, 1779, 1780, 1781 and 1782, and days of thanksgiving in 1777, 1778, 1781 and 1783 (US House History).
- In 1789 Elias Boudinot, a member of the House of Representatives from New Jersey, asked Congress to request a day of thanksgiving for the new Constitution. Both houses agreed. On 3 October 1789, President George Washington named Thursday, 26 November 1789, as a day of thanksgiving (Mount Vernon).
- Washington sent his order to the state governors and asked them to announce it. On the day, he went to church at St. Paul's Chapel in New York City and sent beer and food to the men held in the city jail for their debts (Mount Vernon). Washington named another day of thanksgiving in 1795, to give thanks that the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania was over (Mount Vernon). `drugs-alcohol` tells the rebellion and the army Washington sent against it. <!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas on the plantations" -->
- At Mount Vernon, George Washington's plantation in Virginia, most enslaved workers had four days off at Christmas. They used the days to rest and to visit family on Mount Vernon and on nearby plantations. Enslaved cooks, housemaids and waiters had more work than usual over Christmas, because the Washingtons had extra guests (Mary V. Thompson, Mount Vernon).
- Philip Vickers Fithian, a young tutor from New Jersey, spent Christmas 1773 at Nomini Hall, the Virginia plantation of Robert Carter. He wrote that guns fired all around the house woke him that morning (Fithian's journal, 25 December 1773).
- Before he was dressed, several enslaved men and boys came to his room to wish him a merry Christmas and to ask for a "Christmas Box," a small gift of money. They were Nelson, a boy who made his fire and blacked his shoes, a man who lit the schoolroom fire, Tom the coachman, and Dennis, a boy who waited at table. Fithian gave them five bits in all, about three shillings (Fithian).

- Black governors kept being elected in Connecticut through these years. In Hartford, London was governor in 1755 and was enslaved by Thomas Seymour. Quaw was governor in 1760 and was enslaved by George Wyllys, the secretary of the colony. Cuff was governor from 1766 to 1776 (Connecticut State Library).
- The governors had little power of their own. A Black governor had no power to punish a white person who wronged a Black person (Andy Piascik, Connecticut History).

### John Anderson
> **Who:** An enslaved man who became Hartford's Black governor in 1776, in a dispute that white officials investigated as a possible plot. · **When and where:** Hartford, Connecticut, 1776 to 1780.
- In May 1776, white men in Connecticut elected Jonathan Trumbull governor again. Soon after, Cuff, the Black governor of Hartford, resigned and named John Anderson as governor in his place, without an election. A journal entry by a Hartford man, Major French, dated 11 May 1776, records it (Chris Menapace, Digital Farmington).
- Anderson was enslaved by Philip Skene, who had been a major in the British army. Skene was being held as a prisoner because the Americans suspected he was loyal to Britain (Menapace. Andy Piascik, Connecticut History).
- White people in Hartford feared that Skene planned to use Anderson to bring Black people over to the British side. A committee of the Connecticut government investigated (Menapace. Piascik).
- The two sources disagree on what the committee found. One says Skene had tried to pay voters, and that many Black people refused to back a Loyalist's slave. Skene told the committee it was done for amusement (Menapace). The other says the committee cleared Anderson but found that he had paid some Black people to support him, and that the government approved his four-year term (Piascik).
- Anderson served as governor until 1780 (Piascik).
