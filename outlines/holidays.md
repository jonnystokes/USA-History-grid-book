## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The nations here marked the year with ceremonies. Each one fell at a set point in the year: when a star rose, when the sun reached the end of its path along the horizon, when the corn ripened, or when the salmon came back up the river. Many of them gave thanks for food as it came in: the maple sap, the strawberries, the first ripe corn, the first salmon. Nobody here wrote in an alphabet before 1500, so the written descriptions come from later. Outsiders wrote some of them in the 1700s. The nations wrote others themselves, and many of the ceremonies are still held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Giving thanks through the year in the Northeast" -->
- The Onondaga Nation, one of the Haudenosaunee nations of what is now New York, lists seven ceremonies in its year: Midwinter, Maple Sap, Planting, Bean, Strawberry, Green Corn and Harvest (Onondaga Nation website).
- Men and women called Faith Keepers decide when each ceremony happens. They go by the seasons and the moon, so the dates move from year to year. Midwinter lasts 21 days, with thanks given for different things on each day (Onondaga Nation).
- Haudenosaunee people open and close their gatherings with the Thanksgiving Address, which they call the Words That Come Before All Else. The speaker thanks the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the food plants, the medicine plants and more, one by one. Each part ends with the words "Now our minds are one" (Mohawk version, English translation published 1993, Six Nations Indian Museum and the Tracking Project, via NMAI).
- Nancy Eldredge, a Nauset Wampanoag writer, wrote in 1998 that Wampanoag people held thanksgiving ceremonies in early spring for a good growing season and in the fall for the harvest. They also gave thanks for the strawberries and the cranberries as they ripened, through the thirteen moons of the year (Plimoth Patuxet Museums).
- The Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), on Martha's Vineyard, still holds Cranberry Day each October. The day opens with a greeting at sunrise, and people pick the cranberries, share a meal and set aside a portion for the elders (Aquinnah Wampanoag tribal website).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Green Corn Ceremony in the Southeast" -->
- The Muscogee (Creek) and other nations of the Southeast held the Green Corn Ceremony in midsummer, when the first corn was ripe. It lasted four or eight days. Men fasted for about a day and a half to two days (Eric Bowne, Encyclopedia of Alabama).
- People put out every fire in the town and lit a new one in the town square. Each household then relit its own fire from it. The towns forgave every crime except murder (Encyclopedia of Alabama). `crime-justice` tells the forgiveness as law.
- English traders called the ceremony the busk, from the Creek word *puskita*, which means a fast (Britannica) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- William Bartram, a naturalist from Philadelphia, traveled through Creek towns in the 1770s and published his account in 1791. He wrote that the Creek had a feast for almost every month of the year, and that the busk, in August, was the main one. Each town held its own busk when its own corn was ripe (Bartram, *Travels*, pages 509 to 510, Documenting the American South).
- Bartram wrote that families first made new clothes, pots and pans. They swept their houses and the whole town, piled up worn-out clothes and the old grain, and burned the pile. After three days of fasting, on the fourth morning, the high priest made a new fire by rubbing dry wood together, and every house took its fire from it. The women then brought in the new corn, and the town danced in the square at night (Bartram).
- The written accounts come from the 1700s: James Adair, William Bartram and Benjamin Hawkins (Encyclopedia of Alabama). Creek and Yuchi towns in Oklahoma still hold the ceremony at their stomp grounds (Encyclopedia of Alabama).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Watching the sun in the Southwest" -->
- In the Pueblo towns of the Southwest, a Sun-priest watches where the sun comes up on the horizon each morning. His sightings set the planting calendar and the dates of the ceremonies (High Altitude Observatory, National Center for Atmospheric Research).
- Among the Hopi of northern Arizona, the ceremonial year opens with the Soyal ceremony at the winter solstice in December, the shortest day of the year. A ceremony follows in each village about once a month until the Niman, or Home Dance, in late July (High Altitude Observatory).
- `religion` tells the buildings lined up with the sun, at Chaco Canyon and Cahokia. This chapter tells the calendar.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The First Salmon Feast on the Columbia River" -->
- The Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs and Nez Perce peoples of the Columbia River hold a First Salmon Feast each spring before the fishing season opens (Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission).
- Their tradition says that when the Creator was about to bring people onto the earth, Salmon offered his body to feed them. At the feast people bless and drink water first, then eat the salmon and the other foods (CRITFC).
- The feast at the Celilo Longhouse in Oregon is held on the second weekend of April (CRITFC).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Makahiki in Hawaii" -->
- In Hawaii, which became part of the United States much later, the new year began when the Pleiades, a small group of stars the Hawaiians call Makaliʻi, rose at sunset in late October or early November (Kamehameha Schools, Kaʻiwakīloumoku).
- The Hawaiian historian David Malo, born about 1793, wrote that four months were set aside for the Makahiki season (Malo, *Hawaiian Antiquities*, quoted by Images of Old Hawaiʻi).
- Priests carried an image of the god Lono around each island. At the edge of each land division, the local chief handed over the taxes for the king: pigs, taro, sweet potatoes, feathers, kapa cloth and mats (Malo). Chiefs were forbidden to make war during the season (Kamehameha Schools).
- People held races, boxing and wrestling matches, and games of skill during Makahiki (Kamehameha Schools).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish and French colonists brought the Christian church year to a few places on the coast of Florida and along the Rio Grande. That year was built around Sundays, Easter, Christmas and the feast days of saints. The colonists named places after those days, and they held church services of thanks when they landed. Several towns today call one of those services the "first Thanksgiving." None of them became a yearly holiday. Across almost all the rest of the land, the Native nations kept their own ceremonies, and Europeans saw very few of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Places named for holy days" -->
- Juan Ponce de León reached the coast of what is now Florida in early April 1513, during the Easter season. He named the land *la Florida* after *Pascua Florida*, the Spanish name for the Easter feast (Wikipedia and University of South Florida FCIT) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Pedro Menéndez de Avilés sighted the Florida coast on 28 August 1565, the feast day of Saint Augustine. He named his town St. Augustine after the saint (Florida Museum of Natural History timeline).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Services of thanks on the Florida coast, 1564 and 1565" -->
- French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline near what is now Jacksonville in June 1564. Their leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on 30 June 1564 he had them gather on the beach and sing and pray to thank God for their safe arrival (Laudonnière, published 1586, quoted by the National Huguenot Society via Heidelblog). Timucua people lived on that land.
- On 8 September 1565, Menéndez and about 800 Spanish settlers came ashore at Seloy, a Timucua town. Father Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales said a Mass of thanksgiving. Afterward, the priest wrote, Menéndez "had the Indians fed and dined himself" (National Park Service, Castillo de San Marcos, and Jacksonville History Center).
- The Park Service calls this Mass and meal "the first community act of religion and thanksgiving in the first permanent European settlement," 56 years before Plymouth. It also says the day "did not become the origin of a national annual tradition" (NPS, quoting historian Michael Gannon).
- Two weeks later, on 20 September 1565, Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline and killed most of the men there. At an inlet south of St. Augustine they then killed French survivors who had surrendered: 111 in late September and 134 in October, by the Park Service's count. The Spanish named the inlet Matanzas, which means "slaughters" (NPS, Fort Matanzas). In a letter of 15 October 1565, Menéndez told King Philip II that he killed them because they "spread the Lutheran sect," meaning the Protestant faith, and because they had settled "without permission of Your Majesty" (Menéndez, letter, Early Visions of Florida). `war` tells the killings in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A thanksgiving on the Rio Grande, 1598" -->
- Juan de Oñate led about 500 colonists, with about 7,000 cattle and other animals, north across the Chihuahuan Desert to colonize New Mexico. The crossing took about 50 days. They reached the Rio Grande near what is now San Elizario, Texas (Texas Almanac, adapted by Mike Kingston).
- On 30 April 1598, Oñate ordered a day of thanksgiving. Franciscan friars said Mass. The colonists ate game they had hunted and fish that local Native people brought them. Oñate then read out a claim to all the land along the river for King Philip II of Spain (Texas Almanac). `storytelling-evolution` tells the play performed that day.
- Some Texans claim this day as the first Thanksgiving. Others made the same claim for a feast held by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's men in Palo Duro Canyon in 1541, and historians later questioned that one (Texas Almanac).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A harvest dance at Secotan, 1585" -->
- In July 1585 the English artist John White visited Secotan, an Algonquian town in what is now North Carolina, and painted a large ceremony there. Men and women from other towns danced around a ring of carved wooden posts. Thomas Hariot, who traveled with White, wrote that each visitor had marks on his back to show which town he came from (Encyclopedia Virginia, "A Festive Dance," British Museum painting).
- Encyclopedia Virginia states that the ceremony was "perhaps connected to the corn harvest." The surviving records do not say what the ceremony was called or what it meant to the people of Secotan.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists in the 1600s did not agree about holidays. In Virginia they kept Christmas as people did in England. In New England the Puritan leaders kept Sundays and nothing else on a fixed date. They called fast days for prayer in times of drought or war, and days of thanksgiving, spent in church, after rain, a harvest or a victory. In 1659 the men of the Massachusetts Bay General Court made keeping Christmas a crime. The Plymouth harvest meal of 1621, which Americans now call the first Thanksgiving, was a three-day feast with about ninety Wampanoag men. Nobody called it the first Thanksgiving until 1841.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and thanksgiving in Virginia" -->
- In the winter of 1608 to 1609, bad weather kept John Smith and his men at Kecoughtan, a Powhatan town where Hampton, Virginia, is now, for six or seven days over Christmas. Smith wrote that they "were never more merry," and that the people of Kecoughtan fed them oysters, fish, meat, wild birds and bread (Smith, *Generall Historie*, 1624, Book 3).
- On 4 December 1619 the ship *Margaret* landed 35 settlers and Captain John Woodlief at Berkeley Hundred on the James River. The land belonged to the Weyanock people, part of the Powhatan alliance (Wikipedia, "Weyanoke people") (unconfirmed: search summary only). The London investors who sent the settlers had ordered that the day of arrival "shall be yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of Thanksgiving to Almighty God" (Berkeley Plantation). The settlers held the service.
- On 22 March 1622, Powhatan men attacked the English settlements along the James River. They killed eleven people at Berkeley (Berkeley Plantation). Virginians began holding the Berkeley thanksgiving again in 1958 (Berkeley Plantation).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The harvest meal at Plymouth, 1621" -->
- The Plymouth colonists built their town in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. Most of the Patuxet people had died in an epidemic between 1616 and 1619 (Marr and Cathey, via `health` bank). Of the 102 people who came on the *Mayflower*, 53 were alive by the fall of 1621: 24 men, 5 women and 24 children and teenagers (Plimoth Patuxet Museums).
- Two people who were there wrote about the harvest that fall. Edward Winslow wrote a letter in December 1621, printed in London in 1622 in a book now called *Mourt's Relation*. William Bradford, the governor, wrote a history of the colony years later (Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- Winslow wrote that after the harvest, Governor Bradford sent four men hunting birds "so we might after a special manner rejoice together." Massasoit, the leader the English called the Wampanoag's "greatest king," came "with some ninety men." The English "entertained and feasted" them for three days. The Wampanoag men went out, killed five deer, and gave them to the governor and other leaders (Winslow, in *Mourt's Relation*, 1622, Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- Massasoit's name was Ousamequin. Plimoth Patuxet Museums states that the Wampanoag understood an alliance as a spoken agreement sealed by giving gifts, and that the three days may have been a show of strength and a renewal of the alliance the two sides had made that year.
- Neither Winslow nor Bradford called the meal a thanksgiving. To the colonists, a thanksgiving was a day of prayer in church. In 1841 a Boston minister, Alexander Young, printed Winslow's letter with a note: "This was the first Thanksgiving, the harvest festival of New England." Plimoth Patuxet Museums names that note as the first time anyone called the 1621 meal the first Thanksgiving. New Englanders had already credited the Pilgrims with starting their yearly thanksgiving. The *Salem Gazette* of 28 November 1816 called it the festival that "our Pilgrim forefathers" began (Jane Hampton Cook, GenealogyBank, 2020). `how-we-know` tells how the story grew after 1841.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### William Bradford
> **Who:** Governor of Plymouth Colony, who wrote the colony's history, *Of Plimoth Plantation*. · **When and where:** Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts, 1621 to 1623.
- In the fall of 1621 Bradford, as governor, sent four men to hunt birds for the harvest meal. The Wampanoag men who came with Massasoit gave him and other leaders five deer (Winslow, in *Mourt's Relation*, Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- On Christmas Day 1621, Bradford called the men of Plymouth out to work. Most of the men who had just arrived on the ship *Fortune* said that working on Christmas went against their consciences, and Bradford let them stay behind (Bradford, *Of Plimoth Plantation*).
- At noon he found them playing games in the street. Some were throwing a heavy bar, and some were playing stool-ball, a bat-and-ball game. Bradford took their equipment away. He told them they could keep the day by praying at home, but that nobody would play in the streets while others worked (Bradford).
- In the summer of 1623 a drought was killing Plymouth's corn. Bradford wrote that the colonists held a day of humiliation, which meant fasting and praying. When rain came and the harvest was good, they held a day of thanksgiving (Bradford).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-bradford-holidays" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fast days and thanksgiving days in New England" -->
- The Puritan leaders of New England called a fast or a thanksgiving when something happened, and each was a day of church services. At Plymouth in 1623, the colonists fasted during a drought and held a thanksgiving after the rain came (Bradford).
- In Massachusetts Bay, the General Court and the church elders set these days. In September 1638 the Court asked the elders of each church to keep a day of thanksgiving on the last Thursday of the eighth month (October, because their year began in March) for the ships that had arrived safely and for the harvest (*Records of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 1).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving days after the Pequot War, 1637" -->
- On 26 May 1637, Connecticut colonists under Captain John Mason, with Mohegan and Narragansett allies, set fire to the fortified Pequot town at Mystic and shot the people who tried to escape. Between 400 and 700 Pequot were killed, most of them old men, women and children, by the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation's own history (`war` bank). `war` and `native-nations` tell the war.
- In early June 1637, the Massachusetts Bay Court set 15 June as a day of thanksgiving in the churches (*Records*, vol. 1). John Winthrop, the governor, wrote in his journal that a day of thanksgiving was kept in all the churches "for the victory obtained against the Pequods" (Winthrop's journal, quoted in search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- In August 1637 the Court asked the magistrates to work out a day of thanksgiving with the church elders for the soldiers' return, with the soldiers to be feasted by their towns. It then ordered that 12 October 1637 be kept as a day of thanksgiving to God for "subdewing the Pecoits, bringing the soldiers in safety," for the end of a church meeting, and for good news from Germany (*Records of the Governor and Company of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 1, 1853).
- Some writers say the 1637 thanksgiving was the first official Thanksgiving Day and the start of the modern holiday. William B. Newell, a Penobscot man and a former head of anthropology at the University of Connecticut, said the first Thanksgiving Day was proclaimed in 1637 to mark the killing of 700 Pequot men, women and children who had gathered for their Green Corn ceremony (quoted on many websites) (unconfirmed: search summary only). The records of the attack do not mention a ceremony. The historian Jeremy Bangs disputes Newell's claim (History News Network, 2005). The court records show that Massachusetts Bay held thanksgiving days for the victory over the Pequot. They also show that the colonists held many other thanksgiving days, before 1637 and after it, for ships, harvests and rain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas made a crime, 1659 to 1681" -->
- On 11 May 1659 the General Court of Massachusetts Bay passed a law that anyone found "observing any such day as Christmas or the like, either by forbearing of labor, feasting, or any other way" would pay a fine of five shillings (Mass Moments, Mass Humanities).
- The Puritans said nothing in the Bible told Christians to keep Christmas. The minister Increase Mather called Christmas "a profane and superstitious custom" (Mass Moments).
- King Charles II's officials in London pressed Massachusetts to bring its laws in line with England's. In 1681 the Court repealed the Christmas law (*Records of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 5, 1681 entries, page 339). Most people in Massachusetts still did not celebrate Christmas for many years after that (Mass Moments).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ceremonies banned in New Mexico" -->
- From 1656 to 1665, the Franciscan leader in New Mexico, Alonso de Posada, forbade the Pueblo people's own ceremonies. He ordered the friars to go into the kivas, the Pueblos' underground rooms for worship, and to burn the masks and destroy the sacred objects there (Dennis Zotigh, NMAI, via `religion` bank).
- In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested for "sorcery." Three were hanged. A fourth killed himself. The rest were whipped in public and put in prison, until Pueblo men came to Santa Fe and forced Treviño to free them. One of the men freed was Po'pay, who led the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (Zotigh, Newberry Library, via `religion` bank). `religion` and `native-nations` tell the revolt.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1700 to 1750 the colonies still had no holiday in common. Each colony kept its own days, and many of them came from Britain. New England governors printed orders for days of thanksgiving and days of fasting, and nobody was allowed to work on those days. Boys and young men in Boston marched through the streets on Pope's Night every 5 November. In New York, enslaved and free Black people gathered for Pinkster, a spring holiday brought by the Dutch. In New England, Black communities began choosing their own governors and kings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and fast days by the governor's order" -->
- A New England governor, with the advice of his council, named each thanksgiving and each fast day. Printers published his order as a large sheet called a broadside, and the order called on people to go to worship (Library of Congress, Printed Ephemera Collection).
- On 14 October 1721, Governor Gurdon Saltonstall of Connecticut set Wednesday, 8 November, as a thanksgiving. His order named the colony's escape from the smallpox then spreading in Boston, the peace, and that year's harvest (Library of Congress, Saltonstall proclamation, 1721).
- Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts ordered a thanksgiving for Thursday, 14 August 1746, because the Duke of Cumberland's army had defeated a rebellion in Scotland. The order ended: "all servile Labour is prohibited on said Day" (Library of Congress, Shirley proclamation, 1746). Servile labor meant ordinary work.
- Massachusetts governors also ordered fast days, such as the public fasts proclaimed by Jonathan Belcher in 1734 and by Spencer Phips in 1745 (Library of Congress).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night in Boston" -->
- In the early hours of 5 November 1605, guards in London found Guy Fawkes under the Parliament building with fuses and matches. He and a group of other English Catholics had planned to kill King James I when Parliament opened. Parliament then passed a law for a public thanksgiving every year on 5 November (Royal Museums Greenwich). In New England the day became Pope's Night, and crowds burned figures of the Pope (Old North Church).
- In Boston, young men and older boys built wagons with moving figures of the Pope, the Devil and other enemies of the king. They dressed up, paraded through town, and asked homeowners for money. The night ended with a meal outdoors and a bonfire (J. L. Bell, *Boston 1775*).
- Boston had two gangs, from the North End and the South End. Each built its own Pope wagon and tried to capture the other's. The winners burned the captured figures on Copp's Hill or on the Common (J. L. Bell). The fights between the gangs began in the 1740s (Wikipedia, "Pope Night") (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The printer Isaiah Thomas, born in 1749, remembered the crowds blowing on conch shells, beating on the sides of houses with clubs, and shouting "North end forever" or "South end forever." As a boy he got too close to a wagon and was knocked out by a brick (Isaiah Thomas, *History of Printing in America*, second edition, quoted by J. L. Bell).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster in New York" -->
- Pinkster is the Dutch name for Pentecost, a Christian feast held seven weeks after Easter. Dutch settlers brought it to New Netherland in the 1620s (Historic Hudson Valley).
- The oldest known written account of Pinkster, in the *New-York Weekly Journal* of March 1737, describes a gathering in the fields outside New York City and African-style musical instruments (Historic Hudson Valley).
- Enslavers gave enslaved men and women several days off at Pinkster. Many walked from farms into the city to see family members who lived apart from them. They sold berries, herbs, sassafras bark, drinks and oysters, and spent the money at the celebration (Historic Hudson Valley).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black governors and kings in New England" -->
- Black communities in New England held their own elections for a governor or a king. In Connecticut and Rhode Island, where white men elected their governor, Black men elected a Black governor. In Massachusetts and New Hampshire, where the king of England appointed the governor, Black men elected a king (New England Historical Society).
- The first known name comes from New London, Connecticut, in 1749. A man named Hercules served as governor there, and he is known from his wife Florio's gravestone (Connecticut State Library). Black election days in Massachusetts may have begun earlier, in 1741 (Wikipedia, "Negro Election Day") (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- In Connecticut the Black election came the day after the white men's election, and the celebration went on for a week. People dressed in their best clothes, some borrowed from the people who enslaved them. The enslaver of the man elected was expected to pay for food, decorations and liquor (Connecticut State Library).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans began celebrating their independence on its first anniversary, 4 July 1777, and within a few years they also celebrated George Washington's birthday. Neither was a national holiday yet. In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official holiday. Congress, and then President Washington, asked Americans to keep days of thanksgiving on dates they chose, but nobody set a yearly date. Older customs changed too. Boston's leaders turned Pope's Night into a protest against British taxes, and General Washington banned it in his army. In the South, enslaved people got a few days off at Christmas, while those who worked in the house served their enslavers' guests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night, 1764 to 1775" -->
- On Pope's Night, 5 November 1764, one of the Pope wagons in Boston ran over and killed a boy named Brown. Sources give his age as five to nine. The surviving records do not give his first name or say who was driving. The death happened early in the day, and the parades and the fight between the gangs went on that night (J. L. Bell, *Boston 1775*).
- In August 1765, crowds in Boston protesting the Stamp Act, a British tax on printed paper, used the Pope's Night customs: figures hanged and burned, marches and bonfires. They broke into three or four large houses and smashed the furniture. Ebenezer Mackintosh, a shoemaker and captain of the South End gang, led crowds at the protests (J. L. Bell).
- On 5 November 1765, Mackintosh and Henry Swift, captain of the North End gang, agreed to march together instead of fighting. Wealthy merchants paid for a dinner for them (J. L. Bell).
- On 5 November 1775, General George Washington ordered his soldiers not to keep "that ridiculous and childish Custom of burning the Effigy of the pope." He wrote that the Americans were asking Catholic Canadians to join them, and that insulting their religion at such a time was wrong (Washington, General Orders, 5 November 1775, Founders Online). Pope's Night ended in Boston during the Revolution (Old North Church).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Fourth of July, 1777" -->
- On 2 July 1776, the Continental Congress voted for independence. The next day John Adams wrote to his wife Abigail that the day "ought to be solemnized with Pomp and Parade, with Shews, Games, Sports, Guns, Bells, Bonfires and Illuminations from one End of this Continent to the other." Congress formally adopted the Declaration of Independence on 4 July, and Americans celebrate that date (Massachusetts Historical Society. National Park Service).
- In July 1776, crowds greeted the public readings of the Declaration with parades, bonfires and gunfire. The first planned Fourth of July celebrations came a year later, in Philadelphia and in Boston (Mount Vernon).
- In Philadelphia on 4 July 1777, the armed ships in the Delaware River were decorated with flags. At one o'clock each ship fired thirteen cannon, one for each state. That night bells rang, fireworks went up on the city's common, and people lit their windows (*Pennsylvania Evening Post*, 5 July 1777, quoted by Mount Vernon).
- In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official state holiday (Mount Vernon).

### Henry Wight
> **Who:** A Congregational minister and Revolutionary War veteran who started a Fourth of July celebration that has been held every year since. · **When and where:** Bristol, Rhode Island, 1785 to the 1820s.
- Henry Wight was born in Medfield, Massachusetts, on 26 May 1752. He left his studies at Harvard to serve in the Revolutionary War. One account says he served as a soldier, and another says he served as a surgeon (New England Historical Society. Rev War Bristol).
- In January 1785 the First Congregational Church of Bristol, Rhode Island, which had 36 members, hired him as its minister (New England Historical Society).
- Bristol stands on Mount Hope, Montaup in the Pokanoket language, where the Pokanoket Wampanoag lived and where Metacom had his base in King Philip's War. Metacom was killed there on 12 August 1676, in an ambush led by the English captain Benjamin Church. After the war four Boston merchants bought the land, as part of Plymouth Colony, and started the town (Wikipedia, "Bristol, Rhode Island"). `native-nations` tells the war.
- Bristol people had already fired cannon on the Fourth. In 1777 they fired 13 cannon at dawn, one for each state (New England Historical Society).
- In 1785, his first year in Bristol, Wight led the town's first Patriotic Exercises on the Fourth of July. He gave a speech that honored the veterans and celebrated the new country's freedoms (New England Historical Society. Bristol Fourth of July Committee).
- He was Bristol's minister for 43 years and spoke at the Fourth of July for about 40 of them (search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only). He died in 1837 (Rev War Bristol).
- Bristol has held the celebration every year since 1785. The town calls it the oldest continuous Fourth of July celebration in the United States. Its parade probably grew out of the walk to the Patriotic Exercises in the early 1800s (Bristol Fourth of July Committee. Town of Bristol).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-wight-holidays" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving days for the new country" -->
- After the American army forced a British army to surrender at Saratoga, New York, in October 1777, the Continental Congress set 18 December 1777 as a day of thanksgiving for the whole country. Samuel Adams, Richard Henry Lee and Daniel Roberdeau wrote the order (search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only). The date is from US House of Representatives History, Art and Archives.
- Congress also named days of fasting in 1775, 1776, 1778, 1779, 1780, 1781 and 1782, and days of thanksgiving in 1777, 1778, 1781 and 1783 (US House History).
- In 1789 Elias Boudinot, a member of the House of Representatives from New Jersey, asked Congress to request a day of thanksgiving for the new Constitution. Both houses agreed. On 3 October 1789, President George Washington named Thursday, 26 November 1789, as a day of thanksgiving (Mount Vernon).
- Washington sent his order to the state governors and asked them to announce it. On the day, he went to church at St. Paul's Chapel in New York City and sent beer and food to the men held in the city jail for their debts (Mount Vernon). Washington named another day of thanksgiving in 1795, to give thanks that the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania was over (Mount Vernon). `drugs-alcohol` tells the rebellion and the army Washington sent against it. None of these became a yearly holiday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Washington's birthday" -->
- On 22 February 1778, at the army's winter camp at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, soldiers gathered to wish Washington a happy birthday (Mount Vernon). Musicians from Proctor's artillery regiment played for him outside his quarters (National Park Service, via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). In 1779 people in Milton, Massachusetts, held a public celebration of the day (Mount Vernon).
- Washington was born on 11 February 1731 by the old calendar. In 1752 Britain and its colonies changed to the calendar used today, and his birthday became 22 February 1732 (search summary of Mount Vernon and National Park Service pages) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas on the plantations" -->
- At Mount Vernon, George Washington's plantation in Virginia, most enslaved workers had four days off at Christmas. They used the days to rest and to visit family on Mount Vernon and on nearby plantations. Enslaved cooks, housemaids and waiters had more work than usual over Christmas, because the Washingtons had extra guests (Mary V. Thompson, Mount Vernon).
- Philip Vickers Fithian, a young tutor from New Jersey, spent Christmas 1773 at Nomini Hall, the Virginia plantation of Robert Carter. He wrote that guns fired all around the house woke him that morning (Fithian's journal, 25 December 1773).
- Before he was dressed, several enslaved men and boys came to his room to wish him a merry Christmas and to ask for a "Christmas Box," a small gift of money. They were Nelson, a boy who made his fire and blacked his shoes, a man who lit the schoolroom fire, Tom the coachman, and Dennis, a boy who waited at table. Fithian gave them five bits in all, about three shillings (Fithian).
- `slavery-freedom` tells what the Christmas days off meant for enslaved people in the 1800s. `drugs-alcohol` tells how enslavers used whiskey at Christmas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black governors in wartime Hartford, 1776" -->
- Black governors kept being elected in Connecticut through these years. In Hartford, London was governor in 1755 and was enslaved by Thomas Seymour. Quaw was governor in 1760 and was enslaved by George Wyllys, the secretary of the colony. Cuff was governor from 1766 to 1776 (Connecticut State Library).
- The governors had little power of their own. A Black governor had no power to punish a white person who wronged a Black person (Andy Piascik, Connecticut History).

### John Anderson
> **Who:** An enslaved man who became Hartford's Black governor in 1776, in a dispute that white officials investigated as a possible plot. · **When and where:** Hartford, Connecticut, 1776 to 1780.
- In May 1776, white men in Connecticut elected Jonathan Trumbull governor again. Soon after, Cuff, the Black governor of Hartford, resigned and named John Anderson as governor in his place, without an election. A journal entry by a Hartford man, Major French, dated 11 May 1776, records it (Chris Menapace, Digital Farmington).
- Anderson was enslaved by Philip Skene, who had been a major in the British army. Skene was being held as a prisoner because the Americans suspected he was loyal to Britain (Menapace. Andy Piascik, Connecticut History).
- White people in Hartford feared that Skene planned to use Anderson to bring Black people over to the British side. A committee of the Connecticut government investigated (Menapace. Piascik).
- The two sources disagree on what the committee found. One says Skene had tried to pay voters, and that many Black people refused to back a Loyalist's slave. Skene told the committee it was done for amusement (Menapace). The other says the committee cleared Anderson but found that he had paid some Black people to support him, and that the government approved his four-year term (Piascik).
- Anderson served as governor until 1780 (Piascik).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these years Christmas changed from a noisy street holiday into a day for families and children, and a poem printed in 1823 gave Americans their picture of St. Nicholas. Thanksgiving was still a New England day that each governor set. Sarah Josepha Hale, a magazine editor, began asking governors and presidents to make it one national day. Enslaved people in the South got a few days off at Christmas, and on New Year's Day many were hired out or sold away from their families. Free Black Americans made their own days of celebration: 1 January 1808, when bringing enslaved people from Africa became illegal, 5 July 1827 in New York, and 1 August after 1834. In 1811 Albany's city council banned Pinkster, the spring holiday of the Black people of the Hudson Valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas becomes a children's day" -->
- In the early 1800s, Christmas in American towns still meant heavy drinking, begging from door to door and rowdy parades. Wealthier families and newspaper editors worked to turn it into a quiet family day centred on children (Mass Moments).
- On 23 December 1823 the *Troy Sentinel*, a newspaper in Troy, New York, printed a poem with no author's name, "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas." It begins "'Twas the night before Christmas" and describes stockings hung by the chimney for St. Nicholas to fill (the newspaper page, Wikisource).
- Clement Clarke Moore, a New York professor, was named as the author in 1837 and put the poem in his own book in 1844. The family of Henry Livingston Jr., a New York farmer and poet, says he wrote it as early as 1808. The question has never been settled (St. Nicholas Center).
- A few years after the poem, Catharine Maria Sedgwick of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, published a story about children finding a Christmas tree with gifts around it. Shopkeepers began advertising gifts for children (Mass Moments).
- By the 1840s several states had made Christmas a legal holiday (Mass Moments). Alabama is often called the first state, in 1836, but Alabama's state archivists searched and found no law from that year. In 1848 Alabama's lawmakers made Christmas a bank holiday, a day when payments on loans were not due (Alabama Department of Archives and History, quoted by Snopes).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and New Year's Day for enslaved people" -->
- Enslavers in the South gave most enslaved workers a few days off at Christmas. On Edwin Epps's cotton plantation in Louisiana in the 1840s it was three days. Other enslavers gave four, five or six (Solomon Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*, 1853).
- Northup wrote that the planters on Bayou Boeuf took turns holding a "Christmas supper" for enslaved people from nearby plantations, and that three to five hundred came. White people came to watch them eat. Northup called Christmas "three days in the year," and the rest "days of weariness, and fear, and suffering, and unremitting labor" (Northup). `slavery-freedom` tells how Northup was kidnapped and enslaved.
- Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. He wrote in 1845 that the days from Christmas to New Year's were holidays, and that enslavers gave them because it "would be unsafe to deprive them of it." He called the holidays "safety-valves" that let off the anger that could lead enslaved people to rebel (Douglass, *Narrative*, 1845). `drugs-alcohol` tells how enslavers used whiskey during these days.
- In Edenton, North Carolina, enslaved men from the plantations marched through town on Christmas morning in costume, in groups Harriet Jacobs called the "Johnkannaus." They beat a drum made of a box covered in sheepskin, sang songs they had made up over the month before, and asked at every door for money or rum (Harriet Jacobs, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl*, 1861).
- In Jacobs's town, 1 January was hiring day. On that day enslavers rented enslaved people to other white men for the coming year. The next day the enslaved person had to go to the new master. A person who refused was whipped or jailed until they agreed. After the hiring, people were sold (Jacobs).
- Jacobs wrote that on one New Year's Day she watched a mother lead her seven children to the auction block. A slave trader bought all seven children, and a man from the town bought the mother. Jacobs did not give the mother's name. On another New Year's Day, Jacobs's friend Fanny was sold at auction to pay her enslaver's debts. A buyer took Fanny, and another master far away bought her four daughters (Jacobs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Days Black Americans chose to celebrate" -->
- On 1 January 1808 a federal law made it illegal to bring enslaved people into the United States from other countries. That day Absalom Jones preached a thanksgiving sermon at St. Thomas's African Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. He asked Black Americans to keep 1 January every year as a day of thanks, to teach their children "the history of the sufferings of our brethren, and of their deliverance" (National Park Service). Enslavers inside the country kept buying and selling people after the law. `slavery-freedom` tells that trade.
- On 31 March 1817 New York's lawmakers voted to end slavery in the state on 4 July 1827. On that day about 4,600 people were freed (Historical Society of the New York Courts).
- Black New Yorkers held church services on 4 July 1827 and paraded on 5 July. A meeting in Albany led by Nathaniel Paul had voted to celebrate on the 5th, because the 4th was the day white citizens celebrated. People feared that white crowds celebrating the Fourth would attack them. In New York City, between 2,000 and 4,000 people marched behind their marshal, Samuel Hardenburgh (*Freedom's Journal*, 13 July 1827, as cited by Wikipedia, "Fifth of July (New York)").
- On 1 August 1834 the British government ended slavery in its West Indian colonies. Black Americans in New York City held a celebration that day, and 1 August became their largest yearly freedom celebration in the North for about thirty years, with parades, picnics and speeches against American slavery (Oberlin Heritage Center. Jeffrey Kerr-Ritchie, *Rites of August First*).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster banned in Albany, 1811" -->
- Pinkster, the spring holiday that enslaved and free Black people in the Hudson Valley had kept since the 1700s, drew large crowds to Albany, New York. White city leaders worried about so many Black people gathering. In 1811 the Albany Common Council, the city's governing board, banned the celebration (Friends of Schuyler Mansion).
- The ban forbade selling liquor from tents on Pinkster days and gathering to dance or parade, with a fine of ten dollars or jail (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and the Fourth in the new states" -->
- Thanksgiving in these years was a New England custom. Each governor named the day each year, and most people in other regions did not keep it (Massachusetts Historical Society).
- In the early 1800s people in New York, Philadelphia and Richmond held balls, dinners and parades on Washington's birthday, 22 February. It was not yet an official holiday anywhere (Mount Vernon).
- John Adams and Thomas Jefferson, two of the men who signed the Declaration of Independence, both died on 4 July 1826, fifty years to the day after it was adopted (Mount Vernon).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-josepha-hale" name="Sarah Josepha Hale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Josepha Hale
> **Who:** A magazine editor who asked governors and presidents for about thirty years to make Thanksgiving one national holiday. · **When and where:** Philadelphia and New England, 1827 to 1879.
- Sarah Josepha Hale was born in Newport, New Hampshire, on 24 October 1788. Her husband, David Hale, died after nine years of marriage and left her with five children. She wrote poems and books to support them. One of her poems for children was "Mary Had a Little Lamb" (National Women's History Museum).
- In her 1827 novel *Northwood* she described a New Hampshire Thanksgiving dinner with roast turkey at the head of the table and "the pumpkin pie" in the place of honour (Massachusetts Historical Society).
- In 1837 she became editor of *Godey's Lady's Book*, a women's magazine read across the country. She held the job until 1877 (Plimoth Patuxet).
- She used the magazine and her letters to ask for one Thanksgiving Day for the whole country, on the same Thursday in every state. The sources disagree on when she began: in the 1830s (Plimoth Patuxet), in 1846 (National Women's History Museum), or about 1848, since she wrote in 1863 that she had asked "for the last fifteen years."
- She sent her articles to the governors of every state and territory. She wrote to presidents too, and the first four she wrote to said no (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Some Southern leaders saw Thanksgiving as a Northern holiday. Governor Henry Wise of Virginia wrote back to her that he wanted nothing to do with "this theatrical national claptrap of Thanksgiving." He meant Northern ministers who preached against slavery on the day (Atlas Obscura, citing historian Melanie Kirkpatrick).
- Hale supported ending slavery, and later she backed a plan to send freed Black Americans to Africa. She opposed votes for women (National Women's History Museum).
- On 28 September 1863 she wrote to President Abraham Lincoln and asked him to proclaim the last Thursday in November as a national Thanksgiving. Five days later he did (her letter, Library of Congress, transcribed on Wikisource. Lincoln's proclamation, 3 October 1863).
- Presidents still had to proclaim the day each year. In May 1877, at 88, her eyes were so weak that a helper wrote her letters. She asked George Washington Warren of Massachusetts to write a resolution for Congress making the last Thursday in November a legal holiday (her letter to George Washington Warren, 3 May 1877, Massachusetts Historical Society). She died in 1879.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1863 and 1894 the United States got its first national holidays. President Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving in 1863, and in 1870 Congress made New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Thanksgiving and Christmas holidays for federal workers in Washington, D.C. Congress added Washington's birthday in 1879, Decoration Day in 1888 and Labor Day in 1894. Freed Black Americans made their own days. In Texas they celebrated 19 June, the day in 1865 when a Union general announced that slavery had ended there. In Charleston, South Carolina, they reburied Union prisoners of war and decorated their graves. White mobs attacked some of these celebrations. In 1891 a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men, and the next year President Benjamin Harrison proclaimed the first national Columbus Day. In 1890 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs ordered the government's Indian schools to celebrate the anniversary of the law that broke up reservation land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving for the whole country, 1863" -->
- In the 1850s some Southern leaders called Thanksgiving a Northern holiday. In 1856 the Richmond *Whig*, a Virginia newspaper, wrote that the day cost working men a day's pay and led to drunkenness (Atlas Obscura).
- On 28 September 1863 Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. She asked him to proclaim the last Thursday in November as a national day of thanksgiving, because getting every state to pass a law "would require years" (her letter, Library of Congress).
- On 3 October 1863 Lincoln did it. Secretary of State William Seward wrote the proclamation, and Lincoln signed it. It asked Americans everywhere to keep the last Thursday in November as a day of thanks and to pray for "all those who have become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers" in the war (Lincoln's proclamation, *Collected Works*).
- After 1863 every president proclaimed a Thanksgiving Day each year. Most chose the last Thursday in November. Andrew Johnson chose the first Thursday in December in 1865, and Ulysses S. Grant the third Thursday in November in 1869 (Congressional Research Service). In Texas, Governor Oran Roberts, who had been a Confederate officer, refused to proclaim Thanksgiving into the 1880s (Atlas Obscura).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal holidays, 1870 to 1888" -->
- On 28 June 1870 Congress made New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Christmas Day and the president's thanksgiving day holidays for federal workers in Washington, D.C. Bankers and businessmen in the city had asked for the law. Representative Burton Cook of Illinois said it would match the holidays of the states around the District (Congressional Research Service).
- Congress added Washington's birthday, 22 February, in January 1879, mainly so that banks would close that day (Congressional Research Service). Massachusetts had made Christmas, Washington's birthday and the Fourth of July state holidays in 1856. Irish Catholic voters in the state helped pass that law (Mass Moments).
- Federal workers outside Washington, D.C., did not get paid holidays until a law of 6 January 1885 (Congressional Research Service).
- In 1888 Congress made Decoration Day, 30 May, a holiday for federal workers in the District. A large number of them were Union veterans who had lost a day's pay to march in the Decoration Day ceremonies (Congressional Research Service).
- On 3 January 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, *Harper's Weekly* printed a drawing by Thomas Nast, an immigrant from Bavaria in Germany. It showed Santa Claus giving out presents in a Union army camp, dressed in a coat of stars and striped trousers. Santa holds a toy of the Confederate president, Jefferson Davis, with a rope around its neck (Smithsonian Magazine).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Juneteenth, 1865" -->
- On 19 June 1865, two months after the Confederate armies in Virginia surrendered, Union general Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas, with about 1,800 soldiers, according to historian Henry Louis Gates Jr. Other accounts give about 2,000 (`slavery-freedom`). He issued General Orders No. 3: "all slaves are free." The same order told freed people to stay in their homes and work for wages for their former enslavers. It said they would not be allowed to gather at army posts (General Orders No. 3, quoted by Gates and by the Texas State Historical Association).
- About 250,000 people were enslaved in Texas. More than 150,000 of them had been brought there during the war by enslavers from other Southern states, who wanted to keep them out of reach of the Union army (Gates, citing historian Leon Litwack).
- Many enslavers did not tell the people they held that they were free until the harvest was in. One formerly enslaved Texan remembered an enslaver who did not tell them "til a whole year after we were" (Texas slave narratives, quoted by Prairie View A&M University). In Galveston the mayor, a former Confederate, forced freed people back to work (Gates, citing historian Elizabeth Hayes Turner).
- Susan Merritt, who had been enslaved in Texas, said that right after freedom she saw many Black people hanging from trees in the Sabine River bottom. She said white men caught them swimming across the river and shot them. She did not name them, and no record names the killers (Gates, quoting Litwack). Katie Darling kept working for her former mistress for six more years, and said the woman whipped her after the war just as before (Gates).
- The Freedmen's Bureau, the federal office set up to help freed people, did not reach Texas until September 1865 (Gates).
- Between 1865 and 1868 Texas had 939 recorded murders. In 379 of them, white people killed Black people. The count comes from a committee of the Texas constitutional convention of 1868 (Texas State Historical Association). Outlaw gangs and armed groups such as the Ku Klux Klan attacked freed people and Union supporters (Texas State Historical Association).
- Freed people in Texas celebrated the day a year later, on 19 June 1866, and they called it Juneteenth (Gates). Some early celebrations were political meetings where freedmen learned about their new right to vote. In some towns, officials made Black people hold their celebrations outside the town. Black Texans raised money to buy land of their own for the day and named many of these places Emancipation Park (Texas State Historical Association).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-yates-holidays" name="Jack Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Yates
> **Who:** A Baptist minister, born enslaved, who led Black Houstonians in buying land for their Juneteenth celebrations. · **When and where:** Virginia, then Houston, Texas, 1828 to 1897.
- John Henry Yates, called Jack, was born enslaved in Gloucester County, Virginia, on 11 July 1828. His enslaver's child taught him to read, which was against the law. He married Harriet Willis, who was enslaved on a nearby farm, and they had eleven children (Texas State Historical Association).
- About 1863 Harriet's enslaver moved to Matagorda County, Texas. Yates did not want to be separated from his wife and children, so he asked to go too (Texas State Historical Association). The Emancipation Park Conservancy says he had already gained his freedom and went back into slavery to stay with his family.
- When freedom came in June 1865, the family moved to Houston. Yates hauled goods by day and preached at night and on Sundays. In 1866 he became the first pastor of Antioch Missionary Baptist Church, the first Black Baptist church in Houston (Texas State Historical Association).
- In 1872 Yates, Richard Allen, Richard Brock and Elias Dibble paid $800 for ten acres in Houston for the city's Black people to hold their Juneteenth celebrations. The members of Antioch Baptist Church and Trinity Methodist Episcopal Church raised the money (Texas State Historical Association. Wikipedia, "Emancipation Park (Houston)").
- They named it Emancipation Park. It is the oldest park in Houston. During parts of the years of legal segregation it was the only public park in the area that Black people were allowed to use (Wikipedia, "Emancipation Park (Houston)"). Juneteenth celebrations are still held there (Emancipation Park Conservancy).
- Yates bought land for his own house in 1869 and started a school for Black children, Houston Academy, in 1885. He died on 22 December 1897 (Texas State Historical Association).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-yates-holidays" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom celebrations, and the attacks on them" -->
- When the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863, Frederick Douglass and other Black leaders in the North held celebrations at midnight (Gates).
- Eleven years earlier, on 5 July 1852, Douglass had spoken in Rochester, New York. He asked, "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?" He answered that it showed an enslaved person "the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim" (Douglass, quoted by Gates).
- In April 1866 Congress passed a Civil Rights Act over President Andrew Johnson's veto. On 16 April Black residents of Norfolk, Virginia, marched to celebrate, with Black Union soldiers in uniform carrying their guns. Someone fired a shot, and a white Confederate veteran was shot and killed in the chase that followed. The records do not say who fired either shot (Library of Virginia).
- White men then attacked Black people. They killed at least two Black people, badly wounded six, and destroyed Black people's property. The attacks went on through the night until United States Army soldiers stopped them. The records do not give the names of the dead (Library of Virginia).
- Joseph T. Wilson, a Black Union veteran, printed his side of the story in his Norfolk newspaper, the *True Southerner*. After that issue came out, white men broke into his printing office and destroyed the press (Library of Virginia).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Decoration Day: who started it" -->
- Several towns claim to have held the first day for decorating soldiers' graves. The National Cemetery Administration, the federal office that runs the national military cemeteries, lists these claims.
- In October 1864 three women in Boalsburg, Pennsylvania, decorated soldiers' graves (National Cemetery Administration).
- In Charleston, South Carolina, the Confederate army had held Union prisoners of war at the Washington Race Course, a horse-racing track. At least 257 of them died there and were buried in a mass grave behind the grandstand (National Cemetery Administration). Historian Adam Domby writes that the prisoners suffered mistreatment while they were held (College of Charleston).
- In April 1865 freed people and Black workmen dug up the bodies and reburied each man in his own grave. They built a fence and an arch that read "Martyrs of the Race Course" (College of Charleston). On 1 May 1865 freed people and Northern white allies held a ceremony there. Historian David Blight writes that as many as 10,000 people came, including 3,000 Black schoolchildren carrying flowers and singing "John Brown's Body" (College of Charleston).
- On 25 and 26 April 1866 white women in Columbus, Mississippi, and Columbus, Georgia, held days to put flowers on the graves of Confederate and Union dead. Mary Ann Williams of the Ladies Memorial Association in Columbus, Georgia, wrote the newspaper letter that asked for the day (National Cemetery Administration). Supporters of the Confederacy kept their own separate Memorial Day in the South (College of Charleston).
- On 5 May 1868 General John A. Logan, head of the Grand Army of the Republic, the organization of Union veterans, ordered its members to decorate the graves of Union soldiers on 30 May 1868. The first national Decoration Day was held that day at Arlington National Cemetery in Virginia, on land that had been Confederate general Robert E. Lee's estate (National Cemetery Administration).
- Historians still disagree about which of these came first and whether the later ceremonies copied the Charleston one (College of Charleston. National Cemetery Administration). `war` tells the Civil War dead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Labor Day, 1882 to 1894" -->
- Union workers in New York City held the first Labor Day parade on Tuesday, 5 September 1882. The city's Central Labor Union planned it (US Department of Labor).
- The Department of Labor says two men have been credited with the idea: Peter J. McGuire of the carpenters' union and Matthew Maguire, a machinist. It says recent research favours Maguire (US Department of Labor).
- Oregon made Labor Day a state holiday on 21 February 1887. By 1894, 23 more states had done the same (US Department of Labor).
- A senator introduced the bill for a national Labor Day in August 1893. The Senate passed it in June 1894 and the House on 26 June. President Grover Cleveland signed it on 28 June 1894 (US House of Representatives History). It gave federal workers the first Monday in September off (Congressional Research Service).
- Cleveland signed it in the first days of a national railroad strike. On 26 June the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs, had started refusing to run trains with Pullman sleeping cars, to support Pullman factory workers who were on strike. On 3 July Cleveland sent federal soldiers to Chicago to break the strike (Wikipedia, "Pullman Strike". `work-workers`). Cleveland left no letter or statement that explains why he signed it.
- `work-workers` tells the Pullman strike and the people killed in it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day, 1892, and the New Orleans lynching" -->
- In October 1890 someone shot and killed David Hennessy, the police chief of New Orleans. Police arrested many Italian Americans, and 19 were charged. Nine were tried. The jury found six not guilty and could not agree on the other three. No solid evidence had been shown against them (Smithsonian Magazine. Wikipedia, "1891 New Orleans lynchings").
- The next day, 14 March 1891, thousands of people gathered in the city. A lawyer, William S. Parkerson, told them to "set aside the verdict." Parkerson led an armed group into the city jail with three other men, Walter Denegre, James D. Houston and John C. Wickliffe (Wikipedia, citing historian Richard Gambino).
- They killed eleven Italian American men. They shot and clubbed nine to death inside the jail. They hanged Emmanuele Polizzi from a lamppost and Antonio Bagnetto from a tree and shot them (Wikipedia).
- The eleven were Antonio Bagnetto, James Caruso, Loreto Comitis, Rocco Geraci, Joseph Macheca, Antonio Marchesi, Pietro Monasterio, Emmanuele Polizzi, Frank Romero, Antonio Scaffidi and Charles Traina. Three had been found not guilty. Three had had a mistrial. Five had never been tried (Wikipedia. Smithsonian Magazine).
- A grand jury said it could not identify the killers and charged no one. Italy's government called its ambassador home. President Benjamin Harrison's government paid $25,000 to the dead men's families (Wikipedia).
- On 21 July 1892 Harrison proclaimed 21 October 1892, the 400th anniversary of Columbus's landing in the Americas, a national holiday. He asked schools to lead the celebrations and to fly the flag over every schoolhouse (Proclamation 335, American Presidency Project).
- The proclamation does not mention Italy or New Orleans. Some historians say Harrison meant the holiday partly to calm Italy and Italian Americans after the lynching (Wikipedia, citing Gambino).
- `native-nations` tells what Columbus's voyages did to the people who already lived in the Americas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Holidays in the government boarding schools" -->
- In the 1880s and 1890s federal officials sent Native children to government boarding schools to make them give up their nations' languages and ways (`native-nations`, `education`).
- In 1890 Thomas J. Morgan, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, ordered the schools to celebrate 8 February as "Franchise Day." It was the anniversary of the Dawes Act of 1887, the law that broke up reservation land into family farms. Morgan wrote that it could be the day "the Indians may strike out from tribal and reservation life." His rules also told the schools to celebrate the national holidays (search summaries of Morgan's circular) (unconfirmed: search summary only).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="holidays" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1908 to 1941 presidents and Congress added new national days and fixed the dates of old ones. Anna Jarvis's campaign made Mother's Day a national day in 1914, and she spent the rest of her life fighting the businesses that sold flowers and cards for it. After the First World War, Americans marked 11 November as Armistice Day, and in 1938 Congress made it a legal holiday dedicated to peace. From 1934 presidents proclaimed Columbus Day each October. Stores used the holidays to sell. Macy's department store began its Thanksgiving parade in 1924, and in 1939 President Franklin Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving a week earlier to give stores more shopping days before Christmas. So many people objected that Congress fixed the date by law in 1941.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mother's Day, 1908 to 1914" -->
- Other Americans had proposed a day for mothers before Anna Jarvis did. In 1872 Julia Ward Howe, who wrote "The Battle Hymn of the Republic," started a Mothers' Peace Day. In 1904 Frank Hering of the Fraternal Order of Eagles gave a speech calling for a national day to honour mothers (National Geographic).
- On 8 May 1914 Congress passed a joint resolution naming the second Sunday in May as Mother's Day. The next day President Woodrow Wilson asked Americans to fly the flag that day "as a public expression of our love and reverence for the mothers of our country" (Proclamation 1268, American Presidency Project). The law did not give anyone a day off, because the day was a Sunday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="anna-jarvis" name="Anna Jarvis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anna Jarvis
> **Who:** The woman who campaigned to create Mother's Day and then fought the businesses that made money from it. · **When and where:** West Virginia and Philadelphia, 1905 to 1948.
- Anna Jarvis was born in Webster, West Virginia, on 1 May 1864, during the Civil War. Her mother, Ann Reeves Jarvis, ran Mothers' Day Work Clubs that nursed sick and wounded soldiers from both armies (National Park Service).
- Her mother died in 1905. To honour her, Anna Jarvis began asking for a national day for mothers. She chose the second Sunday in May because her mother had died near that date (National Park Service. National Geographic).
- On 10 May 1908 she sent 500 white carnations to her mother's church in Grafton, West Virginia, for the first Mother's Day service. She spoke that day at a Mother's Day gathering in the Philadelphia department store of John Wanamaker. National Geographic reports that 15,000 people came and were each given a carnation (National Park Service. National Geographic).
- She wrote letters to business owners, politicians, ministers and newspaper editors across the country. In 1914 Congress and President Wilson made the second Sunday in May a national Mother's Day (National Park Service).
- Florists raised the price of carnations before Mother's Day, and card companies sold printed Mother's Day cards. Jarvis wanted people to write their own letters and visit their mothers. She wrote in the 1920s that she did not want Mother's Day to become a "burdensome, wasteful, expensive gift day" (National Geographic. National Park Service).
- In 1925, at 61, she was arrested for disturbing the peace at a convention of the American War Mothers, a group that sold carnations for Mother's Day (History Facts. WCHS-TV).
- She filed lawsuits against businesses that used the name "Mother's Day" and spent her money on the fight (History Facts. National Geographic). In 1943 she started a petition to end the holiday. That year she was placed in the Marshall Square Sanitarium, a hospital in West Chester, Pennsylvania, where she stayed until she died. The sources do not say who placed her there. People connected with the flower and greeting-card businesses paid her bills there (Wikipedia, "Anna Jarvis", citing historian Katharine Antolini).
- She died on 24 November 1948, at 84, with no money left. She never had children (National Park Service. National Geographic).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anna-jarvis" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Armistice Day, 1919 and 1938" -->
- The fighting in the First World War stopped at 11 o'clock in the morning on 11 November 1918, under an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting. In November 1919 President Wilson asked Americans to mark the first anniversary as Armistice Day, with parades, public meetings and a short stop in work at 11 a.m. (US Department of Veterans Affairs).
- By 1926 the lawmakers of 27 states had made 11 November a holiday. That year Congress asked the president to call for flags and for ceremonies of "friendly relations with all other peoples" (US Department of Veterans Affairs).
- On 13 May 1938 Congress made Armistice Day a legal federal holiday, "dedicated to the cause of world peace." By then all 48 states kept the day. Organizations of First World War veterans backed the law (US Department of Veterans Affairs. Congressional Research Service).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving, stores and the date fight" -->
- On 27 November 1924 employees of Macy's department store in New York City marched in costume to the store with floats and animals from the Central Park Zoo. At the end, Santa Claus arrived. Macy's made the parade a yearly event (Wikipedia, "Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade").
- In 1939 the last Thursday in November fell on 30 November. President Franklin Roosevelt worried that a short shopping season before Christmas would slow the country's recovery from the Great Depression. He moved Thanksgiving a week earlier, to 23 November, to help stores (National Archives. Congressional Research Service).
- The governors of 32 states followed him, and the governors of 16 states kept the last Thursday. For two years Americans kept two different Thanksgivings (National Archives).
- In 1941 Congress set Thanksgiving by law as the fourth Thursday in November. Roosevelt signed it on 26 December 1941 (National Archives).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day, 1905 to 1937" -->
- Angelo Noce, an Italian immigrant in Denver, asked Colorado to honour Columbus. Colorado's governor, Jesse McDonald, proclaimed the first state Columbus Day in 1905 (Wikipedia, "Columbus Day").
- The Knights of Columbus, a Catholic men's organization, and Generoso Pope, an Italian American leader in New York City, asked Congress for a national day. On 30 April 1934 Congress asked the president to proclaim 12 October each year as Columbus Day, with flags on government buildings. President Roosevelt did so that September, and again in 1937 (Proclamations 2101 and 2253, American Presidency Project. Wikipedia).
- It was a day of flags and school ceremonies, and federal workers did not get the day off (Congressional Research Service). Many Italian Americans kept it as a celebration of their own heritage (Wikipedia).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas, Memorial Day and Labor Day" -->
- On Christmas Eve 1923 President Calvin Coolidge lit the first National Christmas Tree, a 48-foot fir from Vermont, near the White House. Washington, D.C.'s public schools had asked for it, and about 3,000 people watched (National Park Service).
- The 1894 Labor Day law gave the day off only to federal workers. Into the 1930s unions told their members to strike if their employers made them work that day (Wikipedia, "Labor Day", citing economist Jay Zagorsky).
- Juneteenth celebrations grew smaller in the early 1900s. Schools taught that the Emancipation Proclamation had ended slavery and did not teach Juneteenth. In the Great Depression of the 1930s many people moved to cities to find work, and employers there would not give them the day off (Boston Public Library).
- On Memorial Day 1937 Chicago police shot and killed ten people marching in support of striking steelworkers. `work-workers` tells it.
- Federal officials in the 1920s still used rules against Native dances and ceremonies. `religion` tells how Commissioner of Indian Affairs Charles Burke's orders of 1921 and 1923 let agents stop them.
