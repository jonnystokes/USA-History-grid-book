## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
No overseas immigration yet. Immigration here means newcomers arriving into an inhabited continent, not an empty one — hundreds of Native nations and hundreds of languages already here. Population north of Mexico in 1492 is genuinely debated: about 3.8 million is a common scholarly figure, with estimates running from about 1 million to as high as 18 million (Denevan; Fixico — no settled number).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The land bridge and the Norse" -->
The first people to reach this land came on foot across the ice-age land bridge — the original arrival, told in full in `exploration`. The only crossing from overseas before 1500, the Norse about 1000 at L'Anse aux Meadows, lasted about ten years and did not stay.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="immigration" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Europeans arrive but almost none stay: soldiers, explorers, sailors, and missionaries — conquest, not settlement. The land stays overwhelmingly Native all century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, and the failures" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, founded September 8, 1565 by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés with about 600 soldiers and settlers — the first enduring European town, small and often in danger (NPS). The failures show how hard staying was: the French Fort Caroline (1564) destroyed by Menéndez in 1565; the English Roanoke colony (1587) found abandoned in 1590, its people's fate unknown (NPS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="immigration" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first people who came to stay. Reasons split between religious freedom and the chance to get land or a living, and a large share of English arrivals came bound to years of unpaid labor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown, Plymouth, and indentured servitude" -->
Jamestown 1607 (the Virginia Company, then tobacco). Plymouth 1620 (about 102 aboard the *Mayflower*). Massachusetts Bay 1630 (the Winthrop Fleet, 11 ships, about 700 people) and the Puritan migration of the 1630s — roughly 20,000 to New England, out of about 80,000 who left England that decade (New England Historical Society). Dutch New Amsterdam on Manhattan, taken and renamed New York in 1664. New Sweden on the Delaware, 1638–1655. Indentured servitude: about 80–90 percent of European immigrants to the Chesapeake in the 1600s arrived under indenture; across all the colonies over the whole colonial era, roughly half to two-thirds (Smithsonian; EH.net — the two figures measure different areas). The first enslaved Africans landed in Virginia in late August 1619 — arrival that was not chosen; the telling belongs to `slavery-freedom`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Jewish refugees, 1654" -->
23 refugees from Recife, Brazil, aboard the *Ste. Catherine*, plus Jacob Barsimson who came earlier that year. Governor Peter Stuyvesant tried to expel them and the Dutch West India Company overruled him. Told as era prose, not a story card, because only Barsimson is named (DECISIONS #16). (Sources: Jewish Women's Archive; Gilder Lehrman.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson
Born 1591 in Alford, England. In the fall of 1634 she crossed on the *Griffin* with her husband William and their children, among about 200 passengers, following their minister John Cotton to Boston to worship as they believed. The colony she joined for religious freedom put her on trial in 1637 for her religious meetings and banished her; she moved to Rhode Island in 1638, then to Dutch territory, where she and several of her children were killed in a Siwanoy attack in 1643. Arrival angle only — the trial and her ideas are told in `religion` and `rights-movements`. (Sources: Mass Moments; National Women's History Museum.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne
An indentured English boy who arrived in Virginia in December 1622 on the *Abigail*, bound to Martin's Hundred plantation. His surviving letters of March–April 1623 beg his parents to buy out his indenture or send food — "I never ate anything but peas and loblollie." He was recorded among the dead at Martin's Hundred by February 1624. One of the very few ordinary colonial arrivals whose own words survive. The daily-labor angle is `work-workers`; ours is the crossing and what the promise of America actually bought him. (Sources: Virtual Jamestown transcription; Encyclopedia Virginia.)

<!-- hb-time:start id="1700-1750" order="04" chapter="immigration" label="1700 to 1750" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonial population diversifies beyond the English, and much of the arriving labor is unfree or bound.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans, Scots-Irish, and convict transport" -->
Germans from the Rhineland (the Pennsylvania "Dutch," from *Deutsch*): about 2,100 Palatines reached New York in 1709–1710, and roughly 12,000 German speakers had come to Pennsylvania by 1749. Scots-Irish from Ulster: about 200,000 between 1710 and 1775, most through Pennsylvania and then down the Appalachian backcountry. French Huguenots after 1685; small Sephardic Jewish communities at Newport, New York, Charleston, and Savannah (42 Jews landed at Savannah July 11, 1733). Britain shipped about 50,000 convicts (some counts up to 60,000) to the colonies as bound labor after the Transportation Act of 1718, until 1775 — roughly a quarter of British emigrants that century (Encyclopedia Virginia). Indentured servitude continued alongside.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" name="John Peter Zenger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Peter Zenger
Born 1697 in Rumbach, in the German Palatinate. His family joined the mass Palatine migration in late 1709; they sailed for New York in April 1710, and his father died on the voyage. Thirteen-year-old John Peter arrived in June 1710 with his widowed mother and two siblings, and in 1711 was apprenticed to the printer William Bradford. The poor refugee boy became the printer whose 1735 acquittal began press freedom in America — that trial is told in `government-politics` and `news-communication`; ours is the arrival. Died 1746. (Sources: Immigrant Entrepreneurship (German Historical Institute); Encyclopedia.com.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="immigration" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Revolution slows arrivals, and the new country writes down for the first time who is allowed to become an American.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Naturalization Act of 1790, and refugees from Saint-Domingue" -->
War interrupted the flow: convict transport ended in 1775, and tens of thousands of Loyalists left during and after the war — an outflow, not an arrival. The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited naturalization to a "free white person" of good character with two years' residence — the first federal word on belonging, a racial limit not fully removed until 1952 (Library of Congress). Refugees from the Haitian Revolution (begun 1791) reached U.S. cities: more than 3,000 in Philadelphia by 1794, nearly 20,000 across the 1790s and 1800s (Smithsonian; 64 Parishes).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Hamilton (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton
> **Movie:** *Hamilton* (2020) — the filmed Broadway musical; a dramatization, not a documentary.

Born on the Caribbean island of Nevis on January 11, 1755 or 1757 (sources genuinely disagree on the year); orphaned young; a teenage trading-house clerk on St. Croix. After a hurricane wrecked the island on August 31, 1772, his published letter describing the storm (dated September 6, 1772) so impressed local men that they raised money to send him to school in North America. He sailed in late 1772 or early 1773 (accounts differ), entered King's College in New York, and stayed — the immigrant who became the first Secretary of the Treasury. His money-and-government work is told in `economy`, `money`, and `government-politics`; ours is the arrival. (Sources: National Constitution Center; Library of Congress; Gilder Lehrman.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-toussaint" name="Pierre Toussaint" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pierre Toussaint
Born about 1766 in Saint-Domingue (Haiti), enslaved. In 1787 the Bérard family brought him to New York as they fled the colony's unrest — an arrival that was not his choice, part of the refugee stream from Saint-Domingue. Apprenticed to a hairdresser, he became one of New York's most sought-after hairdressers and quietly supported the widowed Madame Bérard for years while still enslaved. Freed July 2, 1807, he took the surname Toussaint, and spent his earnings on orphans, plague victims, and the city's first Catholic charities. Died June 30, 1853; declared Venerable by the Catholic Church in 1996; buried in the crypt of St. Patrick's Cathedral. The slavery system itself is `slavery-freedom`'s telling. (Sources: New York Public Library Toussaint papers; New York Almanack.)

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first great voluntary wave — and the first organized movement against immigrants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Irish and German waves begin" -->
Legal arrival from Africa ended January 1, 1808 under the Act signed March 2, 1807 (the earliest date the Constitution allowed); smuggling continued — that story is `slavery-freedom`'s. Irish arrivals surged with the Great Famine (1845–1852): about a million died in Ireland, nearly two million left in that decade, and more than 1.5 million Irish arrived at U.S. ports between 1845 and 1855, most settling poor in northeastern cities. Germans became the largest immigrant group from about 1840 — roughly six million came between 1820 and World War I, pushed by crop failures and, for a few thousand ("Forty-Eighters"), the failed democratic revolutions of 1848. Ports: New York chiefly, also Boston and New Orleans. Anti-Irish and anti-Catholic feeling grew through the 1840s; the organized Know-Nothing movement peaked in the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="patrick-kennedy-bridget-murphy" name="Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy
Patrick Kennedy, born 1823 on a farm at Dunganstown, County Wexford, left famine Ireland in 1848 or 1849 (accounts differ). He worked as a cooper — a barrel maker — in East Boston, and in September 1849 married Bridget Murphy, who had made the same crossing from Wexford. They had five children. Patrick died of cholera on November 22, 1858, at 35, leaving Bridget a widow with four young children. Their youngest son's grandson, John F. Kennedy, became president in 1961 — a famine immigrant's great-grandson. (Sources: JFK Library; NPS.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="patrick-kennedy-bridget-murphy" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jette-bruns" name="Jette Bruns" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jette Bruns
Henriette "Jette" Geisberg Bruns, born 1813 in Oelde, Westphalia (Germany). In 1836 she arrived at the Westphalia Settlement in central Missouri with her husband Bernhard, a doctor sold on the promise of cheap land, plus their baby son, her two brothers, and a maid. For more than fifty years she wrote letters home to her brother Heinrich — published as *Hold Dear, As Always* (University of Missouri Press, 1988) — recording frontier sickness, grief, work, and the long strangeness of a new country. Widowed and left nearly penniless during the Civil War, she ran a boardinghouse across from the Missouri state capitol. Died 1899 in Jefferson City. (Sources: State Historical Society of Missouri; Univ. of Missouri Press.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jette-bruns" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The largest wave yet, and the first door closed by law against a named nationality.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Castle Garden, Ellis Island, and the Chinese Exclusion Act" -->
Castle Garden at Battery Park was the immigrant depot from August 1855 to April 1890, processing about 8 million of roughly 11 million arrivals through New York; Ellis Island opened January 1, 1892 as the federal station. German arrivals peaked — over 950,000 in the 1850s, about 220,000 in 1854 alone — and the nativist Know-Nothing (American) Party swept Massachusetts in 1854, then split over slavery. Chinese immigration began with the Gold Rush — about 325 in 1849, some 20,000 in 1852, about 105,465 Chinese in the country by 1880, most from Guangdong. The Page Act of 1875 was the first restrictive federal immigration law; the Chinese Exclusion Act, signed May 6, 1882, was the first U.S. law to bar a group by nationality — renewed and widened, repealed only in 1943. New sources: Italians, Poles, Hungarians, Greeks, and more than two million Jews from the Russian Empire between 1881 and 1914, fleeing pogroms after 1881. The Statue of Liberty was dedicated October 28, 1886; Emma Lazarus (New York-born, 1849–1887) wrote "The New Colossus" in 1883, and the plaque mounted inside the pedestal in 1903 is how the statue became a symbol of welcome. In 1860 the schooner *Clotilda* landed captives illegally at Mobile — the last known slave ship; that telling belongs to `slavery-freedom`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-schurz" name="Carl Schurz" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Schurz
Born 1829 near Cologne; joined the failed German revolution of 1848–49 and fled when Prussia crushed it; immigrated in 1852 and settled in Watertown, Wisconsin. Union general, first German-born U.S. senator (Missouri, 1869–1875), Secretary of the Interior (1877–1881); died 1906. Sits here by arrival date (1852); the writer may note the 1848 hinge with the era before. (Sources: Britannica; Senate Bioguide.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-schurz" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="annie-moore" name="Annie Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Annie Moore
First immigrant processed at Ellis Island, January 1, 1892 — age 17, from County Cork aboard the *Nevada*, with her two younger brothers; presented a $10 gold piece. For a century the celebrated "Annie Moore who went west" was the wrong woman; genealogist Megan Smolenyak's 2006 search found the real Annie, who never left New York's Lower East Side, married a fish-market clerk, and died in 1924 at 50. Statues stand at Ellis Island and Cobh, Ireland. (Sources: NPR 2006; Smolenyak.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="annie-moore" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="wong-kim-ark" name="Wong Kim Ark" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Wong Kim Ark
Born in San Francisco in 1873 to Chinese immigrant parents; worked as a cook. Returning from a visit to China in August 1895 on the steamship *Coptic*, he was refused permission to land in his own birthplace — the exclusion laws applied to him, said the government, because his parents were Chinese. Held aboard ship while his lawyers filed suit, he took the fight to the Supreme Court and won: on March 28, 1898, the Court ruled 6–2 that anyone born in the United States is a citizen. The door-closing angle is ours; the case as law is `government-politics`, the fight against discrimination `rights-movements`. (Sources: National Archives; Supreme Court Historical Society.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wong-kim-ark" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="irving-berlin" name="Irving Berlin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Irving Berlin
Born Israel Beilin in 1888 in the Russian Empire (accounts differ on the place; the family gave May 11 as his birthday). His only memory of Russia was watching their house burn during anti-Jewish violence. The family of a poor cantor arrived at Ellis Island on September 14, 1893, when he was five. From singing for pennies on the Lower East Side he became the country's most successful songwriter — "White Christmas," "Easter Parade," and "God Bless America," the unofficial anthem written by an immigrant. Died 1989, aged 101. (Sources: Library of Congress; JewishGen; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="irving-berlin" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The wave peaks, then the gates shut — and stay shut through the years people most needed to get out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ellis Island's peak, Angel Island, and the 1924 quotas" -->
Ellis Island's busiest year was 1907: 1,004,756 admitted, 11,747 on the single busiest day (April 17); about 12 million passed through from 1892 to 1954. The phrase "melting pot" spread from Israel Zangwill's play of that name, which opened in Washington October 5, 1908. Angel Island opened in 1910 in San Francisco Bay to enforce the exclusion laws — detentions from days to as long as two years, about 175,000 Chinese processed 1910–1940, poems carved into the barrack walls and saved after a ranger found them in 1970. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Immigration Act of 1924 tied quotas to old censuses — Italy's yearly allotment fell from over 42,000 to fewer than 6,000, nearly all Asian immigration was barred, the total cap sat near 165,000, and the great wave ended. The MS *St. Louis*, May 1939: 937 passengers, almost all Jewish refugees, refused by Cuba, the United States, and Canada; 254 were later killed in the Holocaust. The Displaced Persons Act of 1948 was the first U.S. refugee law. Mexican arrivals grew with the Revolution (about 350,000 in the 1910s), reversed in the Mexican Repatriation of 1929–1936 (roughly 500,000 pressured or forced out, many U.S. citizens), then the Bracero Program brought contract farmworkers from August 1942.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-capra" name="Frank Capra" movie="Frank Capra's American Dream (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frank Capra
> **Movie:** *Frank Capra's American Dream* (1997) — a documentary about him, hosted by Ron Howard.

Born Francesco Capra in 1897 in Bisacquino, Sicily. In 1903, when he was six, the family crossed in the cheapest steerage berths of the steamship *Germania*, passed through Ellis Island, and rode a train across the country to Los Angeles. He became one of Hollywood's most honored directors — three Academy Awards for directing in the 1930s, then *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946). (Sources: Britannica; TCM.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-capra" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="doukenie-bacos" name="Doukenie Bacos" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Doukenie Bacos
Doukenie Babayanie Bacos, born 1904 to a Greek family in Thrace. A teenager determined to leave, she sailed from Piraeus on the *King Alexander* and reached Ellis Island on January 5, 1921, at sixteen (parts of her record also give 1919 — the dates in the file disagree). Traveling underage, she heard older girls whisper she might be sent back, and later said she thought of jumping into the water rather than return. She was admitted, and told her whole story to the Ellis Island Oral History Project in 1991 — one of nearly 2,000 recorded immigrant voices. (Sources: Ellis Island Oral History Project interview EI-049; Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="doukenie-bacos" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-puey-you" name="Lee Puey You" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lee Puey You
Arrived 1939 as a "paper daughter," faced an interrogation of about 170 questions, was detained on Angel Island roughly 20 months — among the longest known — and was deported in November 1940 ("cried a bowlful of tears"). She returned in 1947, after the exclusion law's repeal, and settled in San Francisco. (Source: Judy Yung, "A Bowlful of Tears Revisited.")
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-puey-you" -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The doors reopen and the map of where immigrants come from is redrawn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hart-Celler, refugee waves, and IRCA" -->
The Bracero Program ran to 1964 — about 4.5 million short-term contracts for Mexican farmworkers, labor invited in but not asked to stay. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler) ended the national-origins quotas and rebuilt the system on family ties and skills; over the following decades the main sources of immigration shifted to Asia, Latin America, Africa, and the Caribbean. Cuban waves followed Castro's 1959 revolution, including the Mariel boatlift of April–October 1980 (~125,000). Southeast Asian refugees came after 1975 — Vietnamese, Hmong, Cambodian, Laotian; nearly 281,000 Vietnamese resettled 1981–1990 alone. The Refugee Act of 1980 built the permanent refugee system. IRCA, signed November 6, 1986, legalized about 2.7 million long-term unauthorized residents and set the first employer penalties.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="antonio-garcia-bracero" name="Antonio García" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Antonio García
Born June 13, 1937 in Tepehuanes, Durango, Mexico; left school after fourth grade to work. At seventeen he had his military ID card altered to say eighteen so he could join the bracero program; his first contract sent him to the cotton fields of Texas, where he fell seriously ill within a week. He worked under contracts on and off into the early 1960s in California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas, passing through the crowded contracting centers at Chihuahua and Empalme. His oral history was recorded in Chicago in 2003 for the Bracero History Archive — one of more than 700 such interviews; whether he settled permanently in the United States the record does not say. (Source: Bracero History Archive / UTEP Institute of Oral History, interview March 12, 2003.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="antonio-garcia-bracero" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="tung-trinh" name="Tung Trinh" movie="Bolinao 52 (2008)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tung Trinh
> **Movie:** *Bolinao 52* (2008)

1988: 110 people aboard a boat out of Vietnam whose engine failed, 37 days adrift, more than 20 ships passing without helping, 52 survivors landed at Bolinao in the Philippines; Tung Trinh resettled in Orange County, California. (The bank flags cannibalism during the drift — kept out of the prose; tell it as danger, survival, and rescue.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tung-trinh" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="reinaldo-arenas" name="Reinaldo Arenas" movie="Before Night Falls (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Reinaldo Arenas
> **Movie:** *Before Night Falls* (2000) — a drama based on his autobiography, with Javier Bardem as Arenas.

Born 1943 near Holguín, Cuba; a writer whose books were banned at home and who was imprisoned by the Castro government for his writing and for being gay. In May 1980 he got out through the port of Mariel, one of about 125,000 Cubans in the boatlift. He kept writing in New York, finishing his life story, *Before Night Falls*, while sick with AIDS; he ended his life in December 1990, at 47. Hard parts told plainly: persecution, exile, illness. (Sources: Britannica; Duke University Press; EBSCO.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="reinaldo-arenas" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sergey-brin" name="Sergey Brin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sergey Brin
Born 1973 in Moscow. His Jewish family faced blocked careers and discrimination in the Soviet Union; they got exit permission in 1979 — the peak year of Soviet Jewish emigration, about 51,000 people — and arrived in the United States on October 25, 1979, when Sergey was six. His father taught mathematics at the University of Maryland; his mother became a NASA researcher. In 1998 Sergey Brin and Larry Page founded Google. (Sources: EBSCO; Immigrant Learning Center; Wikipedia cross-checked.)

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The foreign-born share climbs back toward its early-1900s peak, and the argument over immigration becomes one of the country's loudest. Current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A near-record foreign-born share" -->
About 47.8 million foreign-born people, 14.3 percent of the population, in the 2023 American Community Survey — the highest share since 1910 (14.7 percent), near the all-time high of 14.8 percent in 1890, against a low of 4.7 percent in 1970. Largest origins in 2023: Mexico about 11 million, India 3.2 million, China 3 million, the Philippines 2.1 million, Cuba 1.7 million (Pew). The unauthorized population estimated near 11–12 million, with real uncertainty. DHS created after 9/11 (Act of 2002; operating 2003). DACA from June 15, 2012. Voluntary African and Caribbean immigration grew fast — the sub-Saharan African immigrant population roughly tripled from 2000 to about 2.5 million by 2024 (Migration Policy Institute).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="giannis-antetokounmpo" name="Giannis Antetokounmpo" movie="Rise (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Giannis Antetokounmpo
> **Movie:** *Rise* (2022) — a Disney film about the Antetokounmpo family's journey; a dramatization made with the family.

Born 1994 in Athens to Charles and Veronica Antetokounmpo, Nigerian immigrants without legal status in Greece. Because Greece grants no citizenship by birth, Giannis grew up stateless, selling sunglasses and watches on the street with his brothers. Greece granted him citizenship in 2013, weeks before the Milwaukee Bucks drafted him at 18 — his arrival in America. He became a two-time NBA Most Valuable Player and led Milwaukee to the 2021 championship, and his parents and brothers joined him in the United States. (Journalism-sourced: Britannica; EBSCO; NBC.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="giannis-antetokounmpo" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lost-boys-of-sudan" name="Peter Dut and Santino Chuor, Lost Boys of Sudan" movie="Lost Boys of Sudan (2003)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Peter Dut and Santino Chuor, Lost Boys of Sudan
> **Movie:** *Lost Boys of Sudan* (2003) — an Emmy-nominated documentary that follows these two young men themselves.

Peter Nyarol Dut and Santino Majok Chuor, Dinka teenagers orphaned by Sudan's civil war, survived a walk of hundreds of miles and years in Kenya's Kakuma refugee camp. In 2001 they were among roughly 4,000 "Lost Boys" resettled in the United States. The documentary follows their first year: jobs, night classes, loneliness, and small footholds — Santino in Houston, Peter leaving for Kansas to chase a high-school diploma. (Sources: the film; PBS POV.)
