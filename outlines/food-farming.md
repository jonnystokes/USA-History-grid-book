## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Farming here was already old and skilled. Corn had been bred by Native farmers over thousands of years from a wild grass, and it fed towns; each region ate from what its land did best. The crops that would later feed the whole world were already growing in American fields.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Three Sisters" -->
Corn, beans, and squash planted together, each helping the others: the corn stalk is the pole the beans climb; the beans' roots feed nitrogen into the soil all three share; the squash's wide leaves shade the ground, hold water, and crowd out weeds. Eaten together, corn and beans also make a more complete food. Corn itself was bred from teosinte — a grass with a few hard seeds — starting about 9,000 years ago in southern Mexico, then carried and adapted north over millennia. A plant so changed by farmers that it cannot reseed itself without people. (`land-environment` tells the soil side; the food and the technique are ours.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Regional foodways" -->
- The East had invented farming on its own before corn arrived: squash, sunflower, goosefoot, and other local crops domesticated thousands of years ago — the Eastern Agricultural Complex, one of the world's few independent birthplaces of farming.
- Corn fed real towns: Cahokia held roughly 10,000–20,000 people around 1100.
- The Plains ate bison; the Northwest ate salmon; the Great Lakes peoples harvested manoomin — wild rice, "the food that grows on water," gathered by canoe and still central to Ojibwe life today; the coasts ate shellfish; the Northeast tapped maple.
- No named farmer survives from this era at story level, and the book does not invent people; the era carries the wide view and the spans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Food crossed the ocean in both directions and changed what the whole world eats — historians call it the Columbian Exchange, after the 1972 book that named it. People here still ate as they had; the century's food story is what got carried, and by whom.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What left, what arrived, who carried it" -->
- Out of the Americas: corn, potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, peanuts, peppers, chocolate, vanilla, turkeys — corn and potatoes alone became staple food for much of Europe, Africa, and Asia.
- In: wheat, rice, sugarcane, cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens, horses, honeybees. De Soto's expedition landed in Florida in 1539 with a herd of pigs as a walking food supply; escapees became the Southeast's wild hogs. Spanish colonists at St. Augustine (1565) planted wheat and citrus.
- Honest framing: this was not a friendly trade. Europeans carried American crops home from lands they were invading, and sugarcane came to be grown by enslaved people. Africans — most brought in chains — carried okra, watermelon, black-eyed peas, yams, African rice, and the knowledge to grow them; scholars now credit enslaved Africans as the carriers of those crops, not just cargo.
- Thin era for American eating itself, honestly: the exchange is the story, and its harvest lands in every era after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="food-farming" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists arrived not knowing how to feed themselves here. Native crops and Native teaching kept the first colonies alive — and then Virginia planted a crop nobody can eat, and grew it instead of food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corn saves the colonies" -->
Jamestown's Starving Time, winter 1609–10: with Powhatan food trade cut off, about 500 colonists fell to roughly 61 by spring — they ate horses, dogs, rats, leather, and there is evidence some ate the dead. Corn — bought, seized, and finally grown — kept the colony alive. At Plymouth, the colonists' first corn harvest in autumn 1621 is why there was a second year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco instead of dinner" -->
John Rolfe's tobacco (first successful crop 1612) paid so well that Virginia planters grew it instead of food, and the colony's own assembly had to force balance — the 1629–30 inspection law capped each family at 2,000 tobacco plants per member, partly so corn would still get planted. The first cash crop, and the first time American farming chased money instead of meals. (The money is `economy`'s; the labor system is `slavery-freedom`'s.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What colonists ate" -->
Corn learned from Native cooks — hominy, samp, johnnycakes — plus beans, pork once hogs multiplied, and cider. Corn dishes stayed on American tables for centuries. (The 1621 harvest gathering as a holiday is `holidays`' story.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="squanto-food-farming" name="Squanto (Tisquantum)" movie="Squanto: A Warrior's Tale (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Squanto (Tisquantum)
> **Who:** Patuxet man who taught the Plymouth colonists to grow corn — after surviving kidnapping and slavery himself. · **When and where:** Patuxet/Plymouth, Massachusetts; kidnapped 1614, returned 1619, taught the colonists spring 1621, died 1622. · **Movie:** *Squanto: A Warrior's Tale* (1994) — Disney, heavily fictionalized; say so.
Kidnapped in 1614 by an English captain to be sold in Spain; got free, learned English, and came home in 1619 to find his whole town dead of an epidemic. In spring 1621 he showed the Plymouth colonists how to plant corn, and interpreted between them and the Wampanoag. The famous detail — burying fish with the seed — is genuinely disputed: he did teach it, but a 1975 study in *Science* argued fish fertilizer may have been a European trick he picked up abroad, not a Native practice, and historians have argued it since. Tell what is sure, and say what is argued.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Farming settled into regions: rice in the Carolina Lowcountry, wheat in the middle colonies, mixed farms in New England, tobacco in the Chesapeake. The richest of those crops stood on African knowledge — carried here by people who had not chosen to come.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rice, built on African skill" -->
Rice made Lowcountry planters some of the richest people in the colonies — the famous strain came to be called Carolina Gold. The fields were built and worked by enslaved Africans, and planters paid more for captives from West Africa's rice coast *because* they knew rice: the record shows embanked tidal fields, seed pressed in with heel and toe, grain winnowed in coiled fanner baskets — African techniques in Carolina mud. How much of the whole industry rests on that knowledge is a real scholarly argument (the "Black Rice" debate): state the documented skills, and state that historians disagree about the share. The enslavement system itself is `slavery-freedom`'s subject.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The breadbasket, and the potato lands" -->
Pennsylvania, New York, and New Jersey grew wheat past their own needs and shipped flour out through Philadelphia and New York — the "breadbasket colonies." Scots-Irish settlers planted North America's first lasting potato patches at Londonderry, New Hampshire, 1719 — an American plant come home by way of Europe. Most tables held corn, pork, bread, and cider; the rich added imported tea, sugar, and wine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney
> **Who:** teenager left running three South Carolina plantations, who made indigo an American crop. · **When and where:** Wappoo plantation near Charleston; experiments from 1739, first successful dye crop 1744.
Her father was posted to Antigua and left her, at about sixteen, managing three plantations. She experimented with indigo seed for years — helped by an indigo maker brought from the Caribbean and by the enslaved workers who did the tending — and in 1744 produced the colony's first successful indigo dye, then shared seed with her neighbors. Exports went from about 5,000 pounds in 1745–46 to 130,000 pounds by 1748. Indigo is a dye, not a dinner — but it shows what colonial farming was: land, weather, borrowed knowledge, and unfree hands.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
At the first census in 1790, about nine in ten working Americans farmed. The new country's leaders built an ideal around that fact — the independent family farm as the root of freedom — and its first homegrown cookbook put corn on the printed page.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The farm as an idea" -->
Jefferson in *Notes on the State of Virginia* (printed 1785): "Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever he had a chosen people." The belief that family farms make free citizens steered American land law for the next century. Backcountry farmers, meanwhile, turned surplus corn into whiskey because a jug travels where a wagon of corn cannot (`drugs-alcohol` leads on whiskey as money and the 1794 rebellion). Farm tables stayed corn, pork, and cider.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-simmons" name="Amelia Simmons" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Simmons
> **Who:** author of *American Cookery* (Hartford, 1796) — the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens. · **When and where:** Connecticut, 1796.
On her title page she calls herself only "an American orphan." Nothing else about her life is known — say so plainly; it is part of the story that an unknown working woman wrote a founding American book. What the book did is well documented: the first printed recipes using American cornmeal — johnnycake, Indian slapjack, Indian pudding — and the first printed use of pearlash, a wood-ash salt that makes dough rise, the ancestor of baking powder. English cookbooks had ignored corn; hers cooked like America ate.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amelia-simmons" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Steel opened the prairie, cotton crowded food out of the South, and for the first time food was preserved and shipped to strangers instead of kept for the family — in cans, and packed in ice.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Breaking the prairie" -->
Prairie sod gummed up iron plows. John Deere's polished steel plow (Grand Detour, Illinois, 1837) scoured itself clean and cut it; Cyrus McCormick's reaper (first demonstrated 1831) let one family harvest many times the wheat that hand-cutting allowed. The machines belong to `technology` (their story: mccormick-deere); what they meant here is the farm itself — the grasslands of Illinois and beyond turned to corn and wheat within a generation, and a family could work far more land than their arms alone allowed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Food for strangers: cans and ice" -->
Canning crossed from France: William Underwood's Boston cannery (1821), Thomas Kensett's American patent for tin cans (1825). Food sealed in metal could be sold far from where it grew — the beginning of food as a shipped, branded thing. And New England began selling winter itself: ice cut from ponds, packed in sawdust, shipped south and abroad.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton, and the blight that crossed east" -->
Cotton spread across the Deep South and crowded out food crops; plantations often bought Midwestern corn and pork to eat (`economy` and `slavery-freedom` own cotton's money and labor). And a warning traveled the other way: potato blight broke out around Philadelphia and New York in 1843, crossed the Atlantic by 1845, and destroyed the crop of an Ireland that had nothing else — about 1 million dead in the Great Famine. American farmers lost potatoes too, but had other food. The famine ships are `immigration`'s story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederic-tudor" name="Frederic Tudor" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederic Tudor
> **Who:** the Boston "Ice King" — built a business selling New England pond ice around the world. · **When and where:** Boston; first cargo to Martinique 1806, ice to Calcutta 1833.
Boston laughed when he loaded a ship with frozen pond water in 1806 and sailed it to the Caribbean. He kept at it for decades, working out sawdust insulation and ice houses, until in 1833 he landed 180 tons of New England ice in Calcutta, India — still frozen. Ice made the icebox an American kitchen fixture and let fresh food travel; when refrigerated railcars arrive in the next era, they are Tudor's idea on wheels.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederic-tudor" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The government gave farms away by the millions of acres, cattle walked north by the millions of head, and Chicago turned animals into boxed meat for cities a thousand miles off. By the era's end, food was an industry — and farmers had started organizing to survive it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead farming" -->
The Homestead Act (May 20, 1862): 160 acres free to anyone who lived on the land and farmed it five years. Over the law's life, about 4 million claims and about 1.6 million completed farms — roughly 270 million acres, near a tenth of the country. Roughly half of claimants failed: drought, grasshoppers, loneliness, debt. The politics and prices are `economy`'s; the journeys `migration`'s; ours is what those families planted, ate, and endured (see the Oblingers below).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The cattle kingdom and the beef machine" -->
About 5.2 million Texas cattle walked north to railheads between 1866 and 1884; Joseph McCoy's yards at Abilene, Kansas (1867) made the market. In Texas as many as one cowhand in four was Black, and many were Mexican or Mexican-American. Chicago's Union Stock Yards (opened December 25, 1865) became the world's largest livestock market, and Gustavus Swift's ice-cooled railcars (engineer Andrew Chase's design, 1878) let beef killed in Chicago reach New York tables — an animal raised, killed, and eaten in three different places. Barbed wire (Glidden patent, 1874 — the invention is `technology`'s) fenced the open range and ended the drives. The cattle business's money is `economy`'s; the drovers as movers are `migration`'s; beef as food is ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farmers join together" -->
Oliver Hudson Kelley founded the Grange (1867) — the first great national farmers' organization: meeting halls, cooperative buying, and a shared voice against the railroads and middlemen who set their prices (the rate fights belong to `economy` and `big-business`).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="oblinger-family" name="Uriah and Mattie Oblinger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Uriah and Mattie Oblinger
> **Who:** a homesteading family whose ~3,000 pages of letters record the food life of a Nebraska claim. · **When and where:** Fillmore County, Nebraska; claim filed 1872, Mattie and baby Ella arrived May 1873; letters 1862–1912 (Nebraska State Historical Society / Library of Congress).
Uriah went ahead from Indiana, broke sod, and built a sod house; Mattie's letters home tell the food year from inside it — the garden and corn they planted, what they cooked and lacked, "we will all be poor here together," grasshopper plagues and blizzards and low prices. Mattie died in childbirth in 1880. Their letters are among the best-documented ordinary farm lives in American archives — the homestead story without varnish.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="oblinger-family" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-goodnight" name="Charles Goodnight" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Goodnight
> **Who:** Texas cattleman who invented the chuckwagon — the rolling kitchen of the cattle drives. · **When and where:** Texas, 1866.
Feeding a crew for months on open grassland was the drive's daily problem. In 1866 Goodnight rebuilt a surplus Army wagon with a hinged pantry box on the back that dropped flat into a work table: flour, beans, bacon, coffee, and a cook who ranked second only to the trail boss. (The *Lonesome Dove* characters are fiction partly inspired by him — an inspiration note, not a film about him.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-goodnight" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A novel forced the government into the packinghouse, tractors pushed the horses off the farm, the plowed-up Plains blew away, and by mid-century Washington was buying pigs, stamping food, and serving school lunch. The government reached the American plate in this era, and it never left.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Jungle and the 1906 laws" -->
On June 30, 1906, Theodore Roosevelt signed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act on the same day — federal inspectors in the packing plants, honest labels required, the beginning of the FDA. The push came from one book (see Sinclair below). The law as law is `government-politics`'s; the packinghouse job is `work-workers`'s; what was in the food is ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tractors, hybrids, and chickens" -->
In 1920 there were 246,000 tractors on U.S. farms — and about 26 million horses and mules still doing most of the work. The cheap Fordson (1917) and the do-everything Farmall (1924) turned that around within a generation (the machines are `technology`'s). Every retired horse freed about five acres of feed land to grow food for people instead. Henry A. Wallace founded Pioneer Hi-Bred (1926), the first hybrid seed-corn company — the seed that pays off next era. And in Delaware, a mis-shipped box of chicks started the modern chicken industry (see Cecile Steele below).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dust, pigs, and stamps: the New Deal on the farm" -->
On the plowed-up southern Plains, drought from 1931 killed the wheat and the soil blew (the soil is `land-environment`'s telling; the exodus `migration`'s) — farm families lived on gardens, canned goods, government commodities, and jackrabbits. The Agricultural Adjustment Act (May 1933) paid farmers to grow less so prices would rise: about 10 million acres of growing cotton plowed under, about 6 million pigs bought and killed — while people stood in breadlines. Public anger at that contradiction pushed surplus food to relief families and produced the first Food Stamp Program (Rochester, New York, May 16, 1939). Paying farmers became permanent policy (`economy` leads on the money).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Victory gardens, ration books, school lunch" -->
World War II put food on a war footing: sugar rationed from May 1942, coffee that November, meat, cheese, and fats from March 1943 — every person, babies included, carried a ration book of points. Americans answered with about 20 million victory gardens growing about 40 percent of the country's fresh vegetables by 1943–44. Frozen food had already reached stores (Birdseye's first retail case, Springfield, Massachusetts, March 6, 1930; the first supermarket, 1930, is `marketplace`'s story). After the war — pushed partly by how many draftees had been rejected as underfed — Truman signed the National School Lunch Act (June 4, 1946). Territories thread: on annexed Hawaii, sugar plantations ruled and James Dole's Hawaiian Pineapple Company (1901) canned fruit for mainland tables (the workers' arrivals are `immigration`'s; the islands' politics `america-world`'s).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="upton-sinclair-food-farming" name="Upton Sinclair" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Upton Sinclair
> **Who:** novelist whose book about Chicago meatpacking forced the first federal food-safety laws. · **When and where:** Chicago packinghouses (undercover ~7 weeks, 1904); *The Jungle* published February 1906.
He wrote *The Jungle* to show how immigrant packinghouse workers were used up; readers instead fixed on what his pages said was going into the meat. Within months, Congress passed and Roosevelt signed both 1906 food laws. Sinclair's own verdict, written that year: "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach." (`news-communication` tells the muckrakers as journalism; the food is ours.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="upton-sinclair-food-farming" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-washington-carver-food-farming" name="George Washington Carver" movie="George Washington Carver: An Uncommon Life (2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Washington Carver
> **Who:** agricultural scientist who taught worn-out cotton country to feed its own soil and itself. · **When and where:** born enslaved near Diamond, Missouri, about 1864 (exact date unknown); Tuskegee Institute, Alabama, 1896–1943. · **Movie:** *George Washington Carver: An Uncommon Life* (Iowa PBS, 2018).
Cotton had drained Southern soil. Carver taught crop rotation — peanuts, cowpeas, and sweet potatoes to feed nitrogen back, then cotton — through 44 free plain-language bulletins and a mule-drawn demonstration wagon that brought the lessons to farmers who could not come to him. His most famous bulletin (1916) gave 105 ways to cook the peanuts he was asking farmers to grow. Say the true thing and unsay the false one: he did not invent peanut butter — peanut pastes are older than the myth, and the patents belong to others. His real work was soil, self-reliance, and teaching poor farmers, Black and white.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-washington-carver-food-farming" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="cecile-steele" name="Cecile Steele" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Cecile Steele
> **Who:** Delaware farm wife whose mis-shipped chick order started the commercial chicken industry. · **When and where:** Ocean View, Delaware, 1923.
She ordered 50 chicks; the hatchery shipped 500. She raised them indoors through the winter and sold them at 16 weeks for 62 cents a pound — and the profit was so good she ordered 1,000 on purpose the next year, 10,000 by 1926. By 1928 some 500 Delmarva farms were raising "broilers." Chicken had been a Sunday luxury; the industry that grew from her accident made it the cheapest meat on the American plate.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cecile-steele" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="food-farming" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Food got cheaper, faster, and farther from the farm than it had ever been. In 1954, for the first time, tractors outnumbered horses and mules on American farms; farms kept getting fewer and bigger (6.8 million in 1935, falling all era); hybrid corn tripled yields; and dinner came from a drive-through window or a freezer tray. At the era's end came the bill — a farm debt crisis — and the first organized push back toward the local and the organic.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fast food and the freezer" -->
The McDonald brothers rebuilt their San Bernardino stand in 1948 as the "Speedee Service System" — 15-cent hamburgers assembled like factory parts (see Kroc below for what happened next). Swanson's aluminum-tray "TV dinner" went on sale in 1953 at about 98 cents — over 10 million sold in 1954 — and who invented it is honestly disputed: a salesman claimed the idea for decades; the owning family and the company's own scientist said otherwise. Supermarkets, freezers, and highways carried all of it (`marketplace` sells it; `transportation` moves it; the food is ours).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Industrial agriculture, and the people in the rows" -->
Fertilizer, pesticides, irrigation, and hybrid seed pushed yields up era-long; chicken and hogs moved indoors; the Green Revolution carried the same package abroad (see Borlaug below), fed hundreds of millions, and hooked farming on chemicals — both true. The hands that picked the cheap produce organized: Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta, and the farmworkers' movement are `work-workers`' story (their slug chavez-huerta); this chapter keeps one sentence at the table's edge.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1980s farm crisis" -->
After the 1970s export boom came the reckoning: Midwest farmland lost up to 60 percent of its value in the early-to-mid 1980s, farm debt hit about $215 billion by 1984, roughly 300,000 farms defaulted and about 300 rural banks failed. On September 22, 1985, Willie Nelson, John Mellencamp, and Neil Young played Farm Aid to 80,000 people in Champaign, Illinois, raising over $7 million — a concert that has run ever since. The finance is `economy`'s; the lost family tables are ours (see the Jordans below).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organic, from fringe to federal seal" -->
J.I. Rodale had preached compost-and-soil farming since founding *Organic Farming and Gardening* magazine in 1942; by the 1980s the movement was big enough to regulate, and the Organic Foods Production Act (1990) ordered national standards — the USDA organic seal followed in 2002. Alice Waters put the same idea on a restaurant plate (below).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="norman-borlaug" name="Norman Borlaug" movie="The Man Who Tried to Feed the World (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Norman Borlaug
> **Who:** Iowa-born plant scientist whose wheat is credited with saving hundreds of millions from famine — the "Green Revolution." · **When and where:** Mexico from 1944; India and Pakistan in the 1960s; Nobel Peace Prize 1970. · **Movie:** *The Man Who Tried to Feed the World* (PBS American Experience, 2020).
From 1944 he bred wheat in Mexico for a hunger-fighting program, crossing thousands of strains until he had short-strawed, disease-resistant wheat that yielded several times the old crop. Mexico grew its own bread by 1956; in the 1960s his seed broke famine predictions in India and Pakistan. He won the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize — the only farm scientist ever to. He is often credited with saving a billion lives; tell it as the common estimate, not a count — and tell the criticism too: the same seed-fertilizer-irrigation package tied world farming to chemicals and thinned out old crop varieties.

### Ray Kroc
> **Who:** milkshake-machine salesman who turned the McDonald brothers' hamburger stand into the world's biggest restaurant chain. · **When and where:** San Bernardino visit 1954; first franchise Des Plaines, Illinois, April 15, 1955; bought the brothers out 1961. · **Movie:** *The Founder* (2016).
Kroc sold the brothers their milkshake machines, saw the line out their door, and became their franchise agent. He opened his own McDonald's in 1955, built the system nationwide, and in 1961 bought the brothers out for $2.7 million. The brothers said a handshake promise of royalties was never honored, and Kroc opened a McDonald's near their original stand — the record supports telling the hard edges plainly; the film dramatizes them.

### Russel and Mary Jane Jordan
> **Who:** an Iowa farm couple who sold off their livestock and machinery at auction to pay the bank — filmed by their own daughter. · **When and where:** near Rolfe, Iowa; farm in the family 125 years; auction in the mid-1990s, in the long shadow of the 1980s crisis. · **Movie:** *Troublesome Creek: A Midwestern* (1995) — Sundance Grand Jury Prize; Oscar-nominated documentary about this family.
Carrying about $220,000 in debt out of the crisis years, the Jordans learned their bank wanted its money. Rather than lose everything, they sold the cattle, the machinery, even household goods at auction and rented out the land — keeping the farmhouse and their name on the deed. Their daughter Jeanne Jordan filmed the whole year. One documented family standing for the roughly 300,000 farms the crisis took.

### Alice Waters
> **Who:** cook who built a restaurant on local farms and put a garden in a public school — the farm-to-table idea's best-known voice. · **When and where:** Chez Panisse, Berkeley, California, opened 1971; Edible Schoolyard founded 1995. · **Movie:** *Alice Waters and Her Delicious Revolution* (PBS American Masters, 2003).
Chez Panisse served whatever local farms had that was ripe — fresh, seasonal, organically grown — and the idea spread from one Berkeley kitchen into a national movement. In 1995 she started the Edible Schoolyard at a Berkeley middle school: students grow, cook, and eat their own food as part of school. The counter-argument to the drive-through, made with a garden.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans now argue about food the way earlier Americans argued about land: what is in it, who grows it, who profits, and who can reach it. Fewer than 2 million farms remain (1.9 million in the 2022 census, the fewest since before the Civil War), most Americans have never worked one — and food is politics, health, identity, and work all at once. Current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eating and reaching food" -->
- Adult obesity reached 41.9 percent (2017–March 2020, CDC), easing to 40.3 percent by 2021–23; diet-linked illness is a leading health burden (`health` leads on the medicine).
- Food deserts, defined: USDA maps low-income areas more than 1 mile from a supermarket in cities or 10 miles in the country — about 19 million people (about 6 percent) by the 2019 count; the number depends on the yardstick used, so state the measure.
- The other direction: farmers' markets grew from 1,755 (1994) to 8,771 (2019); farm-to-table went mainstream; food banks became permanent infrastructure. The COVID year 2020 showed the system's edges — closed packing plants, dumped milk, mile-long food-bank lines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="GMO arguments and new foods" -->
Over 90 percent of U.S. corn, soybeans, and cotton are genetically engineered. Both truths, stated plainly: the National Academies' 2016 review of about 1,000 studies found no substantiated evidence that approved GE foods harm health — and public argument continued anyway, hard enough that from January 2022 federal law requires "bioengineered" labels. Newer arguments arrived on schedule: plant-based burgers (Impossible 2016; Beyond's 2019 stock debut), the first U.S.-approved lab-grown chicken (June 2023), and vertical farms that boomed on investment and then stumbled (pioneer AeroFarms hit bankruptcy in June 2023, then restructured) — tell the stumble, not just the promise.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who picks the food" -->
Most hired crop workers are foreign-born. The H-2A seasonal visa grew from about 48,000 certified jobs (2005) to about 385,000 (2024) — roughly 15 percent of crop-farm employment, over 90 percent of the workers from Mexico. The organizing is `work-workers`'; the fields are ours (see Benitez below).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first farmers, still farming" -->
Native food sovereignty is a working movement, not a memory: tribal bison herds, seed banks rebuilding old corn and bean varieties, manoomin harvests defended in court and on the water, Native chefs rebuilding Indigenous cuisine. The circle back to the chapter's first page — the Three Sisters are still being planted, on purpose (`native-nations` leads on sovereignty as sovereignty).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nephi Craig
> **Who:** White Mountain Apache/Diné chef cooking Apache foods for his own community — a face of the Native food sovereignty movement. · **When and where:** White Mountain Apache lands, Arizona; 2010s–present. · **Movie:** *Gather* (2020) — documentary featuring him, forager Twila Cassadore, and student scientist Elsie DuBray.
A professional chef who came home to open a café built on Apache-grown and gathered foods, training Native cooks and treating food as recovery — from diet-caused illness and from the history that caused the diet. Documented in the film *Gather* alongside Twila Cassadore, who teaches San Carlos Apache youth to forage, and Elsie DuBray, a Cheyenne River Sioux teenager whose science project compared her nation's traditional buffalo diet with modern beef.

### Lucas Benitez
> **Who:** tomato picker who co-founded the Coalition of Immokalee Workers and helped build the Fair Food Program. · **When and where:** from Guerrero, Mexico, to Immokalee, Florida at 17; CIW founded 1993; Fair Food Program running from 2011. · **Movie:** *Food Chains* (2014) — documentary about the CIW; Benitez appears.
He picked tomatoes in the fields that supply America's winter salads and helped organize the workers who pick them. The Fair Food Program signs up big buyers — McDonald's, Walmart and others — to pay a small premium and buy only from growers who meet a worker-written code: pay floors, shade and water, protection from abuse, checked by audits. The person on the far end of the supply chain from the drive-through window.
