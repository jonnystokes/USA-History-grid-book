## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were no rights movements in this land yet. A movement means people organizing together to change a rule that governs them, and nobody here was doing that. What existed instead was hundreds of nations, each with its own arrangement of who decided what. In some of them women held formal political power, including the power to choose leaders and remove them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the clan mothers of the Haudenosaunee" -->
The Haudenosaunee are five nations in what is now upstate New York. They put the choosing of leaders in women's hands, and the Onondaga Nation, one of the five, still describes how it works.

Each clan has a clan mother. She picks the man who will speak for her clan in the Grand Council. His title is Hoyane. The sign of the office is a set of deer antlers fixed to his headdress, which in the founding account the Peacemaker put there.

She can also take the office back. If he stops representing the clan, she warns him that he is not following the ways of the Great Peace and has to change. If he still does not, the clan removes him in council and the antlers come off. The word for it is de-horned.

Women in these nations grew the food and decided how it was shared out. The National Park Service records two further powers: the final say on transfers of land, and the final say on going to war. If a husband and wife separated, the wife kept everything she had brought into the house, and the children went with her.

When the five nations agreed the Great Law of Peace that set this up is not settled. Haudenosaunee accounts and different scholars give dates anywhere from the 1100s to the late 1400s. Nobody can prove which is right.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what can be known, and what cannot" -->
Hundreds of nations lived here and they did not all do this, so there is no single Native pattern to report. Nearly everything written down about how these nations governed themselves was written later, by Europeans. The nations' own records are wampum belts and spoken account, and the Onondaga still keep theirs.

That is why no single person can be named here. No individual person's claim to a right survives from before 1500 in this land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Still no movements. What this century has instead is Spanish law sorting people into kinds, and one Spanish priest spending forty years arguing in public that Native people had rights the Spanish were taking from them. People also resisted, one at a time and in groups, and this book tells that in two other chapters rather than twice here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what Spanish law did with people" -->
The main instrument was the encomienda. The Spanish crown granted a settler the labour of a named group of Native people. On paper the settler owed them protection and Christian teaching. In practice he took their work and what they grew, and the historians of The American Yawp write that the holders of these grants brutalised the people tied to them.

Spanish officials abolished the encomienda in 1542, in a set of rules called the New Laws, and replaced it with another labour system called the repartimiento. Forced labour went on. A rule written in Spain did not reach a mine in the Indies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bartolome de Las Casas argues in public" -->
Bartolomé de Las Casas was a Spanish priest who had held an encomienda himself and gave it up. He spent the rest of his life telling the Spanish crown what its settlers were doing to Native people, and in 1550 he argued the question in front of a panel at Valladolid, in Spain, against a scholar named Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda. Sepúlveda's case was that Native people were by nature fit to be ruled by force. Las Casas's case was that they were not, and that everything done to them on that basis was theft and murder.

This is as close as the 1500s come to a rights argument. It was two Spaniards arguing in Spain about people who were not in the room.

One more thing has to be said about Las Casas, because leaving it out would make him into something he was not. In 1516 he asked the crown to ship enslaved Africans to the Indies to take the Native people's place in the mines and fields. He got what he asked for. The Atlantic slave trade had already been running for about sixteen years when he advised it, so he did not start it, and he did argue for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English law came to the colonies carrying a rule that took away a married woman's legal existence. There was still no movement in this century. People argued back one at a time and were punished by name for it: Anne Hutchinson was tried and banished from Massachusetts in November 1637, and Mary Dyer was hanged on Boston Common in 1660 for being a Quaker. The nearest thing to organizing came in 1688, when four men in Pennsylvania signed one paper against slaveholding and sent it up through their church meetings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="coverture: what marriage did to a woman in law" -->
The rule was called coverture. A married woman was covered by her husband in law, and the legal name for her was a *feme covert*, a covered woman. She had no separate legal existence.

That meant she did not own property, including property she had brought into the marriage herself. Any wages she earned belonged to her husband. She could not sign a contract, could not be sued, and could not bring a case in court in her own name.

An unmarried woman or a widow was a *feme sole*. She could do all of those things. It was marriage, not being a woman, that took them away.

What happened in practice was less tidy than the rule. Wives ran shops and traded, sometimes on behalf of husbands who were away, and some places let a married woman trade in her own name under an exception. The rule was still the law, and what she held she held because her husband allowed it.

The best-known statement of coverture was written much later, by an English lawyer named William Blackstone, between 1765 and 1769: "the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage." He did not invent the rule. He wrote down one that was already old when the colonies were founded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a law that made belonging to a religion a hanging matter" -->
Being a Quaker was a crime in Massachusetts. In late 1656 and 1657 the General Court outlawed what it called the cursed sect of heretics commonly called Quakers, and fined ship captains who carried them and people who owned their books. In October 1658 the court went further and barred Quakers under pain of death, which meant that a banished Quaker who came back could be hanged.

Four Quakers were hanged in Boston between 1659 and 1661. Dozens more were jailed and beaten between 1656 and 1661.

Mary Dyer met the Quaker founder George Fox in England and came back to Boston a Quaker in 1657. She was jailed as soon as she landed. Her husband got her out by promising she would not preach, and the family moved to Rhode Island. She kept going back to Massachusetts anyway, to stand with Quakers who were in prison there.

She was taken to the Boston gallows with two other Quakers, William Robinson and Marmaduke Stephenson. The two men were hanged. Dyer was made to watch, and the rope was put around her neck before she was reprieved and sent out of the colony. Two sources give different years for that day, 1658 and 1659, so no year is given here.

She came back again. On 1 June 1660 she was hanged on Boston Common. Her husband asked the colony to spare her and she refused to say she was sorry. King Charles II ordered the hangings of Quakers stopped in all his colonies, and by 1677 Friends could meet openly in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Germantown protest, 1688" -->
On 18 April 1688, four men at Germantown in Pennsylvania wrote and signed one paper against slaveholding and sent it up through their Quaker meetings. Their names were Garret Hendericks, Francis Daniel Pastorius, Derick op de Graeff and Abraham op de Graeff. It is the first protest against African slavery made by a religious body in the English colonies.

Their argument was the Golden Rule. In their own spelling, "that we shall doe to all men licke as we will be done ourselves."

Four meetings in turn read it and put off deciding: Germantown Monthly Meeting, then Dublin Monthly Meeting, then Philadelphia Quarterly Meeting, then Burlington Yearly Meeting in New Jersey, which voted to take no stand either way. The protest failed, and nothing changed because of it.

Four men writing together and pushing a demand up through a chain of meetings is the closest thing to organizing in this century. The paper was forgotten until abolitionists found it again in 1844, lost, and found once more in 2005 in a vault at the Arch Street Meetinghouse in Philadelphia. It is now at Haverford College. `slavery-freedom` carries slavery itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Anne Hutchinson
> **Who:** Put on trial for holding religious meetings in her own house, and banished from Massachusetts for it · **When and where:** Boston and Newtown, Massachusetts Bay; meetings from 1636, trial November 1637, excommunicated March 1638

She was born about 1591 at Alford in England and crossed to Boston on the *Griffin* in the fall of 1634, following her minister John Cotton.

From 1636 she held a meeting in her house once a week to go back over the Sunday sermon. It began as a meeting for women. Men started coming as well, including men who mattered in the colony. She said she could tell which ministers and which neighbours had God's grace, and she taught that grace alone decided who was saved, not good behaviour.

In November 1637 the General Court tried her at Newtown, the town now called Cambridge. It ran two days. Governor John Winthrop opened by telling her she was "called here as one of those that have troubled the peace of the commonwealth," and said her meetings were "a thing not tolerable nor comely in the sight of God nor fitting for your sex." She matched his Bible verses with her own for two days. The court could not pin the charge on her until she said God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation," and would curse the Puritans if they harmed her. The court called that blasphemy.

She told the court, "You have power over my body, but the Lord Jesus hath power over my body and soul."

Winthrop gave the sentence: "Mrs. Hutchinson, the sentence of the court you hear is that you are banished from out of our jurisdiction as being a woman not fit for our society." She asked back, "I desire to know wherefore I am banished?"

In March 1638 the church at Boston tried her a second time and put her out of the congregation. Its charge said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer; and a Magistrate than a Subject."

She left in the spring of 1638 for Aquidneck Island, in what became Rhode Island. After her husband died she moved in 1642 onto Dutch land on Long Island Sound. She and several of her children were killed there in 1643 in a Siwanoy attack. One daughter lived. Her crossing is told in `immigration`, and her theology in `religion`.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There is still no movement in these fifty years. The rights arguments of the period are made in print by single men, and the one body that starts to shift is the Quakers, who punished the members who raised the question. The first antislavery society in this land is formed in 1775, in the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a Boston judge writes three pages against slavery, 1700" -->
Samuel Sewall was a merchant and a judge in Boston. In 1700 he published a three-page pamphlet called *The Selling of Joseph*, printed by Bartholomew Green and John Allen. It is the first antislavery tract printed in America.

His argument was that taking and selling people is the crime the Bible calls man stealing, and he pointed at Exodus 21:16. He then went through the usual defences of slavery and answered them one at a time.

He wrote it because of a real case. A petition went round Boston in 1700 asking for the freedom of an enslaved man named Adam and his wife, "unjustly held in Bondage" by a merchant called John Saffin. Saffin had promised Adam his freedom after seven years of good behaviour and then would not give it.

Saffin published an answer in 1701 defending both slavery and his own conduct. Adam won his freedom in court in 1703, after three years of proceedings. Sewall answered Saffin again in 1705 by reprinting an English attack on the slave trade. Adam's court case belongs to `slavery-freedom`, which carries freedom suits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Quakers argue with themselves" -->
The one place an argument about rights kept going in this period was inside one religious society, and it went badly for the men who started it.

Ralph Sandiford published *A Brief Examination of the Practice of the Times* at Philadelphia in 1729, against slaveholding. Quaker authorities denounced it. Benjamin Lay published *All Slave-Keepers That Keep the Innocent in Bondage, Apostates* at Philadelphia in 1737. Benjamin Franklin printed it and left his own name off it. Quaker authorities denounced that too, and Philadelphia Yearly Meeting disowned Lay, which meant putting him out of the society.

In 1738, at the yearly meeting's annual business meeting at Burlington in New Jersey, Lay stood up and accused the Quaker slaveholders in the room of hypocrisy. Then he threw off his plain Quaker cloak and showed a soldier's uniform underneath it, to a room of people who had committed themselves to peace.

The meetings gave way slowly. Philadelphia Yearly Meeting told Quakers to stop buying and selling people in the 1750s, and did not require members to free the people they held until about twenty years after that. From the four signers at Germantown in 1688 to that point is roughly eighty-six years. Every man who raised it early was denounced or thrown out by his own church, and the meetings changed their rules all the same.

This era has no featured person on purpose. Sewall, Sandiford and Lay are all real and documented, and no one life carries what happened here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A country founded on the sentence that all men are created equal left out most of the people living in it, and some of them said so at the time. Two things in this era are genuinely new. People began quoting the Revolution's own words back at the men who wrote them, and the first standing organization appeared: an antislavery society formed in Philadelphia in 1775.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first antislavery society, 1775" -->
On 14 April 1775 a group of mostly Quaker men met at the Rising Sun Tavern in Philadelphia and formed the Society for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage. The Historical Society of Pennsylvania calls it the first abolition society in the western world.

Its first job was practical. Free Black people were being seized and sold as slaves, and the society took up their cases. In 1789 it got a charter from the state and a longer name, the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage, and for Improving the Condition of the African Race.

An organization outlives the people who start it, and it can go on taking one case after another for as long as it lasts. That is what the earlier protests had lacked. `slavery-freedom` carries slavery itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Jersey lets some women and some Black men vote, then takes it back" -->
The New Jersey constitution of 1776 gave the vote to "all inhabitants of this colony, of full age, who are worth fifty pounds ... and have resided within the county ... for twelve months." The word male is not in it, and neither is white.

So in New Jersey, unmarried women who owned fifty pounds voted, and so did free Black men who met the same property test. Married women could not, and the reason was coverture. A married woman could not own fifty pounds of anything, because in law whatever she had belonged to her husband. The rule from the 1600s decided which women got a ballot.

Women voted in New Jersey through the 1790s and into the 1800s. Then in 1807 the state legislature limited the vote to tax-paying white male citizens, which removed both groups in one law. The reason given at the time was party advantage: women mostly voted Federalist, and the Democratic-Republicans wanted the 1808 presidential election.

Women voted in an American state for thirty-one years, and a legislature took it back forty-one years before the Seneca Falls convention asked for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="free Black petitions for schools, Boston" -->
In 1787 Prince Hall and others petitioned the Massachusetts General Court about schools. Black families paid taxes in Boston and their children were kept out of the town's free schools. In their words, they "receive no benefit from the free schools in the town of Boston, which we think is a great grievance, as by woful experience we now feel the want of a common education."

The petition failed. A second one in 1796 failed too.

In 1798 the community stopped asking and built its own. The African School met in the house of Primus Hall, who was Prince Hall's son. What happened to that school next is in the following era. `education` leads on the schools as schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="abigail-adams" name="Abigail Adams" movie="John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abigail Adams
> **Who:** Wrote to her husband while he was helping write a new country's laws, and told him to remember the ladies · **When and where:** Braintree, Massachusetts, letters of 31 March, 14 April and 7 May 1776
> **Movie:** John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries), about John and the family. She is a lead character, not the subject.

John Adams was at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia in the spring of 1776. Abigail Adams was at home in Braintree running the farm. They wrote to each other constantly, and the letters survive at the Massachusetts Historical Society.

On 31 March 1776 she wrote this, in her own spelling: "I long to hear that you have declared an independency — and by the way in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could. If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation."

She was asking him to change coverture, the rule that gave a husband everything his wife owned and earned. The last sentence uses the colonists' own argument against Britain: no laws without representation.

He wrote back on 14 April 1776: "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh." He said the quarrel with Britain had made everyone bold: "That Children and Apprentices were disobedient — that schools and Colledges were grown turbulent — that Indians slighted their Guardians and Negroes grew insolent to their Masters." He would not give up the men's system, he said, because that "would compleatly subject Us to the Despotism of the Peticoat."

She answered on 7 May 1776: "I can not say that I think you very generous to the Ladies, for whilst you are proclaiming peace and good will to Men, Emancipating all Nations, you insist upon retaining an absolute power over Wives." She added that "Arbitary power is like most other things which are very hard, very liable to be broken."

Nothing came of it. No state changed its laws for women because of these letters, and the letters were private: they were not printed while she was alive. She was one woman writing to her husband, which is the honest measure of where this subject stood in 1776. Her farm and household are told in `home-family`.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This is the era when American rights movements start. Women who joined societies against slavery and against drink learned how a campaign is run, were attacked for speaking in public, and turned the same methods to their own position. In July 1848 about three hundred people met at Seneca Falls in New York and wrote down what women were owed. Deaf pupils were schooled together for the first time, which is where disability organizing begins.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="where women learned to organize" -->
Women in this era were not allowed to vote, hold office, or speak at most public meetings. What they could join were societies against slavery and against drink, and that is where thousands of them learned how a campaign works: how to run a meeting, take minutes, raise money, gather signatures, and print what you find.

Lucretia Mott helped found the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society in 1833. Eight years later she and Elizabeth Cady Stanton were among the American women sent as delegates to an antislavery convention in London and refused their seats because they were women. The convention that produced the first American women's rights meeting came directly out of that.

The woman born Isabella Bomfree in Ulster County, New York in 1797 took a new name in 1843. She said the Spirit had called her to preach the truth, and she called herself Sojourner Truth. She travelled Long Island, Connecticut and Massachusetts speaking and singing, and in 1844 she joined the Northampton Association of Education and Industry, a community in Massachusetts founded by abolitionists that backed women's rights and was a stop on the Underground Railroad. Her speaking tours and the speech she is remembered for begin in 1851, in the next era. `slavery-freedom` tells how she got free and how she got her son back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="coverture starts to crack, and where the first crack came from" -->
Mississippi passed the first married women's property act in the United States in 1839. It let a married woman hold property in her own name, so long as it had not come from her husband.

It came out of a court case. Betsy Love was Chickasaw. Her husband owed money to a man called John Fisher, and Fisher moved to have an enslaved man named Toney sold at auction to pay the husband's debt. Betsy Love had inherited Toney from her father and given him to her daughter Susan. In 1837 the Mississippi Supreme Court ruled that Betsy Love was the owner and that her husband had no rights over her property, because Chickasaw society passes descent and property through the mother's line, because Mississippi had recognised Chickasaw marriage contracts in 1830, and because, as the court had it, property in the Chickasaw Nation was owned by the women of the nation.

So the first crack in coverture in American law came from a Chickasaw woman, and the property being argued over was a person.

New York passed its own married women's property act in 1848, a few months before the Seneca Falls convention. Property a woman owned when she married stayed hers, was not her husband's to sell, and could not be seized for his debts. Other states copied it. It removed one part of coverture and left the rest standing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="deaf pupils in one place, and what that made" -->
The American School for the Deaf opened at Hartford, Connecticut on 15 April 1817, in Bennett's City Hotel, with seven full-time pupils. Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet founded it with Laurent Clerc, a deaf French teacher he brought back from Paris, backed by Dr. Mason Fitch Cogswell, whose deaf daughter Alice was one of the first pupils. Teaching was in sign language.

Public money came quickly. In October 1816, before the school opened, the Connecticut legislature voted it $5,000, the first public money in America for teaching disabled people. In 1819 Congress gave it 23,000 acres of public land in Alabama, and the school sold the land to pay its costs. It was the first federal money for special education.

`education` leads on it as a school. What matters here is what a school does that a family cannot. Deaf children who had been the only deaf person in their town were brought together in one building and taught in a shared language. That is where a signing community in America begins, and the disability campaigns of later years were run by people who had schools like this one behind them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="getting girls into school" -->
Getting girls taught the same subjects as boys is one of the first organized demands of this era. Girls' schools existed, but most of them taught manners, drawing and music.

Emma Willard wrote *A Plan for Improving Female Education* in 1819 and sent it to the New York state legislature. Her argument was that young women needed the subjects young men got. She opened the Troy Female Seminary in New York in 1821.

Catharine Beecher opened the Hartford Female Seminary in Connecticut on 20 May 1823 with her sister Mary. Her pupils did geography, world history, Latin, rhetoric, philosophy and the sciences. There were 100 full-time pupils within three years.

Mary Lyon started raising money in 1834 and opened Mount Holyoke Female Seminary at South Hadley, Massachusetts in 1837. She built it out of donations from six cents to $1,000, quilts and bedding from women's sewing circles, and stoves and furniture from her trustees. Tuition was $60 a year. Mount Holyoke's own account of her says she "endured ridicule from those who felt her ambitious undertaking would be 'wasted' on women," and that the idea of a college for women "was horrifying to many people in the 1830s."

College was a separate question. Oberlin in Ohio is where that changed, and it changed in two steps. Oberlin took women students alongside men from its opening in 1833, but put them in something called the Ladies Course, which ended in a diploma and not a degree. In 1837 four women entered the course that led to a bachelor's degree: Mary Kellogg, Mary Caroline Rudd, Mary Hosford and Elizabeth Prall. Three of them graduated in 1841. Kellogg had run out of money and left. Oberlin had also decided in 1835 to admit Black students, the first college in the country to make that a policy. `education` leads on the schools as schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="children shut out because they were Black" -->
Free Black families paid town taxes and their children were kept out of the town schools those taxes paid for. In Boston the families had already stopped asking and built their own school, in 1798, in Primus Hall's house. In 1808 it moved into the basement of the African Meeting House. In the mid-1830s it moved again, into the Abiel Smith School, which became the one school where nearly all of Boston's Black pupils were sent.

In the late 1840s William Cooper Nell and others organized boycotts of the Smith School and petition drives against it, and Benjamin Roberts sued the city of Boston to get his five-year-old daughter Sarah into the school nearest their house. `education` tells the Roberts case. The campaign around it is ours.

Shutting children out was not the worst of it. In Canterbury, Connecticut, a white teacher took Black pupils. The state legislature passed a law to stop her, and the town wrecked her school.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the people who attacked the organizers" -->
Organizing in this era was dangerous, and the danger came from white neighbours, ministers, lawmakers and mobs.

Women started by writing petitions. In December 1829 Catharine Beecher wrote a circular telling women they had a right to be heard by Congress about the plan to force the Cherokee and other nations off their land. More than 1,500 women signed petitions against it. Senator Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri mocked them in public. He told "these ladies" to "sit down on the way side, and wait for the coming of the conquerors." Congress passed the Indian Removal Act in 1830. `native-nations` tells what was done to the nations.

Then the petitions turned to slavery. In 1836 the House of Representatives made a rule that every antislavery petition was set aside unread. It was called the gag rule, and it lasted until 1844. In February 1838 women in Brookline, Massachusetts signed a petition calling it "an assumption of authority, at once dangerous and destructive" to government by the people. The first two names on it were Sarah and Angelina Grimké.

Maria W. Stewart was a free Black woman from Hartford, Connecticut, orphaned at five. In September 1832 she spoke at Franklin Hall in Boston to an audience of men and women. She is one of the first American women on record to speak in public. Audiences turned on her, partly because she was a woman speaking to men and partly because she told Black men off for drinking. She gave up speaking in Boston a year later. In her last speech there she asked, "What if I am a woman," and said it was no use trying to be useful in that city.

On 21 October 1835 the Boston Female Anti-Slavery Society met at its room on Washington Street. About 5,000 men gathered outside. One of the women, Deborah Weston, called it "the day when 5,000 men mobbed 45 women." When the mayor, Theodore Lyman, told them to leave, Maria Weston Chapman said, "If this is the last bulwark of freedom, we may as well die here as anywhere." The mob caught William Lloyd Garrison, put a rope on him and dragged him through the streets. The mayor locked him in the city jail overnight to keep him alive. No record found names a man in that mob.

On 7 November 1837 a mob in Alton, Illinois shot and killed Elijah Lovejoy, a minister who printed an antislavery newspaper. Mobs there had already smashed three of his printing presses. He was defending the fourth. The men tried for the attack were found not guilty.

On 17 May 1838 a mob in Philadelphia burned down Pennsylvania Hall, three days after it opened. Antislavery groups had built it for about $40,000, and a national convention of antislavery women was meeting there that week. Between 10,000 and 15,000 people watched and 200 to 300 did the burning. The mayor, John Swift, did little to stop them. Dozens of men were arrested and none was convicted.

### Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott
> **Who:** Two women who were shut out of an antislavery meeting in London and called a convention of their own eight years later · **When and where:** London 1840, then Waterloo and Seneca Falls, New York, July 1848

Lucretia Mott was born on Nantucket, Massachusetts in 1793 and raised a Quaker. She helped found the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society in 1833. Elizabeth Cady Stanton was born at Johnstown, New York on 12 November 1815.

They met in London in 1840, at the World Anti-Slavery Convention. Women had been elected as delegates and were "refused admission because of their sex." After an argument the organisers decided the women "would sit at the rear of the hall, but not participate." Stanton was there on her honeymoon. She found Mott in the women's section, and the two of them agreed to hold a women's rights convention when they got home.

It took eight years. Stanton moved to Seneca Falls in 1847 and wrote later about what pushed her: "The general discontent I felt with women's portion as wife, mother, housekeeper ... impressed me with a strange feeling that some active measures should be taken."

On 9 July 1848, five women at a tea in Waterloo, New York decided to do it. They were Stanton, Mott, Mary Ann M'Clintock, Martha Coffin Wright and Jane Hunt. They put a notice in the local paper calling people to "a Convention to discuss the social, civil and religious rights and condition of woman." That gave them ten days.

The convention met on 19 and 20 July 1848 at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, three miles from Waterloo. More than 300 people came. Stanton wrote a Declaration of Sentiments with the M'Clintock family, built on the Declaration of Independence, and read it out. It listed what men had done to women, item by item. "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise." "He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns." On schools: "He has denied her the facilities for obtaining a thorough education — all colleges being closed against her."

The line about property and wages is coverture, the rule from the 1600s. It was still in force.

One hundred people signed the declaration, 68 women and 32 men. Eleven of the resolutions passed with nobody against. The one demanding the vote was fought over and passed only narrowly, after Frederick Douglass stood up and argued for it. From that vote to the constitutional amendment that gave American women the vote is seventy-two years.

The first day was for women only. Men joined on the second day, and Lucretia Mott's husband James chaired it. Newspapers mocked the whole thing. James Gordon Bennett printed the entire declaration in his *New York Herald* to make fun of it. Some of the signers later took their names off. The records do not say how many.

### Dorothea Dix
> **Who:** Went and looked inside every jail and poorhouse in a state, wrote down what was being done to mentally ill people, and read it out to the legislature · **When and where:** Massachusetts, 1841 to 1843. Born 1802, died 1887

On 28 March 1841 Dorothea Dix went to the East Cambridge jail in Massachusetts to teach a Sunday school class. She found mentally ill women shut in with convicted criminals, in what the record calls a bare, foul-smelling, unheated jail room. Mentally ill in that century meant a person whose mind worked in a way their family or their town could not manage. Towns kept them in jails, poorhouses and houses of correction.

The jail officials denied her account, and then improved the women's conditions.

Dix spent the next two years visiting every jail, poorhouse and house of correction in Massachusetts and writing down what she saw in each one. In January 1843 she put it in a document called a memorial and handed it to the state legislature.

She wrote: "I come as the advocate of the helpless, forgotten, insane men and women held in cages, closets, cellars, stalls, pens! Chained, naked, beaten with rods, and lashed into obedience!" A stall and a pen are enclosures built for animals, a rod is a stick, and lashed means whipped.

Her notes named the places. She found a woman in a cage at Lincoln. At Medford one person was chained and another had been shut in a narrow stall for seventeen years.

Publishers reprinted the memorial as a pamphlet and newspapers printed extracts, and it caused an uproar. The legislature voted the money to enlarge the state hospital at Worcester. In 1843 there were 13 mental hospitals in the United States. By 1880 there were 123, and Dix had a direct hand in founding 32 of them.

Her method matters as much as what she found. Go and look, write down exactly what you see, publish it, and hand lawmakers a count they cannot argue with. Later campaigners used the same method. `health` tells the story of the hospitals.

### Sarah and Angelina Grimké
> **Who:** Two sisters from a slaveholding family who went north, spoke against slavery in public, and were attacked by the clergy for speaking at all · **When and where:** South Carolina, then Philadelphia and New England. The speaking tour was 1837 to 1838

Their father was a wealthy South Carolina slaveholder. Both sisters left the South, became Quakers, and went to work for the American Anti-Slavery Society, the first women the society hired as agents.

At Poughkeepsie, New York they spoke to an audience of men and women together, which almost no American woman had done. Maria W. Stewart had done it in Boston in 1832. In 23 weeks across 1837 and 1838 they spoke at 88 meetings or more in 67 towns, to more than 40,000 people face to face.

In July 1837 the Congregational ministers of Massachusetts answered them. Their Pastoral Letter, read out from pulpits across the state, said the sisters threatened "the female character with wide spread and permanent injury," and objected to "females to bear an obtrusive and ostentatious part in measures of reform." The ministers did not argue that the sisters were wrong about slavery. They objected to women speaking at all.

Sarah Grimké answered that in print the same month. The first of her "Letters on the Equality of the Sexes" ran in the *New England Spectator* in July 1837, and those letters argue about the position of women rather than about slavery.

On 21 February 1838 Angelina Grimké went to the Massachusetts State House in Boston and handed a legislative committee antislavery petitions signed by about 20,000 Massachusetts women. The crowd overflowed the room. It was the first time a woman had addressed a legislative body in American history, and she used it to make a claim about women as well as about slavery: "We are citizens of this republic and as such our honor, happiness, and well-being are bound up in its politics, government, and laws."

That was ten years before Seneca Falls. Both sisters lived to see slavery abolished, and neither lived long enough to see American women get the vote.

### Prudence Crandall and Sarah Harris
> **Who:** A pupil who asked to be taught, and the teacher who was arrested, jailed and mobbed for teaching her · **When and where:** Canterbury, Connecticut, 1832 to 1834

In the autumn of 1832 Sarah Harris asked to join the Canterbury Female Boarding School. She was Black, she had finished primary school, and her father handed out the abolitionist newspaper *The Liberator*. She wanted enough learning to teach Black children at Norwich. What she said to the teacher was, "I want to get a little more learning, if possible enough to teach colored children." She added, "If you think it will be the means of injuring you, I will not insist on the favor."

Prudence Crandall, who ran the school, admitted her. The white families took their daughters out, and the school lost its income.

In the spring of 1833 Crandall went to Boston to see the abolitionist editor William Lloyd Garrison, then travelled around New England asking Black families to send their daughters. Advertisements said the school would reopen on 1 April 1833 for "young ladies and little misses of color." About twenty-four pupils came, most of them boarding in the house.

On 24 May 1833 the Connecticut General Assembly passed what people called the Black Law. It made it illegal for a school in the state to take Black pupils from outside Connecticut without the town's permission. It was written to close this one school.

Crandall was arrested on 27 June 1833 and spent a night in jail. Her first trial ended with a jury that could not agree. At the second, in October 1833, Judge David Daggett told the jury that Black Americans were not citizens, so the constitutional protection her lawyers were arguing for did not apply to her pupils. She was convicted. On 26 July 1834 the Connecticut Supreme Court threw the conviction out on a technical point. That left the Black Law standing.

The man leading the campaign was Andrew T. Judson, a lawyer and politician who lived next door to the school and prosecuted Crandall. He told the minister Samuel J. May, "we mean there shall not be such a school set up anywhere in our State." The town went after the school the whole time. The school's opponents turned the state's vagrancy law on Ann Eliza Hammond, a sixteen-year-old pupil from Providence who arrived in April 1833. Its punishment was whipping "on the naked body not exceeding ten stripes," meaning up to ten lashes. Crandall paid so it was not carried out. Shopkeepers would not sell to it. Pupils walking outside were pelted with stones and eggs. In January 1834 someone tried to set the building on fire. On the night of 9 September 1834 armed men attacked the house while Crandall and her pupils were inside and smashed the windows. One account counts more than ninety windows broken, another five large ones on the ground floor. No one was ever charged. She closed the school the next morning.

Crandall married a Baptist minister named Calvin Philleo and moved west, to Illinois and then Kansas. Connecticut voted her a pension of $400 a year four years before she died there in 1890, aged 87. Sarah Harris became an abolitionist and a conductor on the Underground Railroad, helping people escape slavery. In 1877 she travelled to Kansas to visit Crandall, and the two of them went on writing to each other about their old pupils.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Slavery ended in this era, and the people who had campaigned against it together broke apart over who should get the vote next. Three amendments to the Constitution were added and then read narrowly by the Supreme Court, one case at a time, until very little of them was left. So this is the era when Americans learned to organize a court case on purpose: pick the law, raise the money, hire the lawyer, find someone willing to be arrested, and lose in a way that leaves an argument standing for the next generation. Susan B. Anthony did it in 1872, the Tape family in 1884, the Comité des Citoyens in 1892. All three lost. Meanwhile women got the vote first in two western territories with few settlers, Congress wrote one nationality out of citizenship, and a newspaper editor in Memphis started counting the people killed by mobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what was won, and how it was taken back" -->
The three amendments passed after the Civil War ended slavery, made people born in the United States citizens, and said the vote could not be denied on account of race. Whether Native Americans were included in that citizenship was a separate fight, and the courts had not settled it. Congress then passed the Civil Rights Act of 1875, which made it illegal to refuse anyone "the full and equal enjoyment of any of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters and other places of public amusement."

In 1883 the Supreme Court struck that act down. Its reasoning was that the 14th Amendment let Congress restrain states, not private people, so a hotel or a theatre owner could refuse whom he liked.

States then began writing separation into law. Louisiana passed Act 111 on 10 June 1890, requiring railroads to seat passengers by race in separate cars. Black New Orleanians protested it, and there were sixteen Black members of the state legislature at the time. It passed anyway. A passenger who sat in the wrong car could be fined $25 or jailed for 20 days.

Before 1877 the people who organized under those amendments were attacked, and many were killed. Mobs that included police killed 46 Black residents of Memphis in May 1866. In April 1873 armed white men killed between 62 and 153 Black men at Colfax, Louisiana. In 1877 the federal government pulled its soldiers out of the South. **`slavery-freedom` tells the killings of Reconstruction and the end of federal protection. The organizing after 1877 is told here.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a committee plans an arrest" -->
In September 1891 a group of French-speaking men of African descent in New Orleans formed the Comité des Citoyens, which means the Citizens' Committee. The full name they used was the Citizens' Committee to Test the Constitutionality of the Separate Car Law. That is what it was for. They raised money and hired a lawyer, Albion W. Tourgée.

Then they needed someone to be arrested, and the arrest had to be the right one. Homer Plessy volunteered. He had been born Homère Patris Plessy in New Orleans on 17 March 1863 to free parents, and he was a shoemaker.

On 7 June 1892 he bought a ticket to Covington, Louisiana on the East Louisiana Railroad and sat down in the car for white passengers. The railroad company knew about the plan and went along with it, because running extra cars cost money. A private detective had been hired in advance to make the arrest, so that Plessy would be charged with exactly the offence the committee wanted to argue about. Judge John H. Ferguson of the New Orleans criminal court ruled against him, which is how the case got the name it has.

The Supreme Court decided it on 18 May 1896, seven to one. Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote that laws "permitting, and even requiring, their separation, in places where they are liable to be brought into contact, do not necessarily imply the inferiority of either race to the other," and that if Black Americans felt marked as inferior by such a law, that was "solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it."

One justice dissented. John Marshall Harlan wrote: "But in view of the constitution, in the eye of the law, there is in this country no superior, dominant, ruling class of citizens." And: "Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." And: "What can more certainly arouse race hate, what more certainly create and perpetuate a feeling of distrust between these races, than state enactments?"

The ruling stood for nearly sixty years. Homer Plessy died in New Orleans in 1925, twenty-nine years before it was overturned. **`government-politics` tells the ruling as law.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a wife's wages" -->
Until 1860 a married woman in New York did not own the money she earned. Her wages belonged to her husband. That was coverture, the old rule that a wife had no legal self apart from her husband.

Women's rights campaigners set out to change it. They ran petition drives in every county of the state. They held conventions in 40 of its 60 counties and gave talks in 150 towns and villages. Susan B. Anthony gave a draft bill to a state senator, Andrew J. Colvin, and he put it to the legislature. The senate passed it on 28 February 1860 and the assembly on 20 March. Governor Edwin D. Morgan signed it. One newspaper printed the act as passed on 30 March, so the records give two dates.

The law said that "the earnings of any married woman, from her trade, business, labor or services, shall be her sole and separate property." In November 1860 the campaigners asked for more, including the vote. Then the Civil War began and the campaign stopped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the movement splits, and how it split" -->
Slavery ended, and the people who had campaigned against it together fell out over who got the vote next.

The 14th Amendment, ratified in 1868, put the word "male" into the Constitution for the first time. Section 2 counted a state's "male inhabitants" when it worked out how many seats in Congress that state got. The 15th Amendment passed Congress on 26 February 1869 and was ratified on 3 February 1870. It says the right to vote shall not be denied "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." It says nothing about sex.

The break has a date. On 12 May 1869, at the third annual meeting of the American Equal Rights Association, Frederick Douglass argued that Black men needed the vote first. Abolitionists and women's rights campaigners had built that organization together in 1866. Douglass said: "I do not see how any one can pretend that there is the same urgency in giving the ballot to woman as to the negro. With us, the question is a matter of life and death, at least, in fifteen States of the Union ... when they are in danger of having their homes burnt down over their heads; when their children are not allowed to enter schools; then they will have an urgency to obtain the ballot equal to our own."

Susan B. Anthony answered: "If you will not give the whole loaf of suffrage to the entire people, give it to the most intelligent first." Elizabeth Cady Stanton said that if "intelligence, justice, and morality are to have precedence in the government, let the question of women brought up first and that of the negro last."

That is what they said. Stanton also called Black Americans ignorant of the laws and customs of the country. Two of the best-known leaders of the women's movement argued that educated white women should get the vote before Black men, and they lost Black allies over it, Douglass and the poet and campaigner Frances Ellen Watkins Harper among them. Harper had told the same organization in 1866: "You white women speak here of rights. I speak of wrongs."

Two rival organizations came out of that meeting, both in 1869. Stanton and Anthony's National Woman Suffrage Association went after a change to the Constitution. Lucy Stone, Henry Blackwell and Julia Ward Howe's American Woman Suffrage Association went state by state and backed the 15th Amendment. They stayed apart for twenty-one years and merged in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Two of the founders' daughters helped arrange it: Alice Stone Blackwell and Harriot Stanton Blatch.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Court closes the short route" -->
Virginia Minor tried to register to vote in St. Louis, Missouri, and the registrar, Reese Happersett, refused her. She sued. Her argument was that she was a citizen of the United States and that citizens vote.

The Supreme Court decided against her, unanimously. The case is reported at 88 United States 162, decided in the Court's term beginning October 1874 and usually dated 1875. The Court held that "the Constitution of the United States does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one," and that a state may hand the vote to men only. Women were citizens. Citizenship did not carry a vote with it.

That shut the short route. After it, the only way left was to amend the Constitution, and that took another forty-five years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the vote arrives in the West first" -->
The first law in American history that gave women the vote was passed in a territory where few settlers lived. The settlers' town of South Pass City was a gold camp that had grown up after 1867 on land the Eastern Shoshone had held under an 1863 treaty. A new treaty in 1868 cut the Shoshone homeland of about 44 million acres down to a reservation of about 3.2 million. William H. Bright, a legislator from South Pass City, put the bill to Wyoming Territory's first legislature, and Governor John Allen Campbell approved it on 10 December 1869. It gave women the vote and the right to hold office in the same act.

Utah Territory did the same thing two months later. Its acting governor signed on 12 February 1870, and two days after that a Salt Lake City schoolteacher named Seraph Young voted in the city election. She was the first woman in the United States to vote under a law that gave women the vote on the same terms as men. Wyoming granted it first and Utah used it first. Louisa Swain of Laramie voted on the morning of 6 September 1870.

Wyoming women were also the first in the country called to sit on juries, in the spring of 1870. Later the rule about who could serve was read to mean "male citizens over 21 years of age." That kept women off Wyoming juries until 1949. On 17 February 1870 Esther Hobart Morris was made justice of the peace at South Pass City, the first woman in the country to hold that office.

The reasons were not the movement's reasons. The National Park Service records that Wyoming's lawmakers had mixed motives, one of them getting more women to move to a territory that had very few. Congress took Utah women's vote away again in the Edmunds-Tucker Act of 1887, as part of a campaign against the Mormon church, and Utah women got it back when Utah became a state on 4 January 1896. Wyoming kept it at statehood in 1890, so Wyoming is the first state where women could vote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the laws that shut people out" -->
While one set of Americans was arguing about who should be added to the vote, Congress was writing laws to keep other people out.

The Page Act, signed by President Ulysses S. Grant on 3 March 1875, was the first federal law that restricted immigration. It let agents at the ports stop and question "any subject of China, Japan, or any Oriental country" to decide whether that person had come "without their free and voluntary consent," and it banned bringing in women "for the purposes of prostitution," leaving officials to decide which women those were. They decided it about Asian women in general, and the law became a near-total bar on Chinese women entering the country.

The Chinese Exclusion Act followed on 6 May 1882, signed by President Chester A. Arthur. It suspended the arrival of Chinese labourers for ten years, and Section 14 said that "no State court or court of the United States shall admit Chinese to citizenship." Congress extended it in 1892, made it permanent in 1902, and repealed it in 1943, when China was an ally in a war. It was the first and only major federal law that openly stopped immigration by one named nationality, and it kept Chinese immigrants from becoming citizens. `immigration` tells the arrivals and the ports.

On 3 March 1873 Congress passed "An Act for the Suppression of, trade in, and circulation of obscene literature and articles of immoral use," which everyone called the Comstock Act. It made it a crime to send certain things through the mail. Among them were anatomy books, some novels, photographs of boxing matches, and information about contraception, which means ways to avoid pregnancy. The act never said what "obscene" meant. Anthony Comstock, who had pushed Congress to pass it, was made a special agent of the Post Office and decided in practice what it meant. In court he often refused to let jurors see the thing he had seized, saying it was too vile to show them. He claimed that in his life he had seized 150 tons of books, made 4,000 arrests, and driven 15 people to suicide.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a man walks four hundred miles and sues the government" -->
In 1877 the United States forced the Ponca off their land at the mouth of the Niobrara River in Nebraska and moved them to Indian Territory. The Ponca had never fought the United States and had never sold that land. An 1868 treaty with the Sioux had handed it to the Sioux anyway. In 1876 Congress set aside $25,000 to move the Ponca "if they consented to go." Eight Ponca chiefs were taken south to look at new land. They turned down every place and walked more than 500 miles home in winter. The government ordered the move anyway. An Indian Office inspector, E. C. Kemble, took the first 170 people south on 30 April 1877. He ordered the agent, E. A. Howard, to move the rest, and they left on 16 May with 25 soldiers guarding them. In July they arrived on Quapaw land in Indian Territory, and later they were placed on land that had been the Cherokee Nation's. A third of the nation died after they got there. Standing Bear's daughter died on the way.

In late December 1878 his son Bear Shield died of a fever, aged sixteen. In early January 1879 Standing Bear set out on foot for Nebraska carrying his son's bones, to bury him at home. The walk took about two months. The Army arrested him and the people with him and held them at Fort Omaha under General George Crook.

Two Omaha lawyers, John L. Webster and Andrew J. Poppleton, took the case, and Standing Bear sued the federal government. The legal tool was a writ of habeas corpus, an old rule that says a person being held has the right to make the government show its legal reason for holding him. The trial ran on 1 and 2 May 1879 before Judge Elmer S. Dundy in the United States District Court in Omaha.

The government's argument was that habeas corpus was for persons, and that a Native American was not a person in the meaning of the law. On 12 May 1879 Dundy ruled that "an Indian is a person within the meaning of the laws of the United States," and ordered the Ponca released.

Standing Bear had said in court: "That hand is not the color of yours. But if I pierce it, I shall feel pain ... I am a man. The same god made us both."

From June 1879 he toured the country making the argument in public. He spoke in Chicago, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Boston, New York, Washington and Baltimore. With him went the journalist Thomas Tibbles and with Susette La Flesche of the Omaha, called Bright Eyes, who interpreted for him, and her brother Frank. The ruling covered Standing Bear and the people arrested with him. It did not settle Native citizenship.

In April 1880 John Elk, a Native man who had left his nation and lived in Omaha, tried to register to vote. The registrar of his ward, Charles Wilkins, refused him. On 3 November 1884 the Supreme Court ruled against Elk, seven to two, in *Elk v. Wilkins*. Justice Horace Gray wrote that a Native person born into a Native nation was not made a citizen by being born in the United States. **`native-nations` leads on what was done to the Ponca.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the reformers who asked for allotment" -->
Standing Bear's tour reached white reformers in the East. Some of them called their work a fight for Native rights. What they won cost the Native nations most of the land they still held.

Helen Hunt Jackson heard Standing Bear speak in Boston in 1879. In 1881 she published *A Century of Dishonor*, a book about how the United States government had treated Native nations. She sent a copy to every member of Congress with a note: "Look upon your hands: they are stained with the blood of your relations."

Two Philadelphia women, Mary Lucinda Bonney and Amelia Stone Quinton, founded the Women's National Indian Association in 1879. In December 1882 Herbert Welsh and Henry S. Pancoast founded the Indian Rights Association in Philadelphia. Its members believed that Native people would survive only by becoming Protestant and living like white Americans. From 1883 a Quaker hotel owner, Albert K. Smiley, gathered these reformers every year at his resort at Lake Mohonk, New York. They asked Congress to divide reservation land into plots owned by single families.

Congress did that in the Dawes Act, signed on 8 February 1887 and named for Senator Henry Dawes of Massachusetts. It gave 160 acres to the head of each family and made the people who took the plots citizens. It also let the government sell the rest of the reservation land to settlers. **`native-nations` tells what the Dawes Act took, which was about two-thirds of the land the nations still held.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a doctor says school will ruin girls" -->
In 1873 a professor at Harvard Medical School published a book arguing that education was damaging girls' bodies. Edward H. Clarke's *Sex in Education; or, A Fair Chance for the Girls* came out in Boston that year, 181 pages long.

His argument was that a girl's teenage years are spent building the organs that carry a pregnancy, and that studying the way boys study takes the energy that job needs. A girl who did it, he said, would be permanently damaged. He warned that the results were "monstrous brains and puny bodies," and that if the work of building the reproductive system is not finished at the right age "it will never be perfectly accomplished afterwards." He held that women should study no more than four hours a day, and not at all during their monthly bleeding. Smith College's own account of him lists what he said the risks were: the shrinking and shutting down of the uterus and ovaries, an inability to have children, insanity, and death. His example case, a girl he called Miss A, "graduated at nineteen, the first scholar, and an invalid."

He was a Harvard Medical School professor, so this was a doctor's claim, and it landed exactly when the first women's colleges were opening.

Two answers came. Smith opened in 1875 with its founding president, Laurenus Clark Seelye, saying "It is to preserve her womanliness that this College has been founded," and a system of supervised residences built to meet the worry. The other answer was evidence. Mary Putnam Jacobi had a pharmacy degree from 1863, a medical degree from 1864, and a Paris medical degree from 1871. She sent out a thousand questionnaires through women's rights networks and collected answers from hundreds of women, rich and poor, town and country, and she used their answers instead of stories about a Miss A. Her essay *The Question of Rest During Menstruation* won Harvard's Boylston Prize in 1876, the first time a woman had won it. She found no support for Clarke's claim.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="getting into the colleges, and getting the licence" -->
The colleges built for women in this era: Vassar, established at Poughkeepsie, New York in 1861 by Matthew Vassar and opened on 26 September 1865 with 353 students. Smith, founded in 1871 and opened in the fall of 1875 with 14 students. Wellesley, founded in 1870 by Pauline and Henry Fowle Durant, opened on 8 September 1875 with 314 students. Bryn Mawr opened in September 1885. Spelman began on 11 April 1881 in the basement of Friendship Baptist Church in Atlanta, founded by two New England teachers, Sophia B. Packard and Harriet E. Giles, with help from the church's pastor Frank Quarles. It had eleven pupils: ten women, some of them formerly enslaved, and one girl.

State universities opened to women one at a time, and the dates are slipperier than they look. The University of Iowa says it has admitted women since it began, the first public university in the country to do so, and that from 1860 women were admitted on an equal basis with men. Those are two different statements. Madelon Stockwell walked onto the University of Michigan campus in February 1870, the first woman there since it opened in 1817. Cornell admitted its first woman in 1870, Jennie Spencer of Cortland, who won a state scholarship and then had to withdraw because there was nowhere near the campus for a woman to live. Cornell admitted women regularly from 1872. A college saying it admits women and a woman actually being able to take the degree are two different things, usually years apart.

Getting the degree was not the end of it. Rebecca Lee Crumpler received a Doctress of Medicine from the New England Female Medical College on 1 March 1864, the first African American woman in the United States to earn a medical degree, and after the war she went to Richmond, Virginia and worked with the Freedmen's Bureau treating people who had just come out of slavery and had no other doctor.

In law the pattern was pass the exam, then be refused the licence. Arabella Mansfield took the Iowa bar examination on 15 June 1869 and became the first woman admitted to a state bar. Charlotte E. Ray graduated from Howard University School of Law and was admitted to the District of Columbia bar in 1872, the first Black woman lawyer in the country. The usual account is that she had applied as "C. E. Ray" and the committee took her for a man. Myra Bradwell passed the Illinois bar examination in 1869 and the Illinois Supreme Court refused her a licence because she was married. She took it to the Supreme Court of the United States and lost, eight to one. Justice Joseph Bradley wrote separately in that case that "The paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother. This is the law of the Creator," and that "A married woman is incapable, without her husband's consent, of making contracts which shall be binding on her or him." That last sentence is coverture, the rule from the 1600s, still being quoted by a Supreme Court justice in the 1870s.

Belva Lockwood was refused admission to the Supreme Court's own bar in 1876. The justices said that "none but men are permitted to practice before [us] as attorneys and counselors." So she lobbied Congress until it passed "An Act to relieve certain legal disabilities of women," and on 3 March 1879 she became the first woman admitted to argue there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="parents at the schoolhouse door" -->
Boston finished a fight that had started in 1787. Benjamin Roberts had sued the city in the late 1840s to get his daughter Sarah into the school nearest their house, and lost. William Cooper Nell and others kept up boycotts of the segregated Abiel Smith School and petition drives, and in 1855 the Massachusetts legislature outlawed segregated public schools. That was sixty-eight years after Prince Hall petitioned for it. The National Park Service records what happened next as well: in the decades after, race-based segregation policies still shaped Boston's schools despite the laws forbidding them. `education` tells the Roberts case.

San Francisco built its schools segregated from the start. The first public schools opened in 1851 for white children only. A separate school for Black children opened in 1854 and one for Chinese children in 1859. The district closed the Chinese school in 1870, which left Chinese American children with no public school at all. In January 1874 the California Supreme Court decided a case brought by Harriet A. Ward for her daughter Mary Frances, who was eleven. Noah F. Flood, the principal of the Broadway Grammar School, had refused to take her. The court held that separate schools were lawful, and that a Black child could be kept out of a white school only where a separate school existed. San Francisco had one, so the Wards lost.

Chinese Americans took the same route through the courts, and sometimes they won. San Francisco required a permit to run a laundry in a wooden building. About 200 Chinese laundry owners applied, and the Board of Supervisors refused every one of them. About 80 other owners applied, and all but one got a permit. More than 150 Chinese men were arrested for working without one. On 10 May 1886, in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, the Supreme Court ruled against the city. The justices said that a law run "with an evil eye and an unequal hand" broke the 14th Amendment. Wong Kim Ark was born in San Francisco in 1873 to Chinese parents. In August 1895 officials refused to let him back into the country after a visit to China. On 28 March 1898 the Supreme Court held, six to two, that he was a citizen by birth. Chief Justice Melville Fuller and Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented. People a statute had shut out of citizenship were using the Constitution against the statute.

In 1892 the Geary Act ordered every Chinese laborer to register and carry a certificate at all times. Anyone caught without one could be given a year of hard labor and then deported. To prove he had lived in the country, a man needed a white witness. The Chinese Six Companies, a Chinese American association, told people not to register. It collected a dollar from each person to pay lawyers. About 100,000 Chinese people lived in the United States, and only about 13,000 registered. Historians call it the first mass resistance to deportation in the United States.

Three men became the test case. One of them, Lee Joe, had tried to register and been turned down because his only witnesses were Chinese. On 15 May 1893 the Supreme Court upheld the law, five to three, in *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*. Justice David Brewer wrote in dissent that deportation meant "removal from home, from family, from business, from property."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a city government overthrown" -->
In November 1898 armed white men overthrew the elected government of Wilmington, North Carolina. Black and white Republicans held the city's offices, and the city had a Black newspaper, the *Daily Record*, edited by Alex Manly. Manly had printed an editorial about relationships between white women and Black men, and Democratic Party leaders used it to stir up white voters.

The leader was Alfred Moore Waddell, a former Confederate officer and congressman. On 10 November, two days after the election, about 500 armed white men gathered at the armory. The crowd grew to perhaps 2,000. They broke into the *Daily Record* office and set it on fire. Then they killed Black men in the city. The coroner counted 14 dead. The state's own report in 2006 said as many as sixty. Nobody knows the exact number, because no full record was kept.

That afternoon the mayor, Silas P. Wright, and the city's aldermen were forced to resign. The new aldermen made Waddell mayor. Black leaders and white Republicans were driven out of the city, and Manly escaped. The governor did not ask for federal troops, and President William McKinley sent none. The federal government investigated and closed the files in 1900 without charging anyone. The records do not name the men who fired.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a witness put on the chain gang" -->
In 1866 a Black woman named Frances Thompson told a committee of Congress that seven white men had raped her and another woman during the Memphis massacre. **`slavery-freedom` tells her testimony.**

Ten years later, on 10 July 1876, Memphis police arrested Thompson for wearing women's clothes. Four doctors, one of them Dr. Joseph Nuttall, examined her body at the police station and declared that she was a man. She told police she had lived her whole life as a woman. The city court fined her $50. She could not pay the fine, so she was sentenced to 100 days on the city chain gang, cleaning the streets. Crowds came to stare at her. Today historians describe her as a transgender woman, meaning a woman whose body at birth was counted as male.

Memphis newspapers printed the doctors' finding and used it to try to prove that her testimony about the rape was false. She died of dysentery, a disease of the gut, in the Memphis city hospital on 1 November 1876.

### Sojourner Truth
> **Who:** A woman born into slavery in New York who spent thirty years speaking in public for the rights of Black people and of women, and whose most famous words were written down by somebody else · **When and where:** Akron, Ohio, 28 May 1851. Washington, D.C., 1864. Battle Creek, Michigan, died 26 November 1883

**`slavery-freedom` tells how she walked out of slavery in 1826 and got her son Peter back in court in 1828.** Her story here starts in 1850.

That year she published *The Narrative of Sojourner Truth: a Northern Slave* and bought a house in Northampton, Massachusetts. She paid for part of it by selling photographs of herself. The caption she put on them was "I sell the shadow to support the substance."

On 28 May 1851 she spoke at the Ohio Women's Rights Convention in the Old Stone Church at Akron. She had not been invited.

Two versions of what she said that day exist, and they are not the same document.

The first was written by Marius Robinson, one of the secretaries of the convention, and printed in the *Anti-Slavery Bugle* on 21 June 1851, about three weeks later. Truth was staying with Robinson and his wife Emily in Ohio at the time, and he is thought to have gone over the text with her before it was printed. It begins: "May I say a few words? I want to say a few words about this matter. I am a woman's rights. I have as much muscle as any man, and can do as much work as any man. I have plowed and reaped and husked and chopped and mowed." Robinson added that it was "impossible to transfer it to paper, or convey any adequate idea of the effect it produced upon the audience."

The second was written by Frances Dana Gage, who had chaired the convention, and published in 1863, twelve years afterwards. It is the famous one, the one with "And ain't I a woman?" repeated through it. Gage wrote it from memory while she was in South Carolina during the war, and never checked it with Truth. She called it "but a faint sketch of her speech." It puts a Southern accent in Truth's mouth. Sojourner Truth grew up in Ulster County, New York, about a hundred miles north of New York City, and spoke only Dutch until she was nine. She was never enslaved in the South. Gage's version also has her say she bore thirteen children. She had five.

So the most quoted sentence any Black American woman said in the 1800s was written down by a white woman twelve years later, in an accent that was not hers. Robinson's version is the one to quote.

She kept working. During the war she recruited Black soldiers, including her grandson James Caldwell, who joined the Massachusetts 54th in April 1863. That same year Indiana arrested her under a state law barring Black people from entering the state, and held her for ten days. In 1864 she worked in Washington for the National Freedmen's Relief Association, rode the city's streetcars to force them to carry Black passengers. When a conductor tried to keep her off by force, she had him arrested and won her case. She also and met President Abraham Lincoln at the White House on 29 October. From 1870 she campaigned for the federal government to give land in the West to people who had been freed from slavery. It failed.

In 1872 she went to the polls in Battle Creek, Michigan and was turned away. Susan B. Anthony voted that same year in Rochester, New York, and was arrested for it. Neither of them was allowed to vote.

Sojourner Truth died at Battle Creek on 26 November 1883, about eighty-six years old. She thought she was a hundred and five.

### Susan B. Anthony
> **Who:** One of fifteen women who voted in Rochester in 1872, the only one put on trial for it, and the defendant a judge would not let a jury decide about · **When and where:** Rochester, New York, 5 November 1872. Canandaigua, New York, 17 to 19 June 1873

The plan was to lose. The National Woman Suffrage Association had decided in 1871 that women should try to register, be refused, and then sue in federal court, where they would argue that the 14th Amendment made voting one of the privileges of citizenship. Susan B. Anthony went to register in Rochester and the officials let her register. She went back on election day and the officials let her vote.

On 5 November 1872, in the first district of the Eighth Ward of Rochester, New York, Anthony and fourteen other women voted in the United States election. A poll watcher named Sylvester Lewis challenged her. The inspectors of election did what New York law required on a challenge: they put her under oath and asked whether she was a citizen, whether she lived in the district, and whether anyone had paid her. Her answers satisfied them and they put her ballots in the box.

Nine days later a federal commissioner, William Storrs, issued warrants for all fifteen women. On 18 November a deputy marshal came to Anthony's house. Her account of it: "'What for?' I asked. 'To arrest you,' he said. 'Is that the way you arrest men?' 'No.' Then I demanded that I should be arrested properly." He took her downtown on the streetcar, at the government's expense.

All fifteen were arrested. Only Anthony was examined and only Anthony was tried. The indictment said she had voted "without having a lawful right to vote," being "then and there a person of the female sex." At the examination, the assistant prosecutor insisted on asking the election inspectors, over her lawyer's objection, "Was Miss Anthony dressed in the apparel of a woman and had she the appearance of a woman?" The two inspectors answering that question were themselves under arrest for letting the women vote.

Federal courts at that time did not let a defendant in a criminal trial speak to the jury. So during March and April 1873 Anthony went out and spoke to the people the jury would be drawn from. She spoke in twenty-nine villages and towns in Monroe County, giving a lecture called "Is It a Crime for a U.S. Citizen to Vote?" The prosecutor then had the case moved to the next county. She scheduled a speech in every village of that county too.

The trial opened at Canandaigua on 17 June 1873 in front of a jury of twelve men and Ward Hunt, an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States sitting on that circuit. At the close of the government's case Hunt read out an opinion he had already written. The right to vote, he said, was not one of the privileges of United States citizenship. States could still bar whom they liked except on the grounds of race. Anthony knew New York only let men vote, so she had broken the law knowingly. Then he directed the jury to bring in a verdict of guilty, and the clerk refused her lawyer's request to poll the jury one by one. The jury never discussed the case.

Her lawyer, Henry Selden, worked all night and came back on 19 June asking for a new trial on the ground that Hunt had taken away her right to a jury. Hunt, who was being asked to judge his own conduct, refused. Then he asked her the question a court asks any prisoner before sentence.

The exchange that followed is the most famous thing she ever said. Three accounts of it survive and none of them is the court stenographer's. The words below are the version Anthony published herself.

> "Has the prisoner anything to say why sentence shall not be pronounced?"
> "Yes, your honor, I have many things to say; for in your ordered verdict of guilty, you have trampled under foot every vital principle of our government. ... Robbed of the fundamental privilege of citizenship, I am degraded from the status of a citizen to that of a subject."
> "The Court cannot allow the prisoner to go on."
> "May it please the Court to remember that since the day of my arrest last November, this is the first time that either myself or any person of my disfranchised class has been allowed a word of defense before judge or jury—"
> "The prisoner must sit down—the Court cannot allow it."
> "All of my prosecutors ... not one is my peer, but each and all are my political sovereigns ... native or foreign born, white or black, rich or poor, educated or ignorant, awake or asleep, sober or drunk, each and every man of them was my political superior; hence, in no sense, my peer."
> "The Court must insist—the prisoner has been tried according to the established forms of law."
> "Yes, your honor, but by forms of law all made by men, interpreted by men, administered by men, in favor of men, and against women."
> "The Court orders the prisoner to sit down. It will not allow another word."
> "The sentence of the Court is that you pay a fine of one hundred dollars and the costs of the prosecution."
> "May it please your honor, I shall never pay a dollar of your unjust penalty."
> "Madam, the Court will not order you committed until the fine is paid."

That last line was a tactic. Jailing her for refusing to pay would have given her a way to appeal to the Supreme Court. In July 1873 the marshal sent to collect reported that he had "made diligent search and can find no goods or chattles [sic] land or tenements" to seize. The government let it drop. She never paid.

The three election inspectors were tried the same day and fined $25 each. They refused to pay too, and on 25 February 1874 the marshal put them in jail. Anthony telegraphed a congressman and a senator, and on 3 March President Grant pardoned them. That same day the voters of the Eighth Ward re-elected all three.

### Ida B. Wells
> **Who:** A newspaper editor who counted the lynchings, published the count, showed the reason people gave for them was false, and had to leave the South to stay alive · **When and where:** born Holly Springs, Mississippi, 16 July 1862. Memphis, Tennessee, 1884 to 1892

A lynching is a killing carried out by a crowd, outside the law, with no trial, no evidence tested and no judge. In the American South in the 1890s it happened often enough that Ida B. Wells could count them by the hundred.

She was born into slavery at Holly Springs, Mississippi on 16 July 1862, to James and Lizzie Wells, and was freed as a small child when slavery ended.

In 1884 she bought a first-class railway ticket and the train crew told her to move to the car for Black passengers. She refused, and they took her off the train by force. She sued the railroad and won $500 in the circuit court. The Tennessee Supreme Court threw the award out. She was teaching at a segregated public school in Memphis, and in 1891 the school board fired her for writing about the state of the schools.

On 9 March 1892 three men were taken out of their cells in the Memphis jail in the early hours of the morning and shot to death. One of them, Thomas Moss, was her friend. He was a part-owner of the People's Grocery, a store run co-operatively by Black people, and it was taking custom from a white-owned store nearby. One of the rival owners, William Barrett, persuaded a judge to declare the People's Grocery a public nuisance, got himself deputized by the same judge, and marched on the store with a group of men in plain clothes. Shots were fired, several men were hurt, and Moss and two of his partners were arrested. They were killed in the jail. The other two men were Calvin McDowell and a man the records call either Will Stewart or Henry Steward. His name is not settled.

They were killed for running a shop that competed. Wells wrote that Black Memphis asked the authorities to find the killers and "No attempt was made to do so." No one was ever arrested for the killings.

Black people in Memphis answered in two ways. Thousands left the city. Those who stayed stopped riding the streetcars, and the streetcar company's managers came to the *Free Speech* to ask the paper to tell people to ride again.

Wells investigated it and wrote about it in her newspaper, the *Free Speech*. On 21 May 1892 she printed an editorial that said: "Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread-bare lie that Negro men rape white women." The excuse always given for lynching was that Black men had raped white women. Wells said in print that the excuse was false, and that white men were killing Black men over relationships white women had chosen.

Here is what she wrote about what happened next, in a pamphlet published later that year. On 24 May 1892, editorials in the Memphis daily papers brought a meeting together in the Cotton Exchange Building, and "a committee was sent for the editors of the Free Speech ... and the only reason the open threats of lynching that were not carried out was because they could not be found." Her business partner, Mr. Fleming, "had to leave town to escape the mob, and was afterwards ordered not to return." Wells was in New York on holiday, and letters and telegrams told her "that bodily harm awaited my return." Then "Creditors took possession of the office and sold the outfit, and the Free Speech was as if it had never been."

She did not go back. She wrote later that her return home was "forbidden under penalty of death."

What she did instead was count. Her pamphlet *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* came out in 1892 and set out the record: "the dark and bloody record of the South shows 728 Afro-Americans lynched during the past eight years," and another hundred and fifty in the nine months since. Then she turned the number on the excuse: "only one-third of the 728 victims to mobs have been charged with rape, to say nothing of those of that one-third who were innocent." Two people out of three had not even been accused of the thing lynching was supposed to be a punishment for.

In 1895 she published *A Red Record: Tabulated Statistics and Alleged Causes of Lynchings in the United States, 1892–1893–1894*, a hundred pages of history and figures. In 1893 she wrote *The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian Exposition*, printed in three languages, for the Chicago world's fair. In 1894 she toured Britain speaking about lynching, and a London Anti-Lynching Committee was formed because of it. In 1895 she married Ferdinand Barnett and they had four children.

Some white reformers fought her. Frances Willard led the Woman's Christian Temperance Union. In 1890 Willard had told a newspaper that "The colored race multiplies like the locusts of Egypt" and that white women and children in the South were in danger. Wells made those words public in Britain in 1894. In November 1894 Willard told her union's convention that Wells had "put an imputation upon half the white race in this country." Wells answered her in print in *A Red Record*.

Wells worked the way Dorothea Dix had worked in the 1840s. She collected the facts and published the numbers. **`crime-justice` covers killing outside the law. The campaign against it is told here.**

### Sarah Winnemucca
> **Who:** A Paiute interpreter who worked for the United States Army, watched what it did to her people, and spent the rest of her life saying so in public · **When and where:** Nevada and Oregon, 1844 to 1891. The removal to Yakama, 1878 to 1879

Her Paiute name was Thocmetony, which means Shell Flower. She was born in 1844 in what later became Nevada, the daughter of Chief Winnemucca and the granddaughter of Chief Truckee, both of whom had guided white travellers across Paiute country.

The National Park Service lists what she saw growing up: the murder of two uncles, the attempted rape of her sister, the Pyramid Lake War and the Bannock War, and the burning of Paiute food supplies.

She was very good with languages, and in the 1870s she worked as an interpreter for the Indian Office and as a go-between for the United States Army. Part of that job was persuading Paiute bands to move onto reservations. She was inside the system before she turned on it. During the Bannock War in 1878 she found her father's band, who were being held by Bannock fighters, and brought them out to the Army's protection: 230 miles in three days.

Then the Army removed the Paiute who had not fought at all. Winnemucca wrote that just before Christmas in 1878 she learned that President Rutherford B. Hayes had ordered it. "My people were only given one week to get ready in," she wrote.

They were taken off the Malheur Reservation, which was inside their own homeland, and sent 350 miles north to the Yakama Reservation. About 550 men, women and children went. It took nearly a month, through snow, over two mountain ranges. The women and children rode in wagons. Some of the men were made to walk in leg irons. Five children, one woman and an old man died on the road, and their bodies were left beside it because the ground was too cold to dig. The historian Sally Zanjani estimated that more than a fifth of the group died at Yakama afterwards, most of them from hunger and disease. The agent at Malheur, W. V. Rinehart, wrote a report in August 1879 complaining about what the removal had cost and noting that the Paiute had been sent off without proper supplies even though supplies were on their way.

That is what turned her from an interpreter into a campaigner.

In the early 1880s she gave more than three hundred speeches. In 1880 she met President Rutherford B. Hayes and the Secretary of the Interior, Carl Schurz. The president did not change his position. Schurz gave her a written promise that her people could go back to Malheur. He did not keep it. In 1882 and 1883 President Chester Arthur opened the Malheur land to white settlers. In 1884 she sent Congress a petition demanding that her people get Malheur back. What she was asking for was this: "sweep away the [Indian] agency system" and "give us homes to live in, for God's sake and for humanity's sake."

In 1883 she published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims*, written with help from two reformers, Elizabeth Peabody and Mary Peabody Mann. It is the first autobiography published by a Native American woman.

Then she started a school for Paiute children and taught them in Paiute and in English. It closed after three years because the money ran out. At that same time the government was running boarding schools that took Native children away from their families and set out to stop them speaking their own languages. She was making a rights argument by building the opposite kind of school. **`native-nations` leads on the Paiute and on the boarding schools.**

She died in 1891. Her statue stands in the United States Capitol for the state of Nevada, showing her at thirty-five with a shellflower held up in one hand and a book at her side.

### Mary and Joseph Tape
> **Who:** Two parents who took a school board to court because it would not let their eight-year-old daughter into the school at the end of their street, and won, and lost anyway · **When and where:** San Francisco, 1884 and 1885

Mary Tape was born near Shanghai in 1857 and came to the United States alone in 1868, when she was eleven. The Ladies' Protection and Relief Society took her in. She learned English there and took the name Mary McGladery, after one of the women who looked after her. Joseph Tape was born Jeu Dip and arrived in 1864. He worked as a servant, then ran a milk delivery business, then worked as a broker and an interpreter for the Chinese consulate. They had four children, Mamie, Frank, Emily and Gertrude, and lived in Cow Hollow in San Francisco.

In 1884 they took Mamie, who was eight, to enrol at Spring Valley Primary School, near their home. The school refused her because her parents were Chinese.

Joseph Tape sued. The defendants were the San Francisco Board of Education, the superintendent Andrew Jackson Moulder, and Jennie Hurley, the principal of the school.

On 9 January 1885 the Superior Court of California ruled for the Tapes. Keeping out "children of Chinese parents" broke California law and broke the equal protection clause of the 14th Amendment. The Board appealed, and the California Supreme Court upheld the ruling.

Moulder telegraphed the state legislature as soon as the decision came down. The legislature passed a law letting school districts set up "separate schools for children of Mongolian or Chinese descent." On 8 April 1885 Spring Valley refused Mamie again, this time saying she had no vaccination certificate and the classes were full.

That day Mary Tape wrote to the Board of Education. The *Daily Alta California* printed the letter on 16 April 1885, on page one, with her spelling as she wrote it.

> "I see that you are going to make all sorts of excuses to keep my child out of the Public schools. Dear sirs, Will you please to tell me! Is it a disgrace to be Born a Chinese? Didn't God make us all!!! What right have you to bar my children out of the school because she is a chinese Decend. ... I suppose, you all goes to churches on Sundays! Do you call that a Christian act to compell my little children to go so far to a school that is made in purpose for them. ... It seems no matter how a Chinese may live and dress so long as you know they Chinese. Then they are hated as one. There is not any right or justice for them. ... It seems to me Mr. Moulder has a grudge against this Eight-year-old Mamie Tape. ... Mamie Tape will never attend any of the Chinese schools of your making! Never!!! I will let the world see sir What justice there is When it is govern by the Race prejudice men! ... I guess she is more of a American then a good many of you that is going to prevent her being Educated."

She signed it Mrs. M. Tape. She was writing in her second language, to the men who had shut her daughter out.

On 13 April 1885 Mamie and her brother Frank were the first two pupils at the new Chinese Primary School in Chinatown. The Tapes won their case and their daughter still did not get into the school at the end of her street. That is sixty-nine years before *Brown v. Board of Education*.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Women won the vote in these fifty years. In 1921 white Tulsans burned Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and killed as many as 300 of its people, and no white person went to prison for it. In the same fifty years state governments had about 62,000 people operated on so that they could never have children, and the federal government locked up about 120,000 Japanese Americans without charging one of them with a crime. Both of those were legal, and the Supreme Court said so in 1927 and again in 1944.

This is also the era when the methods people still use were worked out. Charles Hamilton Houston planned a run of court cases twenty years ahead. Alice Paul put a picket line outside the White House gates. Disabled people who had been refused jobs sat down in a relief office and would not leave. A. Philip Randolph told two presidents he was bringing a march to Washington, and got what he wanted both times without holding it. The famous marches of the 1960s used ideas that were already old.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a mob in Lincoln's town, and the organization it produced" -->
On 14 August 1908 the newspapers in Springfield, Illinois reported that a Black man had tried to attack a white woman in her home the night before, and then reported a second attack. A white mob went out looking for the men accused. It did not find them, and it attacked the Black part of the city instead. The violence lasted nearly three days.

The mob lynched two men, Scott Burton and William Donegan. Neither of them had taken part in any crime. Dozens of homes were burned. Nearly 2,000 Black residents left Springfield and never came back. The state militia was called out to stop it. About 150 people were arrested afterwards, and hardly any of them were convicted of anything.

Springfield was Abraham Lincoln's home town.

A group of Black and white reformers answered the riot by starting the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. It was founded on 12 February 1909, Lincoln's hundredth birthday. Among the people who signed the call for the founding conference were W. E. B. Du Bois, Mary Church Terrell, and Ida B. Wells-Barnett, the journalist who had exposed lynching in the 1890s. Mary White Ovington did much of the early work. It was an organization of Black and white members together, and its plan was to fight in courts and in Congress rather than in the street.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1919: attacks in 25 cities, and a court win out of Elaine" -->
In 1919 white mobs attacked Black people in 25 major American cities. It is called the Red Summer. The Equal Justice Initiative says white mobs and police singled out Black veterans home from the First World War.

In Chicago on 27 July 1919 a 17-year-old Black boy, Eugene Williams, was swimming off a raft near the 29th Street beach. A 24-year-old white man, George Stauber, threw stones at the Black boys, and Eugene Williams drowned. A police officer, Daniel Callahan, refused to arrest Stauber. White gangs and neighborhood "athletic clubs" went into Black streets, and the fighting went on for eight days. Thirty-eight people were killed, 23 Black and 15 white, and about 537 were hurt. Two thirds of the people hurt were Black, and two thirds of the 138 people charged afterwards were Black too.

In Elaine, Arkansas, Black sharecroppers had joined a union, the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America, led by Robert Lee Hill. Sharecroppers farmed a white owner's land for a share of the cotton, and the union wanted a fair price for theirs. On the night of 30 September 1919 about 100 of them met in a church at Hoop Spur. Men in a car outside and the union's armed guards shot at each other, and a white security officer, W. A. Adkins, was killed. Between 500 and 1,000 armed white men came to Elaine from the nearby Arkansas counties and from Mississippi. A white witness, H. F. Smiddy, swore in 1921 that "several hundred of them" began "to hunt negroes" and shoot them. Governor Charles Brough sent more than 500 soldiers from Camp Pike, who arrived on 2 October, and there are accounts of soldiers killing Black people too. Four brothers, David, Gibson, Lewis and Leroy Johnston, were killed on 2 October on their way home from a hunting trip. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas says the Black dead "ranged into the hundreds," and the National Archives says more than 100. The army colonel in charge, Isaac Jenks, reported only two. Five white people died.

Phillips County officials charged 122 Black people, and by 5 November twelve of them had been sentenced to death. Walter White of the NAACP went to Arkansas to find out what had happened. A Black lawyer from Little Rock, Scipio Africanus Jones, defended the twelve. On 19 February 1923 the Supreme Court ruled in *Moore v. Dempsey* that the trials had been unfair. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that one trial "lasted about three-quarters of an hour and in less than five minutes the jury brought in a verdict of guilty," with an angry crowd outside the courthouse. Witnesses had said they were whipped until they testified. The last of the twelve went free in January 1925.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="thirty years of bills that never became law" -->
What the NAACP wanted most from Congress was a federal law against lynching. It did not get one in this era, and it did not get one in the whole twentieth century.

The first federal anti-lynching bill was introduced on 20 January 1900 by Representative George Henry White of North Carolina, who was the last Black member of Congress of his generation. Nothing came of it.

Representative Leonidas Dyer of Missouri introduced his on 8 April 1918. It was written "to protect citizens of the United States against lynching in default of protection by the States." It would have let federal courts try lynch mobs for murder, fined the county where a lynching happened, and jailed any sheriff or jailer who handed a prisoner over to a mob or failed to stop one. It also said nobody who had been in a mob could sit on the jury.

NAACP leaders worked for it. In April 1919 they published a report called *Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889-1918*, and sent James Weldon Johnson to Washington to work on members of Congress. Johnson wrote afterwards: "I tramped the corridors of the Capitol and the two office buildings so constantly that toward the end, I could, I think, have been able to find my way about blindfolded."

The report counted 3,224 people killed by lynch mobs from 1889 to 1918. Of them, 2,522 were Black, and 50 of the Black victims were women. On 26 January 1922 the House of Representatives passed the bill, 230 votes to 119, with several hundred Black spectators packed into the public gallery watching. The Senate never voted on it at all. Southern senators talked it out and threatened to keep talking, and Republican senators let it drop.

Congress passed no federal anti-lynching law in this era, or in the next one. **`crime-justice` covers killing outside the law. `rights-movements` has the campaign against it.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the last seven years of the suffrage fight" -->
On 3 March 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson was sworn in as president, women marched down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington. Alice Paul and Lucy Burns had organized it, on the same route the inauguration parade would use the next day. At least 250,000 people came to watch.

The crowd came off the pavements and blocked the road. Marchers were spat on and shoved, and men shouted insults and sexual propositions at them. The police did very little. Army troops arrived about an hour later to clear the street, and afterwards Congress opened an investigation into the police.

More than forty Black women marched in it. Ida B. Wells-Barnett had come with the Illinois delegation, and as the march was forming she was asked to go to the back instead. She refused and walked away. She waited on the side of the avenue until Illinois came past, and stepped in with two white women, Belle Squire and Virginia Brooks, one on each side of her.

From 10 January 1917, women stood at the White House gates six days a week, holding banners. The National Park Service says Alice Paul invented picketing the White House. Two of the banners read "Mr. President, What Will You Do for Woman Suffrage?" and "How Long, Mr President, Must Women Wait For Liberty?" Reporters called them the Silent Sentinels.

Then the arrests began, on a charge of obstructing traffic. In November 1917 forty-one women were arrested on it, and the sentences ran from six days to six months.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Occoquan, and what forcible feeding was" -->
Most of the women were sent to the Occoquan Workhouse in Virginia. On the night of 14 November 1917, the guards attacked them, on the orders of the superintendent, W. H. Whittaker. Alice Paul wrote down what happened: "Seized by guards from behind, flung off my feet, and shot out of the room. All of us were seized by men guards and dragged to cells in men's part. Dorothy Day was roughly used-back twisted. Mrs. Mary A. Nolan (73-year-old picket from Jacksonville, Florida) flung into cell." She was handcuffed all night and chained to the bars part of the time for asking the other women how they were, and was threatened with a straitjacket and a buckle gag. That night is called the Night of Terror.

The women went on hunger strike. The prison doctors answered by feeding them by force. Lucy Burns wrote it down on scraps of paper smuggled out of the jail, and this is what forcible feeding was: "I was held down by five people at legs, arms, and head. I refused to open mouth. Gannon pushed tube up left nostril. I turned and twisted my head all I could, but he managed to push it up. It hurts nose and throat very much and makes nose bleed freely. Tube drawn out covered with blood. Operation leaves one very sick. Food dumped directly into stomach feels like a ball of lead. Left nostril, throat and muscles of neck very sore all night."

A rubber tube was pushed up a woman's nose and down her throat into her stomach while five people held her still, and liquid food was poured through it. Dora Lewis, held the same way, wrote that "everything turned black when the fluid began pouring in." It was done to Alice Paul and Rose Winslow three times a day for three weeks. Rose Winslow got a note out that said: "We think of the coming feeding all day. It is horrible."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what the 19th Amendment did and did not do" -->
The amendment is one sentence. "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex."

The House passed it on 21 May 1919 and the Senate on 4 June 1919. Tennessee was the thirty-sixth state to ratify it, on 18 August 1920, which was the number needed. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby signed the certificate on 26 August 1920.

That is seventy-two years after Seneca Falls, and it did not give every woman the vote. It said states could not use sex as the reason for refusing. States in the South were already refusing Black men the vote using poll taxes, literacy tests and threats, and they used exactly the same methods on Black women from 1920 onward. The National Archives puts it plainly: many women could not vote "long into the 20th century because of discriminatory state voting laws." The law that finally went after those methods is the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and that belongs to the next era. **`government-politics` tells the amendment as law.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa, 1 June 1921: white Tulsans burn Greenwood" -->
On 1 June 1921 thousands of armed white Tulsans went into Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and burned it down. They killed as many as 300 people. The historian Scott Ellsworth gives a range of 50 to 300. The US Department of Justice wrote in 2025 that the dead numbered "as many as 300 Tulsans, perhaps even more." Bodies were thrown in the Arkansas River and buried in unmarked graves, so nobody knows the real count.

About 10,000 Black people lived in Greenwood. It had two newspapers, more than a dozen churches, a library, hotels, four drug stores and the Dreamland Theater, which Loula Williams owned. Greenwood's people spent their money there because many white stores in Tulsa would not serve them, and others made them wait or insulted them. City laws kept Black families out of white blocks unless they worked as servants there. All of Tulsa stood on land in the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's reservation.

The night before, a white crowd had gathered at the courthouse to lynch a 19-year-old Black prisoner, and armed Black men went there to stop it. Shooting broke out. Tulsa police then swore in hundreds of white men as deputies. A little after 5 in the morning a whistle blew, and white men who had been put into companies overnight went into Greenwood from three railroad stations. The 2001 state commission put their number at about 10,000.

They went from house to house. Men broke in, took what they wanted, piled bedding and furniture in the middle of a room, poured kerosene on it and lit it. White women came with shopping bags and took clothes and jewelry. The attackers burned more than 1,200 houses, a dozen churches, five hotels, 31 restaurants, eight doctors' offices and the library. They shot people who ran and people who gave up. Four white men tied a blind man who had lost both legs to the back of a car and dragged him through the streets until he died. Most survivors could not name the men who did these things.

Some Black men fought back. A group fired from the bell tower of the new Mount Zion Baptist Church until National Guard soldiers turned a machine gun on it. J. B. Stradford shot from the second floor of his hotel and gave up when the attackers promised not to burn it. They burned it.

Tulsa's own National Guard soldiers had put themselves under the police chief's command the night before. They went into Greenwood and rounded up Black men, as their own reports admit, and the Justice Department found that capturing Black men was their main job. While they did, other white men burned the empty homes. Airplanes flew over Greenwood, and the men in them watched where Black residents ran and told the police. Many survivors said men in the planes also dropped firebombs or shot at people, and the Justice Department calls that part "hotly disputed." **`crime-justice` tells the courthouse, the deputies, the grand jury and the 2025 federal review. `rights-movements` has Greenwood and its people.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the camps and the passes" -->
Police, deputies and guardsmen arrested Black people all over Tulsa, whether or not they had done anything. Guard patrols also arrested Black servants in the servants' rooms of white homes. They held them under armed guard in three places: Convention Hall, McNulty Park (a baseball park) and the stalls at the fairgrounds. Between 4,000 and 6,000 people were held at once. No white person was held.

To leave, a Black person needed a card, and a white person had to vouch for it. People who worked for white families got green cards. Others got red cards that were good for one day. Anyone found without a card was taken back. By 7 June officials had handed out 7,500 of these tags, and the rule lasted at least a month. People who lived under it said they were treated "as though they [were] dogs."

The day after the burning, the National Guard commander ordered every able-bodied Black man in the camps to work. The men cleared the ruins and buried the dead. The Red Cross paid them up to 25 cents an hour, and a meal cost 20 cents.

Most of Greenwood's people had no home left. Thousands lived through the winter of 1921 to 1922 in Red Cross tents, many of them with no floor, and many stayed in the tents for more than a year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="building Greenwood again, against the city" -->
On 7 June 1921 Tulsa's city commissioners passed a fire rule covering most of Greenwood. Every new building there had to be brick, concrete or steel and at least two stories high, which few burned-out families could pay for. The first newspaper story about the rule said the land would "never again be a [N]egro quarter." In 2025 the Justice Department found that the men who wrote the rule meant to drive Black people out.

B. C. Franklin, a Black lawyer who had watched the burning, set up his office in a tent on Archer Street with two partners, I. H. Spears and T. O. Chappelle. They took the rule to court, and in September 1921 they won an order that stopped it for good. Police had already arrested people for trying to rebuild.

Franklin's office also filed about $4 million in claims against the city and the insurance companies. The companies refused to pay, because their policies did not cover damage done in a "riot." In 1926 the Oklahoma Supreme Court ruled that nobody could sue them over it. Lawsuits against the city, Mayor T. D. Evans and the police were all thrown out.

Greenwood's people rebuilt with their own money. By the 1940s the district had 242 Black-owned businesses. From the 1950s city officials took land along its edges for highways, and the roads finished in the 1970s run along Greenwood's south and east sides. **`money` tells the claims and the insurance clause.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the survivors ask to be paid, 1997 to 2026" -->
In 1997 Oklahoma's lawmakers set up a commission to study the massacre. Its report, in February 2001, asked the state to pay the survivors and their descendants. On 1 June 2001 the lawmakers passed a law for scholarships and a memorial. It paid the survivors nothing. Survivors and descendants sued in 2003, and federal judges ruled that they had waited too long.

The last three known survivors kept asking. Viola Fletcher, her brother Hughes Van Ellis and Lessie Benningfield Randle sued the city, the county, its sheriff and the state's military department in 2020. On 19 May 2021 they spoke to a committee of Congress. On 1 June 2021 President Joe Biden met them and spoke in Greenwood, the first president in office to go there about the massacre. "It wasn't a riot, it was a massacre," he said. On 12 June 2024 Oklahoma's Supreme Court ended their lawsuit, eight votes to one. The justices wrote that the harm left behind could "only be resolved by policymakers, not the courts."

Hughes Van Ellis died on 9 October 2023, aged 102. Viola Fletcher died on 24 November 2025, aged 111. Lessie Benningfield Randle, born on 10 November 1914, was the last known survivor in 2026, and she had never been paid.

In 2018 Tulsa's mayor, G. T. Bynum, started a search for the unmarked graves. By September 2026 the team had dug up the remains of 55 people in Oaklawn Cemetery. They have named two victims of the massacre: C. L. Daniel in July 2024 and James Goings in June 2025. Both men had served in the Army in the First World War.

### Viola Fletcher
> **Who:** A girl of seven who ran from Greenwood in 1921, never went back to school, and at 107 asked Congress for justice · **When and where:** Tulsa, Oklahoma, 1921, and Washington, D.C., 2021

Viola Ford was born on 10 May 1914, the daughter of Lucinda Ellis and John Wesley Ford of Tulsa. She grew up in Greenwood with her parents and five brothers and sisters. "My family had a beautiful home," she said later. "We had great neighbors and I had friends to play with. I felt safe."

On the night of 31 May 1921 she went to bed at home. Her family woke her and told her they had to leave. "I will never forget the violence of the white mob when we left our house," she said. She remembered businesses burning and airplanes flying overhead. By the afternoon of 1 June white Tulsans had burned most of Greenwood.

Her family left Tulsa. She never went to school past the fourth grade. In the Second World War she worked in the shipyards in California. For most of her life she worked as a maid for white families, and she never earned much.

In 2020, at 106, she joined her brother Hughes Van Ellis and Lessie Benningfield Randle in suing the city of Tulsa and others for what was taken in 1921. On 19 May 2021 she came to Washington for the first time in her life and spoke to a committee of Congress. "I live through the Massacre every day," she told them. "I am 107 years old and have never seen justice." She asked them to "open the courtroom doors to us."

Oklahoma's Supreme Court ended the lawsuit in June 2024. In 2023 she and her grandson Ike Howard published a book about her life, *Don't Let Them Bury My Story*. She died on 24 November 2025, aged 111.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="citizens who still could not vote" -->
Congress voted to make American Indians citizens on 2 June 1924, and President Calvin Coolidge signed it. Being a citizen and being allowed to vote turned out to be two different things, because each state wrote its own rules about who could put a name on the register.

Arizona had a rule barring anyone who was an "idiot, insane person, person non compos mentis, or under guardianship." In 1928 the Arizona Supreme Court decided that a Native person living on a reservation was a ward of the federal government, and so fell under that rule. New Mexico's constitution had a different phrase in it, "Indians not taxed," and state officials argued that people living on reservations paid no state taxes and so could not vote.

Both fell in the same year. On 15 July 1948, in *Harrison v. Laveen*, the Arizona Supreme Court threw out its own 1928 decision. Justice Levi Udall wrote that living on a reservation was not a reason to keep a qualified voter off the rolls. In August 1948, in *Miguel Trujillo v. Garley*, a federal district court in New Mexico ruled the "Indians not taxed" clause unconstitutional. Utah was the last state to let Native people vote, in 1957.

That is thirty-three years between the citizenship law and the last state giving way. **`native-nations` leads on the nations themselves.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a society in Chicago, 1924" -->
On 10 December 1924 Henry Gerber started the Society for Human Rights in Chicago. It is the first known organization in the United States set up to defend the rights of gay people, the National Park Service says. Gay means a man who loves men or a woman who loves women. Gerber was a German-born Army veteran who worked for the Post Office. He got a charter from the State of Illinois by describing the members as people "abused and hindered in the legal pursuit of happiness," and he printed two issues of a newsletter called *Friendship and Freedom*.

In 1925 Chicago police arrested Gerber and other members without a warrant. They took his typewriter, his writings and his diaries, and gave back only the typewriter. The Post Office fired him "for conduct unbecoming a postal employee," and the society ended. His house in Chicago became a National Historic Landmark in 2015.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="sent to Mexico, 1929 to 1936" -->
In the Depression, federal, state and county officials pushed hundreds of thousands of people of Mexican ancestry out of the United States, and many of them were American citizens. Counts run from about 500,000 to about 2 million. The State of California found in 2005 that about 1.2 million of the 2 million it counted had been born in the United States, which made them citizens.

The US Secretary of Labor, William N. Doak, under President Herbert Hoover, ran a national campaign that blamed Mexicans for taking jobs. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel of the city's unemployment committee planned the publicity. On 26 February 1931 immigration agents closed the exits of La Placita park in Los Angeles with about 400 people inside. Los Angeles County welfare officers told families their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico, and the first county train left on 23 March 1931. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks for the border. Local officials sold the property families left behind.

On 7 October 2005 California's governor signed a law in which the state apologized "for the fundamental violations of their basic civil liberties and constitutional rights." Los Angeles County's supervisors apologized in 2012. No apology has come from the federal government. **`immigration` tells the Mexican arrivals before and after.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the movement that wanted certain people never to be born" -->
Eugenics was the belief that a country should choose which of its people were allowed to have children and stop the rest from having any. The idea came out of animal breeding, and its supporters applied it to human beings. It was a respectable opinion at the time. Universities taught it and state legislatures wrote it into law.

Harry H. Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office wrote a model sterilization law and sent it out for states to copy. To sterilize a person is to operate on their body so that they can never have a child, and it cannot be undone. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum says that "in part due to Laughlin's efforts, dozens of American states enacted laws that forced certain people to be sterilized against their will," and that "more than 62,000 sterilizations had been performed in the United States by the 1960s." About six in ten of the people operated on were women. In 1927 the Supreme Court said the states could do it, in the case of a young Virginia woman named Carrie Buck.

In 1933 Nazi Germany passed a law of its own, the Law for the Prevention of Offspring with Hereditary Diseases, and the Holocaust Museum says it was "based in part on the practice of forced sterilization in the US." About 400,000 people were sterilized in Nazi Germany under it. In 1936 the University of Heidelberg gave Harry Laughlin an honorary degree for his work. He wrote back on 28 May 1936 that "To me this honor will be doubly valued because it will come from a nation which for many centuries nurtured the human seed-stock which later founded my own country."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="120,000 people, no charges, no trials" -->
President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 on 19 February 1942. It let army commanders order people out of any area they chose. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, who commanded the army on the west coast, used it on people of Japanese ancestry. He gave them as little as 48 hours' notice to leave their homes, and in March 1942 Congress made it a crime to disobey his orders. More than 120,000 people were removed from their homes. The federal courts count two out of every three of them as American citizens. The National Archives counts nearly 70,000 citizens out of about 122,000. Not one of them was charged with a crime, and not one of them had a trial.

They were held first in assembly centers, one of which was Tanforan Racetrack near San Francisco, and then in ten camps built inland.

Topaz, in the desert of west central Utah, is one of the ten. It opened on 11 September 1942 and closed on 31 October 1945. More than 11,000 people passed through it and over 8,000 were there at once at its fullest, most of them from around San Francisco. They lived in barracks of pine planks covered with tarpaper: 42 blocks, twelve barracks to a block, 250 to 300 people to a block. What each family got was a pot-bellied stove, army cots, blankets and mattress covers. One block shared one washroom-toilet-laundry building and one dining hall. Around the whole thing was a fence, with seven watchtowers on it.

In April 1943 a guard shot and killed a 63-year-old man named James Hatsuki Wakasa, who was walking near that fence.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="four people who said no, and the one who won" -->
Four people took the removal to the Supreme Court, and the Court decided all four cases.

Gordon Hirabayashi and Minoru Yasui both broke the curfew on purpose so they could argue about it. The Court upheld the curfew in 1943 in both cases, and put off the bigger question of whether people could be removed from their homes.

It answered that question on 18 December 1944 in Fred Korematsu's case, and it said yes, six to three. Justice Hugo Black wrote for the majority that "all legal restrictions which curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect" and must be given "the most rigid scrutiny." He then allowed this one. Justice Frank Murphy dissented and called it "the legalization of racism," and said it was like the "abhorrent and despicable treatment of minority groups by the dictatorial tyrannies which this nation is now pledged to destroy." Justice Robert Jackson dissented too, and wrote that Korematsu had been convicted for "being present in the state whereof he is a citizen."

On the same day the Court handed down a fourth ruling, and this one the government lost. Mitsuye Endo was born in Sacramento on 10 May 1920 and had a clerical job with the state of California. A lawyer named James Purcell asked her to sue the government. He picked her because she looked, to a judge, like someone nobody could call disloyal: a state employee, a Christian, she spoke only English, she had never been to Japan, and her brother was in the United States Army. Her petition was filed on 12 July 1942.

While the case was going, the War Relocation Authority offered to let her out of the camp straight away if she would drop it. She refused and stayed inside, because walking out would have ended the case for everybody else. She was moved from the Sacramento Assembly Center to Tule Lake and then to Topaz, and she was still behind the fence when the Court ruled.

Justice William O. Douglas wrote that "Mitsuye Endo should be given her liberty," and that the government "has no authority to subject citizens who are concededly loyal to its leave procedure." The army had announced the day before that the west coast exclusion would end on 2 January 1945. She moved to Chicago in May 1945, worked as a secretary for the Mayor's Committee on Race Relations, married Kenneth Tsutsumi, raised three children, and died on 14 April 2006. She is the only one of the four who won.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the long campaign to open the schoolhouse" -->
Charles Hamilton Houston had a plan. He was born in Washington, D.C. on 3 September 1895, was the first Black editor of the *Harvard Law Review*, became dean of Howard University Law School in 1929, and then became the first special counsel of the NAACP. His idea was to start at the top: "start with the law schools and higher education, and integrate schools from the top down." A state that had built no law school for Black students had to admit them to the one it had, or build a second one. Either answer cost the state something. Thurgood Marshall was his student at Howard.

Donald Gaines Murray applied to the University of Maryland School of Law on 24 January 1935 and was turned down because he was Black. Houston, Marshall and a Baltimore lawyer named Nicholas Gosnell took the case. Judge Eugene O'Dunne ordered the university's president, Raymond A. Pearson, to admit him, and the Maryland Court of Appeals agreed on 15 January 1936. Murray graduated in 1938.

The next case, Lloyd Gaines's, reached the Supreme Court in 1938.

Ada Lois Sipuel applied to the University of Oklahoma College of Law on 14 January 1946. The president, George Lynn Cross, told her there was no academic reason to refuse her and refused her anyway, because state law would not let Black and white students sit in the same room. Marshall took her case. The Supreme Court decided it on 12 January 1948. Oklahoma's answer was to set up a separate law school for her in five days, in rooms at the State Capitol. She was admitted to the University of Oklahoma on 18 June 1949, three years and five months after she applied, and graduated in August 1952.

On 5 January 1931 the principal of Lemon Grove Grammar School near San Diego, Jerome T. Green, stood in the doorway and let every child in except the Mexican American ones. He told them to go to a separate two-room building instead. The school had 169 pupils: 87 counted as European American, about 75 Mexican American, and 7 Japanese American.

The parents formed a committee, the Comité de Vecinos de Lemon Grove, kept their children away from the separate building, and hired two lawyers, Fred Noon and A. C. Brinkley. They sued in the name of one of the pupils, Roberto Alvarez. On 30 March 1931 Judge Claude Chambers of the San Diego County Superior Court ordered the district to admit the children "on a basis of equality with other children of said school district, and without separation or segregation in a separate school because of race or nationality." Separating "all the Mexicans in one group," he said, "can only be done by infringing the laws of the State of California." It is the first school desegregation case won in the United States.

Fifteen years later five families in Orange County (the Mendez, Guzman, Palomino, Estrada and Ramirez families) sued four school districts. Their lawyer was David C. Marcus, who had already won a case against San Bernardino in 1944 over segregated pools and parks. Judge Paul J. McCormick ruled for the families on 18 February 1946 and wrote that "a paramount requisite in the American system of public education is social equality."

When the districts appealed, the case stopped being five families' case. Five groups sent friend-of-the-court briefs, which are arguments from people who are not part of a case. They were the NAACP (its brief written by Thurgood Marshall, Robert L. A school did not need a law to refuse a disabled child, because no law said it had to take him in.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a sit-in, twenty years before the lunch counters" -->
In 1935 the New York City Home Relief Bureau stamped disabled people's job applications "PH" for "physically handicapped." The stamp meant unemployable, and it kept them out of the Works Progress Administration jobs the government was handing out in the Depression. About 300 people, most of them living with the effects of polio or cerebral palsy, formed the League of the Physically Handicapped.

Six of them sat down in the Home Relief Bureau office in May 1935 after the director refused to see them, and would not leave. Others picketed outside. Later they sat in at the WPA headquarters. One of them said afterwards: "What started it was finding out that jobs were available, that the government was handing out jobs ... everybody was getting jobs ... those of us who were militant just refused to accept the fact that we were the only people who were looked upon as not worthy, not capable of work."

By 1936 about 1,500 jobs had been made in New York City for disabled people. The league broke up by 1938. It was a sit-in for jobs, twenty-five years before the lunch counter sit-ins.

In the same year Congress wrote the Social Security Act, and it defined disability as "inability to engage in substantial gainful work." So the law that paid a disabled person also said that a disabled person could not work.

On 12 February 1946 Sergeant Isaac Woodard left the Army after four years and got on a bus home to South Carolina. After he argued with the driver, police at Batesburg took him off the bus. ### Alice Paul
> **Who:** The organizer who put a picket line outside the White House and went to prison six times for it · **When and where:** born Mount Laurel, New Jersey, 11 January 1885, and Washington, D.C., 1913 to 1920

She was born on 11 January 1885 at Mount Laurel, New Jersey, into a Quaker family. She took a degree at Swarthmore in 1905, a master's in 1907, and a doctorate in economics from the University of Pennsylvania in 1912. She studied in London and Birmingham as well, and while she was in England she joined Emmeline Pankhurst's campaign for the vote and was arrested for it.

She came home and went to work on the American campaign the same way. She was made chairman of the Congressional Committee of the National American Woman Suffrage Association in 1913, then founded the Congressional Union, then the National Woman's Party in 1916.

Her method was to make the president uncomfortable in public and then keep doing it. She organized the 1913 procession, and she put the picket line at the White House gates on 10 January 1917. It was the first time anyone had picketed the White House.

In October 1917 she was arrested for picketing and sentenced to seven months for obstructing traffic. On her way out of the courtroom she told reporters: "We are being imprisoned, not because we obstructed traffic, but because we pointed out to the President the fact that he was obstructing the cause of democracy at home, while Americans were fighting for it abroad."

She went on hunger strike in the District jail. The prison doctor, Dr Gannon, fed her by force through a tube three times a day for three weeks. Then they moved her out of the jail and into the psychopathic ward, the part of the hospital for people about to be committed to an asylum. A nurse was ordered to check on her every hour all night by shining an electric light into her face, and a workman was sent to nail boards across her window from outside. Two doctors' signatures were all it took to have a person sent to St. Elizabeth's Asylum. She wrote afterwards: "No trial, no counsel, no protest from the outside world!"

It did not work. The psychiatrist sent to examine her, Dr William A. White, reported instead that "I felt myself in the presence of an unusually gifted personality," and praised the "most admirable, coherent, logical and forceful way" in which she argued her case. A lawyer named Dudley Field Malone forced his way in and got her out of the ward, and the boards came off her window. After five weeks of a seven-month sentence, the government let every suffrage prisoner go.

She served six prison terms for the vote in all, three in England and three in the United States.

The 19th Amendment was certified on 26 August 1920, and she did not stop. She wrote the Equal Rights Amendment and had it introduced at Seneca Falls on 20 July 1923, where it was called the Lucretia Mott Amendment. She took a law degree in 1922. She worked on the wording about women in the United Nations Charter in 1945, and on the clause about sex in the Civil Rights Act of 1964. She died on 9 July 1977 at Moorestown, New Jersey, at ninety-two. The Equal Rights Amendment she wrote in 1923 is still not in the Constitution.

### Grace Fryer
> **Who:** A watch-dial painter who sued the company that poisoned her, under a state law written so that her claim would be too late before she knew she was ill · **When and where:** Orange, New Jersey, 1917 to 1928
> **Movie:** Radium Girls (2018)

She painted numbers on watch dials at the U.S. Radium Corporation plant in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917 to 1920. The paint glowed because it had radium in it. To keep a fine point on the brush the painters put it between their lips between strokes. The physicist Robley Evans wrote down why they did it: a cloth or their fingers "were too harsh, but by wiping the brush clean between their lips the proper erasing point could be obtained."

Here is what radium does inside a person. Radium behaves in the body almost exactly like calcium, so the body builds it into the bones and the teeth as if it were ordinary bone-making material. Once it is in there it stays, and it keeps giving off radiation from the inside. In autopsies of these women, ninety-nine parts in a hundred of the radiation was in the skeleton. What it caused was the marrow failing to make blood, jaw bones dying and breaking apart, and bone cancers.

Grace Fryer left the plant in 1920. In 1924 she went to a dentist, Theodor Blum, who found that her jaw bone was wasting away. He had never seen anything like it. Other dentists gave it a name: radium jaw.

In May 1927 a lawyer named Raymond Berry filed suit against the company for her and four co-workers: Katherine Schaub, Quinta Maggia Macdonald, Albina Maggia Larice and Edna Hussman.

The law was the problem. New Jersey gave a worker two years from the last day of exposure to bring a claim about an illness caused by work. Radium poisoning takes longer than two years to show itself. So by the time a dial painter knew what was wrong with her, her two years were already gone.

The women gave evidence in January and April 1928. The company was then granted a delay until September, while the plaintiffs were dying. They settled instead. On 4 June 1928 a federal judge, William Clark, brought both sides to an out-of-court settlement that paid the sick women. The company admitted no wrongdoing.

In Ottawa, Illinois, women who had painted dials for the Radium Dial Company fought the same fight a decade later. Catherine Wolfe Donohue died in July 1938, aged thirty-five. The last ruling in her case came that October, after her death, and her family was paid a modest sum.

### Carrie Buck
> **Who:** A young woman whose own lawyer helped the state win the right to operate on her so that she could never have a child · **When and where:** Virginia, 1924 to 1927. She died in 1983

To sterilize a person is to operate on their body so that they can never have a child. What was done to Carrie Buck was a salpingectomy. A doctor cut a section out of each of the two tubes that carry an egg from an ovary to the womb. After that no egg can reach the womb. It is permanent, and in 1927 there was no way to undo it.

Virginia's legislature passed a law allowing this on 20 March 1924. A lawyer named Aubrey Strode wrote it. Albert Priddy, who ran the Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feeble-Minded, wanted to know whether the law would stand up in court, so he picked a patient and had a case brought. The patient was Carrie Buck. Priddy died on 13 January 1925 and the man who took his job, Dr John H. Bell, took his place on the case. That is why it is called *Buck v. Bell*.

Here is how the case was arranged. Strode, who had written the law, also argued for the Colony in court. Carrie Buck's lawyer was Irving P. Whitehead, who had sat on the Colony's own board and who wanted people sterilized. The historian Paul Lombardo went through the record and found that Whitehead did not press a single witness under cross-examination and did not call one witness of his own. Both lawyers wanted the same verdict.

A eugenics researcher named Arthur Estabrook interviewed the family for one day and then testified that Carrie, her mother Emma and her baby daughter Vivian were "a defective strain." A social worker said of the baby that she had "a look about it that is not quite normal." Vivian had been born that March.

The trial court, with Judge Bennett T. Gordon sitting, ruled for the Colony on 18 November 1924. The Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals agreed on 12 November 1925. The Supreme Court of the United States heard the case on 22 April 1927 and ruled eight to one on 2 May 1927. Dr Bell operated on 19 October 1927. Carrie Buck was released from the Colony the next month.

Her sister Doris went into the Colony in June 1926. An order to sterilize her was signed in December 1927 and the operation was done in January 1928.

About 8,300 people were sterilized in Virginia between 1927 and 1972, and the law stayed on the books until 1974. The American Civil Liberties Union sued for the survivors in 1980 and they settled out of court. On 2 May 2002, seventy-five years to the day after the ruling, Governor Mark Warner apologized for what he called "a shameful effort in which state government never should have been involved."

Carrie Buck married twice. She died on 28 January 1983 in a nursing home in Waynesboro, Virginia. *Buck v. Bell* has never been overruled. **`education` tells how the school tests produced the labels that put people on these lists.**

### Fred Korematsu
> **Who:** A welder who stayed behind when his family was taken away, lost at the Supreme Court, and had the conviction thrown out in 1983 · **When and where:** Oakland, California, 1942, and San Francisco, 1983

He was born in Oakland, California on 30 January 1919 and named Toyosaburo. A teacher told him Fred would be easier, so he used Fred. His father Kakusaburo had come to the United States in 1905 and his mother Kotsui came through Angel Island in 1914. He had three brothers, and the family ran the Stonehurst Flower Nursery in East Oakland.

He was twenty-three when the removal orders came in 1942. His family went. He did not. He had a girlfriend named Ida Boitano, he had a job as a welder, and he wanted to stay where he lived. So he got himself identification papers in a false name, Clyde Sarah, and carried on working.

The FBI arrested him on the street with his girlfriend on 30 May 1942. The charge was breaking Civilian Exclusion Order No. 34. What he had done was stay in the town where he lived.

A lawyer from the American Civil Liberties Union, Ernest Besig, came to see him in the Presidio stockade and asked whether he would let them take the case up. He said yes. He was convicted and given five years' probation, and then sent to the Tanforan Racetrack on 28 June 1942 and to the Topaz camp in Utah with his family that September.

The Supreme Court heard the case on 11 October 1944 and ruled against him on 18 December 1944, six to three.

He came out of the camp, moved to Detroit, married Kathryn Pearson, came back to California and raised two children.

Then, in the early 1980s, researchers going through government files found that the lawyers who argued the case for the United States had been told by their own intelligence agencies that Japanese Americans were no military threat, and had kept that out of the Supreme Court. A team of lawyers reopened the case for him.

On 10 November 1983, in the same San Francisco courthouse where he had been convicted at twenty-three, Judge Marilyn Hall Patel said: "the conviction that was handed down in this court and affirmed by the Supreme Court in *Korematsu v. United States* is ... vacated and the underlying indictment dismissed."

Asked about a pardon, he said: "I don't want a pardon. If anything, I should be pardoning the government."

He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998 and died at Larkspur, California on 30 March 2005, aged eighty-six. In California, 30 January is Fred Korematsu Day.

### A. Philip Randolph
> **Who:** The organizer who got two presidents to sign orders by threatening a march and then not holding it · **When and where:** New York and Washington, 1941 and 1948

He was born on 15 April 1889 at Crescent City, Florida. In 1925 he founded the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, and in 1935 it became the first union of Black workers admitted to the American Federation of Labor. **`work-workers` tells that part.**

In 1941 the country was building weapons and hiring fast, and Black workers were being turned away from the factory gates. Randolph had a plan for that. With Bayard Rustin he began organizing a march on Washington against discrimination in the defense industries.

Then he told the government it was coming. He and other Black leaders met Eleanor Roosevelt and members of the cabinet, and Randolph said he would bring "ten, twenty, fifty thousand Negroes on the White House lawn."

On 25 June 1941 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 8802. It said: "There shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries and in Government, because of race, creed, color, or national origin." It set up a Committee on Fair Employment Practice to take complaints and look into them. It covered federal agencies, companies with war contracts, and the unions working on them.

Randolph called the march off, which was the bargain he had made.

The committee was weak. The National Archives says historians have mostly judged it ineffective, especially in the South, and Congress refused to make it permanent after the war ended.

In 1948 he did it again, this time about the army. Black soldiers had fought a war against Nazi Germany in units segregated by race. Randolph planned another march. On 26 July 1948 President Harry Truman signed Executive Order 9981: "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." It set up a President's Committee on Equality of Treatment and Opportunity in the Armed Services. The services fought it, and it took until about the end of the Korean War, in 1953, before nearly all of them had actually integrated.

Fifteen years later he did hold the march. He was the principal organizer of the March on Washington in 1963, the one where Martin Luther King Jr. spoke, and that belongs to the next era. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and died at his home in New York City on 16 May 1979, aged ninety.

### Lloyd Gaines
> **Who:** A history graduate who wanted to study law in his own state, won the first school case the NAACP took to the Supreme Court, and then disappeared · **When and where:** Missouri, 1935 to 1938, and Chicago, March 1939

He was born in 1911 at Water Valley, Mississippi. His father, Henry Richard Gaines, was a tenant farmer who had been a teacher. His mother was Callie Gaines. There were eleven children.

He finished Vashon High School in St. Louis in 1931, spent a year at Stowe Teachers College, and graduated from Lincoln University in Missouri in August 1935 with a degree in history and honors.

Then he applied to the University of Missouri School of Law. The registrar, S. W. Canada, did not say no in so many words. He told Gaines to write to the president of Lincoln University, and Lincoln's president pointed at a Missouri law that would pay a Black student's fees to study law in Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa or Illinois instead. Missouri's offer was to pay him to leave the state.

He said no and sued. Charles Hamilton Houston of the NAACP and a St. Louis lawyer, Sidney Redmond, took the case. It was filed in circuit court on 24 January 1936. The circuit court ruled against him in July 1936. The Missouri Supreme Court heard it in May 1937 and ruled against him on 9 December 1937.

The Supreme Court of the United States heard it on 9 November 1938 and decided it on 12 December 1938, six to two. Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote: "The State was bound to furnish him within its borders facilities for legal education substantially equal to those which the State there afforded for persons of the white race." A state's promise of equal protection only works where its own laws work, he said, "that is, within its own jurisdiction." Justice McReynolds dissented and Justice Butler joined him.

It was the first time the NAACP won a school case at the Supreme Court. Missouri's answer was to build a separate law school for Black students at Lincoln University in 1939 rather than let him into the one it already had.

He never went to either. He was last seen in Chicago some time in March 1939, at a fraternity house, and he left a bag of his things behind. Nobody formally noticed he was gone until October 1939. He has never been found, and in 2007 the FBI opened a file on it again.

He won his case and was never seen again, and the school he had gone to court for is not the one Missouri opened.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000, Americans who organized campaigns won changes in the country's laws. The Supreme Court's justices ended segregated public schools in 1954 and segregated Montgomery buses in 1956. Congress outlawed literacy tests for voting in 1965, barred sex discrimination in schools that took federal money in 1972, and in 1990 made it illegal to refuse a disabled person a job or a way into a public building.

Every one of those changes followed a campaign, and the people who ran the campaigns lost jobs, homes and lives. Police officers beat marchers with nightsticks, and firemen turned high-pressure hoses on them. In Birmingham in 1963 police arrested hundreds of children, and members of the Ku Klux Klan killed four girls with a bomb in their church. A Neshoba County deputy sheriff handed three civil rights workers over to the Klan in 1964, and an Alabama state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson in 1965.

Doctors and officials also did things in these years to people who were not marching. In 1961 a doctor in Mississippi cut out Fannie Lou Hamer's womb without asking her or telling her. In June 1973 nurses at a clinic in Montgomery, Alabama, paid for with federal money, told Minnie Relf her daughters would get birth control shots. Surgeons then cut or tied the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so the two girls, aged 12 and 14, could never become pregnant. The records do not name the surgeons. At Willowbrook State School on Staten Island, New York, built for 4,000 disabled children and adults and holding 6,000 by 1965, parents of 5,000 residents sued the state on 17 March 1972. **`health` tells the sterilizations and Willowbrook in full.** Federal officials pushed between 5,000 and 10,000 gay people out of government jobs, by one historian's count, and a Los Angeles court jailed Bayard Rustin in 1953 for having sex with men who had agreed to it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="four movements running at once" -->
Four movements were organizing at the same time in these years, and their members learned from each other.

Black Americans in the civil rights movement started first. They ran boycotts, sit-ins, bus rides, marches and voter registration drives, and they pressed Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Women organized for equal pay, equal jobs, and a constitutional amendment that ended three states short. Gay and lesbian Americans, who could be fired from government jobs and arrested in bars, began organizing openly after a police raid in New York in 1969. Disabled Americans, who could be shut out of a school, a job or a building, occupied a federal office in 1977 and won a law of their own in 1990.

Other groups organized in the same years. Mexican American students and workers built the Chicano movement, told below. Native activists held Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay from 20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971, and held the village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, for 71 days in 1973. **`native-nations` tells both.** Farmworkers organized under Larry Itliong, Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta. **`work-workers` tells that campaign.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the last three steps of a twenty-year plan" -->
The court campaign that Charles Hamilton Houston laid out in the 1930s finished in this era. He did not live to see it. He died on 22 April 1950, six weeks before the Supreme Court decided the two cases his plan had been building toward.

Both came down on 5 June 1950, and all nine justices agreed with both. Heman Marion Sweatt had applied to the University of Texas Law School in February 1946 and was turned away for being Black. Texas answered by opening a separate law school for Black students in Austin in February 1947. It had four part-time teachers borrowed from the university, about 10,000 books ordered of which few had arrived, no full-time librarian, and no accreditation. Chief Justice Fred Vinson wrote that the education offered there was not "substantially equal" to the one Sweatt would get at the university, and that "Equal protection of the laws is not achieved through indiscriminate imposition of inequalities." Texas had to admit him.

George W. McLaurin already had a master's degree and wanted a doctorate in education. The University of Oklahoma let him in and then wrote rules for him. The Court printed them: he "was required to sit apart at a designated desk in an anteroom adjoining the classroom." He had to sit at a set desk on the library's mezzanine floor and not use the desks in the main reading room. He had "to sit at a designated table and to eat at a different time from the other students in the school cafeteria." Before the argument the university loosened the rules: a marked row instead of the anteroom, a table on the library's main floor, and the cafeteria at normal hours but still at his own table. The justices struck the whole thing down anyway.

Four years later the same court took the last step. *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* was decided on 17 May 1954, and again nobody dissented. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote: "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." Separating children by race in public schools broke the Fourteenth Amendment.

*Brown* was five lawsuits, not one. The Court took them together in the fall of 1952. They were the Topeka, Kansas case, *Briggs v. Elliott* from Clarendon County, South Carolina, *Davis* from Prince Edward County, Virginia, *Belton v. Gebhart* from Delaware, and *Bolling v. Sharpe* from Washington, D.C.

**`education` tells what the ruling changed inside classrooms, and how slowly. `government-politics` has the ruling as law. The campaign that produced it is told here.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what it cost the families who signed" -->
Somebody had to put their name on those lawsuits. In Clarendon County, South Carolina, 107 parents and children did, and the county made them pay for it.

The district ran more than thirty school buses for white children and none for Black children. The parents started by asking for one bus. The white schools in the case record are "modern, safe, sanitary, well equipped, lighted and healthy." The Black schools are "inadequate…unhealthy…old and overcrowded and in a dilapidated condition."

The Reverend Joseph A. DeLaine, a minister and teacher, brought the NAACP together with the Pearson brothers and got the case started. Six white men attacked him. He got away by pretending he had a gun in his pocket and jumping into a passing truck driven by another man who had signed.

The petition went round for signature on 11 November 1949. The suit was filed on 16 May 1950. It is named for Harry Briggs because his name was first on the list.

The National Park Service says what happened to the signers: they were "fired from their jobs, denied service when trying to purchase supplies for their farms, or being turned down for loans on their properties." A Black farmer in Clarendon County who signed could lose his work, his seed, and his mortgage in the same season.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the years after the ruling" -->
Winning in court did not open a single school by itself. Somebody had to walk in, and somebody had to keep the campaign going while they did.

Prince Edward County, Virginia, was the home of one of the five *Brown* cases. On 26 June 1959 its county supervisors voted to stop paying for public schools, so the county had none at all. They stayed shut until 8 September 1964. About 2,000 Black children had no school for five of their growing-up years. In 1963 Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy said the only places on earth with no free public education were "Communist China, North Vietnam, Sarawak, Singapore, British Honduras" and "Prince Edward County, Virginia." The Supreme Court ordered the county to open its schools on 25 May 1964, in a case called *Griffin*, after the Reverend L. Francis Griffin, the Farmville minister who had called the parents together thirteen years before.

Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1957 is usually told as nine students and a crowd. The organizing behind it was Daisy Bates. She was president of the Arkansas NAACP and, with her husband L. C. Bates, published a newspaper called the *Arkansas State Press*. The nine met at her house every school morning and were driven to Central High from there. In August 1957 a rock came through her living room window with a note tied to it: "STONE THIS TIME. DYNAMITE NEXT." Crosses were burned in her front yard twice, and somebody fired shots into the house from a passing car. **`education` tells what happened inside the school.**

City officials then went after the organization instead of the students. The city councils of Little Rock and North Little Rock passed rules ordering every group to file its membership list with the city clerk, to be made public. Daisy Bates kept the Little Rock branch's records and refused to hand them over, and she was convicted for refusing. On 23 February 1960 the Supreme Court threw her conviction out. Justice Potter Stewart wrote that keeping who belongs to a group private "may in many circumstances be indispensable to preservation of freedom of association, particularly where a group espouses dissident beliefs."

New Orleans, 14 November 1960, is usually told as one six-year-old girl. There were four. Judge J. Skelly Wright had ordered the city to desegregate, and on that morning Ruby Bridges went into William Frantz Elementary, and Leona Tate, Tessie Prevost and Gail Etienne went into McDonogh 19 in the Lower Ninth Ward together, walked in by federal marshals. By the end of that month fewer than ten white children were attending the two schools put together. From January to May 1961 Ruby Bridges was the only pupil in William Frantz. **`education` has her story. The other three girls belong in the same sentence as she does.**

Then the fight moved north, and the movement lost it. In *Milliken v. Bradley*, decided 25 July 1974, five justices to four ruled that a judge could not bus children across the line between a city district and its suburbs unless it was proved that one district's actions had segregated the other. The ruling stopped a judge's plan that would have covered Detroit and 53 suburban districts. Justice Thurgood Marshall, who had argued *Brown*, was one of the four who dissented.

Boston is the fight people remember. Tallulah Morgan, a Black mother of three, and thirteen other families sued the Boston School Committee in 1972. On 21 June 1974 Judge W. Arthur Garrity ruled that "the entire school system of Boston was unconstitutionally segregated," and that school officials "had knowingly carried out a systematic program of segregation affecting all of the city's students, teachers and school facilities." Buses began on 9 September 1974, pairing South Boston with Roxbury. Crowds threw rocks and bottles at the buses, broke the windows and shouted racial insults at the children inside. Of the 800 Black students assigned to South Boston High that first day, 56 came. A federal judge was still supervising Boston's schools in 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="sitting down and staying, 1960 and 1961" -->
On 1 February 1960 four freshmen from the Agricultural and Technical College of North Carolina walked into the Woolworth store in Greensboro, took seats at the whites-only lunch counter, ordered coffee and waited. Their names were Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil, David Richmond and Ezell Blair Jr.

Nobody served them. The waitress and the manager acted as though the seats were empty, a policeman walked up and down behind them, and some white customers shouted at them while others said keep going.

The next day nineteen people came. The day after that eighty-five came, from several colleges. Before the week was out there were 400, taking turns so they could still go to their classes. Students started sit-ins in seven other North Carolina towns and in Hampton, Virginia and Nashville, Tennessee. By that summer 33 Southern cities had opened their restaurants and lunch counters, and a year later 126 had.

The next year they did it on buses. In 1960 the Supreme Court had ruled in *Boynton v. Virginia* that segregation in bus and railway stations used by passengers travelling between states was illegal. On 4 May 1961 thirteen Black and white riders left Washington on a bus for New Orleans to find out whether the ruling meant anything. They were called the Freedom Riders, and the Congress of Racial Equality had organized them.

On 14 May 1961 a white mob, including members of the Ku Klux Klan, met the Greyhound bus at Anniston, Alabama. The National Park Service says the mob slashed the tyres, followed the bus out of town and firebombed it. Other accounts add that the mob broke the windows and held the door shut. The riders were "trapped on the burning bus," got out after an explosion, and then "the mob beat them," in the Park Service's words. The Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth put together a convoy of cars and got them to Birmingham within a few hours.

The same day, young white men climbed onto the second bus, a Trailways bus, at Anniston and beat the riders until they moved to segregated seats. When that bus reached Birmingham, Klansmen attacked the riders at the station. Bull Connor, the city's public safety commissioner, had ordered his police to stay away from it. No record in these sources shows anyone convicted for the Anniston attacks.

Photographs of that burning bus went round the world, and more riders came.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Birmingham, 1963: the children and the church" -->
On 2 May 1963 schoolchildren in Birmingham, Alabama, aged from eight to eighteen, walked out of the 16th Street Baptist Church to march downtown and ask the mayor to end segregation.

Police acting on the orders of Bull Connor, the public safety commissioner, arrested hundreds of them that day. Two National Park Service pages give two counts: 600 children arrested, some as young as six, or 1,200 by the end of the day. By the next day at least 1,000 children were in jail, and over the following days Connor's police jailed more than 3,000 demonstrators.

Then Connor's men used water and dogs. In the National Park Service's words: "Firemen blasted the protestors, including children, with high-pressure fire hoses, and police used their nightsticks indiscriminately. Police K-9 units loosed their dogs into crowds of peaceful demonstrators, pinning down one woman and severely wounding three teenagers." Those are the hoses used to fight building fires. The water comes out hard enough to knock a grown person off their feet, and it was turned on children.

Photographs of that week ran in newspapers all over the country and the world.

On Sunday 15 September 1963, just before eleven in the morning, members of the Ku Klux Klan set off a bomb under the steps of that same church. The bomb killed four girls. Addie Mae Collins, Carole Robertson and Cynthia Wesley were fourteen, and Denise McNair was eleven. They had been in the basement women's room a few minutes earlier, in their Sunday clothes, ready to sing and to show people to their seats, talking about the new school year. Addie Mae's sister Sarah lived and lost her right eye.

FBI agents named Robert Chambliss as their main suspect, but FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover closed the case in 1968 without charging anyone and had the evidence sealed. The case was reopened after Hoover died in 1972. Chambliss was convicted on 14 November 1977, fourteen years after the bombing, and died in prison in 1985. Thomas Blanton was convicted on 1 May 2001, got four life sentences and died in prison in 2020. Bobby Frank Cherry was convicted in 2002, thirty-nine years after the bomb. A fourth Klansman, Herman Cash, died in 1994 without ever being tried.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the organizers who were killed" -->
Organizing in Mississippi could get a person killed, and it did.

Medgar Evers was born at Decatur, Mississippi on 2 July 1925 and fought in Germany and France in the Second World War. From 1954 he was the NAACP's field secretary for the whole state of Mississippi. He ran marches, prayer vigils, boycotts and voter registration drives, and he worked to get James Meredith admitted to the University of Mississippi in 1962.

Byron De La Beckwith, a member of the White Citizens' Council, shot him in the back in the driveway of his own house in Jackson in the early hours of 12 June 1963. His wife Myrlie and their children had stayed up to wait for him and were watching television. When they heard the shot the children did what they had practised: they got down on the floor and crawled to the bathroom. Myrlie found her husband outside, bleeding, still holding the T-shirts he had carried in from the car. He died at University Hospital of blood loss.

Beckwith was tried twice in 1964, and both juries failed to agree on a verdict. He was convicted on 5 February 1994, thirty-one years and three trials after the killing, and sentenced to life in prison. He died in 2001, aged 80.

The next summer, hundreds of students came to Mississippi to run Freedom Schools and register Black voters. It was called Freedom Summer. On 21 June 1964 three of those workers went to look at a Black church that had been burned down after a civil rights meeting was held in it. James Chaney was twenty-one, a Black college student from Mississippi. Andrew Goodman was twenty and Michael Schwerner was twenty-four, both white New Yorkers working for CORE.

Local police arrested the three of them for speeding. The FBI's account says a Neshoba County deputy sheriff, Cecil Price, made the arrest at five in the evening and let them go about half past ten at night, and that Ku Klux Klan members were waiting for them by arrangement. The National Archives puts it plainly: they were "released after dark into the hands of the Ku Klux Klan."

Their bodies were found on 4 August 1964, buried near a place called Old Jolly Farm in Neshoba County. Goodman and Schwerner had each been shot once in the head. Chaney had two broken arms, a broken jaw, a crushed right shoulder and injuries to his groin.

Twenty-one men were arrested. On 20 October 1967 a federal jury convicted seven of the eighteen men on trial, among them Deputy Sheriff Cecil Price and Samuel Bowers, the imperial wizard (the top leader) of the White Knights of the Ku Klux Klan of Mississippi. The charge was violating the three men's civil rights, not murder, and the sentences ran from three to ten years. One juror would not convict Edgar Ray Killen, a preacher who had organized the killings. No Mississippi jury convicted anyone of killing them until 21 June 2005, forty-one years to the day, when a jury found Killen guilty of manslaughter. He was sentenced to sixty years and died in the state prison at Parchman on 11 January 2018.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the March on Washington, 28 August 1963" -->
About 250,000 people came to Washington on 28 August 1963 for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. The National Park Service counts about 190,000 of them Black and about 60,000 white.

A. Philip Randolph had been planning a march like this since 1941, when he threatened one and got an executive order instead. This time he held it. Bayard Rustin did the actual work of organizing it, and the whole march was put together in under three months.

It had ten demands, and they were about money as much as about law: fair wages, fair hiring, and desegregated schools.

The speakers were men. Roy Wilkins of the NAACP, Whitney Young of the National Urban League, Martin Luther King Jr. of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, James Farmer of the Congress of Racial Equality, John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, the union leader Walter Reuther, and the clergymen Joachim Prinz, Eugene Carson Blake and Matthew Ahmann. Dorothy Height of the National Council of Negro Women helped plan it. No woman gave a speech. The National Park Service says Height "operated in the background of this male dominated, leadership group."

John Lewis had written the sharpest speech of the day, and he was asked to soften it. The Park Service says his remarks were "toned down...at the request of white liberals and moderate black allies." What survived included this: "we do not want our freedom gradually but we want to be free now."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selma, 1965, and the right to vote" -->
The Fifteenth Amendment had said since 1870 that a citizen could not be kept from voting because of race. Ninety-five years later, officials in Southern states were still keeping Black citizens off the voting lists with literacy tests, poll taxes and threats. In 1965 marchers in Selma, Alabama, set out to end that.

On 18 February 1965, during a protest in Marion, Alabama, Alabama State Trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Jimmie Lee Jackson, who was 26, in the stomach inside a cafe while Jackson was protecting his mother and grandfather. Jackson died of an infection from the wound on 26 February. Fowler was charged with murder in 2007. In 2010 he pleaded guilty to misdemeanor manslaughter, a minor charge under Alabama law, and served five months of a six-month sentence.

On Sunday 7 March 1965 about 600 people, most of them Black, walked out of Brown Chapel in Selma to march to the state capital. At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, Alabama State Trooper Major John Cloud gave them two minutes to turn round. Then, in the Park Service's words, the marchers were "attacked by the law enforcement officers with nightsticks and teargas." At least 50 of them needed hospital treatment. The country watched it on television that night, and it is called Bloody Sunday.

Two days later, on 9 March, about 2,000 people came back. So many ministers had travelled to Selma that it is called the Ministers March. King led them to the bridge and turned them round. That evening four white men attacked the Reverend James Reeb, a minister from Boston, and two other ministers outside a restaurant in Selma. One of the men hit Reeb in the head with a club, and he died on 11 March. Elmer L. Cook, William Stanley Hoggle and Namon O'Neil Hoggle were tried for his murder, and on 10 December 1965 a jury acquitted all three. The prosecutor had let a man whose brother was a suspect in the attack sit on the jury.

The march finally went on 21 March 1965 with more than 8,000 people. It reached the capitol in Montgomery on 25 March with a crowd of nearly 25,000.

That night four members of the Ku Klux Klan fired into Viola Liuzzo's car on Highway 80 in Lowndes County and killed her at the wheel. She was a 39-year-old white mother of five from Detroit who had come to Selma to help, and her passenger, Leroy Moton, a 19-year-old Black volunteer, lived. The men were Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., William Orville Eaton, Eugene Thomas and Gary Thomas Rowe, and Rowe was a paid FBI informant. Alabama juries did not convict the three others. A federal jury convicted them in December 1965 of violating her civil rights, and each got ten years. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover called Liuzzo an "outside agitator" and passed rumours about her private life to reporters. Her family sued the FBI in 1979, and a federal judge dismissed the suit in 1983.

President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965. It outlawed literacy tests and stopped their use where they had been used, and it sent federal examiners into those places with the power to register people themselves. It also said that a covered state or county had to get permission from Washington before changing any of its voting rules.

It worked quickly. By the end of 1965 a quarter of a million new Black voters were registered, a third of them by the federal examiners. By the end of 1966, only four of the thirteen Southern states still had fewer than half their Black citizens registered. **`government-politics` has the act as law.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Chicano movement, 1954 to 1970" -->
Mexican Americans ran their own rights campaigns in these years. Many called themselves Chicanos, and their campaigns the Chicano movement.

The first big win came in court. A jury in Jackson County, Texas, convicted a Mexican American man, Pete Hernandez, of murder and he was sentenced to life. About 14 out of every 100 people in the county had Mexican or Latin American surnames. In twenty-five years no one with such a name had served on a jury commission, the board that picked jurors, and more than 6,000 jurors had been called without one. At the courthouse, one of the two men's toilets was marked "Colored Men" and "Hombres Aqui" ("Men Here"). On 3 May 1954 all nine justices ruled in *Hernandez v. Texas* that shutting Mexican Americans out of juries broke the Fourteenth Amendment. That was two weeks before *Brown*.

From 1 to 8 March 1968 about 15,000 students walked out of seven high schools in East Los Angeles. About 60 out of every 100 Mexican American students there were leaving high school without finishing. Staff forbade them to speak Spanish and put them in trade classes instead of classes that led to college. The students asked for bilingual teaching, changes in what they were taught, and Mexican American principals and administrators. The Board of Education said no, because of the cost. On 31 March police arrested thirteen organizers for felony conspiracy, the serious crime of planning together to break the law. One of them, the teacher Sal Castro, was fired. He got his job back only after months of sit-ins at the board's offices, where police arrested 35 people on 2 October.

On 29 August 1970 between 20,000 and 30,000 people marched through East Los Angeles in the National Chicano Moratorium. They were protesting the number of Mexican Americans drafted, wounded and killed in the Vietnam War. About 1,500 police officers came to Laguna Park, fired tear gas into the crowd and beat protesters. Four people died that day, and 150 were arrested. Los Angeles County Sheriff's Deputy Thomas Wilson fired a tear gas projectile into the Silver Dollar Bar on Whittier Boulevard. It hit Rubén Salazar, a reporter for the *Los Angeles Times*, in the head and killed him. The coroner ruled it a homicide, a killing by another person. District Attorney Evelle Younger decided not to charge Wilson. The city later renamed Laguna Park after Salazar.

**`work-workers` tells the farmworkers' campaign. `education` has the schools.**

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="gay Americans organize, 1953 to 1979" -->
On 27 April 1953 President Dwight Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450. It listed "sexual perversion," the government's term for being gay, among the reasons to remove someone from a federal job. The historian David K. Johnson estimates that federal officials pushed 5,000 to 10,000 people out of their jobs under it. Frank Kameny, a scientist at the Army Map Service, was fired in 1957 and fought his firing as a civil rights case. On 21 May 1965 he and others picketed the White House over the firings. The Civil Service Commission stopped firing federal workers for being gay in 1975.

In New York in the 1960s, bars were forbidden to serve alcohol to gay people. Police raided gay bars, arrested customers and photographed them. In the early hours of 28 June 1969 police led by Deputy Inspector Seymour Pine raided the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village, a gay bar opened in 1967 by a Mafia member. This time the crowd outside stayed. People threw coins, bottles, bricks, garbage cans and burning trash, and the police shut themselves inside the bar until the Tactical Patrol Force, police trained to break up riots, arrived. The protests went on for six nights, and police arrested 13 people the first night. National Park Service historians count fifty to sixty gay groups in the country before Stonewall and at least 1,500 a year later. On 28 June 1970, the first anniversary, gay men and lesbians marched in New York, and marches were held in other cities.

Harvey Milk was one of the first openly gay people elected to office in the United States, as a member of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors. On 27 November 1978 Dan White, a former supervisor, shot Mayor George Moscone and then shot Milk five times at City Hall. In May 1979 a jury convicted White of voluntary manslaughter, a lesser crime than murder, and he was sentenced to seven years and eight months. He served about five. Crowds rioted at City Hall after the verdict. **`health` tells AIDS and ACT UP.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the women's movement and the Equal Rights Amendment, 1966 to 1982" -->
Twenty-eight people started the National Organization for Women, known as NOW, on 30 June 1966, at a government conference on women in Washington. Federal law already banned sex discrimination at work. They had wanted the conference to demand that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the federal office in charge of that ban, enforce it, and the conference officials ruled the motion out of order. That night they met in the hotel room of Betty Friedan, the author of *The Feminine Mystique*, and set up NOW "to take action to bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society NOW."

The Equal Rights Amendment is one sentence long: "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Abridged means cut back. Alice Paul had written the first version in the 1920s. Representative Martha Griffiths of Michigan got it through the House in 1971, and senators passed it 84 to 8 on 22 March 1972. Thirty-eight states, three quarters of them, had to ratify it within seven years. Hawaii went first, and 35 states had ratified it by 1977.

Phyllis Schlafly started a campaign called STOP ERA in 1972. She argued that the amendment would make women liable to the military draft and take away widows' Social Security benefits and mothers' advantage in custody cases. Lawmakers in Nebraska, Tennessee, Idaho, Kentucky and South Dakota voted to take back their states' ratifications. Congress extended the deadline to 1982, and no other state ratified before it ran out. The amendment ended three states short. **Era 10 has what happened to it after 2000.**

In October 1974 Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. It made it illegal for a lender to refuse a woman credit because of her sex or because she was married or single.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Title IX, 1972: thirty-seven words" -->
Title IX is one sentence long. It became law on 23 June 1972, and it says: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

There is no mention of sport in it. It covers any school or college programme paid for even partly with federal money, which is what gave one sentence its reach.

Three members of Congress carried it. Patsy Takemoto Mink of Hawaii and Edith Green of Oregon in the House, and Birch Bayh of Indiana in the Senate. Green, a former teacher, ran the House hearings. When Mink died in 2002 Congress renamed the law after her, the Patsy Takemoto Mink Equal Opportunity in Education Act.

The change in school sport is the part people can count. Before Title IX fewer than 300,000 girls played high school sports. By 2012 it was over three million, about ten times as many. That is not the same as equal. The same year, the White House said American high schools still offered girls 1.3 million fewer chances to play than boys. In 2013-14 the Government Accountability Office found that girls were 49 out of every 100 students at public high schools that offered sport, and 43 out of every 100 of the players.

**`education` has what changed in classrooms and admissions. `sports-play` has the games themselves. `government-politics` has the statute.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the parents who sued to get their children into school at all" -->
In 1971 Pennsylvania's school law let a school board turn a disabled child away. Two lawsuits brought by parents, argued in 1971 and 1972, ended that in Pennsylvania and in Washington, D.C.

Pennsylvania had written the refusal into its school code. One section let a school board "refuse to accept or retain beginners who have not attained a mental age of five years." Another provided for the "temporary or permanent exclusion from the public school of children who are found to be uneducable and untrainable in the public schools." A child who scored under 30 on an intelligence test was called uneducable and handed to the state welfare department. The words "uneducable" and "untrainable" are the state's own words, printed in its law, and what they meant in practice was that the child never went to school.

The parents had an organization: the Pennsylvania Association for Retarded Children. "Retarded" was the ordinary word for an intellectual disability then, and it was the word in the statutes and the case. On 7 January 1971 the association sued the state on behalf of fourteen children, with a Philadelphia lawyer named Thomas K. Gilhool. It estimated that about 100,000 children in Pennsylvania were being kept out of free education and training. Three federal judges signed the settlement on 8 October 1971 and the final order on 5 May 1972. Pennsylvania had to find those children, enrol them, and give a parent a hearing before moving or excluding any child.

The second case was *Mills v. Board of Education*, brought by seven Washington, D.C. families and decided on 1 August 1972. **`education` tells that one in full, child by child.**

Suits like these were running in twenty states at once. President Gerald Ford signed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act on 29 November 1975. The federal Bureau of Education for the Handicapped estimated that even in 1975 about a million disabled children were completely shut out of school, and at least another 300,000 were getting schooling that did not suit them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="504, Gallaudet, and the Americans with Disabilities Act" -->
Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 was the first federal law that made it illegal to shut a disabled person out. It says that no otherwise qualified individual with a disability "shall, solely by reason of his or her disability, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

Federal officials could not enforce it until someone signed the rules that said how it worked. For four years nobody did. The Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, Joseph A. Califano Jr., would not sign them after schools and hospitals objected to what they would cost.

On 5 April 1977 disabled people demonstrated at federal offices in city after city on the same day: San Francisco, Washington, Seattle, Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, Dallas, Atlanta, New York, Boston and Philadelphia. San Francisco is the one that held. About a hundred people occupied the HEW offices at 50 UN Plaza and stayed for twenty-six days.

They could not have stayed without help from outside. The Black Panther Party brought in one hot meal every day, plus the makings of two more. The International Association of Machinists, a union, paid the bills and paid for a delegation to fly to Washington. A lesbian cooperative called the Brick Hut and Glide Memorial Church helped too. Judy Heumann, Kitty Cone and Dennis Billups ran the occupation from inside. Heumann and Billups went to Washington. Califano signed.

Eleven years later, deaf students shut down a university. Gallaudet, in Washington, is the country's university for deaf students, and in March 1988 its board of trustees picked a hearing candidate, Elisabeth Zinser, over deaf ones. Students marched, stopped going to class, and let the air out of the tyres of the buses parked across the gates. They made four demands: Zinser to go and a deaf president to be chosen, the board chair Jane Spilman to resign, at least 51 out of every 100 board members to be deaf, and no punishment for anyone who had protested. After eight days the board gave way. I. King Jordan became Gallaudet's first deaf president, and said in his acceptance speech: "A Deaf person can do anything a hearing person can, except hear."

Before the Americans with Disabilities Act was passed, protesters left their wheelchairs, walkers and crutches at the bottom of the Capitol steps in Washington and pulled themselves up the stone by hand, in front of the cameras. It is called the Capitol Crawl. President George H. W. Bush signed the Americans with Disabilities Act on 26 July 1990. It made it illegal to refuse a disabled person a job, a bus, a shop, a restaurant or a public building.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="an apology and $20,000, 1980 to 1990" -->
Japanese Americans who had been locked in camps during the Second World War won an apology from the government in 1988. The Japanese American Citizens League and the National Council for Japanese American Redress campaigned for it.

On 31 July 1980 President Jimmy Carter signed a law setting up a commission to study the camps. Its members held 20 days of hearings in 11 places in 1981 and heard more than 750 witnesses. Their report of February 1983 found the causes were "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership."

Senators Daniel Inouye and Spark Matsunaga and Representatives Norman Mineta and Robert Matsui led the bill through Congress. President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act on 10 August 1988. Under it, each person still living who had been held got an apology and $20,000. The first letters and checks went out on 1 October 1990.

### Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr.
> **Who:** The NAACP branch secretary whose arrest set off the boycott, and the twenty-six-year-old minister the boycotters chose to lead them · **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, December 1955 to December 1956

Rosa Parks had been an officer of the Montgomery NAACP for twelve years on the day she was arrested. Many accounts describe her as a tired seamstress. She was a trained organizer.

She was born in Tuskegee, Alabama on 4 February 1913, and married Raymond Parks in 1932. She started the Montgomery NAACP Youth Council in the early 1940s and served as the branch's secretary from 1943 to 1956. As secretary of the Alabama State Conference of the NAACP, she travelled the state interviewing victims of discrimination and witnesses to lynchings. She had broken the bus seating rules before, and a driver had once put her off a bus for it.

The police arrested her on 1 December 1955 for refusing to give up her bus seat.

Black women in Montgomery had been organizing about the buses for six years already. The Women's Political Council, a group of Black professional women started in 1949, had been asking the city to change the rules and getting nowhere. Its leader, Jo Ann Robinson, had proposed a boycott more than a year before. When Parks was arrested, the council ran off 50,000 leaflets calling for a one-day boycott on Monday 5 December.

Nobody went back to the buses after that Monday. On the same day, Montgomery's Black ministers and organizers formed the Montgomery Improvement Association, and elected as its president Martin Luther King Jr., twenty-six years old, pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, still finishing his doctorate at Boston University.

There were 40,000 Black people in Montgomery. Most of them walked to work or to school, shared cars, took taxis or hitchhiked, for a year.

City officials fought back. They indicted nearly 100 of the boycott's leaders for conspiracy. On 30 January 1956 someone bombed King's house while his wife Coretta, their seven-week-old daughter Yolanda and a neighbour were inside. Nobody was hurt. On 1 February a bomb went off in the yard of E. D. Nixon, the NAACP organizer who had been working on the buses in Montgomery for years. No one was ever prosecuted for the bombing of King's house.

What actually ended the buses' segregation was a different lawsuit, with four other women's names on it. *Browder v. Gayle* was filed on 1 February 1956 by the lawyer Fred Gray for Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Mary Louise Smith, and Claudette Colvin, who had been arrested on a Montgomery bus at fifteen, on 2 March 1955, nine months before Parks. Colvin died on 13 January 2026, aged 86. A federal court in Montgomery struck down Alabama's bus segregation laws in June 1956, and the Supreme Court agreed in November. Black riders got back on the buses on 21 December 1956.

Rosa Parks lost her job as a seamstress and got death threats. She and Raymond left Alabama for Detroit in 1957. She worked in Representative John Conyers's office from 1965 to 1988 and died in Detroit on 24 October 2005.

Martin Luther King Jr. led the movement for twelve more years. He was one of the organizers of the March on Washington in 1963 and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, at thirty-five. He was shot in Memphis, Tennessee and died at 7:05 in the evening on 4 April 1968, at St. Joseph's Hospital. He was thirty-nine. James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to his murder in March 1969 and was sentenced to 99 years. Ray tried for the rest of his life to take the plea back until he died in April 1998. In 1999 a jury in a civil case brought by the King family found that a Memphis man and "others, including government agencies" had plotted the killing. A Justice Department investigation in 2000 found no credible evidence for that plot.

### Bayard Rustin
> **Who:** The organizer who built the March on Washington in under three months, and whom a California court had jailed for being gay · **When and where:** West Chester, Pennsylvania, 1912 · Los Angeles, 1953 · Washington, 1963

Bayard Rustin was born in West Chester, Pennsylvania on 17 March 1912 and raised a Quaker in a family already doing civil rights work. Because of that upbringing he was a pacifist all his life, someone who refuses to support war or violence.

He was arrested many times. In 1947 he and George Houser organized the Journey of Reconciliation, a bus trip meant to test a Supreme Court ruling that had banned racial discrimination in travel between states. It was the first of the Freedom Rides. Rustin was arrested for breaking a state law on segregated seating and served twenty-two days on a chain gang, which meant working outdoors on the roads with his ankles chained.

He taught Martin Luther King Jr. how nonviolent protest actually works, and he did the daily organizing for A. Philip Randolph.

On 22 January 1953 the Los Angeles County Superior Court convicted him under a California law against vagrancy. What he had actually done was have sex with two other men, which both of them had agreed to. The court gave him sixty days in jail and put his name on the register of sex offenders. In the state's own words, seventy years later, "Mr. Rustin was sentenced pursuant to a charge commonly used to punish gay men for engaging in consensual adult sexual conduct."

His opponents used that court record for the rest of his life. When Governor Gavin Newsom pardoned him in 2020, the pardon said the injustice of the conviction "was compounded by his political opponents' use of the record of this case to try to undermine him, his associates, and the civil rights movement."

He kept organizing anyway, and he did it from behind the platform rather than on it. A. Philip Randolph made him deputy director of the March on Washington, which meant he planned the whole of it. The march was put together in less than three months. On 28 August 1963 about 250,000 people came.

He died in New York City on 24 August 1987, of a burst appendix, aged seventy-five. On 8 August 2013 President Barack Obama gave him the Presidential Medal of Freedom, calling him "an unyielding activist for civil rights, dignity, and equality for all." On 4 February 2020 California cancelled the 1953 conviction and started a process to pardon everyone else it had convicted the same way.

### Judy Heumann
> **Who:** Refused a place at school as a child and a teaching job as an adult, she sued, won, and then helped lead the 26-day sit-in that won the Section 504 rules · **When and where:** New York City, 1949 to 1970 · San Francisco, 1977

She was born in Philadelphia on 18 December 1947. At eighteen months old she got polio, a virus that attacks the nerves that work the muscles. It paralysed her, and she used a wheelchair for the rest of her life.

New York City's public schools would not let her in. Her mother, Ilse, spent years arguing with them. Judy got into school at about fourth grade, into a separate programme for disabled students called Health Conservation 21.

She finished university and applied for a teaching licence. The New York Board of Education turned her down because she used a wheelchair. She went to the American Civil Liberties Union and it refused to take her case, on the grounds that a medical reason for refusing someone is not discrimination.

Then the *New York Times* put her story on its front page in 1970, and a lawyer named Roy Lucas read it and offered to represent her. She sued the Board of Education at twenty-two. The judge was Constance Baker Motley. The Board settled before trial and gave her the licence.

She and the people who had helped her started a group called Disabled in Action.

In April 1977 she was one of about a hundred disabled people who occupied the federal health and education offices at 50 UN Plaza in San Francisco for twenty-six days, to force the government to sign the rules for Section 504. She and Dennis Billups flew to Washington while the occupation was still going, on money raised by a machinists' union. The Secretary signed.

She spent the rest of her life in the same work, including eight years as the federal official in charge of special education and rehabilitation, from 1993 to 2001, four years at the World Bank, and seven as the federal government's adviser on disability rights around the world.

She died in Washington on 4 March 2023. She once put it this way: "Some people say that what I did changed the world. But really, I simply refused to accept what I was told about who I could be. And I was willing to make a fuss about it."

### Fannie Lou Hamer
> **Who:** A plantation timekeeper who tried to register to vote, was fired the same day and beaten in a jail for it, and then said so on national television · **When and where:** Ruleville, Sunflower County, Mississippi, 1962-63 · Atlantic City, New Jersey, 22 August 1964

She was born on 6 October 1917 in Montgomery County, Mississippi, the youngest of the twenty children of Ella and James Lee Townsend. She began working in the cotton fields at six, weeding first and then picking. In 1944 she married Perry Hamer, whom everybody called Pap. She worked eighteen years on the W. D. Marlow plantation outside Ruleville, keeping the time records for the other workers.

In 1961 she went into hospital and came out unable to have children. A doctor had performed a hysterectomy on her: he cut out her womb, the organ inside a woman's body where a baby grows. It cannot be put back, and it cannot be reversed. Nobody had asked her, and nobody had told her. This was done to enough Black women in Mississippi that Hamer gave it a name that stuck: a Mississippi appendectomy.

On 31 August 1962 she led seventeen people twenty-six miles to the county courthouse at Indianola to register to vote. Police stood at the door and let only two of them in to take the literacy test. That evening the plantation owner told her: "If you don't go down and withdraw your registration, you will have to leave." She answered: "I didn't try to register for you. I tried to register for myself." She lost the job she had held for eighteen years that same day. On 10 September somebody fired sixteen bullets into a house in Ruleville belonging to a family called Tucker, and shot at other houses the same night.

On 9 June 1963 she was coming home from a training course on citizenship, one of ten people on a Continental Trailways bus. At Winona, Mississippi, some of the group tried to use the restaurant and the washroom at the bus station. The police arrested them all.

In the jail, state highway patrolmen made two Black prisoners, Roosevelt Knox and Sol Poe, beat her. They handed the first prisoner a blackjack (a short leather club with lead inside it, made to knock a person down) and made her lie face down on a bunk. He beat her until he had no strength left. Then the patrolman gave the blackjack to the second prisoner and told the first one to sit on her feet so she could not move them. In her words: "I began to scream and one white man got up and began to beat me in my head and tell me to hush." She lost the sight of one eye and had kidney damage for the rest of her life. In December 1963 federal prosecutors tried five Winona and Montgomery County officers, among them Winona Police Chief Thomas Herrod, for violating the prisoners' civil rights. An all-white jury found them not guilty on every count. Hamer said afterwards: "After I got out of jail, half dead, I found that Medgar Evers had been shot down in his own yard."

Leaders of Mississippi's Democratic Party kept Black people out of it, so Hamer and others built their own party, the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, and sent their own delegates to the national party's convention in Atlantic City in August 1964. On 22 August she told the story above to the convention's credentials committee, and it went out on national television. The National Archives puts what happened next plainly: her testimony "was interrupted by President Lyndon Johnson, in an effort to suppress the truth and stabilize the Democratic Party."

She finished: "All of this is on account of we want to register, to become first-class citizens. And if the Freedom Democratic Party is not seated now, I question America. Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave, where we have to sleep with our telephones off the hooks because our lives be threatened daily, because we want to live as decent human beings, in America?"

In 1969 she started the Freedom Farm, growing food and running a co-operative so that families in Sunflower County would not depend on a plantation for a meal. She died on 14 March 1977, of heart failure brought on by cancer, diabetes and high blood pressure. She used to say: "Nobody's free until everybody's free."

### Barbara Rose Johns
> **Who:** A sixteen-year-old who tricked her principal out of the building and led her whole school out on strike, three years before *Brown* · **When and where:** Farmville, Prince Edward County, Virginia, 23 April 1951

She was born in New York City on 6 March 1935 and grew up in Prince Edward County, Virginia. She went to Robert Russa Moton High School in Farmville, the county's high school for Black students.

The building had been made for 180 students. It held between 360 and 450. Classes met in the auditorium, in parked school buses, and in three wooden huts covered in tar paper that people said looked like chicken coops. There was no cafeteria, no science laboratory and no gym.

What decided her was a bus. A half-empty school bus for white children drove past her without stopping.

On 23 April 1951 a student named John Watson telephoned the principal's office, disguised his voice, and said there was trouble with Moton students down at the bus station. Principal M. Boyd Jones went to see. While he was out, Johns got the whole school into the auditorium and told them to walk out. They stayed out for two weeks.

Then she wrote to two NAACP lawyers in Richmond, Oliver W. Hill and Spottswood William Robinson III. They agreed to take the case on one condition: the parents had to sue to end segregation itself, not to ask for a better Black school. The parents agreed. Seventy-four of them, for 118 children, put their names on *Davis v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, filed on 23 May 1951.

That lawsuit is one of the five the Supreme Court decided together as *Brown* in 1954.

Her parents sent her out of the county. She finished her last year of high school in Montgomery, Alabama, living with her uncle Vernon Johns, a pastor, and his wife Altona. The family's house in Prince Edward County later burned down. The family believed somebody set it on fire.

She died in Philadelphia on 25 September 1991, of bone cancer, aged fifty-six.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these years American rights campaigns won things no earlier movement had won, and courts and legislatures took back rights that earlier movements had already won. Several of these fights were still going on in September 2026.

Same-sex couples got the right to marry in every state in 2015. Twelve years before that, in some states, two people of the same sex could still be arrested for what they did at home. In 2022 Congress passed the first federal law against lynching, after about two hundred earlier bills had failed over 122 years.

In the same years the Supreme Court cut back the Voting Rights Act of 1965 twice, in 2013 and again in 2026, so that the states and counties once watched most closely no longer need federal permission to change a voting rule and a person suing over a map has to prove that officials meant to discriminate. The Court ruled in 2025 that a state may stop doctors giving certain treatments to transgender children, and in 2026 that a state may keep girls' school sports teams for the students it counts as female by biology.

The tools changed too. A seventeen-year-old with a phone recorded a Minneapolis police officer kneeling on George Floyd's neck in 2020, and that recording is what the rest of the country saw. Two words posted on Twitter in October 2017 turned into 19 million uses of a hashtag inside a year. Organizers still did most of their work in meetings, lawsuits and marches.

Some older fights did not close. American schools are still counted for how separate they are, and in 2021 about one American public school in five had a student body that was at least 90 percent non-white. States still keep disabled people in institutions when they could live outside them. In 2026 the Justice Department asked a federal judge to strike the federal rule against it, and the judge did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the right to vote, taken off the list" -->
In 1965 Congress made certain states and counties get permission from the federal government before they changed any rule about voting. A place went on the list if it had used a test to decide who could vote and fewer than half of its voting-age residents had registered or voted. The rule was applied to the figures for November 1964, and applied again to the figures for November 1972. Nine states were on it in full: Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Virginia from 1964, and Alaska, Arizona and Texas from 1972. Named counties in California, Florida, New York, North Carolina and South Dakota were on it too, and so were two townships in Michigan.

On 25 June 2013 the Supreme Court took the list away. The case was *Shelby County v. Holder*, and the justices split five to four. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion. He struck down Section 4(b), the part of the law that said which places were on the list, and gave this reason: "the Act imposes current burdens and must be justified by current needs." He also wrote that the Court issued "no holding on §5 itself, only on the coverage formula," and that "Congress may draft another formula based on current conditions." Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg wrote the dissent, and Justices Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan signed it.

The Justice Department explains the result on its own website. The places that had been on the list "no longer need to seek preclearance for the new voting changes." Preclearance is the permission. Congress has not written a new list. That was still true in September 2026.

The second cut came thirteen years later. The other main part of the Voting Rights Act is Section 2, which lets people go to court over a voting rule that leaves a racial group with less chance to elect anyone. Congress rewrote Section 2 in 1982 so that a person suing did not have to prove that officials meant to do it. Proving what is in someone's head is very hard, and Congress said the result was what mattered.

On 29 April 2026, in *Louisiana v. Callais*, the Supreme Court put the proof of intent back. Justice Samuel Alito wrote for a majority of six. From now on, Section 2 "imposes liability only when the evidence supports a strong inference that the State intentionally drew its districts to afford minority voters less opportunity because of their race." The Congressional Research Service, which explains new law for members of Congress, put it this way on 14 May 2026: a person suing "now must show that a state drew a redistricting map based on racial, and not political, considerations and present evidence of current-day, intentional voting discrimination that is based on race."

Justice Elena Kagan dissented, with Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Ketanji Brown Jackson. She explained the problem with an imaginary map. Picture a round county in the middle of a state. In the county, 90 out of every 100 people are Black. In the rest of the state, 90 out of every 100 people are white. People of each race mostly vote for different parties. On its own the county elects the representative its people want. Then the legislature cuts the circle "into six pie pieces" and gives one piece to each of six surrounding white districts. Nobody has stopped anyone voting. But now, Kagan wrote, "election after election, Black citizens' votes are, by every practical measure, wasted." The name for it, she says, is cracking.

Five weeks later, on 2 June 2026, the Court let Alabama keep using a congressional map with one majority-Black district, over a lower court that had twice ordered otherwise.

Louisiana's legislature drew a new map in May 2026. It dropped one of the state's two majority-Black districts. About a third of Louisiana's people are Black, and the new map gives them a majority in one district out of six. State Representative Beau Beaullieu, the Republican who led the map-drawing in the Louisiana House, said, "We focused on the Democrat numbers, not the racial numbers when drawing." To use the new map, Louisiana put off its primary elections and threw out about 40,000 votes that people had already cast.

Congress tried once to write a new list. The John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act, named for the civil rights leader John Lewis, would have done it. On 19 January 2022 the Senate voted 49 to 51 on ending debate over the bill that carried it. Sixty votes were needed, so the bill died.

As of September 2026, Congress had written no new list of covered places and no new rule for Section 2.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the schools, counted" -->
Since 1954 it has been against the law for school officials to send children to different schools because of their race. Schools that are all or nearly all one race are still common, and the federal government counts them.

The Government Accountability Office audits the government for Congress. In a report issued on 16 June 2022 it found that in the school year 2020-21, more than a third of American public school students, about 18.5 million children, "attended schools where 75 percent or more students were of a single race or ethnicity." Fourteen percent of students were at a school where the figure was 90 percent or more.

A research centre at UCLA called the Civil Rights Project counts a longer stretch, using the federal government's own school data. Its report of April 2024 gives the share of American public schools that were 90 to 100 percent non-white as 7.4 percent in 1988 and 19.8 percent in 2021. That is about one school in five. In those schools in 2021, 78 out of every 100 children were poor.

The same report follows the South on a different measure. The share of Black students in the South who attended a majority-white school reached a peak of about 43 percent in the mid-1980s and was about 16 percent in 2021.

Neither count reaches back to 1970, so neither one shows how the schools now compare with the schools then. The federal report covers school years 2014-15 onward. The UCLA count of 90-to-100-percent-non-white schools starts in 1988.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="from arrest to marriage in twelve years" -->
In 2003 a gay couple in some states could still be arrested for having sex at home. Twelve years later the Supreme Court ruled that same-sex couples could marry in every state.

It began with the arrest law. On 26 June 2003, in *Lawrence v. Texas*, the justices voted six to three to throw out a Texas law that made it a crime for two people of the same sex to have sex in private. Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote the opinion. It overruled a ruling from 1986 that had gone the other way, in these words: "*Bowers* was not correct when it was decided, is not correct today, and is hereby overruled."

Massachusetts went first on marriage. Its highest court ruled on 18 November 2003 that the state constitution let same-sex couples marry, and then held its own order back for 180 days so that the legislature could act if it wanted to. The order took effect on 17 May 2004, and clerks began issuing licences that day. It was fifty years and three days after the Supreme Court decided *Brown v. Board of Education*.

Edith Windsor brought the next case. She and Thea Spyer married in Ontario, Canada, in 2007 and lived in New York. Spyer died in February 2009 and left Windsor everything she owned. The federal government did not count them as married, so Windsor had to pay $363,053 in tax on the inheritance. She sued to get it back. On 26 June 2013 the Court agreed with her, five to four, and struck down the part of the Defense of Marriage Act that had told more than a thousand federal laws to treat her as a stranger to her own wife.

Two years later, on 26 June 2015, the Court ruled five to four in *Obergefell v. Hodges* that same-sex couples may marry in every state. Kennedy wrote that opinion too. Four justices wrote four separate dissents.

Congress acted seven years after that. On 13 December 2022 the Respect for Marriage Act became law. It does not make any state hand out marriage licences. It says that no state official may refuse to honour a marriage performed in another state because of the couple's sex, race, ethnicity or national origin. Twelve Republican senators voted for it, and thirty-nine Republican members of the House. Congress opened the law by copying a sentence out of Kennedy's *Obergefell* opinion: "No union is more profound than marriage, for it embodies the highest ideals of love, fidelity, devotion, sacrifice, and family."

Kim Davis was a county clerk in Kentucky. After the 2015 ruling she refused to give a marriage licence to a gay couple, David Ermold and David Moore, and she was jailed for six days. A jury later ordered her to pay the couple damages. In 2025 she asked the Supreme Court to overturn *Obergefell*. On 10 November 2025 the Court refused to hear her case.

### Jim Obergefell
> **Who:** Sued Ohio because it would not put his name on his husband's death certificate, and won the case that made same-sex marriage legal in every state · **When and where:** Cincinnati, Ohio, and Baltimore, Maryland, 2011 to 2015

Jim Obergefell sued the state of Ohio over a death certificate. He wanted his own name written on it as John Arthur's husband.

The Supreme Court set out his facts in its own opinion, and this is what it said. "Petitioner James Obergefell, a plaintiff in the Ohio case, met John Arthur over two decades ago. They fell in love and started a life together, establishing a lasting, committed relation. In 2011, however, Arthur was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS."

ALS destroys the nerve cells that carry orders from the brain to the muscles. The muscles stop getting the message, waste away and stop working. It takes the hands and legs first, then swallowing, then breathing. The Court called it "progressive, with no known cure."

Ohio would not let them marry and Maryland would, so they went to Maryland. In the Court's words, "It was difficult for Arthur to move." So they flew to Baltimore in a plane fitted out to carry a patient and were married inside it on the tarmac. In the Court's words, "the couple were wed inside a medical transport plane as it remained on the tarmac in Baltimore. Three months later, Arthur died."

Then Ohio told Obergefell he could not be named as the surviving spouse on the death certificate. He sued. His case was joined with cases from Michigan, Kentucky and Tennessee, and the Supreme Court decided them together on 26 June 2015.

The last line of the ruling is short. "They ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ferguson, and what the investigators found" -->
Black Lives Matter began as a hashtag. Three Black organizers, Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors and Opal Tometi, made it in 2013 after a Florida jury acquitted George Zimmerman of killing Trayvon Martin. Martin was a 17-year-old Black teenager walking back from a convenience store in Sanford, Florida, on 26 February 2012. He was unarmed. Zimmerman, a neighbourhood-watch volunteer, shot him. The jury found Zimmerman not guilty on 13 July 2013. A University of Michigan library guide, quoting the movement's own account, describes it as a Black-centred project for building a movement.

It became a street movement in Ferguson, Missouri. A Ferguson police officer shot Michael Brown on 9 August 2014. People protested outside the police station for months. On 4 September 2014 the Civil Rights Division of the Justice Department opened an investigation into the whole police department, and its report came out on 4 March 2015.

The investigators interviewed half the department's officers, spent about a hundred person-days in the town, and read over 35,000 pages of police records. They found "a pattern or practice of unlawful conduct within the Ferguson Police Department that violates the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution."

Some of what they found was about money. The city wanted more revenue from tickets and fines, and told the police so. In March 2010 the finance director wrote to the police chief that "unless ticket writing ramps up significantly before the end of the year, it will be hard to significantly raise collections next year." In March 2013 he wrote to the city manager: "Court fees are anticipated to rise about 7.5%. I did ask the Chief if he thought the PD could deliver 10% increase. He indicated they could try."

The department's own data showed who was being stopped. Black people were 67 percent of Ferguson's population. Between 2012 and 2014 they were 85 percent of the drivers police stopped, 90 percent of the people ticketed and 93 percent of the people arrested. They were searched more than twice as often as white drivers and were found carrying something illegal 26 percent less often. Nearly nine-tenths of the force officers used was used against Black people. In every dog-bite case where the report noted a race, the person bitten was Black.

The investigators also looked at how officers treated protesters and people who filmed them. One officer arrested a father whose family was filming him, then pulled the mother over and arrested her too. He said "no more mercy, since she wanted to videotape," and "nobody videotapes me." He took the phone out of their daughter's hands. When the parents got out on bond, the video had been deleted. In November 2014 a federal judge ordered Ferguson officers to stop interfering with people who lawfully and peacefully record the police. On 9 February 2015, six months to the day after Michael Brown was shot, officers arrested a man who was filming other arrests while, in the report's words, he "was neither interfering nor resisting." They also arrested a protester in a wheelchair who was live streaming.

On the evening of 25 May 2020, four Minneapolis police officers answered a call about a counterfeit twenty-dollar bill. What they did to George Floyd is set out below in the words of the officer who did it. Derek Chauvin signed a written admission of these facts in a federal court on 15 December 2021, so none of it depends on anyone else's account.

The officers took Floyd out of a squad car and put him face down on the street. He was handcuffed. He "remained restrained, prone and handcuffed on the ground for approximately ten minutes." Through all of it, Chauvin "held his left knee on Mr. Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder area and his right knee on Mr. Floyd's left arm and upper back."

Chauvin admitted that Floyd stopped resisting and that he kept kneeling on him anyway. He admitted that he knew he had no legal reason to keep going, "because he was aware that Mr. Floyd not only stopped resisting, but also stopped talking, stopped moving, stopped breathing, and lost consciousness and a pulse." He admitted that he "heard Mr. Floyd repeatedly explain that he could not breathe, was in pain, and wanted help." He admitted that bystanders asked him to check for a pulse and to get off, and that another officer checked twice and said twice that he could not find one.

A seventeen-year-old named Darnella Frazier was walking her nine-year-old cousin to a shop. She stopped and recorded the whole thing on her phone. In 2021 the Pulitzer Prize Board gave her a special citation "for courageously recording the murder of George Floyd, a video that spurred protests against police brutality around the world, highlighting the crucial role of citizens in journalists' quest for truth and justice." The wording comes from news reports of the announcement.

Protests followed in towns and cities all summer. The Pew Research Center, an organization that runs surveys, asked 9,654 American adults between 4 and 10 June 2020 about it. Six in every hundred said they had been to a rally or protest about race in the past month. Two-thirds said they supported the Black Lives Matter movement.

The House of Representatives passed the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act on 3 March 2021, by 220 votes to 212. The Senate never voted on it. The Justice Department under President Biden sued the cities of Minneapolis and Louisville to make them change how their police worked. On 21 May 2025 Harmeet Dhillon, the new head of the department's Civil Rights Division, dropped both lawsuits. She called them "factually unjustified."

Chauvin pleaded guilty in federal court on 15 December 2021. In the same document he admitted something from four years earlier: on 4 September 2017 he answered a call from a mother who said her fourteen-year-old son had hit her, and he held the boy by the throat, hit him in the head with a flashlight hard enough to cut him near the ear, and then knelt on the boy's neck, shoulders and upper back "for between fifteen and sixteen minutes" while the boy lay handcuffed, face down and not resisting, and "at times, cried from pain." Chauvin admitted that he left all of that out of the report he wrote that night.

On 29 March 2022 the Emmett Till Antilynching Act became law. It was the first federal law against lynching in American history. Lynching is a killing carried out by a mob instead of by a court.

Congress had been failing to pass one since either 1900 or 1918, and its own 2022 committee report cannot decide which. In one section the report says the first bill came from Congressman George Henry White in 1900, when he was the only Black member of Congress. In another section of the same report it says the first came from Congressman Leonidas Dyer of Missouri in 1918. Both sections agree that about two hundred anti-lynching bills were introduced after that, that the House passed three of them between 1920 and 1940, and that not one became law.

The law is named for Emmett Till, who was, in the report's words, "a 14-year-old African American youth from Chicago who was lynched in 1955 while visiting an uncle in Mississippi." It makes conspiring to commit a hate crime that kills or seriously injures someone punishable by up to thirty years in prison. The House passed it on 28 February 2022 and the Senate on 7 March 2022.

Ida B. Wells counted lynchings and printed the numbers in the 1890s. The Dyer bill passed the House in 1922 and died in the Senate.

On 15 October 2017 an actress named Alyssa Milano asked people who had been sexually harassed or assaulted to reply to her with the words "me too," so that everyone could see how many there were. The Pew Research Center, which counts things like this, went through public English-language posts on Twitter afterwards. It found the hashtag used more than 19 million times between that day and 30 September 2018, an average of 55,319 times a day.

The two words were not new. A youth worker in Selma, Alabama named Tarana Burke had been using them with girls and young women for about a decade, in person, in rooms with no cameras in them. Her story is below.

### Tarana Burke
> **Who:** The organizer who was using the words "me too" with survivors of sexual violence about eleven years before they became a hashtag · **When and where:** the Bronx, New York, and Selma, Alabama, 1973 to 2018

Tarana Burke was born on 12 September 1973 in the Bronx, in New York City. She joined a group called the 21st Century Youth Leadership Movement when she was fourteen. She went to Alabama State University and then Auburn University, and afterwards she moved to Selma, Alabama, and kept working for the same group there.

Burke is a survivor of sexual abuse herself. The National Women's History Museum traces the start of her work to 1996, when a girl at a youth camp told her about being abused, and says that years later she began using the phrase "me too" to give survivors what she called empowerment through empathy. That is the idea that hearing someone else say it first is what lets you say it at all.

She built an organization for Black girls and called it Just Be. Sources disagree about the year she founded it. Her own website says 2005. The National Women's History Museum says 2007. Colorado State University says that in 2006 she made a "Me Too" page on MySpace, which was a social network people used before Facebook and Twitter.

Then, on 15 October 2017, the words went everywhere without her. Alyssa Milano posted them during the reporting about the film producer Harvey Weinstein, and millions of people replied. Burke became a leader of the movement around the world. *Time* magazine put her among "the Silence Breakers" and named that group its Person of the Year for 2017.

In 2018 she started an organization called "me too." International.

Congress passed the Americans with Disabilities Act in 1990. The fights after that were about who it covered and where disabled people could live.

Courts narrowed it first, by narrowing who counted as disabled. In 1999 twin sisters who wanted to fly for United Air Lines sued because the airline turned them down over their eyesight. Without glasses they could see 20/200 or worse. With glasses they could see 20/20. United required 20/100 without glasses. The Supreme Court ruled on 22 June 1999 that whether someone counts as disabled must be judged with their glasses on. So the airline could turn them down because of their eyes, and they could not sue about it, because with their glasses on they were not disabled. A second ruling in 2002 narrowed the definition again. Congress wrote that after those two rulings lower courts had "incorrectly found in individual cases that people with a range of substantially limiting impairments are not people with disabilities."

On 25 September 2008 President George W. Bush signed a law that put the definition back. Its title says what it is for: "To restore the intent and protections of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990." Congress named the two rulings in the text of the law and said they had "narrowed the broad scope of protection intended to be afforded by the ADA, thus eliminating protection for many individuals whom Congress intended to protect." The new law says an illness that comes and goes still counts, and that the question is decided without regard to medication, prosthetic limbs, hearing aids and cochlear implants, mobility devices or oxygen equipment. Ordinary glasses and contact lenses are the one thing the law leaves off that list.

The other fight is about where disabled people live. On 22 June 1999, in a case brought by two Georgia women, Lois Curtis and Elaine Wilson, the Supreme Court ruled that keeping a disabled person in an institution when they could live in the community is discrimination. Both women had been admitted to the psychiatric unit of a state hospital, and both had stayed there for years after doctors said they were ready to leave. The Court wrote that "confinement in an institution severely diminishes the everyday life activities of individuals, including family relations, social contacts, work options, economic independence, educational advancement, and cultural enrichment."

That ruling did not end it. On 3 March 2022 the Justice Department wrote to the governor of Colorado to say the state was breaking the law by "unnecessarily" keeping people with physical disabilities in nursing homes. More than 15,000 people in Colorado were in one of the state's 200-plus nursing facilities in a given year, and, the letter says, "some individuals have remained in nursing facilities for many years with no movement toward discharge."

Four years later the federal government changed sides. In 2024 seventeen states, led by Texas, sued to overturn the federal health department's rules under Section 504, the 1973 law that bans disability discrimination by anyone who takes federal money. By 2026 five states were left in the case, and they were asking a judge to strike the rule that says disabled people should get services in "the most integrated setting," meaning at home or in their own community when that is possible. On 18 June 2026 the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel wrote that federal law does not require it. The Justice Department then joined the five states in asking the judge to strike the rule. On 23 September 2026 the judge struck it, for the whole country. The 1999 Supreme Court ruling and the law Congress passed still stand.

The Census Bureau reported on 22 July 2026 that 45.8 million Americans, or 13.7 percent, had a disability in 2024. Read the small print on that figure. It counts the "civilian noninstitutionalized population." That means everyone except the people living in institutions. The government's main count of disabled Americans leaves out the people this part of the fight is about.

### Alice Wong
> **Who:** Collected and published disabled people's own accounts of their lives, and served on the federal council that advises the President on disability · **When and where:** San Francisco, California, 2002 to 2025

Alice Wong was born in 1974. She used a power wheelchair and she had a tracheostomy. That means a hole cut through the front of the neck into the windpipe, with a short tube held in it, and a machine pushing air through the tube into her lungs so that she kept breathing. The University of California, San Francisco, which recorded her life, does not name the condition behind it.

She earned a master's degree in medical sociology at UCSF in 2004, after starting a disability group on campus as a student in 2002. She worked at three research centres there, all of them studying how disabled people actually live.

On 25 January 2013 President Barack Obama appointed her to the National Council on Disability, the federal agency that advises the President and Congress. She served until 2015.

In 2014 she started the Disability Visibility Project, which collects disabled people's own accounts of their own lives. For most of American history, disabled people were written about by doctors, judges and officials and were hardly ever quoted.

On 20 July 2015 the White House held a reception for the twenty-fifth anniversary of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Wong could not travel to it. She took part through a robot. It was a screen on wheels that carried her face and her voice into the room. The official White House photograph of that evening, taken in the Blue Room by Pete Souza, is captioned: "President Barack Obama greets Alice Wong, Disability Visibility Project Founder and Project Coordinator via robot."

She wrote two books, *Disability Visibility* and *Year of the Tiger*. She put it this way: "Disability is part of the greater continuum of diversity, and we need to allow for variation and not get so focused on what is the norm."

She died on 14 November 2025, aged fifty-one.

Three Supreme Court rulings between 2020 and 2026 decided most of what is now law about transgender Americans. Transgender people won the first and lost the other two.

The first was about jobs. On 15 June 2020, in *Bostock v. Clayton County*, the Supreme Court ruled six to three that "an employer who fires an individual merely for being gay or transgender violates Title VII." Title VII is the part of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that bans job discrimination because of sex. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion. One of the three people in the case was Aimee Stephens, who worked at a funeral home in Michigan. She had been hired presenting as male, and six years later she told her employer she meant to "live and work full-time as a woman." The funeral home fired her before she went on holiday, saying the arrangement "is not going to work out."

Five years later the Court took up medical treatment for children. Tennessee had passed a law stopping doctors from giving puberty blockers or hormones to anyone under eighteen for the purpose of living as a gender other than the one recorded at birth. A puberty blocker is a drug that pauses the hormones that start puberty. Hormone treatment gives a person the hormone associated with the sex they identify as. The same drugs stay legal for children in Tennessee for other reasons, such as puberty arriving far too early. On 18 June 2025, in *United States v. Skrmetti*, the Court upheld the Tennessee law. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the opinion. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented in whole or in part.

A year after that came school sports. Idaho passed the Fairness in Women's Sports Act in 2020 and West Virginia passed the Save Women's Sports Act in 2021. Both say that girls' teams are for students the law counts as female by biology. The Supreme Court heard the two cases together and decided them on 30 June 2026. Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote for six justices that Title IX "allows schools to provide separate women's and men's sports teams defined by biological sex," and that the two state laws do not break the Constitution's promise of equal protection. The Court gave its reasons as the physical differences between the sexes, the safety and fairness of the competition, and the fact that "27 States and various sports-governing bodies have all drawn the same line."

The student in the West Virginia case is named in the record only by her initials, B.P.J. Justice Sotomayor described her in the dissent: "Respondent B. P. J. is a transgender girl who wants to live her life consistent with her gender identity. When B. P. J. was 11 years old, she sought the opportunity to do what she and so many other children love to do: play sports." She wanted the cross-country and track teams. Sotomayor also recorded this: "in the five years since the ban has been in place, B. P. J. is the only transgender girl publicly identified in the State who has sought to play sports with other girls." Sotomayor agreed that the Title IX claim failed. On the equal protection question she said the case should have gone back to the trial court to find the facts before anyone decided it.

The President acted too. On 20 January 2025, his first day back in office, President Donald Trump signed an executive order titled "Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government." A week later he ordered the Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth, to bar people with gender dysphoria from the military. Gender dysphoria is the medical term for the distress a person feels when the sex they were assigned at birth does not match their sense of their own gender. Commander Emily Shilling, a Navy pilot for nearly twenty years, sued with other transgender service members. On 6 May 2025 the Supreme Court let the ban take effect while the case went on. On 6 November 2025 it let the State Department print the sex recorded at birth on every new passport. In February 2025 the National Park Service took the words "transgender" and "queer" off the website of the Stonewall National Monument in New York, which President Barack Obama had created on 24 June 2016 at the site of the 1969 uprising.

On 31 March 2026 the Court ruled eight to one for Kaley Chiles, a Colorado counselor. Colorado had banned licensed counselors from trying to change a young person's sexual orientation or gender identity, which is called conversion therapy. Justice Gorsuch wrote that the law, applied to a counselor who only talks with clients, punished her for her point of view. Justice Jackson dissented.

In 2016 the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe fought the Dakota Access oil pipeline. The company building it, Energy Transfer, planned to run it under Lake Oahe, a reservoir on the Missouri River less than a mile north of the reservation. The tribe gets its water from that river. The route crosses land that treaties in the 1800s recognized as belonging to the Sioux Nation, and the Sioux never gave that land up.

Thousands of people camped near the crossing. On 19 August 2016 North Dakota's governor, Jack Dalrymple, declared an emergency. He set up a joint police command. It was made up of the Morton County Sheriff's Office, led by Sheriff Kyle Kirchmeier, and police from other counties and towns. Each police chief left it to his own officers to decide when to use force.

On the night of 20 November 2016 protesters dragged one of two police barricade trucks off a closed bridge on Highway 1806. The crowd grew to several hundred. Officers fired tear gas, rubber bullets and bean bags filled with lead into it. They also sprayed people with a fire hose. A lawsuit for the protesters says more than 200 were hurt that night.

Sophia Wilansky had run about 30 yards from a barricade truck on the bridge when a police round hit her. A federal appeals court described her account of it in 2026: it was fired by Officer Jonathan Moll, and "It tore the flesh off her arm." The round was a flash-bang, a small explosive, packed inside a bullet. Her left hand and forearm are permanently injured.

No officer had to pay. In 2023 and again on 24 June 2026 the federal appeals court ruled against the injured protesters. It said the law in 2016 had not clearly told officers that firing at a crowd to drive it away was a "seizure" under the Constitution. Without that, the officers were protected by a rule called qualified immunity. That rule shields government officials from being sued unless the law they broke was already clear.

Police made hundreds of arrests. Judges threw out many of the charges, often because no one had told the protesters that they were standing on private land.

The federal government's answer kept changing. On 4 December 2016 the Army refused permission to cross under the lake. On 24 January 2017, four days after he took office, President Donald Trump ordered the Army to approve the pipeline "in an expedited manner." The Army approved it on 8 February 2017. Oil began to flow in June 2017. In 2020 a federal judge ruled that the Army had broken the law by approving it without a full study of the risks. The Army finished that study in December 2025 and gave permission again in 2026.

Energy Transfer sued the environmental group Greenpeace over the protests. In March 2025 a jury in Mandan, North Dakota, ordered Greenpeace to pay about $667 million. Judge James Gion cut that to $345 million. Greenpeace said it had only six employees at the camps and called the lawsuit an attempt to punish it for supporting the protest.

In September 2026 Nola Taken Alive, a member of the Standing Rock tribal council, said: "The oil is still flowing. The pipeline hasn't been shut down."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Dreamers" -->
Some young people had been brought into the United States as children by adults, without the government's permission to live here. They grew up here and went to school here. From 2001 members of Congress kept introducing a bill called the DREAM Act. It would have let these young people become citizens by finishing two years of college or serving two years in the military.

On 1 January 2010 four students from Miami Dade College set out to walk 1,500 miles from Miami to Washington. They were Gaby Pacheco, Carlos Roa, Felipe Matos and Juan Rodriguez. Three of them had no legal right to be in the country, and they said so in public all the way, which put them at risk of arrest and deportation. They gathered 30,000 signatures. They reached the White House on 1 May 2010. President Barack Obama would not meet them.

On 18 December 2010 the DREAM Act came up in the Senate. Fifty-five senators voted to bring it to a final vote, and forty-one voted against. It needed sixty, so it died.

On 15 June 2012 the Obama administration started a program called Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, or DACA. It did not make anyone a citizen. People who had arrived as children and met rules about age, schooling and criminal records could ask the government to put off deporting them for two years at a time, and could get permission to work. About 700,000 people signed up, by the Supreme Court's count.

In September 2017 Attorney General Jeff Sessions told the Department of Homeland Security that it should end DACA, and the acting secretary, Elaine Duke, ended it. DACA holders, universities and states sued. On 18 June 2020 the Supreme Court ruled five to four that the government had ended the program the wrong way, so DACA stayed. A separate lawsuit brought by Texas is still going. In January 2025 a federal appeals court ruled that DACA's work permits are unlawful in Texas. By July 2026 the judge in Texas had not yet said how to carry that out.

About 455,000 people held DACA on 31 March 2026, by government figures. The government was renewing permits but not approving new ones. Congress has never passed the DREAM Act.

In January 2026 federal immigration agents shot and killed two American citizens in Minneapolis, Renée Macklin Good and Alex Pretti. The government was carrying out a large immigration operation there that it called Operation Metro Surge. Reporters named ICE officer Jonathan Ross as the man who shot Good. They named Border Patrol agent Jesus Ochoa and Customs and Border Protection officer Raymundo Gutierrez as the agents who shot Pretti. Federal officials said Good had used her car as a weapon and called Pretti a domestic terrorist. Local officials said video contradicted the account of Good's death, and NPR reported that video contradicted the account of Pretti's. Federal agents kept state investigators away from the evidence for months. The acting Attorney General, Todd Blanche, said there would be no civil rights investigation into Good's killing. As of September 2026 no one had been charged in either death.

On 21 January 2017, the day after Donald Trump became president, women marched in cities across the country. The idea started with a Facebook post by Teresa Shook, a grandmother in Hawaii. Between 3.2 million and 5.3 million people marched in the United States, about 500,000 of them in Washington. Researchers who count protests called it the largest one-day demonstration in American history up to that time.

In 2022 the Supreme Court took away a right that women had held since 1973. An abortion is a medical procedure that ends a pregnancy. In 1973, in *Roe v. Wade*, the Supreme Court had ruled that the Constitution protected it. Mississippi passed a law banning most abortions after fifteen weeks of pregnancy, and the case reached the Court. On 24 June 2022, in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, Justice Samuel Alito wrote for five justices: "We therefore hold that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion. Roe and Casey must be overruled." Chief Justice John Roberts agreed that the Mississippi law could stand but did not vote to overrule *Roe*. Justices Stephen Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan dissented together.

After *Dobbs*, each state made its own law. On 10 August 2026, by the count of KFF, a health research group, 13 states banned abortion in almost every case. Seven more banned it after six to twelve weeks of pregnancy. Nine states and the District of Columbia set no limit by stage of pregnancy.

The Equal Rights Amendment came back too. It says: "equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any State on account of sex." Congress had set a deadline of 1982, and only 35 states had approved it by then. Thirty-eight were needed. Then Nevada approved it in 2017, Illinois in 2018 and Virginia in 2020. The Archivist of the United States, the official who publishes amendments, did not add it. The three states sued, and in February 2023 a federal appeals court upheld the dismissal of their lawsuit. In December 2024 the Archivist, Colleen Shogan, and her deputy wrote that the amendment "cannot be certified as part of the Constitution." On 17 January 2025, three days before he left office, President Joe Biden declared it "the law of the land." Shogan did not publish it. As of September 2026 it is not part of the Constitution.
