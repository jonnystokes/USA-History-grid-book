## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Hundreds of nations across the land, each with its own government, laws, alliances, economy, and wars — farming towns, fishing kingdoms, trade routes crossing the continent. Population north of Mexico at contact: estimates run from about 1 million to about 18 million, and scholars genuinely do not agree — state the range. Not a prologue to anyone else's story; this is the country before the name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cities and kingdoms" -->
- **Cahokia** (near present-day St. Louis): the largest town north of Mexico — peak about 1100, roughly 10,000–20,000 people (about London's size then); dispersed by about 1350; causes debated (drought, floods, resource strain).
- **Chaco Canyon** (New Mexico): great houses; broke up about 1140 after long droughts; people regathered into the Pueblo towns that stand today.
- **The Calusa** (southwest Florida): a kingdom that farmed nothing — fish and shellfish fed about 20,000 people; shell-mound capital at Mound Key; shell-walled "watercourts" penning live fish, built 1300–1400; tribute collected from Tampa Bay to the Keys.
- Trade carried turquoise, seashells, copper, and obsidian hundreds of miles between nations along trails newcomers would later follow.
- Ancestors of the Navajo (Diné) and Apache were finishing a centuries-long migration from the far north; scholars put their arrival in the Southwest anywhere from the 1200s to about 1525 — say the timing is not settled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Law of Peace" -->
Five warring nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca — accepted the Great Law of Peace carried by the Peacemaker and Hiawatha and became the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, governed by a Grand Council and recorded on wampum belts. Founding date disputed: one dating (from an eclipse in the founding tradition) says 1142; most scholars say the mid-1100s to late 1400s. Often called one of the world's oldest continuing governments; in 1744 its speaker would advise the British colonies to try union themselves (era 4). No featured story in this era on purpose: documented individual lives are scarce this far back, and the book does not invent people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="native-nations" label="The 1500s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
First contact at the edges — and the Great Dying. European diseases traveled ahead of the Europeans themselves and killed a large share of many communities before most nations ever saw a newcomer. Because the starting population is disputed (1–18 million), no one can give one death toll; the honest anchor is the far end — by 1900 the census counted about 250,000 Native people. The century's other story: nations did not dissolve; they reorganized, absorbed refugees, and rebuilt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The entradas" -->
Hernando de Soto's expedition (1539–1543, 620+ men) cut through the Mississippian chiefdoms of the Southeast — violence, forced labor, and disease behind it; the great chiefdoms his men described fragmented in the decades after, and survivors regrouped into new nations over the following century. Coronado crossed the Southwest and southern Plains (1540–42; his route is `exploration`'s story). The Spanish also brought the horse back to the continent (Columbus's second voyage 1493; New Mexico colony 1598) — what nations did with it comes next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="paquiquineo" name="Paquiquineo (Don Luís)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Paquiquineo (Don Luís)
> **Who:** a young man from the Chesapeake Bay country taken aboard a Spanish ship — willingly or not, the records do not say — who met King Philip II, was renamed Don Luís de Velasco, and spent nine years getting home. · **When and where:** taken 1561; returned September 1570 as translator for a Jesuit mission on the James River; in February 1571 he led the attack that ended it. Spain gave up on the Chesapeake; Jamestown came 36 years later — into a country that already knew what missionaries meant.
Documented in Spanish records (Encyclopedia Virginia) — the target "individual named in Spanish accounts" filled.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paquiquineo" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acoma, 1598 and 1599" -->
- Onate's New Mexico colony, 1598. Acoma had already given the act of obedience. Dec 1598: Juan de Zaldivar's party demanded food; Acoma killed him and 11-14 men (sources differ). Jan 1599: ~70 soldiers under Vicente de Zaldivar stormed and burned the pueblo.
- **Death toll disputed; the range IS the finding** (no count made; only eyewitness account is Villagra's 1610 poem): ~300 | ~600 | 600-800 | ~800 (All Pueblo Council of Governors) | ~1,500 (NPS).
- Sentence, Feb 12 1599: men over 25, one foot cut off + 20 years' servitude; males 12-25 and females over 12, 20 years; children under 12 taken from parents; ~70 girls sent to convents in Mexico ("in all probability, none ever returned home" - NPS); two Hopi captives each lost a right hand.
- Mutilation's execution disputed (Simmons recanted; Kessell doubts) - but the ORDER is in the record and Onate was convicted for Acoma: 30 charges, convicted on 12, banished for life, exiled, fined.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="native-nations" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Nations still held nearly the whole continent; the newcomers were a coastal fringe that survived on Native food, Native trade, and Native permission. The century's pattern from the nations' side: first accommodation and alliance-building on their own terms, then war when the newcomers stopped needing permission — and, in the Southwest, the most successful Native rising against a European power in North America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tsenacommacah" -->
In eastern Virginia the mamanatowick Wahunsenacawh ("the Powhatan") built a tribute state of more than 30 nations — about 15,000 people across some 10,000 square miles, each town under its own weroance paying tribute upward. Jamestown (1607) was planted inside it and spent its first years dealing with him as the power in the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum diplomacy and the Covenant Chain" -->
Diplomacy ran on wampum — agreements recorded in beadwork and memory. The Two Row Wampum (Guswenta) holds the Haudenosaunee's first agreement with the Dutch (their tradition dates it 1613): two vessels on the same river, side by side, neither steering the other. With the English the alliance became the Covenant Chain (first recorded 1677, Albany) — a chain of silver, because silver tarnishes and must be polished: the alliance had to be renewed, council after council, and was.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The coastal wars" -->
- **Pequot War (1636–38):** at Mystic on May 26, 1637, an English-led force burned the Pequot town and killed hundreds, mostly elders, women, and children, in under an hour. The Treaty of Hartford (1638) declared the nation dissolved and outlawed its very name — and failed: the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation exists in Connecticut today.
- **King Philip's War (1675–76):** Metacom's coalition against the New England colonies; about 9,000 dead — by share of population the bloodiest war in American history, killing roughly half the Native people of New England.
- The Beaver Wars — Haudenosaunee campaigns over the fur trade — remade the eastern interior.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pueblo Revolt" -->
August 10, 1680: the Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande rose together — coordinated across many towns and languages by Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh — took Santa Fe, and drove Spain out of New Mexico. 401 settlers and 21 friars were killed; most Spaniards were allowed to flee. The Pueblos governed themselves for 12 years, through drought and famine, until the reconquest began in 1692. Horses: the traditional account has the revolt's captured herds (1,500+) spreading horses west and north along Native trade routes; a 2023 study in *Science*, working with Lakota, Comanche, and Pawnee partners, dated horses among Plains nations to the early 1600s — matching oral histories. The timing is unsettled; both accounts go in.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pope" name="Po'pay (Popé)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Po'pay (Popé)
> **Who:** Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who organized dozens of towns speaking different languages into one rising — by Pueblo accounts, knotted cords carried the countdown from town to town. · **When and where:** New Mexico, August 1680.
His statue stands in the U.S. Capitol today — a leader of the one revolt that pushed an empire off Native land for a decade.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pope" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="metacom-native-nations" name="Metacom (King Philip)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Metacom (King Philip)
> **Who:** Wampanoag sachem, son of Massasoit who had made the peace with Plymouth; led the coalition war of 1675–76 when the peace had been eaten away. · **When and where:** southern New England; killed at Mount Hope, August 12, 1676.
His wife and nine-year-old son were sold into slavery (Bermuda per most sources); his head was displayed at Plymouth; hundreds of captives were sold to pay the war's bills. Told plainly. Topic doc for the era (not about him alone): *We Shall Remain: After the Mayflower* (PBS, 2009).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="metacom-native-nations" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The age of play-off diplomacy: nations held the balance of power between the French and British empires and traded on their own terms — while dependence on trade goods quietly grew. Confederacies expanded, adopted whole nations, and ran a foreign policy the empires had to respect.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Neutrality as strategy" -->
At the Great Peace of Montreal (August 4, 1701) about 1,300 delegates from more than 30 nations made peace with New France, and the Haudenosaunee committed to neutrality between France and Britain — a position they worked for half a century, selling their weight to neither side cheaply.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuscarora move north" -->
After defeat in the Tuscarora War in North Carolina (1711–13), about 1,500 surviving Tuscarora walked north and asked the Five Nations for sponsorship; the Oneida took them in, and in 1722 the Tuscarora became the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee — a nation that lost its homeland and was adopted whole by a confederacy hundreds of miles away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Comanche power" -->
On the southern Plains the Comanche built the dominant power of the century — Comanchería eventually covered about 240,000 square miles. They bred and traded horses, controlled the horse trade south-to-north, and in the 1740s traded robes and horses to the French for guns better than Spanish equipment. Horses, trade, raiding, and diplomacy — an economy and a foreign policy, not just fighting.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Treaty councils" -->
Treaty councils were working diplomacy with their own protocol — wampum, condolence, speakers. At Lancaster, Pennsylvania (June–July 1744), the Onondaga speaker Canasatego closed the council on July 4, 1744 by advising the colonies to unite as the Haudenosaunee had: "We heartily recommend Union and a good Agreement between you our Brethren." Benjamin Franklin printed the minutes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="canasatego" name="Canasatego" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Canasatego
> **Who:** Onondaga leader, the Haudenosaunee's speaker at the great treaty councils — his printed speeches are among the best-documented Native diplomacy of the century. · **When and where:** Lancaster, Pennsylvania, 1744.
The diplomat-in-the-treaty-minutes target, filled: a man whose July 4 advice about union was set in type by Benjamin Franklin thirty-two years before another July 4.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="native-nations" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
War between empires became war on Native land. The French defeat (1763) broke the play-off system; the Revolution split the oldest confederacy on the continent; and a new republic began signing treaties — 374 of them, eventually, now held at the National Archives — while a Native confederacy handed its new army the worst defeat it would ever take.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pontiac and the Line" -->
With France gone, Great Lakes and Ohio nations under the Odawa leader Pontiac took or besieged British forts across the west (1763–66). Britain answered with the Royal Proclamation of October 7, 1763: no colonial settlement west of the Appalachians — the land beyond reserved to the nations. Settlers crossed it anyway, but the Line became a root of treaty law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution splits the Haudenosaunee" -->
The Revolution broke the Confederacy's unity for the first time in centuries: Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans; Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga fought with the British, many under Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea). At Oriskany (August 6, 1777) Haudenosaunee men fought each other. In 1779 Washington ordered "the total destruction and devastation of their settlements": Sullivan's army burned more than 40 Seneca and Cayuga towns and their crops; refugees starved at Fort Niagara that winter. Seneca chiefs to Washington, 1790: "we called you the Town-destroyer; and to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The treaty system begins" -->
The first U.S.–Native treaty was signed with the Lenape (Delaware) at Fort Pitt, September 17, 1778 — it recognized Delaware sovereignty and even floated a Native fourteenth state with a seat in Congress (never acted on). Nation-to-nation treaties became the legal spine of everything after — including everything later broken.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Northwest Confederacy" -->
Ohio Country nations — Little Turtle (Miami), Blue Jacket (Shawnee), Buckongahelas (Delaware) — confederated to hold the Ohio River line. On November 4, 1791, they destroyed Gen. Arthur St. Clair's army: over 650 U.S. soldiers killed of about 1,000 — the largest defeat of a U.S. army by Native forces ever, bigger than Little Bighorn, and the trigger of Congress's first-ever investigation. Defeat came at Fallen Timbers (1794); at Greenville (August 3, 1795) the confederacy ceded most of Ohio.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="little-turtle" name="Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)
> **Who:** Miami war leader who co-led the confederacy that beat St. Clair in 1791 — then judged the fight unwinnable, signed at Greenville, and spent his last years arguing for peace. · **When and where:** the Wabash and Maumee country (present Indiana and Ohio), 1780s–1790s.
Both halves matter: the general who won the biggest victory, and the statesman who decided when to stop.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="little-turtle" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="native-nations" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Nations organized to hold their land two different ways — a fighting confederacy in the north, a national rebuilding in the south with a written alphabet, a newspaper, a constitution, and a Supreme Court win. The United States answered both with removal. Removal is told here from the nations' side: governments broken and rebuilt; the journeys themselves are `migration`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa" -->
Tenskwatawa's movement to return to Native ways (from about 1805) and Tecumseh's diplomacy built Prophetstown on the Wabash (1808) as the capital of a many-nations confederacy — the idea that no single nation could sell land all held in common. While Tecumseh was away recruiting, Harrison attacked (Tippecanoe, November 7, 1811) and burned the town. Tecumseh fought on with the British and died at the Thames, October 5, 1813; the confederacy died with him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee renaissance" -->
Sequoyah demonstrated his 86-character Cherokee syllabary in 1821; within a few years most of the nation could read and write it. The Cherokee Phoenix (1828) became the first Native American newspaper, printed in Cherokee and English; a written constitution (1827) set up three branches of government at New Echota. The Cherokee answer to "civilization" policy was to out-build it — and it did not save the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Removal" -->
The Indian Removal Act (1830) authorized removal treaties. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) Chief Justice Marshall ruled Georgia law had no force in the Cherokee Nation — a foundation of tribal sovereignty law to this day — and Georgia simply ignored the ruling. The Cherokee removal of 1838–39 (the Trail of Tears): more than 16,000 removed, over 4,000 dead — the traditional estimate; some scholars argue higher. All five southeastern nations — Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole — were removed to Indian Territory, where they rebuilt governments, courts, newspapers, and schools within a generation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Seminole resistance" -->
The Second Seminole War (1835–42) became the longest and costliest Indian war in the East. Osceola was seized under a white flag of truce (October 21, 1837) — a ploy condemned even then — and died a prisoner. But a few hundred Seminoles withdrew deep into the Everglades and never surrendered; their descendants, the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe, note that the Seminole were never conquered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="tecumseh" name="Tecumseh" movie="We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tecumseh
> **Who:** Shawnee leader who traveled thousands of miles arguing that Native land was held in common and could only be defended in common. · **When and where:** the Ohio country and the South, 1805–1813; died at the Thames, October 5, 1813. · **Movie:** *We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision* (PBS, 2009) — a documentary about him and his brother.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tecumseh" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sequoyah-native-nations" name="Sequoyah" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sequoyah
> **Who:** Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language — and worked about twelve years to build one, the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone without first being literate. · **When and where:** Cherokee Nation (born Tennessee about 1770; syllabary demonstrated 1821; died 1843 in Mexico).
Within a few years his 86 characters made the Cherokee Nation a reading and writing nation — newspaper, constitution, laws, letters between removed families.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sequoyah-native-nations" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="osceola-native-nations" name="Osceola" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Osceola
> **Who:** Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida. · **When and where:** Florida; seized under a truce flag October 21, 1837; died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838.
The capture-under-a-white-flag is told plainly — it was condemned by the public even in 1837.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="osceola-native-nations" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin
> **Who:** a Cherokee woman who was about three when soldiers took her family from Georgia in 1838 — she rode in the wagon while her mother, father, and older brother walked — and who told her memory of it in 1932, at about 97, to historian Grant Foreman. · **When and where:** Georgia to Indian Territory, 1838–39; interviewed in Oklahoma, 1932.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="native-nations" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The hardest half-century: the wars that filled the reservations, the killing of the bison economy, the end of treaty-making, allotment that took two-thirds of the remaining land, and schools built to take the children. Told from the nations' side, it is also a record of decisions — when to fight, when to run, when to sign, how to survive. By 1900 the census counted about 250,000 Native people — the low point. The chapter does not end here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wars, from the nations' side" -->
- **Dakota, 1862 (Minnesota):** war after promised food and payments never came; 392 five-minute trials without lawyers; Lincoln cut the death list to 39; 38 Dakota men hanged at Mankato December 26, 1862 — the largest mass execution in U.S. history; two were hanged by mistake.
- **Sand Creek, November 29, 1864 (Colorado):** Chivington's volunteers attacked Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp — flying a U.S. flag and a white flag — and killed about 230 people, mostly women, children, and elders.
- **Red Cloud's War → Fort Laramie Treaty (1868):** the Bozeman Trail closed; the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, promised "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. Custer announced gold in the Black Hills (1874); the government ignored its own treaty; an ultimatum ordered the winter camps in by January 31, 1876, then the army came.
- **Little Bighorn (the Greasy Grass), June 25, 1876:** the allied camp — Sitting Bull its center, Crazy Horse a battle leader — destroyed Custer's command within about an hour.
- **After:** Crazy Horse surrendered in May 1877 and was bayoneted to death at Fort Robinson that September; the Nez Perce fled about 1,170 miles (NPS trail figure; some accounts longer) and surrendered 40 miles from Canada; Sitting Bull came back from Canada in 1881; Geronimo's surrender (September 4, 1886) was the last — his people were held as prisoners of war for 27 years. The Navajo, marched to Bosque Redondo in 1863–64, negotiated the 1868 treaty that took them home — nearly unique among removed nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison, and the end of treaties" -->
Roughly 30 million bison in the early 1800s; fewer than 1,000 by 1900 (one 1884 count: 325). Railroads, industrial hide-hunting — and army encouragement, because the herds were the Plains nations' food, economy, and religion. In 1871 Congress quietly ended treaty-making with Native nations (existing treaties stayed in force). The two facts together are the mechanism of the reservation era: the economy destroyed, the diplomacy closed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Allotment" -->
The Dawes Act (1887) cut communal land into individual parcels and sold the "surplus": Native landholdings fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934 — about 90 million acres, two-thirds of what was left, gone. Ended only by the Indian Reorganization Act (1934).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding schools — this chapter leads" -->
Carlisle opened November 1, 1879 (founder: Army officer Richard Henry Pratt). His stated purpose, in an 1892 speech to a national charities conference — the exact words, often misquoted: "all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." How the taking worked: an 1891 law let the government compel attendance; an 1893 law let it withhold rations, clothing, and annuity payments from parents who refused; some agents sent reservation police for the children. On arrival: hair cut, uniforms, an English name, home language forbidden — punishments documented by the later federal investigation include beatings, solitary confinement, withheld food, mouths washed with soap. Half the day was unpaid labor that ran the school; Pratt's "outing system" placed children with white families to work. The federal count (2024): 417 federal schools in 37 states and territories, 1819–1969; at least 973 children died at them; at least 74 burial sites beside them — and the report itself says the count is low. The other half of the truth, told in the same breath: children kept their languages in secret, made friendships across nations that seeded the next century's organizing, and some came home and turned the schooling against the policy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee" -->
On January 1, 1889, during an eclipse, a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka had a vision: live rightly, dance the dance, and the dead would return and the world be made whole. The Ghost Dance spread across hungry reservations; agents and newspapers called it an uprising. Sitting Bull was killed December 15, 1890 during an arrest attempt. On December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek, the 7th Cavalry — disarming Spotted Elk's band — opened fire with carbines and Hotchkiss guns: between 250 and 300 Lakota killed, most of them women and children. Wounded Knee is where the wars end, and where this book refuses to end the story: the next era opens with Native doctors, lawyers, and athletes organizing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" name="Sitting Bull" movie="Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sitting Bull
> **Who:** Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man; the resistance camp on the Greasy Grass formed around him. · **When and where:** the northern Plains; Canada 1877–81; killed at Standing Rock, December 15, 1890. · **Movie:** *Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart* (2006) — a documentary built from his own recorded words.
Refused the reservation until hunger left no choice; toured one season with Buffalo Bill (1885, $50 a week) and went home; died in the Ghost Dance panic at the hands of Indian police sent to arrest him. Also portrayed in HBO's *Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee* (2007).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="chief-joseph" name="Chief Joseph" movie="I Will Fight No More Forever (1975)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Chief Joseph
> **Who:** Nez Perce leader in the 1877 flight — about 750 people, only some 200 of them fighters, moving roughly 1,170 miles with the army behind them. · **When and where:** Oregon to Montana, June–October 1877; surrendered October 5, 1877, 40 miles from Canada. · **Movie:** *I Will Fight No More Forever* (1975, TV film).
"From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever" (as recorded at the surrender). The promised return home was overruled: exile to Kansas and Indian Territory, then the Colville reservation, where he died in 1904 — never allowed back to the Wallowa Valley.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chief-joseph" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" name="Sarah Winnemucca" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Winnemucca
> **Who:** Northern Paiute writer, interpreter, and lecturer who argued her people's case across the East. · **When and where:** Nevada and the lecture halls of the East, 1870s–80s.
*Life Among the Piutes* (1883) — the first known autobiography written by a Native American woman (and she was the first Native woman known to hold a U.S. copyright). In the mid-1880s (sources give 1884 or 1885) she opened her own school for Paiute children near Lovelock, Nevada, teaching in Paiute and English — her own answer, before the government's schools swallowed the era.

### Zitkala-Ša
> **Who:** Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and organizer, taken as a child (at eight, by her own account) to a mission boarding school in Indiana. · **When and where:** Yankton reservation, South Dakota, and Washington, D.C.; born February 22, 1876, died January 26, 1938.
Published the boarding schools from the inside — autobiographical essays in the *Atlantic Monthly* (1900, including "The School Days of an Indian Girl"), collected in *American Indian Stories* (1921); co-founded the National Council of American Indians (1926). The strongest first-person school account in the chapter; her story bridges into the next era's organizing.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="native-nations" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The frontier "closes" and the nations do not. This is the half-century the rest of the book most easily drops Native people; this chapter carries them straight through it: the first national Native-run organizations, Osage oil wealth and the murders that followed it, citizenship, the Indian New Deal, two world wars won partly in Native languages — and the boarding schools still running the whole time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organizing" -->
The Society of American Indians (founded October 12, 1911, Columbus, Ohio) was the first national rights organization run by Native people themselves — doctors, lawyers, writers, many of them boarding-school alumni using the schools' English against the schools' purpose; Zitkala-Ša edited its journal. In Alaska, the Alaska Native Brotherhood (1912, Sitka) and Sisterhood (1915) fought for citizenship for half a century. The National Congress of American Indians followed (November 16, 1944, Denver — 80 delegates from 50+ tribes), and it is still the major intertribal body today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Osage and the Reign of Terror" -->
Keeping their mineral rights collective (1906 act) made the Osage, when the oil came in, among the richest people per capita in the world. Then the law put many Osage adults' money under court-appointed white "guardians," and from about 1920 to 1925 more than 60 Osage headright holders died in murders and suspicious deaths — the Reign of Terror. Our angle: a nation's wealth and the legal machinery built to take it; the investigation is `crime-justice`'s. Film: *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2023).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Citizenship and the Indian New Deal" -->
The Indian Citizenship Act (June 2, 1924) made every Native person born in the U.S. a citizen — but voting was state law, and some states barred Native voters until 1957. The Meriam Report (1928) documented allotment's failure (tribal land down from 137 to 47 million acres) and the schools' grim conditions in federal print. The Indian Reorganization Act (June 18, 1934, under Commissioner John Collier) ended allotment and let nations adopt constitutions and charter their own governments — the first turn of federal policy back toward nationhood.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The schools continue" -->
The boarding schools did not stop in 1900 — the federal count of the system runs to 1969, and enrollment kept growing for decades (it peaked around 60,000 in 1973, next era). Collier's 1930s reforms shifted toward day schools and allowed some Native-language teaching, and some schools closed — but tens of thousands of children were still taken from home each year. The plainest documented irony, by name: Chester Nez had his mouth washed with soap at the Fort Defiance boarding school for speaking Navajo — the language the Marine Corps then built its unbreakable code from.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The code talkers" -->
First, Choctaw soldiers in World War I: with German intelligence tapping the lines in the Meuse-Argonne, Choctaw men of the 36th Division began passing orders in Choctaw (first secure messages October 26, 1918; "little gun shoot fast" = machine gun). In World War II, 29 Navajo recruits (May 1942) built a code from Navajo; 350–420 Navajo code talkers served in every Marine division in the Pacific. The 5th Division's signal officer at Iwo Jima: "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima." The code was never broken, stayed classified until 1968, and earned Congressional Gold Medals in 2001.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jim-thorpe-native-nations" name="Jim Thorpe" movie="Jim Thorpe – All-American (1951)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jim Thorpe
> **Who:** Sac and Fox athlete, Carlisle student, first Native American to win Olympic gold. · **When and where:** Stockholm Olympics, 1912 — pentathlon and decathlon, the decathlon world record standing to 1927. · **Movie:** *Jim Thorpe – All-American* (1951).
Medals stripped in 1913 over $2-a-game summer baseball; restored as co-champion 1982; restored as sole champion July 15, 2022 — 110 years to the day. Our angle: a boarding-school student beating the world in the decade the country called his people vanishing (the athletic career itself is `sports-play`'s).

### Ira Hayes
> **Who:** Akimel O'odham (Pima) Marine from the Gila River community, one of the six who raised the second flag on Mount Suribachi. · **When and where:** Iwo Jima, February 23, 1945; died at home in Arizona, January 24, 1955, at 32. · **Movie:** *The Outsider* (1961); also portrayed in *Flags of Our Fathers* (2006).

### Chester Nez
> **Who:** Navajo Marine, one of the original 29 who built the code — and a boarding-school boy punished for speaking the language the code was made of. · **When and where:** Fort Defiance school, then the Pacific war, 1942–45; the last of the original 29, died June 4, 2014.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Federal policy swung from trying to end the tribes altogether to recognizing their right to govern themselves — and the swing was forced, case by case and law by law, by Native people: fishermen who would not stop fishing, students who took an island, nations that sued, and a terminated tribe that won itself back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Termination and Relocation" -->
House Concurrent Resolution 108 (August 1, 1953) declared the policy of ending federal recognition; by 1970 about 60 termination proceedings had hit more than 100 tribes and bands and moved over 3 million acres out of trust. The Menominee of Wisconsin — terminated 1954, pushed into poverty — organized (the DRUMS movement, with Ada Deer) and won the Menominee Restoration Act (December 22, 1973): the tide turning in law. Meanwhile relocation moved about 30,000 people to cities in the 1950s and over 100,000 by the 1970s (the movement itself is `migration`'s); poverty followed many, and so did urban Indian communities — Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland, Los Angeles — where the next movement was born.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Red Power" -->
The American Indian Movement was founded in Minneapolis (July 1968) to patrol police abuse in the city relocation built. "Indians of All Tribes" — Richard Oakes among the leaders — held Alcatraz Island for 19 months (November 20, 1969 – June 11, 1971), offering to buy it for "$24 in glass beads and red cloth"; the occupation made the nations national news and helped end termination: Blue Lake and its 48,000 sacred acres went home to Taos Pueblo (December 15, 1970), and the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (December 18, 1971) settled 44 million acres and $962.5 million through Native-owned corporations. In 1973, about 200 Oglala Lakota and AIM members held the village of Wounded Knee for 71 days (February 27 – May 8) — the site chosen because of 1890.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fish-ins and the Boldt decision" -->
Nisqually, Puyallup, and other Northwest nations fished in open defiance of state arrests — "fish-ins," centered on Frank's Landing on the Nisqually — to force the 1850s treaties into court. United States v. Washington (the Boldt decision, February 12, 1974): the treaties entitle the tribes to half the harvestable salmon, and the tribes became co-managers of the fishery; the Supreme Court affirmed it in 1979. Treaty words a century old, made to mean what they said.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Self-determination in statute" -->
The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (January 1975) let nations contract to run federal programs themselves — schools, clinics, police ("Education" is in the title because the schools were the heart of it). The Indian Child Welfare Act (1978) set federal standards to keep Native children with Native families. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (October 17, 1988) framed tribal gaming — a major revenue source for some nations, small for others; say so. NAGPRA (November 16, 1990): remains and sacred objects in museums belong to their nations, and must go home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nations take the schools back" -->
The Navajo Nation founded the first tribally controlled college in 1968 (Navajo Community College, today Diné College); dozens of tribal colleges followed. The Kennedy Report (Senate, November 1969 — *Indian Education: A National Tragedy — A National Challenge*) found federal Indian education was "coercive assimilation" with "disastrous effects" and recommended Native control. Boarding-school enrollment peaked about 60,000 in 1973, then fell as nations took the schools over or closed them; survivors began publishing their accounts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="wilma-mankiller" name="Wilma Mankiller" movie="Mankiller (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Wilma Mankiller
> **Who:** first woman Principal Chief of the Cherokee Nation. · **When and where:** Cherokee Nation, Oklahoma — chief December 1985 to August 1995; elected 1987, re-elected 1991 with 83% of the vote. · **Movie:** *Mankiller* (2017, documentary).
Her family was moved to San Francisco under the relocation program when she was a child; Alcatraz drew her into the movement; she came home and led ~140,000 citizens, building water lines and clinics community by community. Presidential Medal of Freedom, 1998; died 2010. Her life is the era's arc in one person: relocation → Red Power → self-government.

> **Who:** Ojibwe writer and artist — a Pipestone Indian Boarding School student (Minnesota, 1935–1945) who, three decades later, was a lead organizer of the Alcatraz occupation. · **When and where:** Pipestone, Minnesota, then the San Francisco Bay Area, 1969–71.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
574 federally recognized nations governing themselves — courts, police, schools, clinics, businesses — plus state-recognized nations, nations still seeking recognition, more than 200 Alaska Native villages and their corporations, and Native Hawaiians (territories thread, shared with america-world). 9.7 million people identified as American Indian or Alaska Native in the 2020 census — from about 250,000 counted in 1900. The Navajo Nation's land is larger than 10 states (27,000+ square miles); the Cherokee Nation passed 450,000 citizens. In 2004 the National Museum of the American Indian opened on the National Mall with some 25,000 Native people in the opening procession. Current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sovereignty in court and in office" -->
*McGirt v. Oklahoma* (July 9, 2020) held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation — 3.25 million acres of eastern Oklahoma, including much of Tulsa — was never disestablished; the opinion opens: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise." *Haaland v. Brackeen* (June 15, 2023) upheld the Indian Child Welfare Act, 7–2. In 2018 Deb Haaland and Sharice Davids became the first Native American women elected to Congress; on March 15, 2021 Haaland was confirmed Secretary of the Interior — the first Native American cabinet secretary, heading the department that once ran the boarding schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standing Rock and land back" -->
Standing Rock, 2016: the Standing Rock Sioux opposed the Dakota Access Pipeline's crossing under Lake Oahe, their water supply. Youth carried the message on foot — 500 miles to the Army Corps in Omaha, then 2,000 miles to Washington; thousands of people from hundreds of nations camped at Oceti Sakowin — widely called the largest gathering of Native nations in more than a century; over 2,000 veterans arrived in December 2016 to stand with them. The Army Corps halted the easement December 4, 2016; the next administration approved it and oil moved in 2017 — both truths stated plainly. Land back in concrete cases: the National Bison Range (18,800 acres taken in 1908) was restored to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes by law in December 2020. Film: *Awake, a Dream from Standing Rock* (2017).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding-school reckoning" -->
Secretary Haaland ordered the Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative in 2021 — the government counting its own record. Final report (July 30, 2024): 417 federal schools in 37 states and territories; at least 973 documented child deaths; at least 74 burial sites at 65 schools; and the report says the true toll is higher. October 25, 2024: at the Gila River Indian Community — Ira Hayes's home — the president issued the first formal apology for the system. The children are coming home: the Army returned Little Chief and Horse to the Northern Arapaho from the Carlisle cemetery in 2017, and nine Rosebud Sioux children home to South Dakota in July 2021, escorted by Rosebud youth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Languages come home — the school thread ends here" -->
The direct answer to the boarding schools is the immersion school. Hawaiian: Pūnana Leo preschools from 1984, the ban on teaching in Hawaiian lifted in 1986, Hawaiian-medium schooling now running preschool through graduate school. Wôpanâak (Wampanoag): silent since the mid-1800s, reconstructed from written records by Jessie Little Doe Baird's project (founded 1993) — the first American language with no living speakers brought back to living use, now taught to Wampanoag children. Cherokee, Lakota, Diné, and many others run immersion schools. The languages the schools punished helped win a world war, and are now taught to children on purpose — end the thread on that sentence's facts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
