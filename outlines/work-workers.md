## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Work was organized by season and by household, not by wages or a clock. There were no employers, no set hours, and no job a person could be hired for or fired from — the work is real, but this chapter's subject did not exist here yet.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="household and season" -->
- In Eastern Woodlands communities, women farmed corn, beans, and squash and gathered wild foods; men hunted and fished; everyone joined the big jobs — harvest, maple sugaring, canoe building (Encyclopedia.com; American Yawp).
- Labor gathered into larger groups at planting and harvest; children learned by working alongside adults.
- Where farming supported towns, some people specialized — traders, stoneworkers, religious leaders (American Yawp). Say this plainly and stop; no documented individual survives at story level from this angle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="work-workers" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first work newcomers organized in this land was forced work, and there is almost nothing else to report from this chapter's angle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish labor systems" -->
- The encomienda let Spanish settlers take forced labor and tribute from Native people in Florida and the Southwest; the repartimiento (from the mid-1500s) assigned rotating drafts of Native workers, nominally paid, to Spanish projects (Britannica).
- Native people were compelled to build and farm for missions and towns. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the system as enslavement, lead) · `native-nations` (what it did to the nations).
- February 1599, Acoma Pueblo: after his soldiers attacked the pueblo, Juan de Oñate sentenced the survivors. Men over 25 were to lose a foot and serve twenty years. Boys 12 to 25 and girls and women over 12 were to serve twenty years. Serving meant working for Spanish households with no right to leave. Shared with: `native-nations` (Acoma, lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="work-workers" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most people who worked in the colonies did not work for wages: they were bound — by indenture for a term of years, or, increasingly, for life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="bound labor and the artisan's shop" -->
- Indentured servitude was the era's labor system: between half and two-thirds of European immigrants arrived under indenture (some estimates run higher — state the range); terms of four to seven years; at the end, "freedom dues" — clothing, corn, tools, sometimes land (EH.net; Oxford Research Encyclopedia).
- Apprenticeship bound children to masters to learn a trade. Household production — spinning, brewing, candle-making — filled the rest of the working day (the household itself is `home-family`'s).
- Enslaved labor begins in the English colonies in this century. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the system, lead — one pointer sentence here only).
- In the Chesapeake, about 50,000 servants arrived between 1630 and 1680, three of every four newcomers. They worked tobacco from dawn to dusk, six days a week, and the first summer's sickness killed most newcomers (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Runaways: in July 1640 a Virginia court ordered 30 lashes and extra years for two runaway servants of Hugh Gwyn, a Dutchman named Victor and a Scot, James Gregory. The third runaway, John Punch, a Black man, was ordered to serve for life. A 1643 law doubled a runaway's lost time and marked a repeat runaway with the letter R on the cheek (Encyclopedia Virginia primary documents). The records here do not describe how the mark was made. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (John Punch and slavery for life, lead).
- Martin's Hundred, where Frethorne worked, was 20,000 acres on the James River inside Tsenacomoco, the Powhatan chiefdom. On March 22, 1622 Powhatan warriors killed about 347 colonists, 73 of them at Martin's Hundred's town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne
> **Who:** an indentured servant boy in early Virginia whose begging letters home survive. · **When and where:** Martin's Hundred, Virginia, 1622–1624.
Arrived December 1622 on the *Abigail*, likely in his early teens. In March–April 1623 he wrote his parents asking them to buy out his indenture or send food — since leaving the ship he had eaten nothing but peas and water gruel, "and when we are sick there is nothing to comfort us." A muster list made by February 1624 records him among the dead at Martin's Hundred (Virtual Jamestown; Encyclopedia Virginia).

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A working world of craft shops, sailors, servants, and enslaved workers — with the enslaved share of the workforce growing fast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the craft ladder" -->
- Apprentice, then journeyman (a wage-earner paid by the day), then — with luck and savings — master of a shop: the standard path in printing, shoemaking, carpentry, smithing.
- Enslaved men also worked the skilled trades in towns and were "hired out" for wages their owners kept. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the enslaved work day and the system, lead — do not repeat their gang/task detail).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="sailors and the waterfront" -->
- Sailors and dockworkers were the era's big wage-earning workforce, and the navy could seize them: press gangs took men for forced naval service.
- Knowles Riot, Boston, November 17, 1747 — after press gangs seized 46 men, working Bostonians rioted for three days, held naval officers hostage, and forced the pressed men's release (New England Historical Society; Boston 1775).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-work-workers" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** the most famous runaway apprentice in American history — before everything else, a bound teenage print-shop worker. · **When and where:** Boston and Philadelphia, 1718–1723.
> **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
Apprenticed at 12 to his brother James's Boston print shop; beaten and bound until 21 by the contract. In September 1723, with about four years left, he ran away — to New York, then Philadelphia, arriving October 6, 1723 nearly penniless — and worked as a journeyman printer before ever owning a shop (Massachusetts Historical Society; UPenn Archives). His other lives belong to other chapters; the runaway apprentice is ours.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Working people begin acting together as working people — the first authenticated strike, and the first lasting trade union.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first strike and the first union" -->
- Philadelphia journeymen printers, 1786: struck against a wage cut and won a $6-a-week minimum — the first authenticated strike by hired workers against an employer, and the first strike-benefit fund on record (DOL labor-history chronology; ExplorePAhistory). State the dispute: New York journeymen tailors struck in 1768, so say "often called the first."
- Federal Society of Journeymen Cordwainers (shoemakers), Philadelphia, 1794 — the first sustained trade union in the U.S., striking to hold a standard wage (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
- Thin at story level: no verified individual worker's account found for this era; the spans carry it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="work-workers" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The factory arrives, and with it the bell, the clock, and a working day someone else sets.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the law against unions, then for them" -->
- 1806: eight Philadelphia cordwainers convicted of "a conspiracy to raise their wages" — fined $8 each; unions treated as crimes (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
- 1842: Commonwealth v. Hunt (Massachusetts) — unions are not criminal conspiracies as such; organizing and striking can be lawful (Britannica).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lowell: the mill girls" -->
- Young New England women lived in company boardinghouses and worked 12 to 14 hours a day, timed by factory bells (Britannica; NPS Lowell).
- February 1834: wage cuts of 12–25% announced; about 800 women "turned out" — and lost. October 1836: about 1,500 struck over a boardinghouse rate hike and partly won (History Matters/GMU).
- Winter 1844–45: Sarah Bagley organized the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, which petitioned for the ten-hour day and pushed Massachusetts into the first legislative investigation of factory conditions (Britannica; NPS).
- Lowell stood at Pawtucket Falls, a Pennacook fishing place, where the towns of Pawtucket and Wamesit stood in the 1600s. Their sachem Wannalancet and his people moved north to Canada during King Philip's War (1675 to 1676) (UMass Lowell Libraries).
- The mills belonged to Boston merchants called the Boston Associates. They spun cotton grown by enslaved people and sold cheap cloth back to plantations to dress enslaved workers (NPS). Shared with: `economy` (lead) · `slavery-freedom`.
- Most mill girls were 15 to 25 and stayed about three years. They had to live in a company boardinghouse and attend church (NPS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the ten-hour movement, children, and the gangs" -->
- 1835: the first general strike in the U.S. — Philadelphia coal heavers started it, over forty trades and nearly 20,000 workers joined, and the city granted the ten-hour day (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
- March 31, 1840: President Van Buren ordered a ten-hour day for laborers on federal public works — private workplaces untouched.
- Child labor was normal and legal; children "doffed" bobbins and tended machines. Canal and railroad construction gangs — heavily Irish immigrant labor — dug and graded the routes. Shared with: `transportation` (the canals and lines) · `immigration` (who the diggers were).
- Duffy's Cut, 1832: 57 Irish workers, average age 22, dug a railroad cut for contractor Philip Duffy in Chester County, Pennsylvania. All died that summer as cholera reached their camp. Skulls dug up in 2009 to 2012 show some were beaten to death and one was shot. The records do not say who killed them (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
- Hopewell Furnace, Pennsylvania, 1835 to 1837: woodcutters paid by the cord and colliers who tended charcoal pits day and night, living in huts beside them (NPS). Shared with: `energy` (the fuel).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" name="Harriet Hanson Robinson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harriet Hanson Robinson
> **Who:** a Lowell doffer who walked out in the 1836 strike at age 11 and wrote the mill girls' story down. · **When and where:** Lowell, Massachusetts, 1835–1848.
Began mill work at 10. In the October 1836 turnout, when the girls in her room hesitated, she said she would go out "whether anyone else does or not" — and led them out. Stayed in the mills until 1848; published the memoir *Loom and Spindle* in 1898 (History Matters/GMU; Internet Archive edition).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Industrial work at its harshest — twelve-hour days, child workers, no safety law — and the first national attempts to organize against it, most of them broken by force.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the eight-hour fight: Knights, Haymarket, AFL" -->
- Knights of Labor, founded December 9, 1869 (Uriah Stephens; later Terence Powderly): about 700,000 members by 1886, including women and — unevenly — Black workers (LOC; Britannica).
- July 1877: B&O Railroad president John W. Garrett cut wages again, and the Great Railroad Strike spread from Martinsburg, West Virginia across six states. Governors called out militia and President Hayes sent federal troops. Pittsburgh's official toll was 26 dead, Reading's at least 10, about 100 nationwide by one count (LOC). The workers won nothing.
- May 1, 1886: national strikes for the eight-hour day. May 3: Chicago police fired on strikers at the McCormick plant and killed at least two. May 4, Haymarket Square: 176 police under Inspector John Bonfield moved to break up the protest meeting, and someone threw a bomb. Seven or eight officers (sources differ) and at least four civilians died in the blast and the police gunfire. Eight anarchists were convicted without proof any threw it; four hanged November 11, 1887; Governor Altgeld pardoned the surviving three in 1893, calling the trial unfair (LOC; Britannica).
- December 8, 1886, Columbus, Ohio: the American Federation of Labor founded — skilled craft unions bargaining for wages and hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead and Pullman" -->
- Homestead, July 6, 1892: 300 Pinkerton agents on barges fought strikers at Carnegie's steel works — seven workers and three Pinkertons killed (some counts more); the state militia followed and the union was broken. Shared with: `big-business` (the company side — Frick, Carnegie, private guards, lead on that half).
- Pullman, 1894: the company cut wages about a quarter to a third but not the rents in its company town. On July 2 federal judges William Woods and Peter Grosscup, at Attorney General Richard Olney's request, ordered the union to stop. On July 3 President Cleveland sent troops into Chicago over Governor Altgeld's protest, and soldiers shot some strikers (Federal Judicial Center). The American Railway Union's national boycott was broken; about 30 people were killed nationwide (counts vary to 34); Debs got six months in jail. Days into the crisis, Congress made Labor Day a federal holiday (June 28, 1894 — the holiday is `holidays`' story). Shared with: `big-business` (the company town and the injunction, from the power side).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what the work was" -->
- Sweatshops in garment lofts and tenements; breaker boys picking slate from coal in the anthracite fields; steel mills on twelve-hour shifts.
- Children at work: from 1890 to 1910 at least 18 percent of children aged 10 to 15 worked. In 1904 Southern cotton mills employed 50,000 children, 20,000 of them under 12, on 12-hour days, six days a week (BLS Monthly Labor Review, 2017).
- Brakemen coupled railroad cars by hand between moving cars, and missing fingers were called "the badge of the experienced worker." Coupling accidents fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902 after Congress required automatic couplers (ASME). Shared with: `transportation`.
- Avondale, Pennsylvania, September 6, 1869: fire in the wooden breaker over the only shaft killed 110, including five boys. The next year Pennsylvania required two exits from every mine (EARTH Magazine). Shared with: `energy` · `disasters`.
- Lattimer, Pennsylvania, September 10, 1897: Sheriff James Martin's deputies fired on about 400 unarmed immigrant miners and killed 19 Polish, Slovak and Lithuanian men, many shot in the back. A jury acquitted Martin and his deputies on March 2, 1898 (ExplorePAhistory).
- Telephone operating became women's work: Emma Nutt, the first woman operator, hired in Boston in 1878 at $10 a month for a 54-hour week. Shared with: `technology`.
- Domestic service was the largest single occupation for women: the 1870 census counted 867,354 servants — about half of all employed women (Women's Bureau via FRASER). Paid housework is ours; the household is `home-family`'s.
- June 25, 1867: several thousand Chinese workers (estimates 2,000–5,000) building the Central Pacific struck for eight days for equal pay and an end to whipping; the company cut off their food and the strike ended without formal concessions (LOC). Shared with: `transportation` (the railroad, lead) · `immigration` (who came).
- Convict leasing put arrested Black men to unpaid work in mines and on railroads. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the system, lead — one sentence here).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-gompers" name="Samuel Gompers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Gompers
> **Who:** cigar maker who built and led the American Federation of Labor for nearly four decades. · **When and where:** London and New York, born January 27, 1850; AFL president 1886–1924 (except 1895).
Rolled cigars from boyhood in London and New York tenement shops, where a reader read aloud to the workers. Built the AFL on "pure and simple" unionism — wages, hours, conditions — from about 140,000 members in 1886 to nearly 3 million at his death (AFL-CIO; Britannica).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-gompers" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" name="Eugene V. Debs" movie="American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eugene V. Debs
> **Who:** locomotive fireman who founded the first industrial union and went to jail for the Pullman boycott. · **When and where:** Terre Haute, Indiana, and Chicago; ARU founded June 1893.
> **Movie:** American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs (2017)
Shoveled coal on locomotives as a young man. His American Railway Union took in all railroad workers, not one craft at a time — the first industrial union. For defying the Pullman injunction he served six months in the Woodstock, Illinois jail (1895). He later ran for president five times as a Socialist — the last time, 1920, from a prison cell, drawing about 900,000 votes (Britannica; EBSCO). `big-business` shows him from the company's side of Pullman.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jennie-curtis" name="Jennie Curtis" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jennie Curtis
> **Who:** the Pullman seamstress whose speech launched the boycott. · **When and where:** Pullman, Illinois, 1894.
Sewed carpets and drapery in Pullman's repair shops. Her pay fell from $1.50–$2.25 a day to about 80 cents, with two cuts in one week in November 1893, while the company kept collecting its rents — including back rent her father owed when he died after 13 years at Pullman. President of the ARU "girls' local" (125 members), she asked the June 1894 ARU convention for help; the delegates voted the boycott. She testified to the U.S. Strike Commission that year (NPS; Strike Commission testimony).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jennie-curtis" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two disasters, a photographer, and a depression turn the daily job into a matter of federal law: by 1938 the country has a minimum wage, a 40-hour week, and a child-labor ban.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the mines and the mills' children" -->
- 1902 anthracite strike: 147,000 miners and breaker boys out about five months; Theodore Roosevelt's commission — the first federal mediation instead of strikebreaking — heard 558 witnesses, including breaker boys younger than nine; the 1903 award: 10% raise, nine-hour day (PA Center for the Book; LOC).
- Lewis Hine quit teaching in 1908 to photograph child workers for the National Child Labor Committee — thousands of pictures, 1908–1917, of mill children, breaker boys, cannery kids, newsies. The photographs sit in this era by date; the practice's peak came earlier (documentary about him: *America and Lewis Hine*, 1984).
- 1907: 3,242 coal miners were killed at work, the worst year on record. The Monongah, West Virginia explosion of December 6 killed 362 by the official count, over 500 by later estimates (MSHA). Shared with: `energy` · `disasters`.
- 1916: Congress banned interstate shipping of goods made by children under 14. Roland Dagenhart, whose sons worked beside him in a North Carolina cotton mill, sued, and on June 3, 1918 the Supreme Court struck the law down. The justices upheld a child-labor ban only in 1941 (U.S. Capitol Visitor Center).
- Lawrence, Massachusetts, 1912: mill owners cut pay when the state cut the week from 56 to 54 hours, and workers who spoke more than a dozen languages struck on January 12. On February 24 police clubbed mothers and children at the train station. On March 12 the owners gave a 15 percent raise (Mass Humanities).
- Ludlow, Colorado, April 20, 1914: Colorado National Guard soldiers, sent by Governor Elias Ammons and paid for by the mine owners, fought striking coal miners and burned their tent colony. At least 19 died, 13 of them women and children who suffocated in a pit under a tent. Guardsmen shot strike leader Louis Tikas three times in the back. The main owner of the company was John D. Rockefeller Jr. (Colorado Encyclopedia). Shared with: `energy`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="mother-jones" name="Mother Jones" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mother Jones
> **Who:** Mary Harris Jones, the miners' organizer a prosecutor called "the most dangerous woman in America" (1902). · **When and where:** born County Cork, Ireland — she claimed 1830; her baptismal record says 1837 (state both); organizing from the 1890s to the 1920s.
A dressmaker who lost her husband and all four children to yellow fever in Memphis (1867) and her home to the Chicago fire (1871), then gave her life to the United Mine Workers' camps. July 1903: she led striking child textile workers on the March of the Mill Children, from Philadelphia toward President Roosevelt's home at Oyster Bay, to make the country look at the mill children's missing fingers (Britannica; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mother-jones" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Triangle" -->
- November 22, 1909: shirtwaist maker Clara Lemlich, bruised from a company beating, called the general strike in Yiddish at Cooper Union — the Uprising of the 20,000, eleven weeks of garment strikes (Jewish Women's Archive). Shared with: `rights-movements` (women organizing).
- March 25, 1911: fire in the Triangle Waist Company's top floors killed 146 workers, most of them young immigrant women, some 14 years old; a stairwell door was kept locked; dozens jumped. The owners were acquitted of manslaughter that December. New York then rewrote its factory laws (Cornell ILR Triangle site). Topic documentaries: *Triangle Fire* (PBS, 2011); *Triangle: Remembering the Fire* (HBO, 2011). Shared with: `disasters` (the fire and response) · `government-politics` (the laws) · `rights-movements` (the organizing before and after).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rose-freedman" name="Rose Rosenfeld Freedman" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rose Rosenfeld Freedman
> **Who:** Triangle survivor who refused to be paid for silence. · **When and where:** New York, March 25, 1911; died 2001.
Two days short of her 18th birthday, she wondered what the executives were doing — and followed them up to the 10th-floor roof, where rescuers pulled survivors across. She said the owners offered money for her silence about the locked doors; she refused, and told the truth for ninety years. Died February 15, 2001, at 107 — the last survivor (Variety obituary; Cornell ILR).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rose-freedman" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frances-perkins-work-workers" name="Frances Perkins" movie="Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frances Perkins
> **Who:** watched the Triangle fire; became the first woman in a U.S. Cabinet and wrote the fire's lessons into federal law. · **When and where:** New York 1911; Secretary of Labor 1933–1945.
> **Movie:** Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)
Having tea near Washington Square when the fire broke out, she ran to the scene and watched workers jump. She investigated factories for New York, then, as FDR's Labor Secretary, drove the Fair Labor Standards Act and Social Security. She later said the New Deal began on March 25, 1911 (Columbia exhibits; DOL history). `economy` features her separately for the New Deal economy.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frances-perkins-work-workers" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="addie-card" name="Addie Card" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Addie Card
> **Who:** the 12-year-old Vermont mill spinner in one of Lewis Hine's most famous photographs. · **When and where:** North Pownal, Vermont, 1910.
Hine photographed her at the spinning frame in 1910, barefoot, and captioned her an "anemic little spinner"; the mill girls said she was ten, and he misspelled her name "Addie Laird." The picture later ran on a 1998 U.S. postage stamp. In 2005–06 researcher Joe Manning traced her real name and long, hard life; she died in 1993 (Met Museum record; LOC; Manning's Mornings on Maple Street).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="addie-card" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the line, the dial painters, and the sit-downs" -->
- The assembly line brought the speed-up: the pace of work set by the machinery, not the worker. Shared with: `technology` (the method as invention) · `big-business` (Ford the company).
- Ford's Highland Park line, 1913: work that took over 12 hours fell to 93 minutes, and so many men quit that turnover hit 370 percent. The $5 day of January 1914 went only to men who passed home inspections by Ford investigators (The Henry Ford). Shared with: `economy`.
- Hawks Nest tunnel, West Virginia, 1930 to 1932: contractor Rinehart and Dennis dug a tunnel through silica rock for Union Carbide, with no dust control and no masks. Almost 5,000 men worked it, most of them Black migrants from the South. Breathing the dust scarred their lungs (silicosis). Death counts run from 109 (the company) to 476 (a House committee, 1936) to 764 (a 1986 study) to close to 1,000 (NIOSH). Lawsuits ended in a $200,000 settlement, $30 to $1,600 a person (e-WV). Shared with: `health` · `disasters`.
- Memorial Day, May 30, 1937, Chicago: 264 police met several hundred marchers supporting the steel strike at Republic Steel. Police fired and swung clubs, killing 10 and shooting 40. A Senate committee found the police used "excessive force" (Chicago History Museum).
- The big builds: 5 died on the Empire State Building (official count), 11 on the Golden Gate Bridge, whose safety net caught 19 men, and 96 at Hoover Dam by the government's count, which leaves out 42 "pneumonia" deaths in the tunnels (USBR). Shared with: `landmarks`.
- The Radium Girls: dial painters taught to point brushes with their lips, poisoned at work, disbelieved by their employers — Grace Fryer's suit and the Illinois cases established that companies answer for slow occupational poisoning. Shared with: `rights-movements` (the court fight) · `elements` (radium itself) · `health` (the disease).
- Flint sit-down strike, December 30, 1936 – February 11, 1937: autoworkers occupied GM's plants for 44 days; the Women's Emergency Brigade (Genora Johnson, 23) guarded the windows; GM recognized the United Auto Workers (Zinn Education Project; UM-Flint). Topic documentary: *With Babies and Banners* (1979). The CIO organized the mass-production plants; the Wagner Act (1935) made organizing a legal right (shared with `big-business` as regulation).
- Fair Labor Standards Act, June 25, 1938: 25-cent minimum wage, 44-hour week falling to 40 by 1940, most child labor under 16 banned (DOL; LOC). New Deal jobs programs (WPA, CCC) — shared with: `economy` (lead) · `government-politics` (the programs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="grace-fryer-work-workers" name="Grace Fryer" movie="Radium Girls (2018)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Grace Fryer
> **Who:** dial painter who sued U.S. Radium while dying — and won. · **When and where:** Orange, New Jersey; painting from 1917, suit 1927, settlement June 1928.
> **Movie:** Radium Girls (2018)
Hired at U.S. Radium from 1917, taught to shape the brush with her lips. As the dial painters sickened and died, the company denied everything. Fryer and four dying co-workers — the press called them "the five women doomed to die" — sued in 1927 and won a June 1928 settlement: $10,000 each, a $600-a-year pension, and medical costs (LOC "Living Dead Women"; History.com). The film is a drama inspired by the case, with invented sister characters. Her court fight is also told in `rights-movements`; radium itself in `elements`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="grace-fryer-work-workers" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="war work" -->
- August 1942: the U.S. and Mexico began the bracero program, bringing Mexican men to farms and railroads on contracts. From 1942 to 1946 employers took 10 percent of every bracero's pay for a savings fund that most never received (California Legislature, 2009). Shared with: `immigration`.
- Women went from 27% of the workforce (1940) to nearly 37% (1945); in 1943 more than 310,000 women built aircraft — about 65% of that industry's workers (Journal of Economic History; wartime records). Documentary about the real "Rosies": *The Life and Times of Rosie the Riveter* (1980).
- After the war: the 1945–46 strike wave, the largest ever recorded — 4,985 stoppages and 4.6 million strikers in 1946 alone (BLS via Encyclopedia.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="naomi-parker-fraley" name="Naomi Parker Fraley" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Naomi Parker Fraley
> **Who:** the Navy-yard lathe worker in the 1942 photograph tied to the "We Can Do It!" poster — after seventy years of the credit going to someone else. · **When and where:** Alameda, California, 1942; identified 2015–16; died 2018.
A real war worker photographed at her lathe in 1942. For decades the woman in the photo was misidentified as Geraldine Hoff Doyle; scholar James Kimble found the original captioned print naming Parker. State the rest honestly: the poster itself was little known in the war, became an icon in the 1980s, and even the photo-to-poster link is not proven. She died January 20, 2018, at 96 (Seton Hall; TIME).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="a-philip-randolph-work-workers" name="A. Philip Randolph" movie="10,000 Black Men Named George (2002)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### A. Philip Randolph
> **Who:** organized the Pullman porters and won the first contract between a major U.S. company and a Black-led union. · **When and where:** union founded August 1925; contract signed August 25, 1937; Washington 1941.
> **Movie:** 10,000 Black Men Named George (2002)
The porters — all Black men, commonly all called "George" — served the sleeping cars for long months of hours. Randolph's Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters fought Pullman for twelve years until the company signed: a sharply shorter working month and $1.25 million in raises (LOC; Britannica). In 1941 his threatened march on Washington made FDR ban discrimination in defense plants (Executive Order 8802, June 25, 1941). Also on film: *A. Philip Randolph: For Jobs and Freedom* (1996, documentary). His 1963 march belongs to `rights-movements`.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Union membership peaks at about one worker in three and then falls for the rest of the century; the office replaces the factory; women flood into paid work; and by the end, the mills are closing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the postwar deal" -->
- 1954: union membership peaks at 34.8% of wage and salary workers (NBER/BLS). December 5, 1955: the AFL and CIO merge — about 15 million members, George Meany president.
- By 1956, white-collar workers outnumber blue-collar workers for the first time; the typical job starts moving to a desk (Encyclopedia.com/BLS milestone).
- Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake and Akwesasne — the "skywalkers" — kept walking high steel on bridges and skyscrapers through the century, a Native trade running from the 1920s to the World Trade Center (History.com; 9/11 Memorial Museum). Shared with: `landmarks` (the structures).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="new voices organizing" -->
- Memphis, 1968: sanitation workers Echol Cole and Robert Walker were crushed to death in their truck's compactor on February 1; eleven days later 1,300 Black sanitation men struck under signs reading "I AM A MAN." Many were paid so little that they were on welfare. Mayor Henry Loeb refused to recognize their union or take the worn-out trucks off the street, and on March 28 police shot and killed a 16-year-old during a march (King Institute). Dr. King came to stand with them and was assassinated in Memphis April 4; the city then recognized the union (King Institute; AFSCME). Shared with: `rights-movements` (the civil-rights campaign, lead on King).
- 9to5, founded 1973 in Boston by office workers Karen Nussbaum and Ellen Cassedy — women clerical workers organizing over pay, promotion, and harassment (Britannica). Topic documentary: *9to5: The Story of a Movement* (2020).
- OSHA: the job-safety law signed December 29, 1970. In 1970 about 14,000 workers a year, 38 a day, died on the job. In 2024 it was 5,070, about 14 a day, in a much larger workforce (OSHA, and BLS, released February 2026).
- Farmington, West Virginia, November 20, 1968: a mine explosion killed 78, and the 1969 coal-mine law followed, with inspections and black-lung benefits. More than 76,000 miners have died of black lung since 1968 (MSHA, Department of Labor). Shared with: `energy` · `health`.
- Braceros: at border centers such as Rio Vista in El Paso, officials stripped the men and sprayed them with DDT powder. They worked six- or seven-day weeks in 26 states until the program ended in 1964 (UTEP Institute of Oral History). Shared with: `immigration`.
- Pesticides: farm workers in sprayed fields had high rates of cancer, burns and birth defects, and the farmworkers' union campaigned to ban DDT on grape and lettuce ranches (Reuther Library). Shared with: `food-farming` · `health`.
- Airline rules: into the late 1960s airlines fired stewardesses who married or turned 32, until flight attendants such as Barbara "Dusty" Roads fought the rules under the 1964 Civil Rights Act. Shared with: `transportation`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="chavez-huerta" name="Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta" movie="Cesar Chavez (2014) · Dolores (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta
> **Who:** organizers who built the farmworkers' union — with Larry Itliong's Filipino strikers, who walked out first. · **When and where:** Delano, California, 1965–1975.
> **Movie:** Cesar Chavez (2014) · Dolores (2017)
September 8, 1965: about 1,500 Filipino American grape pickers of AWOC, led by Larry Itliong, struck the Delano growers; Chavez and Huerta's association voted September 16 to join. The 300-mile march to Sacramento (March 17 – April 11, 1966), Chavez's 25-day fast for nonviolence (1968), and a national grape boycott brought the growers to sign the first contracts on July 29, 1970. The merged union became the United Farm Workers; California's 1975 Agricultural Labor Relations Act made farmworker union rights law for the first time anywhere in the U.S. — federal labor law had excluded farmworkers since 1935. Huerta negotiated the contracts and coined "Sí, se puede" (NPS; UFW). Shared with: `food-farming` (farm labor in the food system) · `rights-movements` (the movement).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chavez-huerta" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the unraveling" -->
- Manufacturing employment peaked at 19.6 million in June 1979 and fell for forty years (BLS). Plants closed across the industrial Midwest — Youngstown's "Black Monday" (September 19, 1977) alone ended 5,000 steel jobs. Shared with: `big-business` (the corporate decisions) · `economy` (the macro shift).
- August 3, 1981: about 13,000 air traffic controllers struck; President Reagan fired 11,345 of them two days later and their union was decertified — widely read as open season on strikes (Reagan Library; EBSCO).
- Union membership: 20.1% in 1983; about 13% by 2000 (BLS). Women's labor-force participation: 34% in 1950, 60% by 2000 (BLS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-lumpkin" name="Frank Lumpkin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank Lumpkin
> **Who:** a Black steelworker who lost his job, his last paycheck and his pension when his Chicago mill shut, and fought for 17 years to get the money back. · **When and where:** Wisconsin Steel, South Deering, Chicago, 1950s to 1997.
Born 1916 in Washington, Georgia, to a sharecropping family. He left school at 15, boxed as "KO" Lumpkin, served in the Merchant Marine, and moved north in 1940. He worked at Wisconsin Steel for about 30 years. International Harvester had sold the mill in 1977 to Envirodyne, a firm with no steel experience. In late March 1980 the mill closed without warning, and more than 3,000 workers lost their jobs, health benefits and pensions. In April the bank told them their final paychecks were worthless. Lumpkin and his wife Bea started the Save Our Jobs Committee, which brought Black, Mexican American and other steelworkers and their families together. By 1987 the committee counted 600 former workers who had died since the closing. Lawyer Thomas Geoghegan won a $14.5 million settlement from International Harvester in 1988, and the committee won about $17 million in all, about a third of the $40 million owed. Lumpkin died in 2010 (Chicago History Museum finding aid, Chicago Public Library, and the People's World obituary, a Communist Party paper, labeled). Shared with: `economy` (the Rust Belt closings, told there through Gerald Dickey).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most Americans now work in services; millions work through an app with no boss they can see; and organizing starts again in places nobody expected — coffee shops and warehouses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the service floor and the app" -->
- Union membership in 2025: 10.0% of wage and salary workers, 14.7 million people, down from one in three at the 1954 peak. Public workers 32.9%, private 5.9% (BLS, released February 2026).
- The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009.
- In July 2023, 11.9 million people, 7.4% of workers, were independent contractors in their main job (BLS).
- Gig work: by August 2021, 16% of U.S. adults had earned money through an online gig platform, 9% within the past year (Pew Research survey). App drivers and couriers are classed as independent contractors, outside most labor law — the legal fights are ongoing (journalism where cited).
- 2020: in May, 35% of employed people were working from home because of the pandemic (BLS) — while "essential" workers in groceries, warehouses, delivery, and hospitals kept reporting in person. Remote and hybrid work stayed for much office work after the emergency.
- Heat: 345 workers died of heat on the job from 2011 to 2019, 43 of them in 2019 (BLS). OSHA proposed a heat rule in August 2024 and held hearings in 2025. As of September 2026 there is no final federal heat rule.
- Children in slaughterhouses: in February 2023 the Labor Department found that Packers Sanitation Services had 102 children aged 13 to 17 cleaning saws and head splitters with caustic chemicals on night shifts at 13 meatpacking plants. At least three were hurt. The company paid $1.5 million, the legal maximum (KCUR, journalism).
- Amazon warehouses: a December 2024 Senate committee staff report (Chairman Bernie Sanders) found Amazon warehouses had over 30% more injuries than the industry average in 2023, from repeating the same motions thousands of times in 10- to 12-hour shifts to meet tracked quotas (Senate HELP report, majority staff, labeled). Amazon disputed it.
- Wage theft: in the 10 largest states, 2.4 million workers lost $8 billion a year by being paid below the minimum wage (Economic Policy Institute, 2017, labor-aligned, labeled).
- Upper Big Branch, West Virginia, April 5, 2010: an explosion killed 29 miners. Massey Energy's CEO Don Blankenship got one year in prison for conspiring to break safety laws, not for the deaths (MSHA, NPR). Shared with: `energy`.
- Farmworkers: the Coalition of Immokalee Workers' Fair Food Program (from 2011) makes big buyers pay a little more per pound of tomatoes and buy only from growers who follow a worker-written code. Shared with: `food-farming` (Lucas Benitez, lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="organizing returns" -->
- December 9, 2021: baristas at one Buffalo Starbucks voted 19–8 for the first union among the company's roughly 9,000 corporate-owned U.S. stores; hundreds of stores followed (Washington Post — journalism).
- April 1, 2022: workers at Amazon's JFK8 warehouse on Staten Island voted 2,654–2,131 for the independent Amazon Labor Union — the first unionized Amazon warehouse in the U.S. (NLRB count; NPR — journalism).
- Status in 2026: no Starbucks store has a first contract. Baristas at about 600 stores had unionized by June 2025 and held a 131-day strike from November 2025. The Amazon Labor Union joined the Teamsters in June 2024, and on April 2, 2026 the National Labor Relations Board ordered Amazon to bargain at JFK8 (union timelines and the Teamsters, labeled).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="chris-smalls" name="Chris Smalls" movie="Union (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Chris Smalls
> **Who:** the fired Amazon warehouse worker who organized the first Amazon union in the U.S. (journalism-labeled). · **When and where:** JFK8, Staten Island, New York, 2020–2022.
> **Movie:** Union (2024)
A process assistant at JFK8, fired in March 2020 after leading a walkout over COVID safety. He and fellow workers built the independent Amazon Labor Union from a bus-stop table across the road — food, conversations, months of signatures — and won the April 2022 vote (NPR, CNBC — journalism; the vote count is the NLRB's record). The documentary follows the campaign itself.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="raef-lawson" name="Raef Lawson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Raef Lawson
> **Who:** a Grubhub food-delivery driver whose court case asked whether an app driver is an employee. · **When and where:** Los Angeles, 2015 to 2016; the case ran in federal court in San Francisco from 2015 to 2026.
Lawson was an actor and writer who signed up to deliver for Grubhub in August 2015. He chose shifts of two to five hours around mealtimes and was paid for 35 hours in November, 105 in December and 60 in January. Grubhub paid nothing toward his gas or his phone. It classed all 4,000 of its California drivers as independent contractors, meaning people in business for themselves with no right to minimum wage or overtime. Grubhub ended his contract on February 15, 2016, saying he had skipped too many shifts he signed up for. He sued. In 2018 Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley ruled he was a contractor. California's Supreme Court then changed the test, the appeals court sent the case back, and on March 30, 2023 the same judge ruled he had been an employee. She awarded him $65.11 in unpaid minimum wages. In 2026 Grubhub agreed to pay $24.75 million to about 60,000 California drivers (Ninth Circuit opinion, 2021, court order, March 2026, and Gizmodo and Courthouse News, journalism, labeled). Shared with: `transportation` (the app car and its costs, told there through Barbara Ann Berwick).
