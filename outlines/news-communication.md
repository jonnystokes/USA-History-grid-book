## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
News moved on foot, by fire, and along trade routes, and it moved reliably over long distances.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="runners, signal fires, and wampum" -->
Runners carrying messages; signal fires and smoke; wampum belts as record and message; news traveling with traders; council protocol for delivering it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="news-communication" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="seed" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For newcomers, news crossed the ocean at the speed of a ship, and could be a year out of date on arrival.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="letters and printed accounts by ship" -->
Letters and reports carried by ship; printed accounts of the Americas published in Europe and read as news.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="news-communication" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonies get a post route and their first newspaper — which is shut down after one issue.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first post route and the first newspaper" -->
Letters by ship and by rider; the first colonial post route, 1673 [VERIFY]; *Publick Occurrences*, Boston, 1690, suppressed after a single issue; the first printing press at Cambridge, 1638 (the press as a machine belongs to `technology`).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1700-1750" order="04" chapter="news-communication" label="1700 to 1750" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Newspapers become a normal part of colonial town life, and a jury decides that printing the truth is not a crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Boston News-Letter and the Zenger trial" -->
The *Boston News-Letter*, 1704, the first continuously published colonial newspaper; the Zenger trial, 1735, and the argument that truth is a defense against libel; Franklin as printer, newspaper owner, and later postmaster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Printed words organize a revolution, and the new country writes the post office and a free press into its founding documents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="pamphlets, the Post Office, and the First Amendment" -->
Committees of Correspondence; pamphlets, including *Common Sense*, 1776; the Post Office written into the Constitution; the First Amendment, 1791; partisan newspapers appearing at once.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-paine" name="Thomas Paine" movie="" kind="famous" status="candidate" -->
### Thomas Paine
Wrote the pamphlet that argued a country into existence.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-paine" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="news-communication" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
News gets cheap enough for anyone to buy, and then it gets faster than a horse.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the penny press and the telegraph" -->
The penny press from 1833; the growth of the post; abolitionist papers, including Garrison's *Liberator* and Douglass's *North Star*, 1847 [VERIFY]; the telegraph, 1844, breaking the link between news and travel; foreign-language and Native-language newspapers, including the *Cherokee Phoenix*, 1828 [VERIFY].
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-douglass-publisher" name="Frederick Douglass" movie="" kind="famous" status="candidate" -->
### Frederick Douglass
Published his own newspaper, the *North Star*, after escaping slavery.

### Elias Boudinot

War reporting, wire services, and photographs — plus newspapers that will print nearly anything to sell copies.

The Associated Press; Civil War correspondents and photographs; the rise of big-city papers and yellow journalism; rural free delivery [VERIFY 1896]; the immigrant-language press; Ida B. Wells's investigative reporting on lynching.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Reporting becomes a profession that investigates, and a voice in a box reaches every kitchen at once.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the muckrakers and the radio age" -->
The muckrakers, including Ida Tarbell and Upton Sinclair; radio from KDKA, 1920; Roosevelt's fireside chats; the *War of the Worlds* broadcast, 1938; newsreels; wire photographs; television beginning.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-tarbell-news-communication" name="Ida Tarbell" movie="" kind="famous" status="candidate" -->
### Ida Tarbell
Investigated Standard Oil in nineteen installments.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edward-r-murrow" name="Edward R. Murrow" movie="" kind="famous" status="candidate" -->

## 1950 to 2000

Murrow and the McCarthy broadcasts; Cronkite; civil rights and Vietnam shown in living rooms; Watergate reporting; the growth of cable and CNN, 1980; talk radio; local newspapers still profitable.
