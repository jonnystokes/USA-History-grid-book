## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, news in this land moved only as fast as a person could walk, run or paddle a canoe. People carried messages along foot trails and rivers between towns. Most of what is known about how they did it comes from later accounts by Native nations and by Europeans, and from objects that archaeologists have dug up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="runners and wampum" -->
Among the Haudenosaunee (the Six Nations, also called the Iroquois) of what is now New York, runners called the nations to council, carried news from one nation to another, and warned of danger. A runner carried a string of wampum to show that his message was official. Wampum is small beads cut from the white and purple parts of a clam shell.
The Onondaga Nation, which keeps the Confederacy's wampum, describes how a council was called. A string of wampum told each nation what the meeting was about. A wooden stick tied to the string told them when. Each day, someone cut a notch off the stick.
Shell beads found in the Northeast are about 4,500 years old. Beads drilled with stone drill bits were larger than later wampum and less common, because stone made drilling slow. After Europeans arrived, Native bead makers used iron awls and nails as drill bits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="hand signs and trade" -->
Many different languages were spoken in this land. Near the Gulf coast and in what is now Texas, nations that could not understand each other's speech talked with their hands. Spanish travellers reported this sign language from 1527 on, and language scholars think it was in use long before they came.
Trade goods moved hundreds of miles. Copper from Lake Superior and shells from the Gulf of Mexico turned up far from where they came from. No record survives of what news the traders passed along with the goods.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish, French and English newcomers could send news home only by ship. Commanders wrote reports to their kings. Survivors and explorers wrote books, and printers in Europe sold them. Native nations went on sending messages by runner and by hand sign, as they had before.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a letter to a king and a book by a survivor, 1565 and 1566" -->
In September 1565 the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his soldiers killed French Protestants at Fort Caroline, in Florida, in the homeland of Timucua-speaking peoples. They then killed more of the French at an inlet to the south. On 15 October 1565 Menéndez wrote to King Philip II of Spain. By his own count his men cut the throats of 132 men at the fort and 10 more the next day. At the inlet, he wrote, the Frenchmen gave up their weapons, and "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife."
The French side of the story reached Europe in print. Nicolas Le Challeux, a carpenter who escaped from Fort Caroline, wrote an account of the killings. A printer in Dieppe, France, published it in 1566. Its title called the killings the cruelty of the Spaniards against the French king's subjects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="hand signs on the plains, 1541" -->
In 1541 the Spanish army of Francisco Vázquez de Coronado met a buffalo-hunting people on the plains of what is now the Texas Panhandle. The Spaniards called them Querechos. Pedro de Castañeda, a soldier who later wrote the army's history, recorded that "although they conversed by means of signs they made themselves understood so well that there was no need of an interpreter."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a message carved in a post, Roanoke, 1587 to 1590" -->
In 1587 John White left 117 English colonists on Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, and sailed to England for supplies. His granddaughter Virginia Dare, born on the island that August, was one of the colonists. The island and the coast around it were the land of Algonquian-speaking peoples, the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan among them. Before he left, White and the colonists agreed on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of the new place on a tree or a doorpost. If they left because they were in danger, they would carve a cross above the name.
War between England and Spain kept White from sailing back until 1590. On 18 August 1590 he found the houses gone and a fence of posts around the empty site. One post had CROATOAN carved on it "in fayre Capitall letters," and a tree had the letters CRO. There was no cross. White wrote that Croatoan was the place where Manteo was born. Manteo was an Algonquian man who sailed to England in 1584, learned English on the voyage and in London, and came back in 1585 as an interpreter for the English. White called the people of Croatoan "our friends." A storm drove White's ship out to sea before he could search there. No record shows that anyone from England ever found the colonists.
Readers in Europe learned about this coast from printed books. Thomas Harriot, who had been there in 1585 and 1586, published *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* in 1588. In 1590 the engraver Theodor de Bry printed it again in four languages, with engravings copied from John White's paintings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="manteo-news-communication" name="Manteo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Manteo
> **Who:** An Algonquian man, born on Croatoan by John White's account, who learned English and interpreted for the English · **When and where:** the Outer Banks of North Carolina and London, 1584 to 1590

In 1584 the English captains Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe spent six weeks on the Outer Banks. When they sailed home, Wingina, a leader of the Algonquian people there, chose two men to go with them: Manteo and Wanchese. The ship reached England in mid-September. Both men began to learn English from the sailors during the voyage. In London they met Sir Walter Raleigh, who sent the voyages.

By the end of 1584 Manteo could speak enough English to interpret. He sailed home on 9 April 1585 on the *Tyger*, the lead ship of Raleigh's fleet.

In 1587 he helped the new colonists set up a village near the old English fort on Roanoke Island. On 13 August 1587 the English baptized Manteo into their church and named him Lord of "Dasamongueponke." White wrote in 1590 that the colonists had gone to Croatoan, "the place where Manteo was borne." When White came back, he found neither the colonists nor Manteo. No record says what happened to him.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s the English colonies got their first mail services and their first newspaper. The men who governed the colonies decided who could print news. In 1690 the governor and council of Massachusetts shut down the first newspaper four days after it appeared. In Virginia the governor and council stopped all printing in 1683. In New Mexico, Pueblo runners carried the plan for a revolt against Spanish rule from town to town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="letters by ship, by tavern and by planter" -->
Most letters in the early colonies went across the ocean to England and back. In 1639 the General Court of Massachusetts named Richard Fairbanks's tavern in Boston as the place to leave and collect letters sent "beyond the seas." Inside the colonies, people sent letters with friends, merchants and Native messengers.
In Virginia, around the middle of the 1600s, officials marked government letters "for public service." Each planter had to send them on to the next plantation. A planter who failed to do it lost a hogshead (a large barrel) of tobacco. On the big Southern plantations, the messengers were usually enslaved people, according to the Postal Service's own history.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first post rider, 1673, and the first postal system, 1692" -->
On 22 January 1673 the first post rider left New York for Boston, about 250 miles, on a monthly mail route between the two colonies. Governor Francis Lovelace of New York set it up with John Winthrop Jr. of Connecticut. The rider was to go once a month and took about two weeks each way. The service did not last long, but his route became the Boston Post Road. William Penn set up Pennsylvania's first post office in 1683.
In 1692 the English Crown gave Thomas Neale the right to run a mail service for all the colonies for 21 years. Neale never came to America. Andrew Hamilton, governor of New Jersey, ran it as his deputy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia: no printing allowed, 1671 to 1730" -->
In 1671 Governor William Berkeley of Virginia wrote to the king's officials in London: "I thank God, there are no free schools nor printing, and I hope we shall not have these hundred years." He gave his reason: printing spread new religious groups, disobedience and attacks on the government.
In 1682 a merchant named John Buckner brought a printer, William Nuthead, to Virginia. By February 1683 Nuthead had printed the colony's new laws. Governor Thomas Culpeper and his council ordered both men to stop printing. The next governor, Francis Howard, arrived in 1684 with orders from King Charles II that barred printing in Virginia. Virginia had no printer again until William Parks arrived about 1730.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first newspaper, 1690, closed after one issue" -->
From 1667 on, printers in Massachusetts printed single sheets of news, called broadsides, each about one event. On 25 September 1690 Benjamin Harris printed *Publick Occurrences, Both Forreign and Domestick*. He marked it "Numb. 1" and planned to print it once a month. It was three printed pages and a blank fourth page. Harris's notice suggested that readers could write more news on the blank page and pass the paper on.
Four days later, on 29 September, Governor Simon Bradstreet and the Massachusetts council ordered it "Suppressed and called in." They said Harris had printed it without their permission. They also objected to what it said. It accused Native allies of the English of "barbarous" cruelty to French prisoners, and it complained that colonial officials told the Mohawks too much about English war plans. It also printed a rumour about the private life of King Louis XIV of France. The council ordered that no one could print news in the colony without a licence from the government. There was never a second issue. One copy is known to survive, in London.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" name="Benjamin Harris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Harris
> **Who:** The London printer who printed the first newspaper in the English colonies · **When and where:** London, then Boston, 1679 to 1695

Benjamin Harris was born about 1647. In London in the 1670s and 1680s he printed pamphlets for Protestants who had broken away from the Church of England. He was a Baptist and spoke out against King Charles II. In 1679 he printed a newspaper without a licence, *The Domestick Intelligence*. English officials jailed him twice. After a third arrest in 1685 he fled to the Netherlands. A year later he sailed to Boston and opened a print shop.

On 25 September 1690 he printed *Publick Occurrences*. His opening notice said he wanted to stop the "many False Reports, maliciously made, and spread among us." The paper reported a fire that burned about 20 houses and smallpox spreading through Boston. On 29 September the governor and council ordered every copy called in and forbade him to print any newspaper again.

Harris obeyed. In 1692 he became the colony's official printer. He went back to England in 1695 and started a London paper, the *London Post*, in 1699. He died in 1720.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="knotted cords and the Pueblo Revolt, 1680" -->
In 1680 the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico planned to drive out the Spanish. Spanish soldiers and settlers had come in 1598 under Juan de Oñate and had taken Pueblo land, labour and food. In 1675 the Spanish governor Juan Francisco Treviño ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo religious leaders. Four were sentenced to hang. Three were hanged, and one killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the others in public and put them in prison. One of the men released was Po'pay, a Tewa leader from Ohkay Owingeh.
Po'pay planned the revolt with towns that spoke at least five different languages. To make them all rise on the same day, he sent runners with knotted cords. Each town untied one knot a day. When the last knot was untied, the revolt would begin. Sources differ on the day planned: 11 August or 13 August.
The Spanish learned of the plan when they caught two of the runners on 9 August. The revolt began a day early, on 10 August 1680. Pueblo fighters killed about 400 people, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in New Mexico. On 21 August Governor Antonio de Otermín led about 2,000 Spanish settlers out of Santa Fe, south to El Paso. The Spanish did not return until 1692.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="catua-and-omtua" name="Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua
> **Who:** Two young runners from Tesuque Pueblo who carried the knotted cords of the Pueblo Revolt · **When and where:** New Mexico, 8 and 9 August 1680

On the morning of 8 August 1680, Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua set out from Tesuque Pueblo with knotted cords for the Tano towns to the south. Tesuque is Tewa for "village of the narrow place of the cottonwood trees." The first town they reached was Pecos.

After they left Pecos, Christian Pueblo people told the Franciscan missionary Fernando de Velasco that two Tewa messengers had visited the war chief's house. Two Pueblo leaders from the Galisteo Basin who sided with the Spanish also sent word of the revolt to Governor Antonio de Otermín. On 9 August Otermín ordered the messengers arrested. Spanish officials tortured Catua and Omtua to make them explain the knotted cord, and then hanged them. The surviving accounts do not name the men who tortured and hanged them.

When Po'pay heard that the runners had been caught, he ordered the revolt to start a day early. It began on 10 August.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1704 and 1750, weekly newspapers started in Boston, Philadelphia, New York, Charleston and Williamsburg. Most were run by printers, and several printers were also postmasters. Colonial governors and assemblies still jailed printers who offended them. The papers made money from advertisements, and many of the advertisements offered enslaved people for sale or offered rewards for people who had escaped from slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Boston News-Letter, 1704" -->
On 24 April 1704 John Campbell, the postmaster of Boston, printed the first issue of the *Boston News-Letter*. It was one small sheet printed on both sides, once a week. The words "Published by Authority" appeared in its title from the first issue until 1720, which told readers that Campbell printed it with the government's approval. Most of the first issue was copied from London newspapers of mid-December, more than four months old. Local news filled one column.
The *News-Letter* came out every week until February 1776, the first newspaper in the colonies to keep going. It had no rival in Boston until the *Boston Gazette* began on 21 December 1719. The *American Weekly Mercury* started in Philadelphia the next day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Courant: a paper the government did not approve, 1721 to 1723" -->
James Franklin started the *New-England Courant* in Boston in August 1721, during a smallpox epidemic. The minister Cotton Mather and the doctor Zabdiel Boylston urged inoculation. In inoculation, a doctor put a little pus from a sick person into a cut in the skin of a healthy person, to give a milder case. Mather had learned of the method from Onesimus, an African man he held in slavery, and from other enslaved Africans. Almost all Boston doctors except Boylston opposed it. The *Courant* printed attacks on the people who supported it. On 14 November 1721 someone threw a homemade bomb through a window of Mather's house, with a note that read "Cotton Mather, You Dog, Dam You: I'll inoculate you with this, with a Pox to you." The thrower was never identified. About 6,000 people in Boston caught smallpox that year, and 844 died. Of the 248 people Boylston inoculated, 98 out of 100 survived. Among people who caught smallpox the ordinary way, 85 out of 100 survived.
In June 1722 the *Courant* printed a short item hinting that the Massachusetts government was slow to send a ship after pirates. The General Court, the colony's assembly, had James Franklin jailed for a month. His younger brother Benjamin, then 16, ran the paper while he was locked up. James was let out on 2 July 1722. Early in 1723 the General Court forbade James Franklin to print the *Courant* unless officials checked it first. The paper then came out under Benjamin's name instead, until it closed in 1726.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Weekly Journal and the Zenger trial, 1733 to 1736" -->
In New York the only newspaper, the *New-York Gazette*, printed Governor William Cosby's side. In 1733 Cosby removed the chief judge, Lewis Morris, after Morris ruled against him. Morris and his friends started the *New-York Weekly Journal* on 5 November 1733 to attack the governor, with the German-born printer John Peter Zenger as its printer. Cosby had copies of the paper burned in public by the hangman.
Officials arrested Zenger on 17 November 1734 for seditious libel. Seditious libel then meant printing anything that lowered people's opinion of the government, whether or not it was true. He could not pay his bail of £400 and stayed in jail for more than eight months. His wife Anna kept the *Journal* coming out. On 4 August 1735 a jury found him not guilty. The verdict changed no law, but in 1736 Zenger printed an account of the trial, *A Brief Narrative of the Case and Tryal of John Peter Zenger*, and printers reprinted it many times.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" name="Anna Catharina Zenger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anna Catharina Zenger
> **Who:** The printer's wife who kept the *New-York Weekly Journal* coming out while her husband was in jail, and ran the print shop after he died · **When and where:** New York City, 1734 to 1748

Anna Catharina Maulin married John Peter Zenger on 11 September 1722. When officials jailed him on 17 November 1734, she took charge of the shop and kept the *Journal* coming out every week. Zenger later wrote that he gave her his instructions "through the hole of the door of the prison."

After the trial Zenger became New York's official printer in 1737. He died on 28 July 1746. Anna Zenger ran the family print shop for more than two years, until their eldest son, John, took over in December 1748.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="printers who were postmasters, and papers in German" -->
Many printers were also postmasters, because the post office brought them letters and news from other towns first. A postmaster could also refuse to let the post riders carry a rival's newspaper. Andrew Bradford, printer of the *American Weekly Mercury*, did that to Benjamin Franklin's paper while Bradford kept Philadelphia's post office. Franklin wrote that he got his papers carried only by bribing the riders to take them in secret. In 1737 Alexander Spotswood, the deputy postmaster general for the colonies, took the post office away from Bradford and gave it to Franklin, who was 31.
Franklin also set up partners as printers in other towns. In November 1733 he set up Lewis Timothy as printer of the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston. Virginia got its first newspaper, the *Virginia Gazette*, on 6 August 1736, printed by William Parks.
Franklin tried a German-language paper, the *Philadelphische Zeitung*, in May and June 1732, and it failed. On 20 August 1739 Christopher Sauer, a German printer in Germantown, started a German-language paper. His family printed it, under changing names, until 1777. Sauer took his news of Europe from sixteen European newspapers that reached him each month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="elizabeth-timothy" name="Elizabeth Timothy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Elizabeth Timothy
> **Who:** A Dutch-born widow who ran the *South-Carolina Gazette* and became the first woman in the colonies to own and publish a newspaper · **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, 1738 to 1746

Elizabeth Timothy's husband, Lewis Timothy, printed the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston under a six-year partnership with Benjamin Franklin. He died in December 1738 after what the paper called "an unhappy Accident." He left her with six small children and a seventh about to be born.

The partnership said that their eldest son, Peter, would carry on the business. Peter was too young, so she ran the shop herself. She printed the next issue on 4 January 1739 and promised to make the paper "as entertaining and correct as may be reasonably expected." By the end of 1739 she had bought out Franklin's share. The *South Carolina Encyclopedia* calls her the first woman in the American colonies to own and publish a newspaper. Franklin praised how exactly she kept her accounts.

She handed the shop to Peter when he came of age in 1746 and opened a bookstore next to it. She died in 1757. Her will left her children eight enslaved people, along with her other property.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-timothy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="advertisements for people as property" -->
Newspaper owners made much of their money from advertisements. Franklin's *Pennsylvania Gazette* printed advertisements from slave owners and slave traders. Some offered enslaved men, women and children for sale. Others offered rewards for people who had escaped. By the 1750s these made up nearly a quarter of the paper's advertisements. Franklin was paid for printing them.
Franklin's own household held enslaved people from about 1735 until 1790. Records show he bought at least seven people: Joseph, Jemima, Peter, King, Othello, George and Bob.
In 1740 lawmakers in South Carolina made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to write.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-news-communication" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** A Boston printer's apprentice who became Philadelphia's leading newspaper owner and its postmaster · **When and where:** Boston, 1718 to 1723, and Philadelphia, 1723 to 1753
> **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns about his life

Benjamin Franklin was born in Boston in 1706. In 1718 he was apprenticed to his brother James, a printer. He carried copies of the *New-England Courant* through the streets to the people who paid for it. In 1722, at 16, he wrote letters to the paper under the name "Silence Dogood." He later wrote that his brother "had often beaten me." When James was jailed in 1722, Benjamin ran the paper. In 1723, at 17, he broke his apprenticeship and ran away to Philadelphia.

On 2 October 1729 he bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from the printer Samuel Keimer. Advertising became its main source of money, and he grew it from two pages to four partly to hold more advertisements. In 1732 he began *Poor Richard's Almanack*, which he wrote under the name Richard Saunders. He wrote that he sold "near ten thousand" copies a year, and that people "bought scarcely any other books."

In 1737 he became postmaster of Philadelphia. He wrote that the post office "facilitated the correspondence that improv'd my newspaper" and brought more readers and more advertisements. In 1753 the Crown made him one of two deputy postmasters general for all the colonies.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1765 to 1800, printers, letter writers and post riders spread the arguments and the news that led to the American Revolution. Supporters of independence wrecked the press of a New York printer who took Britain's side. In 1791 the new country wrote freedom of the press into the First Amendment. In 1798 the Federalist majority in Congress passed the Sedition Act, and federal officials jailed newspaper editors who criticized President John Adams.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Stamp Act and the printers, 1765" -->
Parliament in London passed the Stamp Act in March 1765, and it took effect on 1 November 1765. It taxed newspapers, pamphlets, almanacs and legal papers in the colonies. Each newspaper printed on half a sheet of paper owed a halfpenny, and a paper on a whole sheet owed a penny. Every advertisement in a newspaper owed two shillings. The tax had to be paid on stamped paper made in London.
On 31 October 1765, the day before the tax began, William Bradford of Philadelphia printed his *Pennsylvania Journal* with black borders, like a tombstone. In place of the official stamp he printed a skull and crossbones. His front page said the paper was "EXPIRING: In Hopes of a Resurrection to LIFE again." Bradford never actually stopped. He printed the next issue on 7 November. John Hughes, the man appointed to hand out the stamps in Philadelphia, had promised not to enforce the act after opponents of the tax threatened him with violence and with destroying his property. Parliament repealed the tax in 1766.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="committees of correspondence, 1772 to 1774" -->
On 2 November 1772 Boston's town leaders voted to set up a Committee of Correspondence of 21 men, with Samuel Adams among them. The committee wrote a report on the colonists' rights and on how Parliament had broken them. Printed as the Boston Pamphlet, it went to every town in Massachusetts. On 12 March 1773 Virginia's House of Burgesses, the colony's elected assembly, set up an 11-man committee, including Patrick Henry and Thomas Jefferson, to write to the other colonies' assemblies. The Virginia committee asked the other assemblies to share news of laws from London and of what each colony planned to do about them. Within a year, eleven colonial assemblies had their own committees. Only North Carolina and Pennsylvania did not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the news of Lexington, April 1775" -->
On the morning of 19 April 1775 British soldiers fired on the militia at Lexington, Massachusetts. At about 10 o'clock that morning, in Watertown, Joseph Palmer of the Massachusetts Committee of Safety wrote a letter "To all the Friends of American liberty." It said the soldiers had killed six men and wounded four. He gave it to a rider, Isaac Bissell, to carry toward Connecticut. At each town, the local committee copied the letter, added a line saying the copy was true, and sent riders on with copies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-bissell" name="Isaac Bissell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Bissell
> **Who:** A 26-year-old blacksmith from Suffield, Connecticut, who carried a written report of the Lexington shooting from Massachusetts to Connecticut · **When and where:** Watertown, Massachusetts, to Connecticut, 19 and 20 April 1775

Isaac Bissell was a blacksmith from Suffield, a town on Connecticut's northern border. On 19 April 1775 he was in Watertown, Massachusetts. At about 10 in the morning Joseph Palmer handed him the letter about the killings at Lexington. The letter said that "the Bearer Mr. Isaac Bissell" was "charged to alarm the country quite to Connecticutt," and it asked people along the way to give him fresh horses.

Bissell reached Worcester in the early afternoon. The town clerk, Nathan Baldwin, copied the letter and signed the copy. At Springfield another copy was made. On the morning of 20 April the letter reached Connecticut's leaders. In Wethersfield, the politician Silas Deane wrote in his journal that the news "was discredited, from the suddeness of its arrival." Governor Jonathan Trumbull believed it and called Connecticut's assembly together to raise soldiers.

Bissell then marched to the siege of Boston as a private in the Suffield militia. In July 1775 the Massachusetts committee approved his bill as a post rider for two pounds and one shilling, but he had trouble getting paid.

Many books call the rider "Israel Bissell" and say he rode all the way to Philadelphia. The historian J. L. Bell found that his name was Isaac and that other riders carried copies of the letter beyond Connecticut.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-bissell" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Common Sense and the Declaration in print, 1776" -->
On 10 January 1776 the Philadelphia printer Robert Bell published *Common Sense*, a pamphlet by Thomas Paine that argued for independence from Britain. Twenty-five printings are known, sixteen of them in Philadelphia. Paine claimed that 120,000 copies sold in the first three months, and later claimed 150,000. A biographer in 1892 guessed half a million. No sales records survive. The historian Trish Loughran counted the known printings and put the highest possible number at 75,000, and she thinks the real number was much lower.
Congress approved the Declaration of Independence on 4 July 1776. That night the Philadelphia printer John Dunlap printed about 200 copies, called broadsides, to send to the states and to the army. On 9 July George Washington's soldiers in New York City heard it read aloud. Twenty-six of Dunlap's copies are known to survive.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-paine" name="Thomas Paine" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Paine
> **Who:** An English-born writer whose pamphlet *Common Sense* argued that the colonies should declare independence · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1774 to 1776, and with the army, 1776

Thomas Paine was born in Thetford, England, on 29 January 1737. He met Benjamin Franklin in London, and Franklin gave him letters of introduction. He arrived in Philadelphia on 30 November 1774. He helped edit the *Pennsylvania Magazine* and wrote articles under false names, including one called "African Slavery in America" that attacked the slave trade.

*Common Sense* came out on 10 January 1776 without his name on it. Paine was paid nothing for it. He fell out with its first printer, Robert Bell, and other printers printed it without asking him. Paine later wrote that he gave the right to print it to every state.

On 19 December 1776, when Washington's army was close to breaking apart, Paine published *The American Crisis*. It began, "These are the times that try men's souls." Washington ordered it read to his troops. Paine died in New York City on 8 June 1809.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-paine" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a Loyalist printer's press wrecked, 1775" -->
Supporters of independence also attacked printers who took Britain's side. James Rivington printed the *New-York Gazetteer*, one of the most widely read newspapers in the colonies, which took the British government's side. In November 1775 Isaac Sears led a band of armed Sons of Liberty from New Haven, Connecticut, to Rivington's shop in New York City. They wrecked his press and his type, the metal letters a printer sets by hand. Rivington left for England a few weeks later. After the British army took New York in 1776, he came back with the title of King's Printer and printed his paper again on the British side until the British left in November 1783.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a postal service for the new country, 1775 to 1792" -->
In 1774 British officials dismissed Benjamin Franklin as deputy postmaster general for being, in the Postal Service history's words, "too much of an American." The printer William Goddard set up a rival mail service, which he called the Constitutional Post. He warned that the British post let officials open letters and read them as proof of treason. On 26 July 1775 the Continental Congress made Franklin its postmaster general. Goddard's network of post offices became the base of the new American postal system.
The Constitution, approved in 1788, gave Congress the power "To establish Post Offices and post Roads." In 1789 the country had 75 post offices and about 2,400 miles of post roads for almost four million people. The Post Office Act of 20 February 1792 let newspapers travel by mail at very low rates and made it a crime for postal officials to open letters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Mary Katharine Goddard
> **Who:** A printer and newspaper publisher who ran Baltimore's post office for 14 years and printed the first copy of the Declaration that listed the signers · **When and where:** Baltimore, Maryland, 1774 to 1789

Mary Katharine Goddard was born on 16 June 1738 in Connecticut, in a family of printers and postmasters. She learned printing in the 1760s in her brother William's shop in Providence, Rhode Island. In 1774 she moved to Baltimore to run his print shop and his newspaper, the *Maryland Journal*, while he travelled to promote his Constitutional Post. She reprinted *Common Sense* in the *Journal*.

In 1775 she became postmaster of Baltimore, the first woman known to be a postmaster under the new American government. Postmasters then earned a share of the postage, not a salary, and prices rose fast during the war. When Congress did not pay her, she paid the post riders and the rent herself. In 1778 she let readers pay for the paper in beeswax, flour, lard, butter, beef or pork.

In January 1777 the Continental Congress, then meeting in Baltimore, ordered new copies of the Declaration of Independence. Goddard printed them on 18 January 1777. They were the first printed copies to show the signers' names. At the bottom she printed "Baltimore, in Maryland: Printed by Mary Katharine Goddard." Nine are known to survive.

In January 1784 her brother pushed her out of the *Maryland Journal*. In October 1789 the new postmaster general, Samuel Osgood, replaced her with a man. His assistant, John Burrell, said the job might need "more travelling ... than a woman could undertake." More than 200 people in Baltimore signed a letter asking that she keep the job. She wrote to President George Washington and to the Senate. Neither helped her. She ran a bookshop in Baltimore until about 1810 and died there on 12 August 1816.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-katharine-goddard" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the First Amendment and the party papers, 1789 to 1793" -->
The First Amendment took effect on 15 December 1791. It says that "Congress shall make no law ... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press."
Newspapers took sides at once. From 1789 John Fenno's *Gazette of the United States* supported Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson and Congressman James Madison wanted a paper on their side. Jefferson gave the poet Philip Freneau a part-time State Department job as a translating clerk, paid $250 a year, and Freneau started the *National Gazette* on 31 October 1791. It attacked President George Washington's government and had about 1,700 subscribers before Freneau closed it on 26 October 1793. Benjamin Franklin's grandson, Benjamin Franklin Bache, attacked Washington and later John Adams in his Philadelphia paper, the *Aurora*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Sedition Act, 1798 to 1801" -->
In 1798 the United States was close to war with France. President John Adams's party, the Federalists, held a majority in Congress. The Federalist majority passed the Sedition Act, and Adams signed it on 14 July 1798. It made it a crime to write or print "any false, scandalous and malicious writing" against the federal government, Congress or the President. The punishment was a fine of up to $2,000 and up to two years in prison. Unlike the old law in the Zenger trial, the act let a defendant prove that his words were true, and it let the jury decide the law as well as the facts.
Federal judges and prosecutors used it mostly against editors of newspapers that supported Jefferson's party, the Democratic-Republicans. Historians have long counted 17 indictments and 10 convictions. The legal historian Wendell Bird found 11 more prosecutions of 16 more people. Among those charged were Congressman Matthew Lyon of Vermont, William Duane of the *Aurora*, Ann Greenleaf of New York's *Argus*, and Anthony Haswell of the *Vermont Gazette*. Luther Baldwin and Brown Clark of Newark, New Jersey, were charged for words against President Adams spoken in a tavern.
Federalist supporters assaulted Benjamin Franklin Bache in Philadelphia, and he was barred from attending the House of Representatives. Officials arrested him in 1798 for libelling President Adams. He kept printing the *Aurora* through a yellow fever epidemic and died of the fever on 10 September 1798, at 29, a month before his trial date. His widow kept the paper going, with William Duane as editor. The act ended on 3 March 1801.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-lyon" name="Matthew Lyon" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Lyon
> **Who:** An Irish-born congressman and newspaper owner, the first person tried under the Sedition Act · **When and where:** Vermont, 1798 to 1799

Matthew Lyon came to America from Ireland, fought in the Revolutionary War, and in 1797 went to Congress for Vermont. He owned a newspaper. In a letter that a newspaper printed, he wrote of seeing the public good "swallowed up in a continual grasp for power, in an unbounded thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, or selfish avarice." He also read aloud at campaign rallies a letter by the poet Joel Barlow that said Adams should be sent to a "madhouse."

On 5 October 1798 a federal grand jury in Vermont charged him on three counts under the Sedition Act. A deputy marshal arrested him the next day. He was the first person tried under the act. At his trial in Rutland on 9 October he defended himself and argued that the act broke the Constitution. Justice William Paterson of the Supreme Court told the jury that its job had "nothing whatever to do with the constitutionality or unconstitutionality of the sedition law." The jury, drawn from towns that supported Adams's party, found him guilty within an hour. Paterson sentenced him to four months in jail and a $1,000 fine.

He was held in the jail at Vergennes. He ran for Congress again from his cell, and the voters of his district re-elected him. He was let out on 9 February 1799.
