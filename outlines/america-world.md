## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There is no United States, so there is no American foreign policy. There will not be one for nearly three hundred years.

What there is instead is diplomacy between Native nations: councils, alliances, agreed ways of meeting, and gifts given as part of an agreement. That story belongs to the Native Nations chapter, and this book tells it there. No Native diplomat from before the Europeans arrived is known to us by name, so this chapter names none.

Far out in the Pacific, Polynesian voyagers sailed to the islands that are now Hawaii. Archaeologists do not agree on when. The older view put the first arrivals somewhere between about AD 300 and 750. Newer radiocarbon dating points to about AD 1000 to 1200, and some studies to about 1200 to 1290. The argument is still going on.

Six years before this era ends, on 7 June 1494, two European kingdoms signed a treaty in the Spanish town of Tordesillas. Spain and Portugal drew a line down the Atlantic Ocean, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, and agreed that everything east of it was Portugal's to claim and everything west was Spain's. Nobody who lived in the Americas was there, was asked, or was told.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Still no United States. This land is the edge of other people's empires, and the decisions about it are made in Madrid, Lisbon, Paris and London by men who have never seen it.

When those empires quarrelled, the quarrel could be settled here. France had built a colony called Fort Caroline in Florida, inside land Spain claimed. The fort stood in the homeland of Timucua-speaking peoples, and neither France nor Spain asked them. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, a Spanish commander, arrived in August 1565 to destroy it. On 20 September 1565 Menéndez marched about 500 men overland and took the fort while the French commander Jean Ribault was away at sea. Menéndez wrote to the king that his men cut the throats of 132 men at the fort and killed 10 more caught in the woods the next day. The National Park Service says most of the men at the fort were killed. Other accounts give other numbers.

Then the Frenchmen whose ships a storm had wrecked came ashore south of St. Augustine. In mid-September they surrendered to Menéndez at an inlet. He wrote that he had their hands tied behind their backs and had them stabbed to death, because they were Protestants. His men killed 111 of them and spared sixteen: twelve sailors and four carpenters and caulkers he needed. On 12 October his men killed 134 more, including Jean Ribault, who had surrendered first. The inlet is still called Matanzas, which is Spanish for slaughters.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Still no United States. The colonies on this coast are possessions. England, the Dutch Republic, France and Spain own them, and those governments decide the colonies' borders, their wars, and who they are allowed to trade with. No colony has an ambassador. No colony signs a treaty in its own name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade rules written 3,000 miles away" -->
Parliament in London decided what colonists could buy and sell.

- **1651.** The Navigation Act, aimed at the Dutch, required all trade between England and the colonies to travel in English or colonial ships. It helped start a war with the Dutch the next year.
- **1660.** A second act listed goods that could be shipped only to England or another English possession: sugar, tobacco, cotton, wool, indigo and ginger.
- **1663.** The Staple Act required European goods bound for the colonies to be landed in England first, checked by customs officers, and shipped on from there.

A merchant in Boston could not legally buy a Dutch rope or sell his tobacco to a French buyer. The men who made that rule sat in a building he would never see.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York is traded for a nutmeg island" -->
In 1664 an English fleet arrived in the harbour of New Amsterdam and the Dutch surrendered New Netherland without a fight. Sources give different days for the surrender document, because England and the Dutch Republic were using different calendars.

On 21 July 1667 the Treaty of Breda ended the war. The Dutch had done better in the fighting, but at Breda they let England keep New Netherland and took instead Suriname, with its sugar plantations, and Run, a small island in what is now Indonesia where nutmeg grew. The Dutch took the colony back briefly, and the Treaty of Westminster in 1674 returned it to England for good. It was New York by then.

Two European governments swapped a town back and forth by treaty. The people living in it were not asked.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Still no United States. A colonist in this era was a subject of Britain, or France, or Spain. When his empire went to war he was at war, and when his empire made peace he had no say in the terms. The strongest diplomacy on this continent in these years was not European at all. It was Native.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The summer of 1701: two treaties, two empires" -->
The Haudenosaunee, the Five Nations of what is now upstate New York, dealt with both European empires within three weeks.

- **19 July 1701, Albany.** Haudenosaunee representatives met John Nanfan, the acting English governor of New York, and made a deed to King William covering hunting lands running west from present-day New York toward present-day Chicago. What that deed actually promised is still argued over today, including by Six Nations people themselves.
- **4 August 1701, Montréal.** Louis-Hector de Callière, governor of New France, and about 1,300 representatives of 39 Indigenous nations signed the Great Peace of Montréal. It ended decades of war and was meant to keep the Five Nations neutral if France and the English colonies fought each other.

They made an arrangement with the English and a peace with the French in the same summer, and stayed out of the empires' next war.

*Shared with:* `native-nations` (**lead**: the confederacy's own history).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kondiaronk-america-world" name="Kondiaronk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Kondiaronk
> **Who:** A Huron (Wendat) chief, born about 1649, and one of the chief negotiators of the Great Peace of Montréal. The French called him "the Rat." · **When and where:** Montréal, August 1701

An epidemic was moving through Montréal while the council met, and Kondiaronk had a fever. On 1 August 1701 he stood up and made a speech that moved the gathered leaders toward agreement. He was carried to the Hôtel-Dieu hospital and died within hours. He was given a large funeral at Notre-Dame on 3 August 1701, attended by the governor, French officers, war chiefs and clergy, and was buried there. The Great Peace was signed the next day, 4 August 1701.

*Source:* Pointe-à-Callière museum, Montréal. Note: sources give the death as 1 or 2 August 1701.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kondiaronk-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Treaty of Utrecht, 1713" -->
On 11 April 1713 a treaty signed at Utrecht in the Netherlands ended the war the colonists called Queen Anne's War. France gave Britain the whole drainage basin of Hudson Bay, its claims to Newfoundland, and Acadia, whose capital New England troops had taken in 1710.

Britain also won the *asiento*. That was a contract, handed over as a prize of peace, giving Britain the sole right for thirty years to carry kidnapped African people across the ocean and sell them in Spain's American colonies. A peace treaty between kings counted the right to sell human beings among the winnings.

*Shared with:* `slavery-freedom` (**lead**: the trade itself) · `war` (the fighting).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Louisbourg, taken in 1745 and given back in 1748" -->
On 4 April 1745 almost 3,000 New England troops under Colonel William Pepperrell sailed from Boston. A Royal Navy squadron under Commodore Peter Warren joined them. After a siege of 49 days, the French fortress of Louisbourg surrendered on 17 June 1745.

About 130 New England men died during the siege. At least 561 more died of disease in the damaged fortress that winter, and their commander Pepperrell counted about 1,200 men lost in all. In 1748 British and French diplomats signed the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. Britain gave Louisbourg back to France in exchange for Madras in India, which France had taken from Britain. Nobody asked the New Englanders.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This is where the chapter begins. A group of British colonies declared themselves a country, and a country needs other countries to agree that it exists. So the Revolution was a diplomatic project as much as a military one. The new United States begged for help, got it from France, won its independence at a table in Paris, sent its first ministers to courts that had recently wanted them hanged, discovered it was too poor and too weak to protect its own sailors, and then spent the 1790s arguing about how to stay out of Europe's next war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A congress writes secret letters, 1775-1789" -->
- **29 November 1775.** The Second Continental Congress created the Committee of Secret Correspondence to open and protect secret contact with possible foreign friends. Its first members were Benjamin Franklin, Benjamin Harrison, Thomas Johnson, John Dickinson and John Jay. This is the ancestor of the State Department.
- **17 April 1777.** Congress renamed it the Committee for Foreign Affairs.
- **10 August 1781.** Congress chose Robert R. Livingston of New York as the first Secretary for Foreign Affairs. Sources differ on whether the department itself was set up on 10 January 1780 or 10 January 1781.
- **1789.** The new federal government re-created the department by an act of 27 July, then renamed it the Department of State on 15 September, because it had been given some record-keeping work at home as well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Morocco says yes first, 1777 and 1786" -->
On 20 December 1777, Sultan Mohammed III of Morocco declared that ships flying the American flag could enter Moroccan ports freely. He is usually described as the first head of state to recognise that the United States existed, about six weeks before France signed its treaties. His declaration was part of a wider policy of trading peacefully with Christian countries, and it named others too, so it was not aimed at the Americans alone.

Nine years later came the treaty. Thomas Barclay reached Marrakesh on 19 June 1786. Moroccan officials approved the agreement on 23 June and Barclay signed on 28 June. Thomas Jefferson signed at Paris on 1 January 1787 and John Adams at London on 25 January 1787, and Congress ratified it on 18 July 1787. It was the first American treaty with an African or Muslim country, and it is the longest unbroken treaty relationship the United States has ever had.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The French alliance, 6 February 1778" -->
France had been quietly sending money and gunpowder before it would sign anything. What it wanted first was proof the Americans could win.

Franklin's ship reached Auray in Brittany on 3 or 4 December 1776, and he was in Paris by 21 December. He, Silas Deane and Arthur Lee were the American commissioners. The French foreign minister was the Comte de Vergennes, and the State Department's own history says Vergennes decided for an alliance when the news of the British surrender at Saratoga reached him in December 1777.

On 6 February 1778 two treaties were signed. The Treaty of Amity and Commerce formally recognised American independence. The Treaty of Alliance bound each country not to make a separate peace with Britain, and a secret clause left room for other powers to join.

Spain went to war against Britain on 21 June 1779, but as France's ally. Spain did not sign a treaty with the United States and did not recognise American independence during the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Franklin (2024)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** The United States' first great diplomat, and its representative in France for eight and a half years. · **When and where:** Paris, December 1776 to May 1785 · **Movie:** *Franklin* (2024), an eight-part Apple TV+ drama starring Michael Douglas, about this exact mission. It is a drama, not a documentary.

Franklin was seventy when he landed in Brittany. In Paris he left off his wig and wore a marten-fur cap and plain clothes, and many French people took him for a natural man from the forests. Augustin de Saint-Aubin engraved his portrait in the cap in 1777, and the print is in the Philadelphia Museum of Art. Franklin chose the costume on purpose. It made him famous in Paris, and the fame helped him get meetings and money.

He was appointed Minister Plenipotentiary to France on 14 September 1778 and presented his credentials on 23 March 1779. He was one of the three Americans who negotiated the peace with Britain. Congress also made him minister to Sweden on 28 September 1782. He never went there. He negotiated the Swedish treaty from Paris instead and signed it on 3 April 1783. His mission ended on 17 May 1785.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Treaty of Paris, 1783" -->
Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and John Jay negotiated for the United States. Preliminary articles were signed on 30 November 1782, France signed its own on 20 January 1783, and the formal Treaty of Paris was signed on 3 September 1783.

Britain accepted that the United States was independent and agreed to a western boundary at the Mississippi River. The treaty settled fishing rights off Newfoundland, promised that pre-war debts to British creditors would be paid, promised compensation for Loyalists whose property had been taken, and required British troops to leave the thirteen states. Franklin asked Britain to hand over Canada as well. Britain said no.

The Confederation Congress, meeting at Annapolis, Maryland, ratified the treaty on 14 January 1784. Britain ratified on 9 April, and the two signed copies were exchanged in Paris on 12 May 1784.

The land inside that western boundary was the home of Native nations, among them the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee in what is now New York and Pennsylvania. No Native nation was invited to Paris, none signed, and they had not been beaten. In 1763 the British king had reserved much of that land for Native nations, and now British diplomats signed it over to the United States. At Fort Stanwix in 1784, American negotiators backed by soldiers treated the Six Nations as a conquered people and took large parts of their land.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-adams-america-world" name="John Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Adams
> **Who:** The first United States minister to Great Britain. · **When and where:** London, presented to King George III on 1 June 1785

Nine years before, this king could have had Adams hanged as a traitor. On 1 June 1785 Adams walked into his palace as the representative of a country the king had just been forced to recognise. Adams bought a new coat for it and worked on what he would say. Two days later he wrote to Thomas Jefferson that George III had received him with more kindness and respect than he had expected. The Library of Congress holds that letter, dated 3 June 1785.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Barbary states: Americans enslaved in Algiers, 1785-1796" -->
Once American ships sailed without the Royal Navy behind them, they were on their own.

In 1785 the Dey of Algiers declared war on the United States and took American ships. On 25 July 1785 the schooner *Maria* of Boston was captured about three miles off Cape St. Vincent, Portugal, and brought into Algiers on 4 August. Its crew was enslaved. Other American crews followed.

The United States had almost no navy and very little money, and could neither fight for its sailors nor afford to buy them back. So they stayed. Congress argued about the cost for years while the men worked in Algiers.

In 1795 President Washington's government sent Joel Barlow, Joseph Donaldson and Richard O'Brien to North Africa, and they made treaties with Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli. The treaty with Algiers was signed on 5 September 1795, and the American ratification is dated 7 March 1796. Sources report its cost differently. The State Department says it freed 83 American sailors and gives no dollar figure. The Treasury's own estimate, printed with the treaty text, was about $992,463. That was a first payment of about $585,000 plus more paid later, because the Dey demanded extra when the money arrived late. Other accounts give $642,500 at once plus $21,600 a year in ships' supplies. The counts of captives differ too, from 83 freed to 115 held in 1793.

The men were released on 8 July 1796. Six of them had died shortly before the release, and others were permanently injured.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-leander-cathcart" name="James Leander Cathcart" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Leander Cathcart
> **Who:** An Irish-born American sailor, enslaved in Algiers for eleven years, who became the Dey's chief Christian clerk and helped put the 1795 treaty into English · **When and where:** Born June 1, 1767, in County Westmeath, Ireland. Captured July 25, 1785, off Cape St. Vincent, Portugal. Held in Algiers until May 1796.

Cathcart was 18 and one of six crewmen on the Boston schooner *Maria* when an Algerian warship took her on July 25, 1785. The ship reached Algiers on August 4. On August 12 the *Dauphin* of Philadelphia arrived with 15 more American captives. Dey Muhammad's officials put some of the 21 to work in his palace and garden and sent others to hard labor at the harbor.

About four months in, four men held Cathcart down on orders from the Dey's chamberlains and beat the soles of his feet with sticks. This punishment was called the bastinado. He took 28 blows and lost four toenails. He wrote that old men of sixty and boys of ten got the same.

He moved up from coffee server to clerk of the harbor, then clerk of a slave prison, and at last chief Christian clerk to the Dey. From that post he helped the American negotiator Joseph Donaldson and translated the 1795 treaty's Turkish text into English. By his own list, 9 of the 21 men taken in 1785 died in Algiers, most of plague. The other 12 got home at different times.

Cathcart left Algiers on May 8, 1796, carrying the treaty papers. He later served as the American consul in Tunis and Tripoli and then at Cádiz in Spain. His daughter published his account, *The Captives*, in 1899.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-leander-cathcart" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Staying out of Europe's war, 1793-1796" -->
France and Britain went to war again in 1793, and Americans disagreed furiously about which side to take, or whether to take one.

- **8 April 1793.** Edmond-Charles Genêt, France's new minister, landed at Charleston, South Carolina. He hired American privateers to attack British shipping and, when Washington would not do as he wanted, threatened to go over the President's head to the American people. Washington asked France to take him back.
- **22 April 1793.** Washington issued the Proclamation of Neutrality: the United States would stay out.
- **19 November 1794.** John Jay signed a treaty with Britain. The Senate approved it on 24 June 1795 by 20 votes to 10. Britain agreed to leave the northwestern forts it had promised to leave in 1783 and gave the United States most-favoured-nation trading status, with heavy limits on trade with the British West Indies. The border with Canada, the old debts and Britain's seizures of American ships were all put off to arbitration, and Britain kept the right to take American cargo bound for France and to take French goods off American ships without paying. The State Department's own summary says the treaty "addressed few U.S. interests, and ultimately granted Britain additional rights." It was hated at home. Washington signed it anyway, because the country was in no condition to fight Britain.
- **27 October 1795.** Thomas Pinckney signed a treaty with Spain, negotiated with Manuel de Godoy, and the Senate approved it on 7 March 1796. It fixed the boundary with Spanish West Florida at the 31st parallel and gave Americans free use of the Mississippi River and tax-free use of the port of New Orleans. A farmer in Kentucky could not haul his corn back over the mountains to sell it. He floated it down the river to New Orleans, a Spanish port. This treaty let him sell it there.
- **19 September 1796.** Washington's Farewell Address was published in the *American Daily Advertiser* in Philadelphia. He had written it with Alexander Hamilton's help. He said he would not run again, and argued against permanent attachments to any foreign country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The XYZ Affair and an undeclared war, 1798-1800" -->
President John Adams sent Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and John Marshall to France to settle the quarrel. French go-betweens told them that before talks could start there would have to be a large bribe for the foreign minister, Talleyrand. Adams published the dispatches with the go-betweens' names replaced by the letters W, X, Y and Z. They were Nicholas Hubbard, Jean Hottinguer, Pierre Bellamy and Lucien Hauteval.

Americans were furious, and from 1798 to 1800 the United States Navy fought the French at sea in the Caribbean in a war nobody ever declared. The Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine, ended it. The Senate did not ratify it until 18 December 1801.

The country had now been to the edge of war with both of Europe's great powers within five years of making peace with one of them.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850 the United States bought, bargained, and fought its way from the Mississippi to the Pacific. Every piece of that land already had people living on it, and none of them were asked. In the same fifty years the country fought its first war on the far side of an ocean, and told the empires of Europe to keep out of an entire hemisphere.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
On April 30, 1803, American envoys signed a treaty with France to buy 828,000 square miles west of the Mississippi for $15 million, which roughly doubled the size of the country (National Archives, Milestone Documents). Napoleon, who ruled France, sold it to raise money for his coming war with Britain and to keep Britain from taking it. He had also lost the army he sent to retake Saint-Domingue, the colony now called Haiti, from the enslaved people who had risen against French rule. The American envoys had been sent to Paris to buy the port of New Orleans. French officials offered them all of Louisiana instead.

Article III of the treaty promised that the people living in the territory would be "incorporated in the Union of the United States" and admitted to all the rights of United States citizens. That promise came attached to land the country bought in 1803. A hundred years later the Supreme Court ruled that no such promise applied to Puerto Rico, Guam, or the Philippines. The Native nations who lived across those 828,000 square miles were not party to the sale, were not consulted about it, and were not included in the promise.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tribute, and the first war fought overseas" -->
American presidents had been paying tribute (protection money) to the rulers of Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Morocco so that their ships would not seize American ships and hold American sailors for ransom. When American officials would not raise the payments, the pasha of Tripoli declared war on the United States on May 14, 1801 (Britannica, State Department Office of the Historian). The fighting ended with a treaty signed June 10, 1805. It ended the yearly tribute, but the United States paid a ransom for the captured crew of the frigate *Philadelphia*. A second Barbary war followed in 1815.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Monroe Doctrine, 1823" -->
On December 2, 1823, in his annual message to Congress, President James Monroe said that "the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers" (U.S. Senate, National Archives). Most historians credit his Secretary of State, John Quincy Adams, with writing it and with talking Monroe out of issuing it jointly with Britain (State Department Office of the Historian). In 1823 the US Navy was too small to enforce any of it. British leaders also wanted other European powers out of Latin America, for their own reasons, and the British navy kept those fleets away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Florida, Texas, and the treaty the Senate cut" -->
Spain gave up Florida in the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819. Texas was annexed in 1845. The Oregon boundary with British territory was settled by agreement at the 49th parallel in 1846. Mexico was settled by war.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, took more than 525,000 square miles from Mexico for $15,000,000 (National Archives). Between about 80,000 and 100,000 Mexican citizens lived on that land, depending on the count, and they became residents of the United States without moving. Article IX promised them their liberty, their property, and their religion. The Senate rewrote Article IX and cut its last two paragraphs, and when it ratified the treaty on March 10, 1848 it deleted Article X altogether. Article X was the one guaranteeing that Spanish and Mexican land grants stayed valid under American law. American negotiators then wrote a side agreement, the Protocol of Querétaro, telling Mexico that deleting Article X had not cancelled those land titles. American courts did not treat the Protocol as equal to the treaty. A law of 1851 made Mexican landowners in California prove their deeds before federal officials, and many lost their land in the long court cases that followed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" name="John Quincy Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Quincy Adams
> **Who:** Secretary of State, 1817 to 1825 · **When and where:** Washington, D.C.
Negotiated the treaty that took Florida from Spain in 1819, which is why it carries his name, and wrote the message that became the Monroe Doctrine. He argued that the United States should say it alone, not alongside Britain.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-trist-america-world" name="Nicholas Trist" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Trist
> **Who:** the diplomat President Polk fired for making peace · **When and where:** Mexico, 1847 to 1848
Polk sent Trist to negotiate an end to the war with Mexico, then lost patience and recalled him in October 1847. Trist ignored the recall, kept negotiating without any authority to do so, and signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848. Polk sent the treaty to the Senate anyway, because it gave him California. Then he fired Trist and refused to pay his expenses or his salary for his last months of work (National Constitution Center, Encyclopedia Virginia).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In this half-century the United States began taking places across oceans and keeping them. It took islands for fertilizer, bought a territory from Russia over the heads of the people living there, helped overthrow a kingdom in the Pacific, and came out of a war with Spain governing Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. In none of those places did the people get to vote on any of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Perry opens Japan, 1853 and 1854" -->
Japan had kept nearly all foreign ships out of its ports for more than two hundred years. Commodore Matthew Perry sailed a squadron of American warships into Edo Bay in July 1853 with a letter of demands, and came back in 1854 with a larger squadron. The Convention of Kanagawa, signed March 31, 1854, opened two Japanese ports to American ships (State Department Office of the Historian).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first overseas territories were made of bird droppings" -->
The United States began taking overseas territory in 1856, forty-two years before 1898, and the reason was fertilizer. Guano, the droppings of seabirds, made crops grow. Congress passed the Guano Islands Act on August 18, 1856. It let any American citizen who found an unclaimed island with guano on it take possession in the name of the United States. Americans claimed about a hundred islands under that law (US History Scene, 48 U.S. Code Chapter 8, still in force today). The Supreme Court upheld it in 1890.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alaska, 1867, and the sentence in the treaty" -->
The treaty with Russia was signed March 30, 1867 for $7,200,000, and the flags were changed at Sitka on October 18, 1867 (National Archives, *Prologue*). Russia was selling land it did not really control, and the Alaska Native nations living on it were not part of the sale.

Article III of the treaty said that inhabitants who stayed would be admitted to "the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages, and immunities of citizens of the United States," but it made an exception of "uncivilized native tribes," who would instead be "subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt" (treaty text, Alaska State Library). The diplomats who wrote the treaty that made Alaska American excluded its Native peoples from citizenship by name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1893, and what Hawaiians did about it" -->
On January 17, 1893, a group of mostly American businessmen in Honolulu moved against Queen Liliʻuokalani. One of their leaders was the lawyer Lorrin Thurston. In 1887 Thurston and another lawyer, Sanford B. Dole, had led an armed militia that forced King Kalākaua to sign a new constitution. The United States Minister to Hawaii, John L. Stevens, had 162 American sailors and Marines landed from the cruiser USS *Boston*. The Queen's government had several hundred troops and police, and they did not fight. Dole became president of the new provisional government. The Queen gave up her throne under protest. She addressed the protest to the United States, saying she yielded "to the superior force of the United States of America, whose Minister Plenipotentiary, His Excellency John L. Stevens, has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu."

President Grover Cleveland then sent Commissioner James H. Blount to investigate. Blount reported that Stevens had landed the Marines under a false pretext to help the plotters. In his message to Congress on December 18, 1893, Cleveland said that the "military demonstration on the soil of Honolulu was of itself an act of war," and that "the provisional government owes its existence to an armed invasion by the United States." He could not get the monarchy restored. The next year a Senate committee ran its own inquiry, the Morgan Report, and cleared everyone. Both reports are on the record, and they disagree.

Hawaiians fought annexation with signatures. Between September 11 and October 2, 1897, two Hawaiian organizations, Hui Aloha ʻĀina and Hui Kālaiʻāina, held meetings on the five main islands and gathered 21,269 signatures of Native Hawaiians against annexation. That was more than half of the 39,000 Native Hawaiian and part-Hawaiian people counted that year. Four delegates, James Kaulia, David Kalauokalani, John Richardson, and William Auld, carried the petition to Washington and presented it to the Senate on December 9, 1897. When they left Washington on February 27, 1898, only 46 senators were still willing to vote for the annexation treaty. The treaty failed (National Archives, "The 1897 Petition Against the Annexation of Hawaii").

A treaty needs the votes of two-thirds of the Senate. A joint resolution needs only a simple majority of each house. Supporters of annexation in Congress used a joint resolution instead, the Newlands Resolution, and President McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898. Hawaiians had defeated the treaty, and Congress annexed Hawaii anyway by a method that did not need the votes they had blocked.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898: Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines change owners" -->
The battleship USS *Maine* blew up in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, and Congress declared war on Spain in April. Spanish officials on Guam surrendered to the United States Navy on June 21, 1898. American troops landed in Puerto Rico at Guánica on July 25, 1898. Spanish and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Paris on December 10, 1898. Spain gave up Cuba, ceded Puerto Rico and Guam to the United States, and sold the Philippines for $20 million (Library of Congress, Britannica).

Nobody from Puerto Rico, Guam, or the Philippines was in the room in Paris. Two empires signed a paper in France and several million people got a new government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Philippine-American War begins, February 4, 1899" -->
Filipinos had declared their independence on June 12, 1898 and had been fighting Spain before the Americans arrived. When the United States kept the islands instead of recognizing that republic, the two went to war on February 4, 1899. About 4,200 Americans and about 20,000 Filipino soldiers were killed. Estimates of Filipino civilian deaths do not agree and the gap between them is very wide: Britannica gives more than 200,000, other estimates run from 250,000 to 1,000,000, and the Philippine-American War Centennial Initiative gives 510,000. Much of that came from hunger and from the cholera epidemic of 1902, which killed more than 200,000 on its own and was made worse by the war. There is no single agreed number.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samoa divided, 1899 and 1900" -->
The United States and Germany divided the Samoan islands between them in 1899. The chiefs of Tutuila signed a deed of cession on April 17, 1900, and Tui Manuʻa Elisala signed one for the Manuʻa islands on July 16, 1904. Congress did not get around to ratifying either until 1929. The Navy governed American Samoa from 1900 until 1951.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" name="Queen Liliʻuokalani" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Queen Liliʻuokalani
> **Who:** the last monarch of the Hawaiian Kingdom · **When and where:** Honolulu, deposed January 17, 1893
She yielded her throne under protest and addressed the protest to the United States government, asking it to undo what its own minister had done. President Cleveland agreed that Stevens had acted wrongly but did not restore her. In January 1895 her supporters tried to restore her by force and failed within days. Officials of the new Republic of Hawaii arrested her, tried her before a military court in her own throne room, and sentenced her to five years in prison at hard labor. They reduced the sentence to confinement in an upstairs bedroom of ʻIolani Palace, the palace she had ruled from, for nearly eight months, with one lady companion allowed. She prayed, read, composed music and quilted there. She wrote many songs, including "Aloha ʻOe," and she lived until 1917, nearly a quarter century after she lost her throne.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" name="Emilio Aguinaldo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Aguinaldo
> **Who:** Filipino leader who declared the Philippines independent and then led the war against the United States · **When and where:** Born March 22 or 23, 1869, near Cavite on Luzon. President of the Philippine Republic from January 1899. Captured at Palanan on March 23, 1901. Died February 6, 1964.

In 1896 Aguinaldo was a town mayor and a local leader of the Katipunan, a secret society fighting Spanish rule. In December 1897 he agreed with the Spanish governor to leave the islands in return for money and a promise of reforms. From Hong Kong and Singapore he made plans with American consuls and with Commodore George Dewey's officers to come back and help fight Spain. He landed on May 19, 1898.

On June 12, 1898, Filipinos declared independence with Aguinaldo at the head of their government. Six months later Spanish and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Paris, and Spain handed the islands to the United States. In January 1899 Filipinos proclaimed a republic and elected Aguinaldo president. On February 4 the fighting with American troops began.

On March 23, 1901, US General Frederick Funston captured him by a trick. Macabebe scouts, Filipinos working for the US Army, dressed as rebel soldiers and marched Funston and other officers in as fake prisoners. At Palanan the scouts shot two of Aguinaldo's guards and seized him. He swore loyalty to the United States. On April 19, 1901, he told his fighters to lay down their arms. He lived until 1964 and saw the Philippines become independent in 1946.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the twentieth century the United States governed an empire while arguing about whether to use the word. It stayed out of two world wars and then entered both, refused to join the League of Nations it had proposed, and afterward built the alliance system the world still runs on. It also decided, in court, that the people of the islands it had taken were not entitled to the whole Constitution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Insular Cases: part of the country, not all of the Constitution" -->
Between 1901 and 1922 the Supreme Court decided a group of cases about the islands taken in 1898. They are called the Insular Cases, and they are the reason the territories are the way they are.

In *Downes v. Bidwell*, decided May 27, 1901, the Court held that Puerto Rico "belongs to, but is not a part of, the United States." Out of these cases came the idea of the "unincorporated territory": a place the United States owns and rules, whose people do not get the whole Constitution unless Congress decides to give it to them. In *Balzac v. Porto Rico* in 1922 the Court held that Puerto Ricans, by then United States citizens, still had no constitutional right to a jury trial at home, because the island was unincorporated (U.S. Commission on Civil Rights, 2024).

The reasoning was about race, and the Court has now said so itself. In 2022 Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that the Insular Cases "have no foundation in the Constitution and rest instead on racial stereotypes," and that "They deserve no place in our law."

No case has overturned them. In 2026 the Insular Cases are still law, and they are still being argued about in court.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who is a citizen, island by island" -->
Citizenship in the territories arrived at different times, in different forms, and always by act of Congress rather than by right.

**Puerto Rico.** The Foraker Act of April 12, 1900 made Puerto Ricans "citizens of Porto Rico," entitled to the protection of the United States but not citizens of it. The Jones-Shafroth Act, signed March 2, 1917, made them United States citizens, one month before the country entered the First World War. Wilson signed the draft law two months after that. Sources disagree on how many Puerto Ricans were drafted: about 18,000 is the figure usually given, and History.com says 20,000. The citizenship came from a law passed by Congress, not from the Constitution, and it did not include a vote for president.

**The U.S. Virgin Islands.** The United States bought the Danish West Indies from Denmark for $25 million in gold. Diplomats signed the treaty on August 4, 1916, and the transfer was completed on March 31, 1917. The people who came with the islands were not made citizens until 1927, ten years later.

**Guam.** Navy officers governed Guam from 1899. Its people did not become United States citizens until President Truman signed the Organic Act of Guam on August 1, 1950, fifty-two years after the Navy took the island.

**American Samoa.** Its people were made United States nationals, not citizens. That is still true. American Samoa is the only permanently inhabited United States territory whose people are not citizens at birth.

**The Philippines.** The Tydings-McDuffie Act of March 24, 1934 promised independence on July 4, 1946 after a ten-year commonwealth, and in the same law reclassified Filipinos for immigration so that far fewer could enter the United States. Independence came on the promised date.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Law 116 and la operación, 1937 to the 1970s" -->
In 1937, under Blanton Winship, the governor appointed from Washington, Law 116 took effect in Puerto Rico. Under it, officials set up a Eugenics Board, and public money paid for sterilizations. Sterilization is an operation after which a person can never have children. For a woman in Puerto Rico it usually meant tubal ligation, which Puerto Ricans called *la operación*: a doctor cut or tied the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb. Some women instead had a hysterectomy, in which a surgeon removed the womb.

Members of the Eugenics Board ordered 97 sterilizations by force before the law was repealed in 1960. Many thousands more women had the operation under pressure. Some were told it could be undone. Some were told employers would not hire them unless they had it. Some pregnant women were turned away from hospitals unless they agreed to be sterilized after giving birth. Clarence Gamble, a rich American who believed in eugenics (the false idea that some groups of people should be kept from having children), paid for clinics on the island. By 1968 about one Puerto Rican woman in three of childbearing age had been sterilized, and a 1982 study found 41 percent of married women sterilized.

Some women chose the operation because it was the only birth control offered to them. The Puerto Rican doctor Helen Rodríguez-Trías said: "Women make choices based on alternatives, and there haven't been many alternatives in Puerto Rico."

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Finishing the Philippine war, 1901 and 1902" -->
On September 28, 1901, in the town of Balangiga on the island of Samar, townspeople and guerrillas attacked an American infantry company at breakfast and killed about 48 soldiers. It was the Army's worst single loss since the Little Bighorn in 1876.

The general sent to answer it, Jacob H. Smith, gave his orders in words that were written down and later read out at a court-martial: "I want no prisoners. I wish you to kill and burn. The more you kill and burn, the better it will please me." He said the interior of Samar "must be made a howling wilderness." Asked how young a person could be and still be a target, he set the age at ten. On Samar, Marine Major Littleton Waller had eleven Filipino guides and carriers shot without trial. A court-martial acquitted Waller because he had followed Smith's orders.

How many people were killed on Samar is disputed, and the range is very wide. An American soldier who was there said 39. A British researcher, Bob Couttie, spent ten years on it and concluded about 2,500. Most estimates fall between 2,000 and 2,500. Some Filipino historians say 5,000. An old figure of 50,000 is now thought to come from misread documents. Smith was court-martialed, found guilty of "conduct to the prejudice of good order and military discipline," reprimanded, and forced to retire. He was not imprisoned.

American soldiers also used torture they called the water cure. At Igbaras on November 27, 1900, soldiers tied up the town's mayor, Tobeniano Ealdama, held his throat so he had to swallow, and poured water into him until his stomach filled. Then they forced the water out by pressing on his stomach with a foot or their hands. Captain Edwin F. Glenn, an Army lawyer, supervised. That night Glenn ordered the town, 400 to 500 houses, burned. A former sergeant, Charles S. Riley, described what he saw to a Senate committee on April 14, 1902. An Army court found Glenn guilty and gave him a one-month suspension and a $50 fine. President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902. Fighting in the Moro provinces went on until 1913.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panama Canal" -->
President Theodore Roosevelt's government backed Panama's separation from Colombia in 1903, and American and Panamanian representatives signed the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty on November 18, 1903. Panama's side was signed by Philippe-Jean Bunau-Varilla, a Frenchman. The treaty gave the United States, forever, a strip ten miles wide across the isthmus. Panama got $10,000,000 and $250,000 a year starting nine years later (Britannica, Theodore Roosevelt Center). The canal opened August 15, 1914.

The official count of the dead during the American construction is 5,609 out of about 56,000 workers. Most of those workers came from Barbados, Jamaica, and other Caribbean islands, and many historians think the real toll was several times the official one (The Conversation, National Archives).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Occupying the neighbors" -->
President Woodrow Wilson sent the Marines into Haiti in 1915, and they stayed until 1934, nineteen years. Under a 1915 treaty, American officials took control of Haiti's money and set up a Haitian police force commanded by Marines. A new Haitian constitution, drafted under American direction, went to a vote on June 12, 1918. Officials recorded it as approved by 99 percent, with fewer than 5 percent of the population voting. Marines and their Haitian police brought back the corvée, a system of forced labor: they rounded up Haitians and made them build roads without pay. By some estimates the Marines and the police they commanded killed more than 2,000 Haitian fighters, called cacos. A US military count at the time said 3,250. In 1929 Marines at Les Cayes killed at least 12 Haitian protesters. President Franklin D. Roosevelt pulled the Marines out in 1934.

Charlemagne Péralte led the armed resistance from 1917. A Marine order to get him led Sergeant Herman H. Hanneken to enter his camp in disguise and shoot him dead near Sainte-Suzanne on November 1, 1919. The Marines photographed the body and passed the picture around to show that he was dead. Sources disagree on his birth year, giving 1885 or 1886.

Wilson also sent Marines into the Dominican Republic in 1916. They set up a military government, fought guerrillas called gavilleros in the east from 1917 to 1921, and left when Dominican President Horacio Vásquez took office on July 13, 1924. Marines were in Nicaragua from 1912, when President Taft sent them to back President Adolfo Díaz, until January 1933, except for a short gap in 1925 and 1926. From 1927 Augusto César Sandino fought them from the northern mountains. The Marines trained a Nicaraguan National Guard and left Anastasio Somoza García in command of it. In February 1934 Somoza's guardsmen seized Sandino after a peace meeting and killed him, and in 1937 Somoza made himself president.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Guam under Japanese occupation, 1941 to 1944" -->
Japanese forces invaded Guam on December 8, 1941, hours after Pearl Harbor, and held it for two and a half years. Japanese soldiers beat CHamoru people, forced them to work without pay, and executed some of them. By the spring of 1944 every able-bodied man was in a labor battalion, and in the last weeks Japanese troops force-marched the population to camps, the largest at Manenggon (Guampedia). American forces landed on July 21, 1944, a date Guam still marks.

The people who lived through that were people of the United States who were not United States citizens. Citizenship came six years later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two world wars, one refused ship, and the order that followed" -->
The United States entered the First World War on April 6, 1917. Wilson put his terms for peace in the Fourteen Points on January 8, 1918 and proposed a League of Nations. The Senate rejected the treaty and the League in votes in November 1919 and March 1920, so the country that invented it never joined.

Refusing refugees is also foreign policy. The liner *St. Louis* sailed from Hamburg on May 13, 1939 with 937 passengers, nearly all Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany. Cuba admitted 28. The United States refused them, and so did Canada. The ship carried them back to Europe, where Great Britain took 288, the Netherlands 181, Belgium 214, and France 224. Of those passengers, 254 were later killed in the Holocaust (United States Holocaust Memorial Museum).

After the second war American leaders helped build the kind of system the Senate had refused in 1920. Delegates signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco on June 26, 1945, and it took effect on October 24, 1945. Congress passed the Marshall Plan in March 1948, and it sent more than $12 billion to rebuild western Europe. Twelve countries signed the treaty that created NATO on April 4, 1949. It was the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered outside the Western Hemisphere (State Department Office of the Historian).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="woodrow-wilson-america-world" name="Woodrow Wilson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Woodrow Wilson
> **Who:** president, 1913 to 1921 · **When and where:** Washington and Paris
Took the country into the First World War, wrote the Fourteen Points, and proposed the League of Nations at the peace conference in Paris. The Senate refused to join it. He is also the president who signed the law making Puerto Ricans citizens a month before the war, and the president whose Marines occupied Haiti and the Dominican Republic.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woodrow-wilson-america-world" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pedro-albizu-campos-america-world" name="Pedro Albizu Campos" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pedro Albizu Campos
> **Who:** United States Army officer, then leader of Puerto Rican independence · **When and where:** Ponce, Puerto Rico, born September 12, 1891
He was the first Puerto Rican to graduate from Harvard Law School, and he served in the United States Army in the First World War, rising to second lieutenant in the all-Black 375th Infantry Regiment. The segregation he met in that army shaped everything he did afterward. He became president of the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party in 1930 and was imprisoned for sedition.

On Palm Sunday, March 21, 1937, police fired on a Nationalist march in Ponce held to mark the end of slavery in Puerto Rico and to protest his imprisonment. Sources give the dead as between 19 and 21 and the wounded as roughly 200 to 235. It is remembered as the Ponce Massacre.

He was a United States citizen who could be drafted into the United States Army and could not vote for the president who commanded it.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For forty years American foreign policy was organized around one rivalry, with the Soviet Union, and American money, weapons, films, and music reached almost everywhere. It is also the half-century in which the map of what the United States governs stopped changing. Two territories became states. Two more chose new arrangements. The rest stayed exactly where the Insular Cases had left them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Containment, alliances, and the Peace Corps" -->
The policy was called containment. It meant trying to stop Soviet power from spreading anywhere in the world. American presidents paid for alliances, foreign aid, weapons, and two wars, in Korea from 1950 to 1953 and in Vietnam, and they fought those wars mainly to stop the Soviet Union and its allies. The two sides came closest to nuclear war in October 1962, when Soviet leaders put missiles in Cuba.

It also paid for the Peace Corps, created by President Kennedy in March 1961 with Sargent Shriver as its first director. The first volunteers went to Ghana that year. The Berlin Wall was opened on November 9, 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved on December 26, 1991.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Governments overthrown with American help: Iran, Guatemala, Chile" -->
Three times in these years American officials secretly helped overthrow an elected government that they saw as a danger to American interests.

- **Iran, August 19, 1953.** Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh had taken Iran's oil industry out of British hands. President Eisenhower approved a secret plan with British intelligence in June 1953. CIA Director Allen Dulles oversaw it, and CIA officer Kermit Roosevelt ran it in Iran, paying journalists, religious leaders and politicians to turn people against Mosaddegh. On August 19 General Fazlollah Zahedi replaced him, and the Shah (Iran's king) took back power. The Shah had Mosaddegh convicted of treason. He served three years in prison and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. The Shah's government executed one of his advisers and more than a dozen officers and student leaders on his side. In 2013 the CIA released its own history, which says the agency "planned and helped implement the coup."
- **Guatemala, June 1954.** President Jacobo Árbenz had taken unused land from big landowners, including the American-owned United Fruit Company, and given it to about 100,000 families. His government offered United Fruit $2.99 an acre, and the State Department demanded $75. Eisenhower approved a CIA operation called PBSUCCESS in August 1953. CIA officers ran a propaganda campaign and drew up lists of Guatemalan officials to be killed. On June 18, 1954 a force under Carlos Castillo Armas crossed from Honduras, and on June 27 Árbenz resigned. The new government gave the land back to the companies and broke up the labor unions. Between 1954 and 1990, human rights groups estimate, soldiers and police under Guatemala's military governments murdered more than 100,000 civilians.
- **Chile, 1970 to 1973.** On September 15, 1970, President Richard Nixon told CIA Director Richard Helms to stop Salvador Allende, just elected president, from taking or keeping power. Helms's notes record the words "make the economy scream" and "$10,000,000 available, more if necessary." CIA officers gave weapons to plotters who planned to kidnap the army commander, General René Schneider, and another group of plotters killed him on October 22, 1970. On September 11, 1973 General Augusto Pinochet led the army coup that overthrew Allende. Pinochet's government ruled until 1990. Chile's official commissions count 3,216 people killed or disappeared and 38,254 who survived political imprisonment, torture, or both.

*Shared with:* `war` (the Cold War as fighting).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two territories become states, 1959" -->
Alaska was admitted on January 3, 1959 and Hawaii on August 21, 1959.

The Hawaii vote had one question on it. The plebiscite of June 27, 1959 asked: "Shall Hawaii immediately be admitted into the Union as a State?" The only answers were Yes and No. Independence was not on the ballot, although a United Nations resolution of 1953 said that votes like this one should offer a free choice among several possibilities, including independence. Statehood won by a wide margin, and the United Nations then took Hawaii and Alaska off its list of territories that were not governing themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puerto Rico: a new name, bombed towns, and a shooting in Congress" -->
Public Law 600, passed by Congress in 1950, let Puerto Ricans write their own constitution. Governor Luis Muñoz Marín proclaimed the Commonwealth, the *Estado Libre Asociado*, on July 25, 1952, the anniversary of the American landing at Guánica. The name changed. Puerto Rico stayed an unincorporated territory under the Insular Cases.

Some Puerto Ricans rejected it. On October 30, 1950, Nationalists rose in several towns, and the Puerto Rico National Guard attacked Jayuya and Utuado with P-47 fighter-bombers, artillery, mortars, and grenades. Pilots in American military aircraft fired on and bombed towns full of United States citizens. Two days later two Nationalists attacked Blair House in Washington, where President Truman was living.

On March 1, 1954, four Puerto Rican Nationalists, Lolita Lebrón, Rafael Cancel Miranda, Andrés Figueroa Cordero, and Irvin Flores Rodríguez, went into the visitors' gallery of the House of Representatives, unfurled a Puerto Rican flag, and fired down onto the floor. Five Representatives were wounded, and all five recovered (U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art and Archives). All four were convicted and imprisoned.

Meanwhile, on November 27, 1953, the United Nations General Assembly removed Puerto Rico from its list of territories that were not governing themselves, and the United States stopped filing reports about it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pacific: a trust, sixty-seven bombs, and one territory that voted to join" -->
After the Second World War, United Nations members placed the Marianas, the Carolines, and the Marshalls, more than two thousand islands, under American administration as the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands, from 1947 to 1986. On paper, American officials were to look after the people there until they could govern themselves.

Between 1946 and 1958 the US military exploded 67 nuclear weapons at Bikini and Enewetak atolls, on islands the United States ran under that trust, after moving the people off. The largest was Castle Bravo, on March 1, 1954. It had a yield of 15 megatons, about two and a half times what its designers had predicted. The fallout came down on inhabited islands, and sixty-four people on Rongelap were exposed (Arms Control Association, National Security Archive, National Cancer Institute).

The Northern Mariana Islands took a different road. Their representatives signed a Covenant with the United States on February 15, 1975, and 78.8 percent of the people voting approved it in a plebiscite on June 17, 1975. The Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands began in 1978, and its people became United States citizens in 1986. The Marshall Islands and the Federated States of Micronesia became independent countries in free association with the United States in 1986, and Palau in 1994.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Electing their own, and the capital that cannot" -->
Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands elected their own governors for the first time in 1970. Before that, presidents appointed them. American Samoa elected its first governor in 1977.

Washington, D.C. is not a state either. Its residents could not vote for president at all until the Twenty-third Amendment was ratified on March 29, 1961. A non-voting delegate to the House was restored in 1971, and the Home Rule Act of 1973 gave the city an elected mayor from 1975. A constitutional amendment for full representation in Congress passed Congress in 1978 and died in 1985, far short of the states it needed.

The United States also handed a possession back. The Torrijos-Carter treaties, signed September 7, 1977, gave the Panama Canal to Panama on December 31, 1999.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lillian-carter-america-world" name="Lillian Carter" movie="Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother (2021)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lillian Carter
> **Who:** a nurse from Plains, Georgia who joined the Peace Corps at 68 · **When and where:** India, 1966 to 1968 · **Movie:** *Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother* (2021)
She told the recruiter she wanted to go "where it's warm, people have dark skins and need a nurse's service." She was sent to the Godrej Colony, about thirty miles from Mumbai, and turned seventy there. Her son Jimmy became president after she came home. The Peace Corps' award for volunteers who serve at fifty or older is named for her.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The United States is now one large power among several. Its arguments about the world are mostly arguments about trade, about borders, about which alliances to keep, and about two long wars that started with one morning in 2001. The territories are still territories. This account is current through 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="September 11, and the wars after it" -->
The attacks of September 11, 2001 killed 2,977 people, not counting the nineteen hijackers. The next day, NATO's member governments invoked Article 5, the promise that an attack on one member is an attack on all. It was the first and only time they have done so, and they did it for the United States.

American forces went into Afghanistan on October 7, 2001 and left on August 30, 2021. The United States invaded Iraq on March 20, 2003, saying Iraq had weapons of mass destruction. None were found.

Different researchers count the dead differently, and the differences are large. The Costs of War project at Brown University counts more than 940,000 people killed directly by war violence in Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen, and Pakistan between 2001 and 2023. More than 432,000 of them were civilians. The project estimates another 3.6 to 3.8 million deaths from what the wars did to food, water, hospitals, and disease, for 4.5 to 4.7 million in all. Counts of Iraqi civilian deaths built from individual documented cases come out far lower than counts built from household surveys. A study published in *The Lancet* in 2006 estimated roughly 600,000 violent deaths, and American and Iraqi officials disputed it at the time.

In January 2002 the US government began holding prisoners at its naval base at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba, most of them without trial. Of the 779 men held there since, 15 were still there in January 2025.

China joined the World Trade Organization in December 2001. A new agreement with Mexico and Canada replaced NAFTA in July 2020. From 2018, and again from 2025, presidents used tariffs (taxes on imports) as a main tool of foreign policy, aimed largely at China.

On climate the country has changed its mind three times. It joined the Paris agreement, left it effective November 4, 2020, rejoined in February 2021, and left again: an executive order of January 20, 2025 began the withdrawal, and it took effect on January 27, 2026 (Congressional Research Service).

More than 3.6 million people live in the five inhabited United States territories. The 2020 census counted 3,285,874 in Puerto Rico, down 11.8 percent in ten years. It counted 153,836 in Guam, about 87,146 in the U.S. Virgin Islands, 49,710 in American Samoa, and 47,329 in the Northern Mariana Islands. Every one of them lost population between 2010 and 2020.

People born in Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the Northern Marianas are United States citizens. People born in American Samoa are United States nationals, not citizens. Their passports say so, and to vote they must naturalize the way a foreigner does. None of them can vote for president while living at home. Each territory sends one delegate to the House of Representatives who may speak and work in committee but cannot vote on whether a bill passes. Puerto Rico's delegate is called the Resident Commissioner.

Puerto Rico's government debts passed $72 billion. In 2016 Congress passed a law called PROMESA, which created a seven-member board appointed in Washington with power over the island's budget. Puerto Ricans call it *la junta*. The people whose money it controls did not elect it. On August 5, 2025, President Trump fired five of its members, although PROMESA allows a president to remove a member "only for cause." Three of them sued. A federal judge in Puerto Rico put them back on the board in October 2025 and made that ruling permanent in 2026.

Puerto Rico has voted on its status repeatedly, and Congress has not acted on any of the results. Statehood won 52.52 percent in 2020, and in the vote of November 5, 2024 statehood took 58.61 percent, free association 29.57 percent, and independence 11.82 percent. The votes are not binding. Only Congress can change the status.

Washington, D.C. had about 690,000 residents in the 2020 census, more than the states of Wyoming or Vermont. It has one non-voting delegate and no senators. The House of Representatives passed a bill to make it a state on April 22, 2021 by 216 votes to 208. The Senate did not take it up.

### John Fitisemanu
> **Who:** born in American Samoa, a United States national who is not a United States citizen · **When and where:** Utah. His case reached the Tenth Circuit in 2021
He lives in Utah and cannot vote there, because he was born in American Samoa. He sued to be recognized as a citizen by birth. A federal district court in Utah agreed with him. The Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reversed that in 2021, holding that the Insular Cases were still good law, and the Supreme Court declined to hear the case on October 17, 2022.

American Samoa's own elected government opposed his suit. Its leaders said that citizenship handed down by a court could put *faʻa Sāmoa*, the Samoan way of life with communal land and the matai chief system, at legal risk. He wants the citizenship. His territory's leaders did not want it granted that way.

### José Luis Vaello Madero
> **Who:** a man the United States government sued for $28,081 · **When and where:** New York, then Puerto Rico, case decided April 21, 2022
He received Supplemental Security Income lawfully while he lived in New York. He moved to Puerto Rico, where that benefit does not apply, and the payments kept coming without his knowing he had become ineligible. The government sued him to get the money back and took the case to the Supreme Court, which ruled 8 to 1 that the Constitution does not require Congress to pay that benefit to people in Puerto Rico.

Justice Neil Gorsuch agreed with the result and wrote separately to say that the century-old cases the whole arrangement rests on "deserve no place in our law." They are still the law.
