## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Everything ran on muscle, fire, and wood. There were no draft animals north of Mexico — no horse, no ox — so people carried loads themselves, dragged them on frames (dogs pulled the travois on the Plains), and paddled them by canoe. Heat and cooking came from burning wood. Say it plainly: for thousands of years, human strength and fire were the whole energy supply.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire as a managed tool" -->
Native nations used fire deliberately, on a schedule — regular low burns that cleared underbrush, opened travel routes, and kept land productive for food plants and game (NPS "Indigenous Fire Practices Shape Our Land"; the Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa burning cycles in California are documented examples). Our angle: fire as a controlled energy tool, not an accident.
Shared with: land-environment (what burning did to the landscape) · native-nations (whose knowledge it was, and its suppression later).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first new power source in thousands of years walked ashore: the horse, with Spanish expeditions, along with oxen and mules. For nearly everyone living on this land, nothing changed yet — muscle, fire, and wood still did all the work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horsepower arrives, and one water mill" -->
- Spanish expeditions brought horses into the Southeast and Southwest in the 1500s; literal horsepower — riding, hauling, plowing — entered the continent with them.
- In 1598, at the era's very edge, Juan de Oñate's colonists built the first water-powered mills in what is now the United States, in New Mexico — flat-lying "tub wheel" mills, with grindstones hauled up the Camino Real (Santa Fe New Mexican local history — journalism, labeled).
Shared with: native-nations (the horse nations to come) · exploration (whose expeditions carried the animals).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial power meant falling water, wind, pulling animals, and wood — enormous amounts of wood. For the first time, machines here ground grain and sawed boards without a human hand driving them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first mills" -->
- Virginia, 1621: the first recorded windmill in English America, built for Sir George Yeardley at Flowerdew Hundred on the James River, and the first recorded gristmill the same year (Encyclopedia sources; Colonial Williamsburg).
- 1634: carpenters sent by John Mason built a water-powered gristmill and sawmill at the falls of Newichawannock (now South Berwick, Maine) — among the first water mills in New England; several towns claim "first," so state the date, not the trophy (Old Berwick Historical Society; Tide Mill Institute).
- By the end of the century, mills stood on usable falls throughout the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wood, the ox, and the horse" -->
- Wood was THE fuel. Historians' estimates of what one colonial household burned in a year range from about 15 to as many as 40 cords — a cord is a stack 4 feet by 4 feet by 8 feet — because open fireplaces sent most of their heat up the chimney (estimates vary; state the range). Forests near towns went first.
- Oxen and horses did the plowing and hauling in the colonies. On the Plains, horses were in Native hands earlier than the old story said: a 2023 study found horses ridden and raised in Native communities by the early 1600s, decades before the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 put perhaps 1,500 more Spanish horses into Native hands at once (Science, 2023; Smithsonian NMAI).
- Along Long Island and New England shores, colonists began taking whales near shore; whale oil burned in lamps from about 1690 (NPS New Bedford).
Shared with: land-environment (the shrinking forests) · native-nations (the horse changing Plains life) · food-farming (the plow team).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-chadbourne" name="William Chadbourne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Chadbourne
> **Who:** An English carpenter and millwright, one of three men John Mason's Laconia Company sent in 1634 to build a sawmill and a gristmill at Newichawannock. · **When and where:** South Berwick, Maine, 1634.
- July 1634: Chadbourne, James Wall and John Goddard arrived on the ship *Pied Cow*, under contract to build the two mills on the Asbenbedick (Little Newichawannock) River (Old Berwick Historical Society, "Great Works").
- The sawmill stood in the rocky gorge below today's Great Works bridge. The society calls it "thought to be the first" overshot water-powered mill in America. Keep that as the society's claim. An overshot wheel turns under the weight of water falling onto its top.
- Wall's sworn statement of May 21, 1652 says the three men ran a sawmill and a corn mill there for three or four years, and that he and Chadbourne each built a house (OBHS timeline).
- Whose land: Newichawannock is an Abenaki name. Abenaki people lived there. Between 1616 and 1618, European diseases killed about seven in ten of them. No record found shows Mason's men asked anyone's leave to build (OBHS, "South Berwick's First People"). Leave out his birth and death years, which genealogy sites give differently.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Water wheels powered colonial industry, iron furnaces began eating forests as charcoal, and after dark the better-off lit whale oil. The power sources were the same as the century before — there were just far more of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal for iron" -->
Iron furnaces ran day and night on charcoal — wood slow-burned for days in earthen mounds by workers called colliers. Feeding one furnace took forestland by the hundreds of acres a year (the documented numbers are at Hopewell Furnace, next era; the practice was already general in this one). `elements` tells the iron at Saugus; `technology` the machinery; the fuel is ours.
- Baltimore Iron Works, Maryland, 1731: five planters founded it (Dr. Charles Carroll, his cousins Charles and Daniel Carroll, Daniel Dulany and Benjamin Tasker). It opened with 89 workers. 42 of them were enslaved. By 1785 the owners advertised "more than 200 Negroes." Enslaved people and convicts shipped from Britain worked 12-hour shifts hauling carts of charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore to the top of the furnace, around the clock, six or seven days a week. The owners did not give them enough food. Some ran away (Mount Clare Museum House).
- The records of these early furnaces name the owners. They do not name the men who made the charcoal.
Shared with: elements (the iron) · technology (the waterwheel-driven works) · land-environment (the cut forests) · slavery-freedom (enslaved labor, their lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Light after dark, and a stove that saved wood" -->
- Whale oil was a primary lamp fuel from about 1690 on, and spermaceti — the waxy oil from a sperm whale's head — made the brightest, cleanest candles money could buy (NPS New Bedford; New Bedford Whaling Museum).
- 1741: Benjamin Franklin's "Pennsylvania fireplace" — a cast-iron stove meant to give more heat with less smoke from less wood. It sold poorly until David Rittenhouse improved it. The stove as a machine belongs to `technology`; the wood it saved is ours (Britannica; ushistory.org).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Wind, water, wood, and muscle still did nearly all of America's work through the Revolution and after. But in 1755 a machine in New Jersey began pumping water with fire — the first steam engine on the continent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
The Schuyler copper mine at what is now North Arlington, New Jersey, kept flooding. John Schuyler ordered a Newcomen-type steam pumping engine from England in 1748; the parts arrived in 1753, and engine-builder Josiah Hornblower had it pumping by 1755 — the first steam engine in America, decades before steam moved a boat or a wheel here (Rutgers University Libraries journal; Engines of Our Ingenuity).
Shared with: technology (the engine as machine — parked there) · elements (copper, the reason the mine mattered).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal by the acre" -->
Hopewell Furnace, Pennsylvania (founded 1771, now an NPS site), documents an iron furnace's appetite: in blast it burned up to 800 bushels of charcoal a day — about an acre of forest cut for a single day's fuel — and 5,000 to 7,000 cords of wood, more than 200 acres, a year (two NPS pages give 5,000 to 6,000 and 6,000 to 7,000) (NPS Hopewell Furnace).
- How charcoal was made: a collier stacked about 30 cords of wood around a center pole, covered the pile with leaves and dust, and lit it by shovelling hot coals into a chimney left at the center. It smoldered with little air for 10 to 14 days. He watched it day and night and walked on it to find and fill air holes. One pile made about 1,100 bushels, a day and a half of fuel (NPS "Charcoal Making").
- Who did it: in 1780 Hopewell's founder, Mark Bird, enslaved 18 people (10 men, 4 women, 3 boys, 1 girl), the most in Berks County (NPS). At Catoctin Furnace in Maryland, in blast by 1776, at least 271 enslaved people worked as ore miners, colliers, woodcutters and teamsters (Science, 2023). The Johnson family, who owned it, enslaved more than 80 people. One is recorded only as Collier Sam (Catoctin Furnace Historical Society).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whale oil toward its peak" -->
Whaling grew into one of the young country's biggest businesses; spermaceti candles were the premium light of the era, and the fleet kept growing toward its 1846 peak. The ships belong to `transportation`; the ocean's cost to `land-environment`; the light is ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-hornblower" name="Josiah Hornblower" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Hornblower
> **Who:** English-born engine-builder who assembled and ran the first steam engine in America at the Schuyler copper mine. · **When and where:** North Arlington, New Jersey; engine at work 1755.
He crossed the Atlantic with the engine parts in 1753, got the machine pumping in 1755, and stayed in New Jersey the rest of his life (Rutgers University Libraries; History of Information).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Energy started coming out of the ground. Hard Pennsylvania "stone coal" learned to burn in home grates, gas made from coal lit the first city streets, steam engines spread through boats and mills — and the whale fleet reached its peak just before its fall.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone coal" -->
- February 11, 1808, Wilkes-Barre: Jesse Fell burned anthracite in an open iron grate at his tavern — proving the hard, hot, nearly smokeless coal could heat a home without bellows. Anthracite needed a draft from below; his grate supplied it (Luzerne County history; explorePAhistory).
- The Lehigh Navigation company (organized 1818) floated anthracite down to Philadelphia in boxy arks; canals then moved it into the cities, and coal stoves and furnaces followed (Lehigh Coal and Navigation records; Hopkin Thomas Project).
Shared with: transportation (the coal canals) · economy (the coal trade) · work-workers (the miners, their lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gaslight" -->
Baltimore, 1816: Rembrandt Peale lit a room of his museum with gas made from coal; investors chartered the Gas Light Company of Baltimore on June 17, 1816 — the first American gas company — and lit the country's first public gas street lamp on February 7, 1817. Street by street, town gas began replacing oil lamps in cities (Maryland Center for History and Culture; American Oil & Gas Historical Society).
Shared with: city-building (lit streets) · news-communication (evening reading, their call).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whaling's golden age — and who could afford it" -->
The American whaling fleet peaked in 1846 at 735 vessels — most of the world's roughly 900 — with New Bedford the leading port (New Bedford Whaling Museum; Whaling in the United States surveys). Honest framing: whale oil cost roughly $1.30 to $2.50 a gallon, so most ordinary homes actually burned cheaper tallow candles, lard oil, or camphene; the clean bright sperm oil lit the well-off, lighthouses, and public rooms (AOGHS; Nantucket Historical Association).
Shared with: land-environment (what whaling did to the whales) · work-workers (the crew's job) · transportation (the ships).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nelson-cole-haley" name="Nelson Cole Haley" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nelson Cole Haley
> **Who:** A New Bedford boy who went to sea at twelve and became a harpooner; his memoir of a four-year whaling voyage survives. · **When and where:** Aboard the ship *Charles W. Morgan*, 1849–1853, Pacific whaling grounds.
Haley (1832–1900) shipped as harpooner on the *Charles W. Morgan* at seventeen; his account, *Whale Hunt*, published by Mystic Seaport, describes the chase, the kill, and the try-works boiling blubber into the oil that lit lamps back home. The *Morgan* itself survives at Mystic Seaport — the last wooden whaleship in existence (Mystic Seaport Museum).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two new kinds of power arrived within a single generation: rock oil pumped from the ground in 1859, and electric current sold from a wire in 1882. Around 1885, coal passed wood as the country's largest energy source for the first time (EIA).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drake's well, 1859" -->
August 27, 1859, Titusville, Pennsylvania: Edwin Drake's drill struck oil at 69.5 feet — the first well drilled purposely for oil in the United States, and the start of the American oil business. The target product was kerosene, a cheap lamp fuel (Drake Well Museum; AOGHS).
Shared with: economy (the oil business) · big-business (Standard Oil, later).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-drake" name="Edwin Drake" movie="Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake (1954)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Drake
> **Who:** Ex-railroad conductor hired by the Seneca Oil Company to drill for oil; his 1859 well started the industry. · **When and where:** Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859.
> **Movie:** Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake (1954)
The honest details: Drake was never a colonel — investor James Townsend invented the title in letters sent ahead to impress Titusville locals. Drake never patented his key drive-pipe idea, lost his money, and lived his last years ill and nearly penniless until Pennsylvania voted him a $1,500-a-year pension in 1873; he died in 1880 (Drake Well Museum; Britannica; AOGHS). The film is a 30-minute industry-sponsored dramatization (American Petroleum Institute) with Vincent Price as Drake — label it as such.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-drake" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kerosene takes the lamp" -->
Kerosene won the American lamp in the 1860s — but tell it honestly: whaling had already been shrinking for a decade (the fleet peaked in 1846), whale oil at $1.30–$2.50 a gallon lit mainly the well-off, and the Civil War tax that hit alcohol-based camphene at up to $2.08 a gallon (kerosene's tax: 10 cents) handed the market to petroleum. Kerosene did not so much kill whaling as finish a decline already underway — the "kerosene saved the whales" line oversimplifies (AOGHS; environmental-history critiques of the whale-oil myth, labeled as historians' argument).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pearl Street, 1882" -->
September 4, 1882: from 257 Pearl Street in Manhattan, Edison's crews threw the switch on the first central power station in America — six dynamos, direct current, serving about 85 customers and 400 lamps in a quarter-square-mile district on day one; 513 customers and over 10,000 lamps within a year. Edison stood in banker J.P. Morgan's office when the lights came on (IEEE Milestone / ETHW; IEEE Power & Energy Magazine).
Shared with: technology (the bulb and the machines, their lead on the lamp) · city-building (lit blocks) · big-business (the electric companies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-edison-energy" name="Thomas Edison" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Edison
> **Who:** Built the first central power station and the whole system behind it — dynamo, wiring, meter, lamp — and bet it all on direct current. · **When and where:** Pearl Street, Manhattan, September 4, 1882.
Our angle is the current and the grid: DC worked, but it could not travel far from the station — a limit that set up the fight of the next decade. The invention-lab story and the film about Edison live in `technology`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-edison-energy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The current war" -->
- Tesla's alternating-current motor patents issued May 1888; Westinghouse licensed them that July for $60,000 plus a $2.50-per-horsepower royalty. AC could be stepped up to high voltage, sent miles, and stepped back down — DC could not (IEEE Spectrum; The Henry Ford).
- The smear campaign is documented and belongs in the telling: engineer Harold P. Brown, quietly backed by Edison's side, electrocuted animals with AC to brand it the killer current, and pushed AC into the first electric chair. William Kemmler's execution on August 6, 1890 was botched and gruesome (Smithsonian Magazine; primary press accounts).
- Westinghouse won anyway: his AC lit the 1893 Chicago World's Fair (a winning bid of $399,000, about 100,000 incandescent lamps), the Adams Plant at Niagara Falls sent its first AC power August 26, 1895, and Buffalo received Niagara power in November 1896. Alternating current became the way electricity travels — and still is (ETHW; standard histories).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" name="George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla" movie="The Current War (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla
> **Who:** The manufacturer and the immigrant engineer whose alternating current won the fight over how electricity would travel. · **When and where:** Pittsburgh and New York, 1888–1896.
> **Movie:** The Current War (2017)
Westinghouse licensed Tesla's patents in July 1888 and absorbed the electric-chair smear campaign without answering in kind; Niagara-to-Buffalo transmission in 1896 settled the question. The film is a Hollywood dramatization (Cumberbatch as Edison, Shannon as Westinghouse, Hoult as Tesla) — label it as drama, not documentary. Tesla's motor as a machine is `technology`'s story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What coal cost the miners" -->
- Avondale, near Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, September 6, 1869: sparks from the mine's ventilating furnace set fire to the wooden breaker, the building where coal was broken and sorted. The breaker stood right over the mine's only shaft. 110 people died, among them five boys and two men who went in to rescue the miners. The miners suffocated underground (EARTH Magazine, 2018).
- Pennsylvania lawmakers passed the Mine Safety Act of 1870, which required every mine to have more than one exit (EARTH Magazine).
Shared with: work-workers (the miners' job and unions, their lead) · disasters (the fire as event).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal gives way to coal" -->
Hopewell Furnace burned charcoal from about 1771 until it closed in 1883. The National Park Service gives the reason: charcoal furnaces were replaced by new ironworks that burned hard coal (anthracite). Ironmakers had cut forests for charcoal for more than two hundred years. By the 1880s coal had taken their place (NPS Hopewell Furnace).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lafayette-houck" name="Lafayette Houck" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lafayette Houck
> **Who:** A collier (charcoal maker) at Hopewell Furnace, from a family of colliers, and one of the last men who knew how to do the work. · **When and where:** Hopewell Furnace, Berks County, Pennsylvania, later 1800s. Demonstrated the craft in 1936.
- His family's work at Hopewell began with Master Collier Henry Houck in 1818 (NPS "Prominent Families").
- A Hopewell collier lived in a makeshift hut from May to October with one or two helpers, tending eight or nine smoldering piles at once, with no break from lighting until the last load was hauled away (NPS "Charcoal").
- Hopewell closed in 1883. In 1936, after the federal government bought the land, Houck and his son William showed Civilian Conservation Corps men how a collier worked. He taught the Park Service how charcoal was made and gave them a first-hand account of the furnace (NPS).
- Birth year disputed: NPS gives 1846 to 1939 on one page and "82-year-old" in 1936 on another. State both or give no age. The records found do not give his pay or the years he worked.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Oil became the fuel of American movement, electric wires reached city homes and then — by government program — the farms, and dams turned rivers into power plants. By mid-century the United States was fueling a world war largely by itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spindletop, 1901" -->
January 10, 1901, near Beaumont, Texas: the Lucas well blew in at a depth of 1,139 feet, throwing oil over 100 feet high and flowing an estimated 100,000 barrels a day until it was capped nine days later, on January 19. All of Texas had produced 836,000 barrels in 1900 — this one well could beat that in nine days. Cheap, huge Texas oil made gasoline plentiful just as the automobile arrived to drink it (Texas State Historical Association; AOGHS; Lamar University).
Shared with: economy (the oil boom) · big-business (Gulf, Texaco born) · transportation (the car, their lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The price of coal in lives" -->
- Coal was still the country's main fuel, and digging it killed thousands of miners every year. In 1907, 3,242 coal miners were killed at work, the worst year on record. From 1900 through 2025, MSHA's yearly counts add up to about 105,000 coal miners killed in accidents (MSHA).
- Monongah, West Virginia, December 6, 1907: explosions in mines No. 6 and No. 8 killed 362 men by the official count. Later estimates run above 500, because boys and helpers who were not on the company's list also went underground. Many of the dead were immigrants from Italy, Poland and Hungary. About 1,000 children lost a father. Sources name the owner as the Fairmont Coal Company (Clio) or the Consolidation Coal Company (West Virginia Mine Wars Museum). Company president C. W. Watson blamed "the careless use of a mining lamp." Experts disagreed on the cause. The disaster pushed Congress to create the federal Bureau of Mines (MSHA).
- Ludlow, Colorado, April 20, 1914: coal miners striking against the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, whose main owner was John D. Rockefeller, Jr., lived in a tent colony. Governor Elias Ammons had sent the National Guard, paid for by the mine owners. Fighting broke out. Nobody knows who fired first. Guardsmen burned the tents. At least 19 people died, including 13 women and children who suffocated in a pit dug under a tent. Guardsmen shot strike leader Louis Tikas three times in the back (Colorado Encyclopedia). The strike story belongs to work-workers.
Shared with: work-workers (the miners, the strike, their lead) · disasters (Monongah as event).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-insull" name="Samuel Insull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Insull
> **Who:** Edison's onetime secretary who built the modern electric utility — and whose empire's crash helped bring regulation. · **When and where:** Chicago, 1892–1932.
Took over Chicago Edison in 1892 and built Commonwealth Edison and a pyramid of holding companies serving millions; the pyramid collapsed in 1932 in the Depression, wiping out small investors. He fled abroad, was brought back in 1934, tried three times — fraud, bankruptcy violations, embezzlement — and acquitted every time. Candidate Franklin Roosevelt campaigned against "the Ishmael or Insull" in 1932; the crash fed the New Deal's utility laws (ETHW; EBSCO; Britannica). He appears as a character in The Current War (2017), but the film is not about him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-insull" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rivers into power: TVA and Hoover Dam" -->
- May 18, 1933: Roosevelt signed the act creating the Tennessee Valley Authority; work on Norris Dam began that October. Before TVA, about 3 in 100 Tennessee Valley families had electricity (Library of Congress; TVA).
- October 1936: Hoover Dam's first generator began sending power 266 miles to Los Angeles (transmission began October 9; the generator reached full operation October 26 — sources give both dates for "first"). The dam as a structure belongs to `landmarks`; the power is ours (HISTORY; EDN).
- Who was moved: from 1933 to 1945 TVA officials bought or took about 1.3 million acres and moved an estimated 82,000 people from their homes to build 16 hydroelectric dams. Norris Dam alone moved more than 3,000 families. TVA lawyer John Snyder described the method: "a price is set and we buy at that price or we condemn." To condemn land is to take it by law and pay a price the government sets (Facing South, 1995).
- Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia River, built by the Bureau of Reclamation and finished in 1941, had no fish ladders. Its reservoir drowned Kettle Falls, where Native people had fished for salmon for about 9,000 years. In June 1940 the Colville tribes held a three-day Ceremony of Tears there, and 8,000 to 10,000 people came (HistoryLink).
- Oil and Osage land: the Osage Nation kept the oil rights under its Oklahoma land in 1906. In the 1920s oil money made Osage headright holders rich. From about 1920 to 1925 more than 60 of them died in murders and suspicious deaths. Husbands, guardians and others killed them to inherit their shares of the oil money (Osage Nation). Native-nations and crime-justice tell it in full.
Shared with: landmarks (the dam itself) · government-politics (the New Deal) · land-environment (the drowned valleys) · native-nations (Kettle Falls, the Osage).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The night the lights came on" -->
In 1935, fewer than 11 farms in 100 had central-station electricity while most city homes were wired. Roosevelt created the Rural Electrification Administration by executive order May 11, 1935; the Rural Electrification Act of May 20, 1936 funded farmer-owned cooperatives to string the wires the companies would not. About half of farms had power by 1945, 9 in 10 by 1950, roughly 97 in 100 by 1960 (EH.net; Living New Deal; Britannica). A Tennessee farmer's line, recorded in 1930 and famous since: the greatest thing on earth is to have the love of God in your heart, and the next greatest thing is to have electricity in your house (documented in David Nye, *Electrifying America*; it titled the co-ops' own history, *The Next Greatest Thing*, 1984). The REA sent demonstrators like Louisan Mamer town to town — the "electric circus" — teaching farm families what the new power could do (Smithsonian NMAH).
Shared with: home-family (the kitchen and washday after the switch — they ceded the first night itself to us) · government-politics (the New Deal program).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pearl-yates" name="Pearl Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pearl Yates
> **Who:** A high school English teacher in rural western North Carolina whose home got electricity in July 1939. She told the story in a 1984 oral history. · **When and where:** Haywood County, North Carolina, 1939.
- Interview D-0044, Southern Oral History Program, UNC, recorded May 29, 1984 by Bonnie Bishop with Pearl and her husband Oral Yates, for the REA's 50th anniversary (UNC catalog).
- Before the power came she cooked on a wood stove and spent whole days washing clothes in a big pot. Carolina Power and Light would not run lines into their rural area, so neighbors formed a cooperative. Many were wary of it at first, and a door-to-door campaign signed them up (UNC catalog abstract).
- When the lights came on she walked from room to room with her small children: "We'd stop and look and they were so pretty and we'd go look at another room and another room" (HISTORY.com, journalism, citing the UNC collection).
- HISTORY.com calls her a farmer's wife. The UNC catalog lists her as a teacher. Use the catalog. Same collection: Lena Boyce and Sam Oswalt (bank era 08).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil wins a war" -->
Of the roughly 7 billion barrels of oil the Allies used in World War II, about 6 billion came from the United States (AOGHS). When German submarines sank coastal tankers, the government built the Big Inch and Little Big Inch pipelines from Texas to the East Coast (completed 1943–44); together they moved over 350 million barrels before the war in Europe ended (Texas State Historical Association; NIST). The fighting is `war`'s; the fuel is ours.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The atom joined the fuel supply, and for the first time Americans learned what it feels like when the fuel runs short. By the era's end the country had energy laws, an Energy Department, mileage standards — and a stalled nuclear industry.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Atoms for peace" -->
- December 20, 1951: the EBR-1 test reactor in Idaho generated the first usable electricity ever made from atomic energy — enough for four 200-watt bulbs (Idaho National Laboratory; DOE).
- July 17, 1955: nearby Arco, Idaho (population about 1,200) became the first town anywhere lit entirely by nuclear power — for about an hour, from the BORAX III reactor (Argonne/AEC press record).
- December 8, 1953: Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" speech at the UN set the civilian course. Shippingport, Pennsylvania — the first full-scale American nuclear power plant built solely for civilian electricity — sent its first power onto the Duquesne Light grid December 18, 1957 and hit its full 60,000 kilowatts December 23 (DOE; ASME landmark). Say the "first" carefully: first *full-scale, all-civilian* U.S. plant — smaller and dual-use reactors came earlier, and Britain's Calder Hall ran in 1956.
Shared with: elements (uranium and plutonium as elements) · science (the physics) · war (weapons stay there).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the uranium came from" -->
From 1944 to 1986 mining companies took nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore from Navajo land (EPA). About 10,000 people worked in uranium, about a quarter of them Navajo. Navajo miners say no one told them about the danger and no one gave them protective gear or fresh air in the mines. Uranium breaks down into radon, a radioactive gas, and breathing it caused lung cancer (Brugge and Goble, 2002). The Atomic Energy Commission bought all the uranium until 1966. After that, uranium also went to power plants. On July 16, 1979, a United Nuclear Corporation dam holding radioactive mill waste broke near Church Rock, New Mexico. About 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid ran down the Rio Puerco past about 1,700 Navajo people. New Mexico Governor Bruce King turned down the Navajo Nation's request for a federal disaster declaration (elements bank, from Richards 2013 and the state's report).
Shared with: elements (uranium, their lead) · native-nations (the nation and its land) · health (the disease).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dams and the Three Affiliated Tribes" -->
Garrison Dam on the Missouri River was finished in 1953 under the Army Corps of Engineers' Pick-Sloan Plan, for flood control and electric power. Its lake flooded about 152,000 to 155,000 acres (sources differ) of the Fort Berthold Reservation of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara. Farms, homes, schools, a hospital, churches, cemeteries and whole towns went under water, including Elbowoods. More than 300 families had to move, 80 to more than 90 percent of the tribes' people by different counts. At the 1948 signing, tribal chairman George Gillette said the treaty and the tribes' constitution were "being torn to shreds by this contract" (Cultural Survival, 2010. North Dakota Studies).
Shared with: native-nations (lead) · land-environment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1973: the fuel runs short" -->
October 1973 – March 1974: Arab oil producers embargoed the United States over its support of Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Oil went from about $2.90 to $11.65 a barrel — roughly quadrupled in six months. Drivers sat in blocks-long gas lines; many states rationed by license plate (odd numbers on odd days); Congress set a national 55 mph speed limit (Federal Reserve History; U.S. State Dept. history; Britannica). The laws followed: the Energy Policy and Conservation Act (December 22, 1975) created the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and the first car-mileage standards (a doubling to 27.5 mpg by 1985), and the Department of Energy opened October 1, 1977 (EBSCO; DOE history).
Shared with: america-world (the embargo's diplomacy, their lead) · economy (the inflation) · transportation (smaller cars).

### David Falconer
> **Who:** A photographer for the Environmental Protection Agency's DOCUMERICA project who photographed Oregon's gas shortage and wrote a caption for each picture. · **When and where:** Oregon, October 1973 to May 1974.
- His pictures and captions are held by the National Archives (EPA Project DOCUMERICA, 412-DA series).
- October 1973, Interstate 5 at Sutherlin: "By midday gasoline was becoming unavailable along interstate highways." At Lincoln City, stations closed early and on weekends, and "No Gas Today" signs went up.
- December 1973, Portland: a man who had run out of gas stood in line with a gas can.
- February 1974, Portland: drivers with even-numbered license plates lined up on even-numbered days, Monday to Friday. Anyone could try on Saturday. Portland stations closed on Sundays. Falconer's caption says Oregon was the first state to use the odd-even plan. That is his claim, not a checked fact.
- May 1974, after the embargo ended: a downtown Portland station still posted that its day's gas was sold out (Yale Energy History gallery, citing the National Archives). Drivers in the lines are not named in the records found.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-falconer" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three Mile Island, 1979" -->
March 28, 1979: the Unit 2 reactor at Three Mile Island, Pennsylvania, partially melted down — the worst commercial nuclear accident in U.S. history. Governor Thornburgh advised pregnant women and preschool children within five miles to leave; about 140,000 people left on their own amid confused official messages. The radiation released was small; follow-up studies found no detectable health effects, and no deaths are attributed to the accident — but the fear was real and documented, and it mattered: new-plant orders stopped, and no newly built U.S. reactor came online for over four decades (NRC backgrounder; World Nuclear Association; EIA). The accident and response as an event belong to `disasters` (their lead — parked there, incl. the Netflix documentary); the effect on what powered America is ours.
- Who ran the plant, and what failed: General Public Utilities operated Unit 2. A relief valve stuck open and let cooling water out. The operators had no gauge that showed the valve was open, so they cut back the water. At least 45 percent of the core melted. The average radiation dose to people within 10 miles was 0.08 millisieverts (World Nuclear Association).
Shared with: disasters (the accident, lead) · science (reactor physics) · health (the studies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black lung and the 1969 law" -->
On November 20, 1968, an explosion at the Consol No. 9 mine in Farmington, West Virginia, killed 78 coal miners. Congress then passed the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act, which took effect March 30, 1970. Federal inspectors had to check every underground coal mine four times a year and could close a mine that put lives in danger. The law also paid benefits to miners with black lung (MSHA). Black lung is lung disease from breathing coal dust. The dust scars the lungs until miners cough up black phlegm and struggle for every breath. More than 76,000 miners died of black lung from 1968 to about 2017, by the U.S. Department of Labor's count. After 2000 the worst form of the disease came back in Appalachia (Smithsonian Magazine, 2017). Coal miners killed in accidents fell from 311 in 1968 to 38 in 2000 (MSHA).
Shared with: health (the disease) · work-workers (the miners' fight for the law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Panels on the White House roof" -->
June 20, 1979: President Carter dedicated 32 solar water-heating panels on the West Wing roof. They were taken down in 1986 during roof repairs under President Reagan and never reinstalled — state it that plainly; some later went to Unity College in Maine (16 heated the cafeteria's water), one is in the Smithsonian (Smithsonian NMAH; Yale Climate Connections). Natural gas and oil use kept growing through the era regardless (EIA history).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The country now produces more energy than it uses — for the first time since 1957 — while trying to change what it burns. Gas passed coal, the sun and wind grew fast, the grid showed it could fail, and the atom got a small second chance. Current through 2026; state the cutoff.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fracking changes the arithmetic" -->
Texas oilman George Mitchell's company spent nearly two decades cracking the Barnett Shale, and engineer Nick Steinsberger's cheaper "slickwater" fracturing in the late 1990s made it pay. Hydraulic fracturing plus horizontal drilling then rewrote the numbers: U.S. shale gas rose from about 5 billion cubic feet a day in 2007 to about 80 in 2023, and in 2018 the United States became the world's largest crude-oil producer, overtaking Russia (EIA). In 2025 it pumped a record 13.6 million barrels a day (EIA, 2026) (Texas State Historical Association; industry and EIA histories). State the debate honestly, both sides: cheaper energy, jobs, and coal plants closing on one side; earthquakes tied to wastewater injection, water-contamination worries, and methane leaks on the other (USGS on induced earthquakes; the damage details belong to `land-environment`).
Shared with: land-environment (the environmental fight, their lead on damage) · economy (the boom) · big-business (the drillers).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gas passes coal; sun and wind climb" -->
- 2016: natural gas passed coal as the country's largest source of electricity for the first full year (EIA).
- In 2025 natural gas made about 41 percent of U.S. electricity, nuclear about 18, coal about 17, wind about 11 and solar about 7. All renewables together made about 24 percent (EIA, 2026). Wind and solar together set a record at about 17 to 18 percent. The two fastest-growing jobs in federal projections for 2024–34 are wind-turbine technician (+50 percent) and solar-panel installer (+42 percent) — though together they add under 20,000 jobs (BLS).
- Data centers, AI, and electric cars are pushing demand up again; EVs plus hybrids reached 20 percent of new-vehicle sales in 2024 and about 22 percent in 2025. Battery-only EV sales fell in 2025 for the first time, to under 6 percent a month after federal tax credits ended on September 30, 2025 (EIA, February 2026).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The grid fails in Texas, 2021" -->
February 2021, winter storm Uri: generation of every kind froze off the Texas grid; more than 4.5 million households — roughly 10 million people — lost power, some for days, in deep cold. ERCOT later said the grid came within 4 minutes 37 seconds of a total collapse that could have taken weeks to fix. The state's official death count is 246; researchers argue the true toll is higher — journalism and state records, labeled (FERC final report; Houston Public Media/KUT; Texas Tribune).
Shared with: disasters (the storm and deaths — coordinate lead at prose time; parked to them) · government-politics (the deregulated-grid argument).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coal and oil still cost lives and land" -->
- April 5, 2010: a gas and coal dust explosion at the Upper Big Branch mine in Montcoal, West Virginia, killed 29 miners, the worst U.S. mine disaster in 40 years. Massey Energy ran the mine. Federal mine inspectors named the company's "corporate culture" as the root cause and fined it $10.8 million (MSHA). In 2016 Massey's former chief, Don Blankenship, got the maximum one year in prison for conspiring to break mine safety laws. He was not convicted of causing the deaths (NPR).
- 2016 to 2017: the Standing Rock Sioux fought the Dakota Access oil pipeline where it crosses under Lake Oahe, their water supply. The Army Corps of Engineers halted the crossing on December 4, 2016. The next administration approved it in early 2017, and oil began to flow that year (native-nations bank, from ICT). Native-nations tells the camps and the nations who came.
Shared with: work-workers (the miners) · native-nations (Standing Rock, their lead) · land-environment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The atom's small revival, and 'energy independence'" -->
- Vogtle Unit 3 (July 31, 2023) and Unit 4 (April 29, 2024) in Georgia became the first newly built U.S. reactors in over 30 years (EIA; DOE).
- Two closed plants are trying to restart. Holtec International's Palisades plant in Michigan, shut in May 2022, had not set a restart date as of July 2026 (Canary Media). Constellation Energy plans to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1, the reactor that did not melt, in 2027 (2026 news reports). Check both before writing.
- In 2019 U.S. energy production (101.0 quadrillion Btu) exceeded consumption (100.2) for the first time since 1957 (EIA). Define "energy independence" honestly: the country still imports crude oil every day; independence here means producing more energy overall than it uses, not importing nothing.
- In 2025 the country exported a record 31 quadrillion Btu of energy and imported 21, so it sold 11 more than it bought (EIA, May 2026).

### Cristian Pavón Pineda
> **Who:** An 11-year-old sixth grader who died in his family's mobile home during the Texas blackout. · **When and where:** Conroe, Texas, February 16, 2021.
- The home had been without power since the Sunday before. Temperatures there fell to about 10 degrees. The family of five slept in one room to keep warm, and Cristian shared a bed with his younger brother. In the morning his family found him unresponsive (Texas Tribune, Al Día News, journalism).
- Early reports and the lawsuit said he died of hypothermia, a body temperature too low to keep a person alive. An autopsy on February 18 found he died of carbon monoxide poisoning, Conroe police said in May 2021 (Patch, journalism). Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. Breathed in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. The reports found do not say where the gas in the home came from.
- On February 22, 2021 his mother, Maria Elisa Pineda Guzman, sued ERCOT, the grid operator, and Entergy Texas, the power company, for more than $100 million. The suit says they did not tell families how long the outages would last. ERCOT said its operators made the right choice to avoid a statewide blackout (Texas Tribune, CBS News, Al Día). The records found do not say how the lawsuit ended.
