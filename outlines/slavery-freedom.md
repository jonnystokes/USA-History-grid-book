## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Some Native nations took captives in raids. In most of them a captive could be adopted into a family, and the status did not pass to the captive's children. Chattel slavery means holding a person as property for life and passing that status to the person's children. On the Northwest Coast the Tlingit, Haida and their neighbors did hold people that way, and they bought and sold them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="captive-taking, not chattel slavery" -->
- Captives taken in raids could be adopted into families and nations to replace the dead (the Haudenosaunee "mourning war" pattern is the best-described case); enslavement was a status that could end, and it did not automatically pass to children.
- **The honest exception:** on the Northwest Coast (Tlingit, Haida, and neighbors), slaves were property, were traded, and slave status was hereditary (Donald, *Aboriginal Slavery on the Northwest Coast*). Do not flatten in either direction.
- No documented named individual survives from this era's practice — no story block; say so rather than invent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="slavery-freedom" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish raiders enslaved Native people on the Florida and Carolina coasts from the early 1500s. In 1526 Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón brought about 100 enslaved Africans to his colony of San Miguel de Gualdape. In October of that year they rebelled and escaped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish slavery — and the first rebellion" -->
- Spanish raiders took Native captives from the Florida and Carolina coasts from the early 1500s; the encomienda forced Native labor and tribute (the 1542 "New Laws" curbed it on paper; forced labor continued).
- **San Miguel de Gualdape, 1526:** Ayllón's failed colony (Georgia or Carolina coast; exact site unknown) included about 100 enslaved Africans among 600–700 colonists. In October 1526 the enslaved Africans rebelled and escaped — the first documented rebellion of enslaved Africans in what became the United States; the escapees likely found refuge with nearby Native communities.
- **St. Augustine, founded 1565,** counted Africans, free and enslaved, among its first residents; Spanish law recognized certain rights that later English law would not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English law had no category for a person held as property for life. Between 1640 and 1705, judges and lawmakers in Virginia and Massachusetts created one. In 1662 Virginia's assembly ruled that a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life, whoever the father was.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1619 and the Middle Passage — the full telling lives here" -->
- **Late August 1619:** the English privateer *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes" (John Rolfe's words) at Point Comfort, Virginia — people taken from a Portuguese slave ship, originally from the Ndongo/Kongo region of West Central Africa. The exact date is disputed (late August / August 20 / about August 25) — state the range. Whether the 1619 arrivals were treated as servants or slaves the records do not settle — say so.
- **The Middle Passage, the numbers (SlaveVoyages database):** about 12.5 million Africans embarked on Atlantic slave ships over the whole trade; about 10.7 million survived the crossing — roughly 1.8 million people died at sea. Fewer than 4 percent of survivors came to mainland North America (about 305,000 directly from Africa; close to 388,000 counting the Caribbean route); most captives went to the Caribbean and Brazil.
- **The conditions:** chained below decks in spaces too low to stand; voyages of one to three months; disease, dehydration, and suicide killed steadily; the dead were thrown overboard.
- **The death rate per ship:** 13 to 19 percent of the people aboard died on the crossing (Lowcountry Digital History Initiative). Crewmen sexually assaulted enslaved women on the ships. About a quarter of the captives were children.
- **One survivor's words:** Olaudah Equiano (*Interesting Narrative*, 1789) was flogged by two crewmen for refusing food, saw children fall into the toilet tubs, and watched two chained men jump into the sea and drown. His African birth is disputed by one biographer (Carretta, 2005). State the dispute.
- **Who sailed the ships from here:** Rhode Island merchants sent out about 1,000 slaving voyages, about half of all slave ships from mainland North America, carrying about 106,000 people (Coughtry's count). The DeWolf family of Bristol led them.
- Shared with: `immigration` (hands off in one sentence — forced arrival is told here).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="from servitude to hereditary slavery — the legal hinge" -->
- **John Punch, 1640:** three servants ran away together; the two European men got added years of service; Punch, an African, got life — the first documented lifetime slavery sentence in Virginia (story below).
- **Johnson v. Parker, 1654–55:** a Virginia court ruled John Casor a servant for life — the first civil ruling in the English mainland colonies that a person who had committed no crime could be held for life. The man who won the case, Anthony Johnson, was himself a free African landowner; tell that complexity honestly.
- **Massachusetts Body of Liberties, 1641:** the first English mainland colony to give slavery statutory recognition. Slavery was a New England institution too.
- **Virginia, 1662 — *partus sequitur ventrem*:** a child's status follows the mother, a deliberate break from English common law. Slavery now reproduced itself.
- **Virginia, 1705:** the consolidated slave code — enslaved people as real property; baptism frees no one; no penalty for a master who kills while "correcting."
- **Who wrote the codes:** elected colonial assemblies. Virginia's General Assembly wrote the 1662 and 1705 laws. Carolina's assembly based its 1690 and 1696 codes on the Barbados code of 1661. The 1690 law allowed branding, nose-slitting and castration.
- **The punishment words, defined (policy 3b):** a flogging meant the person was stripped, tied by the wrists, and struck again and again on the bare back with a whip, usually a cowskin of twisted, dried cowhide, which cut the skin and left scars for life (Douglass, *Narrative*, ch. 1: Aaron Anthony whipping his Aunt Hester). Branding meant a red-hot iron pressed into the skin to burn a lasting mark.
- **The anti-literacy thread starts later:** no colony banned teaching enslaved people in the 1600s; the first statute is South Carolina's, 1740 (next era). The 1600s codes controlled bodies and status, not yet books.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial assemblies wrote harsher slave codes in these fifty years. South Carolina planters grew rich on rice, a crop that enslaved West Africans already knew how to grow. In 1739 about 20 enslaved men near Charleston rose up and marched toward Spanish Florida, where the king of Spain had promised freedom to people escaping the English colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="rice and the Lowcountry" -->
- South Carolina's rice economy was built on the knowledge and labor of West Africans from rice-growing coasts; planters paid premiums for captives from those regions. Charleston became the largest mainland arrival port.
- Sullivan's Island in Charleston harbor was the quarantine station for arriving slave ships. The claim that about 40 percent of enslaved arrivals passed over the island specifically is disputed by Charleston historians — carry Charleston's leading role, flag the island percentage.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stono, 1739, and the Negro Act of 1740" -->
- **September 9, 1739:** about 20 enslaved men led by Jemmy (described in a contemporary account as Angolan) seized weapons at the Stono River and marched south toward Spanish Florida, gathering recruits, with banners and drums. About 25 white colonists were killed; the militia killed roughly 35 to 50 of the rebels (counts vary — state the range). The largest slave uprising in the mainland colonies before the Revolution.
- **The Negro Act of May 10, 1740** answered it: no assembling, no growing food, no earning money, no moving freely — and the first anti-literacy statute in the future United States, banning teaching an enslaved person **to write** (reading was not named; the distinction matters and runs to 1865).
- **Who put Stono down, and how:** Lieutenant Governor William Bull raised the planters' militia and hired Chickasaw and Catawba men to hunt escapees. Colonists cut off rebels' heads and set them on stakes along the roads. Survivors were sold to the West Indies.
- **The 1740 act in its own terms:** killing an enslaved person became a misdemeanor punished by a fine. Any white person could arrest and punish a Black person under it.
- **The law of 1712 on runaways (South Carolina):** forty lashes for a first escape, the letter R branded on the cheek for a second, an ear cut off for a third, castration for a man's fourth, and the heel tendon cut or death for a fifth.
- **Land:** the Stono River is named for the Stono people. They and the Kiawah, Etiwan, Edisto, Combahee and about ten other groups held the Lowcountry when the English settled Charleston in 1670.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Mose, 1738 — freedom by running south" -->
- Spain had promised (1693) freedom to enslaved people escaping British colonies who reached Florida, accepted Catholicism, and served in the militia. In 1738, Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose — Fort Mose — rose about two miles north of St. Augustine: the first legally sanctioned free Black town in what is now the United States, roughly 100 people. Destroyed in the 1740 British siege, rebuilt 1752, evacuated to Cuba with the Spanish in 1763. Today a Florida state park.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York, 1712 and 1741" -->
- **April 6, 1712:** more than 20 enslaved people in New York City set a fire and killed 8 or 9 white people who came to fight it. Governor Robert Hunter called out the militia. Officials arrested more than 70 Black people and put 21 to death, most by burning alive and one by breaking on the wheel (the counts by method differ between sources).
- **1741:** after a string of fires, judges James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse tried about 200 people for a claimed plot. Officials burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 Black men and 4 white people (30 to 34 executions, by source). They shipped 70 to 84 more to slavery in the Caribbean. The main witness was a 16-year-old servant promised a reward.
- Slavery was a Northern institution too: about one New Yorker in five was enslaved in the early 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native people were enslaved too" -->
- English Carolina ran a large trade in enslaved Native people: an estimated 24,000 to 51,000 Native people of the Southeast enslaved between 1670 and 1715 (Gallay's range); in that period more Native slaves were exported from Charleston than Africans were imported there.
- Shared with: `native-nations` (what the trade did to the nations). The plain point here: the system enslaved Native people as well as Africans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Declaration of Independence of 1776 said that all men are created equal. The first census, in 1790, counted 697,624 people held in slavery in the new country. Enslaved people used the Revolution's words and wars to get free: some sued in court, some joined an army that promised freedom, and some ran.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Revolution's two promises" -->
- **Crispus Attucks**, a sailor of African and Wampanoag descent, was the first person killed at the Boston Massacre, March 5, 1770. Whether he was legally free or a fugitive from slavery is disputed — say so.
- **Dunmore's Proclamation, November 7, 1775:** freedom for enslaved men of rebel masters who joined the British; the "Ethiopian Regiment" grew to about 800. Over the whole war an estimated 20,000 Black people sided with or fled to the British; an estimated 9,000 served the Patriot side (about 5,000 in combat). Both promises of freedom were kept unevenly.
- **Freedom suits:** enslaved people sued. Elizabeth Freeman's case (story below) and the Quock Walker cases (1781–83) ended slavery in Massachusetts through its own courts, using the 1780 constitution's "all men are born free and equal."
- **The man who wrote "all men are created equal":** Thomas Jefferson owned Sally Hemings, his late wife's enslaved half-sister. She was 14 when she went to Paris in 1787 with his daughter, where he was the U.S. minister, and her son Madison said the relationship began there. She had no legal right to refuse him. Monticello's foundation concluded in 2000 that Jefferson was probably the father of her children. Four lived to adulthood.
- Shared with: `war` (the fighting and the armies); this chapter's slice is the freedom bargain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first abolitions — gradual, partial, real" -->
- Vermont's 1777 constitution banned holding adults in slavery — the first constitutional ban. Pennsylvania's gradual-abolition act (March 1, 1780) was the first abolition statute passed by an elected legislature anywhere: children born after it freed at 28. Massachusetts by court decision (1781–83); Connecticut and Rhode Island 1784; New York 1799 (fully free 1827); New Jersey last, 1804. Gradual meant slow — people remained enslaved in the North for decades.
- The **Northwest Ordinance (1787)** banned slavery north of the Ohio River.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Constitution's compromises, 1787" -->
- Three protections for slavery without using the word: the **three-fifths clause** (representation), the **fugitive clause** (escapees "held to Service or Labour" must be delivered up), and the bar on ending importation before **1808**. The 1808 clock mattered — it runs out in the next era.
- Shared with: `government-politics` (the Constitution as law and government).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Bringing enslaved people in from abroad became illegal on January 1, 1808. The number held in slavery still grew, mostly by birth, and reached 3,953,760 by 1860. Planters in the new cotton states bought more than a million people from Virginia, Maryland and the other older states, and those sales split families apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the scale" -->
- Census counts: 697,624 enslaved people in 1790; 3,953,760 by 1860 — growth mainly by birth after the 1808 ban. By 1860 the South grew about 75 percent of the world's cotton, more than half of U.S. exports by value.
- Shared with: `economy` (the cotton economy) · `technology` (the gin as machine) · `food-farming` (the crop).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the domestic slave trade — people as property, families broken" -->
- More than 1 million people moved from the Upper South to the Deep South between about 1790 and 1860 — roughly 200,000 a decade from 1820 — overland in chained coffles and by coastwise ship to New Orleans, the country's largest slave market.
- **The traders, by name:** Isaac Franklin and John Armfield ran the largest firm, from a walled pen on Duke Street in Alexandria, Virginia. They moved about 8,500 people from 1828 to 1836. They also sold enslaved women for sexual use (the "fancy" trade).
- **The land:** the Choctaw ceded more than 10 million acres in 1830 and the Chickasaw ceded theirs in 1832. Mississippi's enslaved population rose from 65,659 in 1830 to 195,211 in 1840 as enslavers brought people in to clear and plant that land.
- **Our lead — what sale did to families:** the trade separated nearly half of the people it moved from a spouse or a parent (EJI's accounting); about one enslaved marriage in three was destroyed by forced sale. Children were sold away from mothers. The "Information Wanted" ads of the 1860s–1900s (next era) are the after-record of these separations.
- Shared with: `migration` (leads on the forced journey — routes, coffles, ships, conditions; ruling 2).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="daily life under slavery" -->
- **Labor:** sunup to sundown, six days a week — the gang system on cotton and sugar, the task system in Lowcountry rice.
- **Food and clothing, from a primary source:** Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) records his plantation's monthly allowance — eight pounds of pork or the equivalent in fish, one bushel of corn meal — and a clothing issue so thin that young children often had only a long shirt. People stretched rations with gardens, fishing, and trapping where allowed.
- **Housing:** one-room cabins, dirt floors, board beds — described from memory in the WPA narratives (era 8).
- **Family under the auction block:** marriages had no legal standing; sale could end any family any day — the fact that organized daily life more than any other.
- **Sexual violence by enslavers, a named account:** Harriet Jacobs (*Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl*, 1861) wrote that Dr. James Norcom of Edenton, North Carolina, pursued her from her early teens. She hid in a crawl space above her grandmother's storeroom for seven years (1835 to 1842) before she escaped north.
- **Punishment, in the owners' own words:** in 1838 Micajah Ricks of Nash County, North Carolina, advertised for a runaway woman and wrote that he had "burnt her with a hot iron, on the left side of her face" to make the letter M. Runaway ads of the 1830s describe backs "much marked by the whip."
- **Worship and music:** Richard Allen, born enslaved, founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church (Philadelphia, organized 1816) — the first Black denomination; in the slave states, worship out of the owners' sight in brush arbors ("hush harbors"); the spirituals carried faith and coded meaning.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="reading made a crime" -->
- After Nat Turner's rebellion and the rise of the abolition press, states tightened or newly passed anti-literacy laws: Georgia 1829, Louisiana and North Carolina 1830, Virginia 1831, Alabama 1832, South Carolina tightened again 1834. Most, but not all, slave states had such laws. Penalties differed: fines for white teachers (North Carolina $200), fines and jail elsewhere; the whip for Black teachers and learners (North Carolina 39 lashes; Virginia up to 20). Several laws covered free Black people too.
- **Why a slaveholding state feared literacy:** passes could be forged, abolition newspapers read, the Bible read without an owner's editing; after 1831, the fear was organized revolt. **Say it plainly: the schooling itself was the crime.**
- **People learned anyway:** historians estimate roughly 5 to 10 percent of enslaved people could read by 1860 (some late-period estimates run to 20 percent — state the spread). Verified methods only: Douglass's trades of bread for lessons (story below); Milla Granson's midnight school in Natchez (story below); Susie King Taylor's secret Savannah schools (era 7 story). The "pit school" claim could not be verified and is dropped.
- Shared with: `education` (the schooling side — ruling 7) · `government-politics` (the statutes as law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="resistance and revolt" -->
- Everyday resistance: slowed work, broken tools, feigned illness, food taken — and above all running away. The runaway advertisements filling Southern newspapers are the system's own record of it (Freedom on the Move is digitizing thousands); ads often note the person "can read" or "can write and may forge a pass."
- **Gabriel's conspiracy, 1800 (Richmond):** betrayed hours before; 26 men hanged, Gabriel among them.
- **The German Coast uprising, January 8 to 10, 1811 (Louisiana):** Charles Deslondes led 64 to 125 people at the start (200 to 500 by some accounts) toward New Orleans. They killed two white men. Governor William Claiborne, General Wade Hampton's U.S. troops and a planters' militia under Manuel Andry killed about 95 of them, in battle and by execution after tribunals of planters. The militia set nearly 100 heads on pikes along the levee. Militiamen cut off Deslondes's hands, shot him and burned him alive, with no trial.
- **Denmark Vesey, 1822 (Charleston):** a free Black carpenter's planned rising, betrayed; 35 executed; Charleston's independent AME church torn down — the congregation that later became Mother Emanuel.
- **Nat Turner, August 21–23, 1831 (Southampton County, Virginia):** the deadliest slave revolt in U.S. history — at least 55 white people killed; in the panic afterward, militias and vigilantes killed more than 100 Black people, many with no part in it (some estimates run to 200 — state the range); 19 of those convicted were executed; Turner hanged November 11, 1831. The anti-literacy and anti-assembly wave above was the answer.
- **The *Amistad*, 1839–41:** kidnapped Mende captives seized the schooner at sea, led by Sengbe Pieh; jailed in Connecticut; the Supreme Court freed them in 1841; 35 survivors sailed home to Sierra Leone in 1842. Never enslaved on U.S. soil — the story here is the freedom fight in U.S. courts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Underground Railroad and the abolitionists" -->
- The network of guides, safe houses, and above all Black communities and churches; the current estimate is about 100,000 people helped over its decades — label it an estimate, and say many more tried and were caught.
- **Harriet Tubman:** escaped September 1849; returned about 13 times and brought out about 70 people, mostly family and friends. **The old "19 trips, 300 people" figure is a legend — state the correction** (NPS).
- **Frederick Douglass:** escaped 1838; *Narrative* 1845; the *North Star* 1847 — the century's most photographed and most heard abolitionist voice.
- **Free Black institutions:** *Freedom's Journal* (1827), the first Black-owned U.S. newspaper; the AME church; the vigilance committees that made the Railroad work.
- **Sojourner Truth:** walked to freedom in New York, 1826; when her five-year-old son was illegally sold south, she sued — and won his return (1828).
- Shared with: `news-communication` (the Black press as press; Douglass as publisher).

### Sojourner Truth
> **Who:** walked to freedom with her infant daughter, then sued to recover her son sold south — and won. · **When and where:** Ulster County, New York; walked free 1826; won Peter's return 1828; died 1883.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1850 members of Congress passed a law that forced people in the free states to help catch those who escaped. Fifteen years later, after the Civil War, the 13th Amendment ended slavery, and about four million people became free. Freedpeople searched for family, married in law, built schools and churches, and voted, until white terror campaigns and the withdrawal of federal troops in 1877 ended Reconstruction.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the crisis" -->
- **Fugitive Slave Act, September 18, 1850:** federal commissioners, no jury, the accused barred from testifying; the commissioner was paid 10 dollars for ruling a person a slave and 5 for ruling them free; bystanders could be compelled to help. It reached into the free states — free Black Northerners fled to Canada.
- **Anthony Burns, 1854:** arrested in Boston; on June 2, 1854, federal troops marched him through the city to a ship back to Virginia, past crowds draped in mourning black.
- ***Dred Scott v. Sandford*, March 6, 1857:** 7–2 against Dred and Harriet Scott — Black Americans were not and could never be citizens; Taney: Black people "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect."
- **Celia, 1855 (Missouri):** Robert Newsom bought Celia at 14 and raped her for five years. In June 1855 she killed him. Judge William Hall refused to tell the jury that the law letting a woman defend herself against rape covered an enslaved woman. She was hanged on December 21, 1855. (Details from search summaries of three agreeing sources. Open one before writing.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="freedom taken, not merely given" -->
- **Fort Monroe, May 1861:** three enslaved men — Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, James Townsend — rowed to the Union fort at Old Point Comfort (the same ground as 1619) rather than build Confederate batteries; General Butler called them "contraband of war" and refused to return them. Word spread; by war's end roughly half a million people had freed themselves by reaching Union lines. The freedom-seekers' name for the fort: "Freedom's Fortress."
- **Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863** — what it did: freed the enslaved in the states in rebellion, made the Union army an army of liberation wherever it advanced, opened enlistment to Black men. What it did NOT do: exempted the loyal border states (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri), Tennessee, and Union-held parts of Virginia and Louisiana — freedom there waited for state action or the 13th Amendment.
- **About 180,000 Black soldiers served in the U.S. Colored Troops** — roughly one Union soldier in ten by war's end — plus about 19,000 in the navy. Fourteen Black soldiers earned the Medal of Honor in a single battle (New Market Heights, September 29, 1864).
- **Peter, "The Scourged Back," 1863:** Peter escaped from John Lyons's plantation in St. Landry Parish, Louisiana, in March 1863 and reached Union troops at Baton Rouge. Army doctors found his back covered in thick raised scars. He said the overseer Artayou Carrier whipped him in October 1862 and that he lay two months in bed from the whipping and the salt brine poured on it. McPherson and Oliver photographed his back about April 2, 1863. *Harper's Weekly* printed it under the name "Gordon" (historians now think that story mixed up three men).
- **Harriet Tubman at Combahee Ferry, June 2, 1863:** guided Union gunboats up the river; more than 700 enslaved people carried to freedom in one night.
- **Juneteenth — June 19, 1865:** General Granger's General Order No. 3 at Galveston: "all slaves are free." More than 250,000 people were still enslaved in Texas, two and a half years after the Proclamation, because the army had not been there to enforce it. The date became the freedom anniversary.
- **The 13th Amendment, ratified December 6, 1865:** slavery abolished everywhere — "except as a punishment for crime." The exception clause matters; convict leasing walked through it (below).
- **The *Clotilda*'s people:** about 110 Africans smuggled into Mobile in July 1860, 52 years after the ban, were freed five years later; unable to afford passage home, about 30 founded Africatown (Kossola's story below).
- **The Five Tribes too:** the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole nations had also held enslaved Black people; their 1866 treaties freed them and promised the Freedmen citizenship — a promise still being fought over in era 10.
- Shared with: `war` (leads on the armies and the fighting; ours is the freeing) · `government-politics` (the amendments as law) · `native-nations` (the Five Tribes and sovereignty).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="freedom, day to day — the searches, the marriages, the names, the churches" -->
- **Searching for family:** freedpeople placed "Information Wanted" newspaper ads for decades, hunting for mothers, children, husbands, wives sold away; the Last Seen project (Villanova) has digitized more than 5,000. Reunions were rare and recorded with joy when they happened. (Thornton Copeland's ad, story below.)
- **Legalizing marriages:** the Freedmen's Bureau recorded thousands of marriages slavery had refused to recognize — couples married for decades stood up to be married in law. The registers survive at the National Archives.
- **Choosing surnames:** some kept a former owner's name to stay findable by sold-away family; others chose new ones — Freeman among them.
- **Churches:** hush-harbor congregations built their own buildings; the AME and AME Zion churches spread across the South within a decade. The church became the freedpeople's school hall, meeting hall, and political hall.
- **The Freedman's Savings Bank — a promise broken early:** chartered 1865 for freedpeople's savings; wrecked by mismanagement and speculation; failed June 1874 with about 3 million dollars belonging to some 61,000 depositors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="schools, the moment freedom came" -->
- The demand arrived with freedom, immediately: schools opened in churches, army camps, and confiscated buildings as soon as Union lines arrived — Susie King Taylor was openly teaching freed children on St. Simons Island, Georgia, in 1862, at age 14, while the war still ran (story below). Grandparents and grandchildren learned in the same rooms; adults filled night classes after full workdays.
- Freedpeople paid for it themselves where they could — school fees out of first wages, land and labor for schoolhouses — alongside Northern aid societies (the American Missionary Association foremost), whose teachers, many women and a growing number of them Black, came south by the thousands.
- **The Freedmen's Bureau's count: by 1870, more than 2,600 day and night schools with about 150,000 pupils** (broader counts that include every kind of school run higher — state the count and its scope).
- **The first Black colleges of the emancipation wave:** Fisk (opened January 1866), Howard (chartered 1867), about 25 institutions for freedpeople by 1872. (Cheyney's forerunner, Lincoln, and Wilberforce predate the war — `education` carries them as institutions.)
- **Schools burned:** in the Memphis Massacre (May 1–3, 1866) mobs and police killed 46 Black residents and burned every Black church and school in the city — 91 homes, 4 churches, 8 schools, by the congressional committee's count. Attacks on schools and teachers recur through Bureau records.
- **Who did it in Memphis, and the rapes:** the mobs included city policemen, firemen and businessmen. The congressional committee counted five women raped. Frances Thompson testified that seven white men, three of them police officers, raped her and her 16-year-old roommate Lucy Smith on May 1, 1866. No record found shows anyone prosecuted.
- **New Orleans, July 30, 1866:** Sheriff Harry Hays's deputized police and white mobs attacked about 200 Black marchers, most of them Union veterans, and the delegates at the Mechanics' Institute. They killed 34 Black people and 3 white delegates and wounded 136. General Philip Sheridan called it "an absolute massacre by the police."
- **Freedpeople wrote schooling into law:** the Reconstruction state constitutions of 1868–69 — drafted by the first public bodies in U.S. history with substantial Black membership — created the South's first statewide free public school systems, open to white children too.
- Shared with: `education` (leads on the schools as schools — ruling 7; the point here is that people forbidden to read made schooling one of freedom's first acts) · `rights-movements` (the later school campaigns).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black political power" -->
- After the 15th Amendment (ratified February 3, 1870), 16 Black men served in Congress within the decade — 14 representatives and 2 senators, all Southern Republicans. Hiram Revels was sworn in as the first Black U.S. senator February 25, 1870; Joseph Rainey became the first Black U.S. representative that December; Blanche K. Bruce served the first full Senate term (1875–81). Robert Smalls served five terms in the House.
- Across the South, about 2,000 Black men held office during Reconstruction; Foner's *Freedom's Lawmakers* documents more than 1,500 by name — give both the estimate and the documented count.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the promises broken" -->
- **"Forty acres":** Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15 (January 16, 1865) set aside about 400,000 acres for freedpeople in forty-acre plots; about 40,000 people settled. President Johnson reversed it that fall and returned the land to the former owners.
- **Black Codes:** Mississippi first, November 1865 — vagrancy laws requiring proof of employment, apprenticeship laws binding Black children, arrest and hired-out labor for violators. Congress answered with the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the 14th Amendment (ratified 1868).
- **Sharecropping:** without land, freedpeople farmed shares against advances at plantation-store prices — debt that rolled over year after year and bound families to the land.
- **Convict leasing:** through the 13th's crime exception, states arrested Black men under the new codes and leased them to planters, mines, and railroads — labor without wages, profitable to the states for decades.
- **Convict leasing in numbers (Alabama, 1875 to 1928):** state officials leased prisoners to coal companies including Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad and Sloss. Over 90 percent were Black. In one 1890s count, 181 of 745 county prisoners in the mines died in two years. Prison mining brought the state over $1 million in 1912.
- **Terror:** the Ku Klux Klan (founded December 1865, Pulaski, Tennessee) ran whipping, arson, and murder campaigns; the Colfax Massacre (April 13, 1873) killed between 62 and 153 Black men, most after surrender — the count itself is uncertain, state the range. The Enforcement Acts briefly broke the Klan; the will to enforce faded.
- **Colfax, who did it:** Christopher Columbus Nash led more than 150 armed white men against Black militiamen holding the Grant Parish courthouse. They fired a cannon, set the courthouse on fire, shot the men who came out, and shot prisoners in the head that night. A jury convicted three men. On March 27, 1876, the Supreme Court threw out the convictions in *United States v. Cruikshank*. No one was punished. 64 Parishes gives at least 60 and up to 150 dead.
- ***Plessy v. Ferguson*, May 18, 1896** (7–1, Harlan alone in dissent): "separate but equal" — the decision's date sits in this era, its shadow over the next.
- **The end:** the disputed 1876 election; the Compromise of 1877; federal troops withdrawn from the last Southern statehouses that April. The amendments stayed on paper; the power to enforce them was surrendered. **From this point the campaigns to win the rights back belong to `rights-movements`.**

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Slavery was over, but Southern lawmakers kept Black citizens segregated and kept almost all of them from voting. Sheriffs, judges and companies kept Black prisoners and debtors working without pay. From 1936 to 1938, writers paid by the federal government interviewed more than 2,300 people who had been born into slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jim Crow as slavery's successor" -->
- Segregation ordered daily life — schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, water fountains, courtrooms, cemeteries — under *Plessy*'s shelter. This chapter tells the system as slavery's successor; the campaigns against it are `rights-movements`'.
- **Disfranchisement by design:** Mississippi's 1890 constitution wrote the model (poll tax, literacy test, understanding clause); Louisiana 1898 added the grandfather clause. Louisiana's registered Black voters fell from 130,344 in 1896 to 1,342 by 1904.
- **Lynching as terror:** the Equal Justice Initiative documents more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings between 1877 and 1950 — counts differ by period and definition, so name the source with the number. The terror drove the Great Migration (`migration` tells the journey).
- **Forced labor continued:** convict leasing and debt peonage ran deep into the 20th century (Blackmon, *Slavery by Another Name*; PBS film 2012).
- **Dated cases:** 123 Black county prisoners leased to Alabama's Banner Mine died in an explosion in 1911. Alabama ended leasing in 1928. Peonage means forcing a person to work off a debt. In *Bailey v. Alabama* (January 3, 1911) the Supreme Court struck down Alabama's law that made quitting after a $15 advance a crime. Georgia kept such a law until 1942 and Florida until 1944.
- Shared with: `rights-movements` (leads on the fight against Jim Crow) · `migration` (the Great Migration).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the last survivors speak" -->
- The **WPA Federal Writers' Project (1936–38)** interviewed formerly enslaved people across 17 states — more than 2,300 first-person accounts and 500 photographs, held at the Library of Congress as "Born in Slavery."
- Separately, the Library of Congress holds **actual sound recordings** of people born in slavery ("Voices Remembering Slavery," recorded 1932–1975) — among them Fountain Hughes (story below).
- Zora Neale Hurston interviewed Kossola in 1927; *Barracoon* waited until 2018 for publication.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The 14th and 15th Amendments promised equal protection of the laws in 1868 and the vote in 1870. The Supreme Court's *Brown* decision in 1954, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 gave federal officials the tools to enforce those promises in the South. In 1976 Alex Haley's book *Roots* started a wave of Black families tracing their ancestors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="emancipation's unfinished business" -->
- *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954, unanimous) overturned *Plessy*'s doctrine in schooling; the **Civil Rights Act (1964)** and **Voting Rights Act (1965)** finally gave the 14th and 15th Amendments enforcement machinery — ninety-nine years after the 13th.
- Mississippi's legislature voted to ratify the 13th Amendment only in **1995** — and the paperwork was never filed; the omission stood for another 18 years (finished in the next era).
- **Keep the frame:** the movement's own story belongs to `rights-movements`; this chapter's angle is the unfinished business of emancipation.
- Shared with: `rights-movements` (leads on the movement) · `government-politics` (the laws as laws) · `education` (Brown as schooling).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture opened in 2016, and the International African American Museum opened in Charleston in 2023. Volunteers indexed 1.8 million names from Freedmen's Bureau records. Through 2026, lawmakers, judges and federal officials argued over reparations and over what national parks may show about slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="memory built in stone" -->
- The Smithsonian's **National Museum of African American History and Culture opened September 24, 2016**; its history galleries begin with the slave trade and hold a preserved slave cabin. The **International African American Museum opened June 27, 2023**, in Charleston at Gadsden's Wharf, a principal disembarkation site.
- **Juneteenth became a federal holiday June 17, 2021.** Shared with: `holidays` (how the country marks it); ours is what is being remembered.
- Mississippi's 1995 ratification of the 13th Amendment was finally **officially recorded February 7, 2013**, after two residents discovered the paperwork had never been filed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="repair debated" -->
- **H.R. 40** (a reparations study commission, named for the forty acres) has been introduced every Congress since 1989; the House Judiciary Committee advanced it for the first time in 2021.
- **Evanston, Illinois** created the first city-funded reparations program (2019 resolution; first 25,000-dollar housing grants approved 2021 from a 10-million-dollar fund, for victims of the city's 1919–1969 housing discrimination).
- **California's task force** delivered its final report June 29, 2023 — more than 115 recommendations, including a formal apology.
- **Updated to September 2026:** Rep. Ayanna Pressley reintroduced H.R. 40 on January 3, 2025. It has not passed. Governor Newsom signed California's formal apology in September 2024 and, on October 10, 2025, a law creating a state Bureau for Descendants of American Slavery (no payments yet). Maryland's legislature overrode Governor Wes Moore's veto on December 16, 2025, to create a reparations study commission. In Evanston, 137 people had received $3.47 million by August 2026. Judicial Watch sued the city in May 2024, and the U.S. Department of Justice moved to join the suit on June 16, 2026. The case was still open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the ledgers and the records opened" -->
- **The GU272:** in 1838 the Maryland Jesuits sold 272 enslaved people to Louisiana plantations, partly to keep Georgetown College solvent — among the best-documented mass sales in U.S. history. Georgetown announced descendant admissions preference and renamings in 2016; more than 8,000 descendants identified; in 2021 the Jesuits and descendant leaders created the Descendants Truth & Reconciliation Foundation with a 100-million-dollar pledge.
- **The records opened:** volunteers indexed about 1.8 million names from the Freedmen's Bureau records (searchable at discoverfreedmen.org) — for millions of Black Americans, the first documents where ancestors appear by name.
- **The *Clotilda* found:** the wreck was confirmed in the Mobile River in 2019; Africatown's descendant community is the subject of *Descendant* (2022).
- **The Freedmen of the Five Tribes:** a 2017 federal ruling (*Cherokee Nation v. Nash*) held the Cherokee Nation to its 1866 treaty — Freedmen descendants are entitled to full tribal citizenship; the nation's supreme court struck "by blood" language in 2021. Shared with: `native-nations` (leads on sovereignty and citizenship politics).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the fight over what the parks show, 2025 to 2026" -->
- On March 27, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an executive order titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." Interior Secretary Doug Burgum ordered a review of park exhibits. In September 2025 officials ordered the photograph of Peter's scarred back taken down at Fort Pulaski National Monument in Georgia.
- Officials removed the slavery displays at the President's House site in Philadelphia, where Ona Judge escaped, in January 2026. A federal judge ordered them restored on February 16, 2026. An appeals court later upheld the government.
- On June 12, 2026, Judge Angel Kelley ordered removed exhibits restored at all national park sites. The First Circuit paused her order on July 2, 2026. The case was still open in September 2026. Re-check before writing an outcome.

She appears in *Descendant*, Margaret Brown's documentary, released on Netflix on October 21, 2022. Facts come from her own on-the-record interviews (Smithsonian 2022, National Geographic 2019).
