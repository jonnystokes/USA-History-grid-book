## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A continent full of working technology — engineered systems in wood, stone, bone, clay, and hide rather than metal machinery. Tools were matched precisely to place and purpose, and some were rebuilt and improved over thousands of years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="weapons and food machines" -->
- The bow and arrow replaced the atlatl (spear-thrower) across western North America around A.D. 600–700; south of the 55th parallel the atlatl vanished from the record fast, while far northern hunters kept both for over 1,000 years (2026 study, Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory — journalism-reported).
- Fish weirs as fixed fishing machines: the wooden stake alignments under Boston's Boylston Street were built in tidal channels roughly 4,700–3,700 years ago and used for over a thousand years (EBSCO; Ancient Fishweir Project).
- Agriculture as an engineered system: the interplanted corn-beans-squash fields (Three Sisters), plus controlled burning to shape the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="craft and calendar" -->
- Pottery, basketry, hide tanning, and the birchbark canoe as built craft (the canoe as construction is here; travel on it → `transportation`).
- Cahokia's Woodhenge: five successive circles of upright posts, about A.D. 900–1100, 24 to 72 posts each, up to 145 meters across, marking sunrise positions through the year — a calendar built in wood (Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site).
- Lake Superior copper worked cold for thousands of years — `elements` leads that story.
Shared with: native-nations (the peoples and their systems) · food-farming (what the fields grew) · elements (the copper).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
New tools arrived from Europe — and they arrived ahead of most Europeans, passed inland along Native trade routes from coastal contacts. No workshop or mill existed yet in this land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="kettle metal, new tools" -->
- Smelted European copper and brass reached the East coast by the 1500s; in 1524 Verrazzano recorded sheets of worked copper among Northeast coastal peoples (NativeTech; archaeological sourcing studies).
- Native smiths cut European kettles into arrow points, knives, pipe stems, and ornaments — adopting the metal into their own toolkits, not the European use (Smithsonian NMAI "Journey of Trade Goods").
Shared with: native-nations (trade and adaptation as their own choice) · marketplace (the trade itself).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first colonial machines ran on falling water and did the heaviest jobs: grinding grain, sawing wood, and hammering iron.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="iron from water power" -->
- Falling Creek, Virginia: the Virginia Company sent ironworkers in 1619; the furnace was ready about 1621–22, and in the Powhatan attacks of March 22, 1622, 27 people were killed there and the works destroyed — the first American ironworks never shipped its iron (Virginia DHR).
- Saugus, Massachusetts: the first sustained, integrated ironworks in British colonial America ran 1646–1670 — blast furnace, forge, rolling and slitting mill, all waterwheel-driven (National Park Service). `elements` has the iron; the machinery is ours.
Shared with: elements (iron the metal) · work-workers (the ironworkers' jobs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Cambridge press" -->
The first printing press in the English colonies reached Cambridge, Massachusetts in 1638 — bought by Rev. Jose Glover, who died on the crossing; owned by his widow Elizabeth Glover; run by locksmith Stephen Daye. Its first full book, the Bay Psalm Book (1640), was the first book printed in what is now the United States (Library of Congress). The PRESS as machine is ours; the press as news → `news-communication`.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial workshops multiplied — furnaces, forges, mills — and a Philadelphia printer began inventing on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Franklin stove" -->
Franklin designed his metal-lined fireplace-stove in 1741–42 (sources give both years) to send more of the fire's heat into the room and less up the chimney — and refused to patent it, or any invention, saying ideas should serve everyone freely (Franklin Institute). Colonial iron furnaces and forges spread through Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-technology" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** Printer, inventor of the Franklin stove (1741–42) and later the lightning rod; refused all patents. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1706–1790. · **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
The working craftsman-inventor: the stove in this era, the lightning rod in the next. His electrical science → `science`; his newspaper and almanac → `news-communication`. The film is Ken Burns's two-part PBS documentary.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="technology" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The new nation's first machines arrive: a rod that tames lightning, a gin that reshapes slavery's economics, and a spinning mill rebuilt from one man's memory.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the lightning rod" -->
Franklin proposed protecting buildings from "electrical fire" in 1750; after the 1752 demonstrations, the first lightning rods went up in Philadelphia (Franklin Institute). The kite and the science → `science`; the rod on the roof is ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the cotton gin and what it did" -->
Whitney's 1793 machine (patent March 14, 1794) pulled cotton fiber off its seeds — the hand-cleaning bottleneck gone. Production rose from under 9,000 bales a year around 1790 to over 200,000 by 1800 and about 4 million by 1860; the enslaved population grew from 697,897 (1790 census) to nearly 4 million (1860). The machine multiplied the demand for enslaved labor — say it plainly, then hand off.
Shared with: slavery-freedom (lead — the system and the lives inside it) · economy (cotton as the export).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="interchangeable parts — the honest version" -->
Whitney took a federal musket contract in 1798 and promoted machine-made interchangeable parts; the famous 1801 demonstration is legend-encrusted. Historian Robert Woodbury (1960) showed the idea came from Europe first, Whitney's own factory never achieved it, and the first real American production use was John H. Hall's rifle works at Harpers Ferry armory by 1819 (Linda Hall Library; Commonplace). Tell the armory version, not the myth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="eli-whitney" name="Eli Whitney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eli Whitney
> **Who:** Built the cotton gin (1793, patented 1794); promoted — but did not achieve — interchangeable parts. · **When and where:** Georgia and New Haven, Connecticut, 1765–1825.
The gin and its consequence, told honestly; the interchangeable-parts legend corrected in the same breath.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eli-whitney" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-slater" name="Samuel Slater" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Slater
> **Who:** Arkwright-trained English mechanic who rebuilt British spinning machinery from memory. · **When and where:** Derbyshire, England → Pawtucket, Rhode Island; mill running December 1790.
British law barred machinery exports and the emigration of skilled textile workers; Slater left in 1789 without declaring his trade and rebuilt the carding and spinning machines from memory with local craftsmen, funded by Almy & Brown (EBSCO). The start of the American factory. Child mill workers → `work-workers`.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Machines began doing what had always needed a person's hands — sewing, reaping, plowing — and in 1844 a message moved faster than any horse could carry it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the telegraph, 1844" -->
Morse sent "What hath God wrought" (chosen by Annie Ellsworth, from Numbers 23:23) from the U.S. Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore on May 24, 1844 (Senate historical office; Smithsonian NMAH holds the tape). Vail built and improved the instruments; historians still disagree how much of the dot-dash alphabet is his — state the disagreement. What the telegraph did to news → `news-communication`.
Shared with: news-communication (news off the stagecoach) · big-business (the wire companies later).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="machines for field, home, and eye" -->
- McCormick's reaper: first public demonstration 1831, patent 1834. Deere's self-scouring steel plow, 1837. What they did to farms → `food-farming`.
- Howe's lockstitch sewing machine, patented September 10, 1846; Singer sold the practical version; courts upheld Howe in 1854, and in 1856 the makers pooled patents in the Sewing Machine Combination (Library of Congress).
- Goodyear's rubber-and-sulfur mix met a hot stove in 1839 and charred instead of melting; accounts differ on exactly how the rubber hit the stove — the accident is documented, the details vary. Patent 1844 (Britannica).
- Daguerre's camera process reached the U.S. in fall 1839; Robert Cornelius's Philadelphia self-portrait that October/November is the earliest surviving American portrait photograph (Library of Congress). Photography as art → `art`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-morse" name="Samuel Morse" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Morse
> **Who:** Painter turned inventor who made the electric telegraph work over distance. · **When and where:** Washington–Baltimore line, May 24, 1844.
The first long-distance message and the partner question: Vail's instruments were essential, his share of the code is disputed — tell both honestly.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-morse" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="mccormick-deere" name="Cyrus McCormick and John Deere" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cyrus McCormick and John Deere
> **Who:** The reaper (demonstrated 1831) and the self-scouring steel plow (1837). · **When and where:** Virginia; Grand Detour, Illinois.
Two machines that replaced field muscle; by 1846 Deere sold near 1,000 plows a year (Britannica; ASME landmark).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mccormick-deere" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Invention became an organized business — patents, lawsuits, and the first laboratories built to invent on schedule. This era's famous claims need the honest version: Bell's same-day rival, Edison's forty predecessors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the telephone, 1876" -->
On February 14, 1876, Bell's attorney filed his application in the morning; Elisha Gray's caveat for a similar liquid-transmitter instrument arrived hours later. Patent 174,465 issued to Bell March 7; his notebook records the first spoken sentence on March 10 ("Mr. Watson — come here — I want to see you"). The priority dispute is still argued (Library of Congress; IEEE Proceedings 2020) — present the filing as fact, Bell as patent holder, the controversy as real.
Shared with: news-communication (talking replaces writing for news) · big-business (the Bell company).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-graham-bell" name="Alexander Graham Bell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Graham Bell
> **Who:** Speech teacher who patented the telephone. · **When and where:** Boston, 1876.
The patent, the first call, and the Gray dispute told straight.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-graham-bell" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="emma-nutt" name="Emma Nutt" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emma Nutt
> **Who:** The first woman telephone operator. · **When and where:** Boston, hired September 1, 1878.
Hired from a telegraph office at $10 a month for a 54-hour week; her sister Stella followed the same day. By the late 1880s operating was an almost entirely female occupation — one of the first big technology jobs open to women (New England Historical Society). The operator's working day at scale → `work-workers`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emma-nutt" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Edison's invention factory" -->
Edison did not invent the first electric light — inventors had worked on incandescent lamps for 40 years. His Menlo Park team (Batchelor, Kruesi, Upton, and others) made the first PRACTICAL one — carbonized cotton-thread filament, about 14.5 hours, October 1879 — and, more important, the whole working system: lamp, dynamo, wiring, meter, and the Pearl Street station (September 4, 1882) lighting the first paying district (U.S. Dept. of Energy; IEEE Milestone). Phonograph: built by Kruesi to Edison's design, first recording December 6, 1877 (Rutgers Edison Papers). The current war and the grid → `energy`.
Shared with: energy (lead — Pearl Street as power, AC vs DC) · home-family (the lit home).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-edison-technology" name="Thomas Edison" movie="Edison (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Edison
> **Who:** Improver and system-builder — the practical light bulb, the phonograph, and the invention laboratory itself. · **When and where:** Menlo Park and West Orange, New Jersey, 1870s–1931. · **Movie:** Edison (2015)
The honest framing: he ran the first industrial invention lab and built the system that made electric light pay — he did not invent the light bulb from nothing. The film is the PBS American Experience documentary.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-edison-technology" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lewis-latimer" name="Lewis Latimer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lewis Latimer
> **Who:** Self-taught draftsman and engineer; patented a better way to manufacture carbon filaments. · **When and where:** Boston and New York, 1848–1928.
Son of parents who escaped slavery; drew the drawings for Bell's 1876 telephone patent; patent 252,386 (January 17, 1882) for a filament manufacturing process at Maxim's U.S. Electric Lighting Co.; joined Edison in 1884 — drawings, expert testimony, lighting installations from New York to London; the only Black member of the Edison Pioneers. He did not invent the bulb or the filament — his real record needs no inflation (Rutgers Edison Papers; Lewis Latimer House).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lewis-latimer" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="granville-t-woods" name="Granville T. Woods" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Granville T. Woods
> **Who:** Black inventor of the Synchronous Multiplex Railway Telegraph — messages between moving trains and stations. · **When and where:** Cincinnati, patent November 29, 1887.
Won patent interference proceedings twice against claims pressed by Edison's side and turned down Edison's job offer; dozens of electrical and railway patents (National Inventors Hall of Fame; APS News).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="granville-t-woods" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nikola-tesla-technology" name="Nikola Tesla" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nikola Tesla
> **Who:** Serbian immigrant engineer whose AC induction motor turned electricity into rotating work. · **When and where:** New York; patents issued May 1888.
Westinghouse licensed Tesla's polyphase patents in July 1888 ($60,000 plus a $2.50-per-horsepower royalty). The motor is ours; the current war and the grid → `energy` (its Westinghouse-and-Tesla story).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nikola-tesla-technology" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the office and the fence line" -->
The Sholes typewriter (patented 1868; sold by Remington from 1874 at $125, carrying the QWERTY keyboard still in use — Library of Congress). Glidden's barbed wire patent (November 24, 1874) fenced the open range — what that did to the Plains → `land-environment` and `native-nations`.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Machines took to the air, moved into the kitchen, and — in the last five years of the era — began to compute. Manufacturing itself became an invention.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-technology" name="The Wright brothers" movie="Wright Brothers' Flying Machine (2003)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers
> **Who:** Dayton bicycle builders who invented the controllable powered airplane. · **When and where:** Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, December 17, 1903. · **Movie:** Wright Brothers' Flying Machine (2003)
Four flights that day: Orville first — 120 feet, 12 seconds; Wilbur's fourth — 852 feet, 59 seconds; five witnesses, and lifesaver John T. Daniels took the photograph (Library of Congress; NPS). Ours is the machine: homemade wind tunnel, three-axis control, a light gasoline engine from their own shop. Flight as travel → `transportation`. The film is the PBS NOVA documentary about the machine.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-technology" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-technology" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford
> **Who:** Made the moving assembly line the way things get built. · **When and where:** Highland Park, Michigan; chassis line started October 7, 1913. · **Movie:** Henry Ford (2013)
Magneto line in spring 1913, then the chassis line: 140 workers along 150 feet, the work moving past them; chassis time fell from over 12 hours to about 93 minutes. January 1914: the $5, eight-hour day, against about $2.34 for nine hours (The Henry Ford; Detroit Historical Society). The line as method is ours; the speed-up as a job → `work-workers`; the Model T → `transportation`. The film is the PBS American Experience documentary.
Shared with: work-workers (the line as a job) · transportation (the car, lead) · big-business (Ford the company).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-technology" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the wired home" -->
- De Forest's Audion (1906), the first triode vacuum tube, made radio receivers practical — the DEVICE is ours; broadcasting → `news-communication`.
- Philo Farnsworth, age 21, transmitted the first all-electronic television image (a straight line) September 7, 1927 in his San Francisco lab (EBSCO; FoundSF).
- GE's Monitor Top refrigerator (1927, about $300): the first widely sold home electric refrigerator — over a quarter million by mid-1929 (Albany Institute). What appliances did to housework → `home-family`.
- Bakelite (Baekeland, 1907; patent 1909): the first fully synthetic plastic; nylon followed from DuPont in the 1930s — the chemist Carothers is `styles`' story.
Shared with: home-family (appliances in the home) · energy (the wires that fed them) · styles (what plastic looked like).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first computers and the transistor" -->
- ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, unveiled at the University of Pennsylvania, February 1946.
- The transistor: Bardeen and Brattain first amplified a signal with a solid-state device December 16, 1947 at Bell Labs, in Shockley's group; announced June 30, 1948; Nobel Prize 1956 (Computer History Museum; IEEE Milestone). The physics → `science`; the device that replaced the vacuum tube is ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="eniac-programmers" name="The six ENIAC programmers" movie="The Computers (2014)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The six ENIAC programmers
> **Who:** Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty Mauchly Antonelli, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence, and Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum — the women who programmed the first general-purpose electronic computer. · **When and where:** University of Pennsylvania, 1945–46. · **Movie:** The Computers (2014)
They made ENIAC actually run — and were not invited to its celebration dinner. Their names stayed out of the story for decades until the ENIAC Programmers Project recovered them (Penn Today). The film is a documentary short.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eniac-programmers" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ernest-lawrence-technology" name="Ernest Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ernest Lawrence
> **Who:** Built the cyclotron — the machine that smashed atoms by whirling them in a spiral. · **When and where:** Berkeley, California, 1930–31 onward.
His first model — glass, sealing wax, and bronze — fit in one hand; the 60-inch machine of 1939 carried a 220-ton magnet (LBL; APS News). The machine is ours; the elements it made → `elements` (its Lawrence story).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="technology" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The machine that mattered most got smaller every year — room, desk, lap, pocket — and in 1969 two of them spoke to each other across California.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the chip — two inventors" -->
Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) demonstrated the first integrated circuit September 12, 1958 — germanium, hand-wired. Robert Noyce (Fairchild) designed the planar silicon version in 1959 that could be printed and mass-produced — the form every later chip took. Both are credited; Kilby's 2000 Nobel came with his own statement that Noyce (d. 1990) deserved a share (Britannica; NobelPrize.org). Silicon the element → `elements`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="grace-hopper" name="Grace Hopper" movie="The Queen of Code (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Grace Hopper
> **Who:** Navy officer and mathematician who taught computers to take instructions closer to English — the compiler. · **When and where:** Harvard Mark I from 1944; A-0 compiler 1951–52; retired a rear admiral in 1986 at 79, then the Navy's oldest active-duty officer. · **Movie:** The Queen of Code (2015)
Wrote the first extensive programming manual (1946); her FLOW-MATIC (1955–59) shaped COBOL (IEEE Milestone; Naval History and Heritage Command). The film is a short documentary.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="grace-hopper" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the personal computer" -->
The Altair 8800 kit (announced January 1975) set off the hobby; the Homebrew Computer Club first met March 5, 1975 in a Menlo Park garage — about 30 people in a rainstorm. Apple founded April 1, 1976; the Apple II (June 1977) put a finished computer in homes; the IBM PC (August 12, 1981) set the office standard (Computer History Museum; Library of Congress; IEEE Spectrum).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jobs-wozniak" name="Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak" movie="Steve Jobs (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak
> **Who:** Founded Apple (April 1, 1976); Wozniak designed the Apple I and II, Jobs built the company — and later the iPhone era. · **When and where:** Los Altos and Cupertino, California, from 1976. · **Movie:** Steve Jobs (2015)
Both came out of the Homebrew hobbyist wave. The listed film is a DRAMATIZATION (Boyle/Sorkin, from Isaacson's book) — label it in prose; documentaries: Steve Jobs: The Man in the Machine (2015), and Something Ventured (2011) on Apple's first funding.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jobs-wozniak" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-felsenstein" name="Lee Felsenstein" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="candidate" -->
### Lee Felsenstein
> **Who:** Engineer who moderated the Homebrew Computer Club's meetings — the documented voice of the hobbyist room the famous founders came out of. · **When and where:** Menlo Park and Berkeley, from 1975.
Oral history preserved by the Computer History Museum; candidate pending a fuller pass through the CHM interview.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-felsenstein" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the network" -->
- ARPANET: October 29, 1969, UCLA student Charley Kline sent the first host-to-host message toward Stanford Research Institute; the system crashed after "L-O" — the first message was "LO"; the full login worked an hour later (UCLA; Kleinrock's account). Defense-funded university infrastructure; switched to the internet's shared protocol (TCP/IP) January 1, 1983.
- The web is NOT an American invention: Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist at CERN in Switzerland, opened it to the public August 6, 1991. America's precise role: the internet underneath grew from ARPANET, and Mosaic — the first widely used graphical browser — came from the University of Illinois' NCSA, April 22, 1993 (CERN; NCSA).
- GPS: first satellite 1978, full constellation 1995; on May 1, 2000 the U.S. ended the deliberate blurring of civilian signals.
Shared with: news-communication (what the net did to news) · science (the physics and labs behind it).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="marty-cooper" name="Martin Cooper" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Martin Cooper
> **Who:** Motorola engineer who made the first handheld cell phone call — to his rival at Bell Labs. · **When and where:** A Manhattan sidewalk, April 3, 1973.
He called Joel Engel at Bell Labs to tell him Motorola had a working handheld. The commercial DynaTAC 8000X followed in 1983: $3,995, 28 ounces, about 30 minutes of talk (EBSCO; anniversary press coverage — journalism).

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="technology" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The general-purpose machine became a phone in every pocket, and by the 2020s it began writing and drawing on request. Current through 2026 — state the cutoff.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the smartphone" -->
The iPhone: announced January 9, 2007, on sale June 29, 2007 ($499/$599 with contract); over a million sold in 74 days (journalism — EDN, MacRumors). Computer, camera, phone, map, and music player folded into one machine. Social media platforms grew on top of it — what the feed did to news → `news-communication`; buying by phone → `marketplace`.
Shared with: news-communication (the feed) · marketplace (shopping by phone) · home-family (the phone in family life).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

In 2012 the AlexNet neural network won the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a wide margin (15.3% vs 26.2% error), starting the deep-learning era (the 2012 NeurIPS paper). On November 30, 2022 OpenAI released ChatGPT; about a million users in five days, an estimated 100 million monthly within two months (journalism — label). These systems write, draw, and code from plain-language requests, and also make documented errors — state both.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="batteries, printers, and gene tools" -->
- The battery in the electric car, honestly: Whittingham's 1970s Exxon work in New Jersey began rechargeable lithium; Goodenough (American, then at Oxford) found the key cathode in 1980; Yoshino and Sony in Japan made and sold the first commercial lithium-ion cells (1991); Nobel 2019 to all three (NobelPrize.org). The vehicle → `transportation`; the grid → `energy`.
- 3D printing: Chuck Hull's stereolithography (invented 1983–84, patented 1986) spread to hobby desks in the 2010s (National Inventors Hall of Fame).
- CRISPR gene editing (2012) noted only as shared: the science → `science`, the medicine → `health`.

### Limor Fried
> **Who:** MIT-trained engineer who founded Adafruit Industries in her dorm room (2005) — open-source electronics kits for the maker generation. · **When and where:** MIT, then New York City, 2005–present.
First female engineer on the cover of Wired; runs a 100%-woman-owned electronics company with over 100 employees (Adafruit; Manufacturing Institute). A documented picture of who builds with technology now.
