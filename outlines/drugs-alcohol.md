## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People in this land used plants that change how the body and mind feel for thousands of years before 1500. Tobacco is the oldest one found so far. Burned tobacco in a hearth at the Wishbone site, in the desert west, is about 12,300 years old. Most of this use was part of prayer and ceremony. Alcohol was different. Before Europeans came, people brewed beer and wine mainly in the Southwest. Elsewhere only a few scattered nations made it. Nobody here distilled strong liquor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco for prayer" -->
- Wishbone site, about 12,300 years ago: burned tobacco in a hearth, 9,000 years older than any earlier find. How the people there used it is not known (Duke et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022).
- A pipe from central Washington State, about 1,430 years old, held traces of a wild native tobacco mixed with sumac leaves (Brownstein et al., 2020).
- Traditional tobacco, mostly the plant *Nicotiana rustica*, was an offering. It was burned in a fire or smoked in a pipe to carry prayers, and the smoke was usually not breathed into the lungs (National Native Network, "Keep It Sacred"). Native health programs today call this traditional tobacco and keep it separate from commercial cigarettes. Practice differed from nation to nation.
- Shared with: `religion` (ceremony as worship) · `food-farming` (tobacco as a crop, from 1612). This chapter's slice is the plant as something taken into the body.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Drink, cacao, peyote and datura" -->
- Black Drink: a tea of yaupon or dahoon holly leaves, full of caffeine. Chemists found its traces in beaker cups at Cahokia, in Illinois, from A.D. 1050 to 1250. The holly grows far to the south, so people carried the leaves north. In later centuries, records describe men in the Southeast drinking it in a ceremony to make themselves pure (Crown et al., *PNAS*, 2012).
- Cacao, the bean chocolate is made from: traces in jars at Pueblo Bonito, Chaco Canyon, New Mexico, about A.D. 1000 to 1125. The beans came by trade from Mexico or Central America (Crown and Hurst, *PNAS*, 2009). Caffeine and cacao drinks turn up at 18 sites across the Southwest from about 750 to 1400.
- Peyote: a small cactus whose top contains mescaline, a drug that causes visions. Two dried peyote tops from Shumla Cave on the Rio Grande in Texas are about 5,700 years old and still hold mescaline (El-Seedi et al., 2005).
- Datura: a flowering plant that holds scopolamine and atropine, drugs that cause hallucinations (seeing and hearing things that are not there). People chewed wads of it in Pinwheel Cave, California, under a painting that the researchers read as its flower. Native Californians are recorded using datura to enter a trance. The dates run from late prehistory into Spanish times (Robinson et al., *PNAS*, 2020).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beer and wine in the Southwest" -->
- Before Europeans arrived, alcohol use in what is now the United States was "primarily confined" to nations in the Southwest, with scattered use elsewhere (Patrick J. Abbott, 1996). The drinks were beer and wine. Most Native nations first met alcohol when Europeans brought it in the 1500s and 1600s.
- The Akimel O'odham and Tohono O'odham (called the Pima and Papago in older books): each late summer the women picked the red fruit of the saguaro cactus. They made most of it into jam and the last of the harvest into wine for the rain ceremony at their New Year. Many Tohono O'odham villages still held the ceremony in the 1990s (Abbott, 1996).
- The Apache made a corn beer called tiswin, learned from peoples of northern Mexico. The Apache reached the Southwest sometime between 1000 and 1550, so the start of tiswin is not firmly dated.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s Europeans carried tobacco home from the Americas, and Spanish colonists brought wine to Florida. Tobacco reached the French court in 1560. By 1588 English men and women were taking it too. The Spanish at St. Augustine, founded in 1565, shipped in wine from Spain in clay jars. Few Native nations in this land had met alcohol yet, and no regular trade in it began in this century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco reaches Europe" -->
- 1560: Jean Nicot, the French ambassador in Portugal, sent tobacco seeds to Queen Catherine de Médicis of France with a letter about its uses as a medicine. In 1561 he showed her how to grind the leaves into a powder to sniff for headaches, and the powder became a fashion at the French court (Britannica, "Jean Nicot").
- The word nicotine, the name of the drug in tobacco, comes from Nicot's name. The Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus named the tobacco plants *Nicotiana* after Nicot in 1753.
- Europeans at first took tobacco as a medicine. Thomas Hariot's report of 1588 says that "men & women of great calling" and "some learned Phisitions" in England were using it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" name="Thomas Hariot" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hariot
> **Who:** English mathematician who learned to smoke tobacco from the Algonquian people and described how they used it. · **When and where:** Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, 1585 to 1586, and London until 1621.
- Went on the English expedition of 1585 to Roanoke Island, on the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples. He learned the Algonquian language from two Algonquian men, Manteo and Wanchese (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- In *A Briefe and True Report* (1588) he described a plant the people called uppowoc and planted in its own plots. They dried the leaves, ground them to powder, and drew the smoke through clay pipes. They believed the smoke kept the body healthy. They also threw the powder on sacred fires and onto the water as an offering, in storms and after escaping danger.
- He wrote that the English "vsed to suck it after their maner" while they were there, and kept smoking after they went home.
- The colony ended in June 1586 after Ralph Lane's men killed the werowance (chief) Wingina, and the English sailed home a week later (NPS, Fort Raleigh).
- An ulcer grew in his nose. Hariot died in London on 2 July 1621 of cancer of the nose, which Encyclopedia Virginia says was "probably caused by heavy tobacco use."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish wine in Florida" -->
- Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine on 8 September 1565, at the Timucua town of Seloy (Florida Museum of Natural History).
- The Spanish colonists counted wine and olive oil as more important than wheat and beef. Ships brought both from Spain in clay "olive jars" (Florida Museum). The colonists got their corn and cooking pots from Timucua women.
- The historian Peter C. Mancall writes that some Europeans offered alcohol to Native people when they met. The trade in alcohol with Native nations did not begin until about 1650. No record from this century describes the Spanish trading wine to Native nations as a regular business.
- Shared with: `food-farming` (what the colonists ate) · `native-nations` (the Timucua). This chapter's slice is the wine.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English and Dutch colonists in the 1600s drank beer, cider and wine every day, and the Chesapeake colonies grew rich selling tobacco. Colonists believed alcohol kept the body warm and strong. Some had also fallen sick from bad water. Colony leaders licensed the taverns and punished drunkenness with fines, the stocks and public shame. From about 1650, traders sold rum and brandy to Native nations for furs, and colony leaders passed laws against those sales that traders kept breaking.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beer, cider and bad water" -->
- The first drinks colonists made were cider and beer, brewed from corn, wheat, oats, persimmons and green cornstalks (Ed Crews, *Colonial Williamsburg Journal*, 2007). Grapevines brought from Europe died of American pests and diseases, so the colonists' wine came by ship.
- Colonists believed alcohol warmed the body, helped digestion and made people strong. Water did make some sick. George Percy wrote that the Jamestown settlers drank river water that was salty at high tide and "full of slime and filth" at low tide, which was "the destruction of many of our men." In Europe people drank alcohol in place of dirty water, and the colonists kept the habit.
- In 1622 the Virginia Company of London wrote to Governor Francis Wyatt that the colonists' drinking was hurting the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Laws about drinking" -->
- Virginia's first General Assembly, 1619: a man found drunk was scolded in private by the minister the first time and in public the second time. The third time he was held for 12 hours in bolts, which are iron shackles, in the provost marshal's house and paid the marshal's fees.
- Massachusetts, laws of 1633 to 1647: only a person licensed by the county court could run a tavern. A tavern keeper could not serve one person more than half a pint of wine at a time, or let anyone sit drinking for more than half an hour or after nine at night. A drunk person was fined 10 shillings. A person who could not pay was put in the stocks, a wooden frame that locked the ankles in public, for up to three hours.
- Prices were set by law. In 1634 a Boston innkeeper could charge sixpence for a meal and one penny for a quart of beer, and paid a 10 shilling fine for charging more (Samuel Adams Drake).
- Shared with: `government-politics` (the assemblies that passed these laws) · `crime-justice` (the courts and punishments). This chapter's slice is the drinking the laws controlled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" name="Samuel Cole" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Cole
> **Who:** a maker of sugar-coated sweets who kept Boston's first licensed tavern. · **When and where:** Boston, Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1634 to 1638.
- Came to Boston with Governor John Winthrop's fleet in 1630 (Samuel Adams Drake, *Old Boston Taverns and Tavern Clubs*).
- Opened his ordinary, the word then for a tavern, on Washington Street in 1634. Drake gives the date as 4 March 1634 and calls it the earliest Boston ordinary known to have had a license. His house had a brew-house for making beer.
- In 1636 Governor Henry Vane made a peace treaty with the Narragansett at Boston. The sachems Miantonomi and two sons of Canonicus ate at the governor's house, and about twenty other Narragansett men ate at Cole's inn.
- The colony court gave him leave to sell the inn in 1638, and he sold it to Robert Sedgwick. He died in 1666.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" name="Robert Coles" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Robert Coles
> **Who:** a farmer the Massachusetts court punished four times for drunkenness, the last time by making him wear a red letter D. · **When and where:** Roxbury and Agawam (now Ipswich), Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1631 to 1634.
- Came from England with the Winthrop Fleet in 1630.
- 1631: fined five marks for being drunk on the ship *Friendship*, which was carrying spiced honey wine, and at Winnisimmet, now Chelsea.
- 1633: fined £10 at Agawam for being drunk, for getting John Shatswell's wife to drink, and for urging her to be unfaithful to her husband. He had to stand in public with a paper on his back that said "drunkard" in large letters.
- 1634: the court ordered him to wear a red cloth D on a white patch around his neck for a year, whenever he was among people, and took away his right to vote. Governor John Winthrop wrote that Coles "having been oft punished for drunkenness, was now ordered to wear a red D about his neck for a year" (Massachusetts court records, through Wikipedia, "Robert Coles (settler)").
- The court gave back his vote about two months later, and no court charged him with drunkenness again. He moved to Providence and in 1640 helped write the Providence Combination, a written plan for governing the town.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling alcohol to Native nations" -->
- In September 1609, near what is now Albany, Henry Hudson and his mate gave Mahican leaders "so much Wine and Aqua vitae," a strong liquor, that they got drunk. Robert Juet, who kept a journal on the ship, wrote that Hudson and the mate did it to find out whether the leaders were planning "treacherie." One man got drunk and "could not tell how to take it."
- From about 1650, English traders sold rum and French traders sold brandy to Native nations in the fur trade (historian Peter C. Mancall). Young men traded the furs from a season's hunt for liquor and drank it fast, and more families became poor. Native and colonial observers recorded drunken men fighting their families, drowning, falling into fires and killing others.
- In New Netherland (now New York), Director General Willem Kieft and his council banned selling liquor to Native people on 18 June 1643, because "large quantities of strong liquors are daily sold to the Indians." They passed the ban again in 1645. Director General Petrus Stuyvesant passed it a third time in 1647 and raised the fine to 500 guilders.
- Mancall writes that Native people also organized against the trade and complained to colonial officials about traders.
- Shared with: `native-nations` (the fur trade and what it cost the nations) · `exploration` (Hudson's voyage and the killings of October 1609). This chapter's slice is the liquor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco and rum" -->
- John Rolfe, a heavy smoker, grew milder Spanish tobacco at Jamestown by July 1612. Virginia shipped 20,000 pounds of it to England in 1617 and grew 29 million pounds in 1709 (Encyclopedia Virginia). Planters took cleared Powhatan land to grow more.
- King James I of England attacked smoking in *A Counterblaste to Tobacco* (1604). He called it "lothsome to the eye, hatefull to the Nose, harmefull to the braine, dangerous to the Lungs," and called Native Americans "barbarous and beastly" for starting it.
- Massachusetts banned smoking within about 330 feet of a house, barn or haystack, because a pipe could start a fire, and in the public rooms of an inn (1638 and 1647).
- Rum is a strong liquor distilled from molasses, the syrup left after sugar is made from sugarcane. A Barbados writer of about 1651 called it "Rumbullion, als Kill-Divill" and "a hott hellish and terrible liquor." New England merchants began trading fish and lumber to the West Indies for molasses and making it into rum in the 1600s ("Rum Trade," *Dictionary of American History*).
