## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
No nation here had police, prisons or written laws before 1500. When someone killed or stole, the families and clans of the two people settled it. In many nations the dead person's relatives could take a life in return, or accept wampum (strings of shell beads), furs or other goods instead. The nations kept their rules in memory and in ceremony. Everything known about those rules comes from their own oral traditions and from accounts written down after 1600, so no one can say exactly how any of them worked in a given year before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee law of blood" -->
Among the Cherokee, a killing was a matter between two clans. A clan was a large family group traced through the mother, and there were seven of them. The Cherokee Nation's own newspaper, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, set down the old rules in 1829. The whole nation "had nothing to do with murder." The dead person's clan had the duty to take a life in return. If the killer ran away, his brother or nearest relative could be killed in his place. Any killing counted as murder, even an accident. In a council about twenty years before 1829, Cherokee leaders ended clan revenge and made murder a crime against the nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Condolence and the Great Law" -->
The Haudenosaunee are five nations in what is now New York: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca. Their tradition says the five nations were once locked in killings and revenge. A man named Hiawatha, whose daughters had been murdered, strung shells into wampum and used them to speak words of comfort instead of calling for revenge. Those words of condolence became part of their Great Law of Peace. The Great Law was spoken aloud for generations and first printed in English in 1916. In that version, a chief who committed murder lost his title and was sent out of the five nations' land for good. An outsider adopted into a nation who did wrong got a warning first. A second offense meant being driven out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forgiveness at the Green Corn" -->
The Muscogee (Creek) and other nations of the Southeast held a Green Corn Ceremony each midsummer, when the first corn ripened. People fasted, then relit a sacred fire in the town square. At that time the towns forgave every crime except murder. Traders and travelers wrote down the ceremony in the 1700s.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
European law reached this land in the 1500s only at a few Spanish outposts, St. Augustine in Florida from 1565 and New Mexico from 1598. There were no sheriffs, jails or juries. In a Spanish colony the same officials governed the settlers, commanded the soldiers and judged the cases. A local official called an alcalde heard most crimes, and a person could appeal to the governor. Everywhere else, the Native nations kept their own laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Acoma trial, 1599" -->
In February 1599 Juan de Oñate, the governor of New Mexico, put about 500 Acoma people on trial. His soldiers had attacked and burned their town, Acoma Pueblo, the month before, after Acoma men killed eleven or twelve Spanish soldiers. Oñate had ordered the attack, and Oñate was also the judge. He named a Spanish officer to defend the prisoners. Acoma witnesses said many of them had been working in their fields during the first fight.
- The sentence: every man over 25 was to have one foot cut off and to serve twenty years. Serving meant working for Spanish colonists with no pay and no right to leave.
- Boys from 12 to 25 and women and girls over 12 were to serve twenty years.
- Children under 12 were taken from their families and given to Catholic priests. About 60 or 70 Acoma girls were sent to convents in Mexico. As far as any record shows, none came home.
- Two Hopi men had their right hands cut off and were sent home to tell their people.
- Reporters and the Acoma people's own history give 24 as the number of men whose feet were cut. Two historians who studied the Spanish papers, Marc Simmons and John Kessell, say the record does not show whether the cutting was carried out. The record gives the order. It does not say how it was done.
- Oñate himself was tried in Mexico City years later (see the 1600s).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s the English colonies punished crime on the body and in public. Colonial judges ordered whippings, brandings and cut-off ears, and they sent people to sit in the stocks where their neighbors could see them. There were no police and almost no prisons. A jail held people until their trial or their punishment. At night, ordinary settlers took turns keeping watch. The same colonial courts tried Native people, and by 1669 Virginia lawmakers had written a law that let a master kill an enslaved person during a beating without being charged. In 1692 judges in Massachusetts put 19 people to death for witchcraft that no one had done.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown under martial law, 1610 to 1619" -->
From 1610 to 1619 the Jamestown colony in Virginia ran under military rules. Sir Thomas Gates issued the first orders in May 1610, and Sir Thomas Dale added more in June 1611. William Strachey printed them in 1612 as the *Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall*. Martial law means rule by army officers instead of ordinary courts.
- Death was the punishment for murder, robbery, blasphemy (insulting God), speaking against the company that ran the colony, and trading with Indians without permission (*Encyclopedia Virginia*).
- Whipping was the punishment for gambling, missing church, killing livestock without need, and stealing crops or tools.
- George Percy, one of the colony's leaders, wrote in 1625 that Dale had men who ran away to the Powhatan executed. "Some he apointed to be hanged some burned some to be broken upon wheles others to be Staked and some to be shott to deathe." Breaking on the wheel was a European way of killing a person: the executioner broke the person's arms and legs with a heavy bar and tied the body to a large wheel. Percy does not say how it was done at Jamestown, and he does not explain "staked."
- Percy wrote that Dale had men who robbed the colony's storehouse tied fast to trees and left there until they starved to death. Percy gives no names and no count.
- Percy wrote partly to defend his own record in Virginia. His account is the only one of these killings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oñate convicted, 1614" -->
Juan de Oñate, who had judged the Acoma prisoners in 1599 (see the 1500s), was put on trial himself in Mexico City. Spanish officials charged him with 30 crimes in 1612 or 1613 (the sources differ). In 1614 they convicted him on 12, including using too much force against Acoma and hanging two Indians without cause. They banished him from New Mexico for life, barred him from Mexico City for four years (one source says five) and fined him 6,000 ducats. No record read for this chapter shows that any Acoma person was asked to testify.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The watch and the whipping post in Massachusetts" -->
The court records of the Massachusetts Bay Colony, which Puritan settlers founded in 1630, show how justice worked in its first years.
- On 12 April 1631 the colony's court ordered a watch of four men every night at Dorchester and at Watertown, "to begin att sunsett." In July it ordered a watch of six men and an officer at Boston. The watchmen were settlers taking turns. The same court fined Daniel Abbott "for refuseing to watch."
- Whipping meant being struck on the bare back with a whip, usually tied to a post in a public place. The court ordered it for Bartholomew Hill, who stole a loaf of bread, and for John Baker, who shot at birds on the Sabbath. The records do not say how many strokes anyone received.
- The stocks were a wooden board with holes that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place. In 1630 or 1631 the court ordered John Boggust and John Pickryn to sit in the stocks at Salem for four hours.
- The pillory was a wooden frame on a post that locked a person's neck and wrists while the person stood. Anyone passing could insult the person or throw things (*Britannica*).
- The bilboes were iron rings locked around the ankles. The court set Edward Seale in the bilboes for drunkenness and then had him "severely whiped."
- Branding meant pressing a red-hot iron into the skin. It burned a mark into the flesh that stayed for life. In September 1632 the court ordered Richard Hopkins whipped and "branded with a hott iron on one of his cheekes" for selling guns and gunpowder to Indians. In 1635 it ordered Robert Scarlett, "a knowen theife," whipped and branded on the forehead with a T, for thief.
- In September 1658 the Boston hangman's deputy cut off the right ear of each of three Quakers, Christopher Holder, John Copeland and John Rous, in the Boston prison. Massachusetts law set that punishment for Quakers who came back after being sent away (George Bishop, 1661).

### Philip Ratcliffe
> **Who:** a settler whose ears were cut off for criticizing the Massachusetts government and the Salem church. · **When and where:** Boston, June 1631.
On 14 June 1631 the court at Boston ordered that Philip Ratcliffe "shalbe whipped, haue his eares cutt of, fyned 40£, & banished." His crime was "vttering mallitious & scandulous speeches against the govmt & the church of Salem," proved by witnesses under oath. He was punished for words. The court record gives the order and not the method, so it does not say who cut off his ears or how. After the whipping and the cutting he was to be put out of the colony for good. The same court that day ordered Philip Swaddon whipped for running away from his master (*Records of the Governor and Company of the Massachusetts Bay*, vol. 1).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-ratcliffe" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native people in colonial courts" -->
The Massachusetts court fined the Massachusett sachem (leader) Chickatabut one beaver skin in 1631 for shooting a pig that belonged to Sir Richard Saltonstall. From the colony's first year, its judges tried and fined Native people who lived near them.
- In January 1675 John Sassamon, a Christian Native man, was found dead under the ice of Assawompset Pond. In June the Plymouth Colony court tried three Native men for his murder: Tobias, his son Wampapaquan, and Mattashunannamo. The charge said they had struck him or twisted his neck until he died and pushed his body through a hole in the ice.
- The jury was twelve English colonists. The court added six Native men, Hope, Maskippague, Wannoo, George, Wampye and Acanootus, to advise the jury. They were not sworn jurors.
- The jury found all three guilty, and the Plymouth court sentenced them to hang. Plymouth officials hanged Tobias and Mattashunannamo on 8 June 1675. The court gave Wampapaquan a month's delay, and within that month he was shot to death. The record does not name who shot him (*Records of the Colony of New Plymouth*, vol. 5).
- King Philip's War began at Swansea later that June.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A killing the law did not punish, 1669" -->
In October 1669 the members of Virginia's assembly passed "An act about the casuall killing of slaves." Under it, if an enslaved person resisted and died under "correction" by the master or by someone the master sent, the death "shall not be accompted felony." Correction meant a beating. The lawmakers wrote that no one would kill on purpose when the enslaved person was his own "estate," meaning his property (Hening, *Statutes at Large*, vol. 2, p. 270). Under this law a master who beat an enslaved person to death was not charged with any crime.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Salem, 1692" -->
In 1692 and 1693 colonial judges in Massachusetts tried people for witchcraft, meaning using the Devil's power to hurt others. Salem was built on Naumkeag, a home of the Pawtucket people. A plague brought by English traders had killed many of them between 1616 and 1619 (City of Salem tourism office). More than 200 people were accused (*Smithsonian*). The judges had 19 of them hanged, and Sheriff George Corwin carried out the hangings. Corwin also pressed one man to death with stones. At least five more people died in jail (*Britannica*).
- The first accusers were three girls in Salem Village: Betty Parris (9), Abigail Williams (11) and Ann Putnam Jr. (about 12). They said three women were hurting them by witchcraft. The three women were Tituba, a woman enslaved in the house of the minister Samuel Parris, and Sarah Good and Sarah Osborn. The magistrates John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin questioned them on 1 March 1692. Tituba denied it, then confessed after being questioned again and again.
- Governor William Phips set up a special court on 27 May 1692. William Stoughton was its chief judge. The judges accepted spectral evidence: accusers' testimony that the accused person's spirit had come to them in a dream or a vision and hurt them. No one could prove or disprove such testimony.
- Giles Corey, who was 71 or 81 (the sources disagree), refused to plead guilty or not guilty. English law let a court force a plea by pressing: the prisoner lay on the ground under a board, and officers piled heavy stones on it. On Sheriff Corwin's orders Corey was stripped and laid face up, and the sheriff placed large rocks on the board one at a time. It went on for two days. Corey died on the third, 19 September 1692 (Massachusetts Historical Society). Because he was never convicted, the law let his children keep his land.
- Dorothy Good, Sarah Good's daughter, was four or five years old when magistrates had her jailed with her mother in March 1692 (one archive says six). In 1710 her father wrote that she was kept in prison seven or eight months, chained in the dungeon, and had needed care ever since. Sarah Good gave birth in jail, and the baby died there. Sarah Good was hanged on 19 July 1692 (University of Virginia archive).
- In October 1692 Phips shut the special court after his own wife was accused. The new court would not allow spectral evidence and condemned 3 of 56 defendants. By May 1693 Phips had pardoned everyone still held.
- In 1711 Massachusetts lawmakers paid £600 to the heirs of people who had been convicted. They formally apologized in 1957.

### Rebecca Nurse
> **Who:** a member of the Salem church, found not guilty by her jury and then hanged. · **When and where:** Salem, Massachusetts, March to July 1692.
Edward and Jonathan Putnam filed the complaint against Rebecca Nurse on 23 March 1692. They said she had hurt Ann Putnam Sr., Ann Putnam Jr. and Abigail Williams by witchcraft. She had been sick in bed for eight or nine days. At her questioning she said, "I am as clear as the child unborn." Thirty-nine neighbors signed a statement that they had never had any cause to suspect her. Women searched her body for a mark of the Devil. In a petition she asked for other women, two of them midwives, to examine her again. She signed it with her mark because she could not write her name.
At her trial the jury found her not guilty. The judges objected and pointed to something she had said about two other accused women. The jury went out again. When she did not answer in court, one juror, Thomas Fisk, took her silence as evidence against her, and the jury found her guilty. She wrote afterward that she was "hard of hearing, and full of grief," and had not understood what the court wanted. Governor Phips pardoned her, then took the pardon back after several men from Salem pressed him. She was hanged on 19 July 1692. Her house in Danvers still stands (University of Virginia, *Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive*, case file 94).

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750 colonial lawmakers set up one kind of criminal court for free white people and another for enslaved Black people. In Virginia, an enslaved person accused of a crime that carried death was tried by a panel of local justices, with no jury. In New York in 1741, white defendants could reject up to twenty jurors, and enslaved defendants had no lawyer. In South Carolina, white men rode on patrols at night to stop and search enslaved people. Local courts met about once a month, and a sheriff carried out their orders. A person who could not pay a debt could be locked in the county jail. Britain also shipped more than 52,000 convicts across the ocean to work in the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The county court and the debtors' jail" -->
In Virginia, local landowners called justices of the peace sat as the county court. They had met about once a month since the 1620s, and from 1642 their meetings were called county courts. They settled quarrels, gave out licenses to marry and to run taverns, and tried local crimes. Serving as a justice was a step toward a seat in Virginia's assembly (*Encyclopedia Virginia*). The sheriff carried out what the court ordered.
- A jail in these years mostly held people waiting for a trial or a punishment, and people who owed money. From the first colonies until the middle 1800s, a creditor could have a person who did not pay a debt locked in jail. Under English law the debtor even paid the cost of being kept there. The point was to force the family and friends to pay (*Encyclopedia of North Carolina*). A person in jail could not work to earn the money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts shipped from Britain" -->
British judges sentenced about 52,200 people to be shipped to the American colonies between 1700 and 1775, more than 20,000 of them to Virginia (*Encyclopedia Virginia*). This was called transportation. A 1718 act of Parliament paid merchants to carry them. The first merchant with the contract was Jonathan Forward. Ship captains sold the convicts when they landed, and most worked seven to fourteen years in tobacco fields or at ironworks. In 1722 Virginia's assembly passed a law to make the shipping too expensive. Forward complained, and the Board of Trade in London overturned the law. The last convict ship reached Virginia in 1776.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Slave patrols and courts without juries" -->
White South Carolinians set up official slave patrols in 1704, copying the island colony of Barbados. The patrollers were the same men who served in the militia. At first planters rode alongside poorer white farmers, and from 1734 to 1737 the colony paid them. After enslaved people rose up at Stono in 1739, the members of the South Carolina assembly ordered "constant, regular patrols" in the Negro Act of 1740. In later years a typical patrol was a few men on horseback. They searched the cabins where enslaved people lived, broke up their gatherings and guarded the roads. That patrol system lasted until the end of the Civil War (*South Carolina Encyclopedia*).
- In 1692 Virginia's lawmakers set up special courts to try enslaved people for crimes that carried death, with no jury. A Virginia law of 1723 spelled out how those courts worked. Justices named for each case tried the accused person at the county courthouse. They could convict on a confession, on one witness under oath, or on any testimony that "shall seem convincing" to them, "without the solemnity of a jury." The owner of the accused person could come and speak in the person's defense.
- The same 1723 law set a punishment for any Black or Native witness found to have lied. The person's ear was nailed to the pillory, and the person was left standing there for an hour. Then the ear was cut off. An hour later the other ear was nailed and cut off the same way. Then the person got 39 lashes of the whip "on his or her bare back." The law names the punishment and not the officer who carried it out.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New York trials of 1741" -->
In 1741 New York judges tried about 200 people for a plot to burn the city, after a string of fires. The judges were James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse. Officials burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 more. They also hanged four white people. Burning at the stake meant chaining the person to a post with wood piled around it and setting the wood on fire. The first witness, Mary Burton, was a 16-year-old servant. She began naming people as she was being taken to the jail under City Hall. Other enslaved witnesses were promised their lives if they named others (Horsmanden's own journal, 1744). Historians still disagree about whether any plot existed.

### Quack and Cuffee
> **Who:** two enslaved men in New York City, burned at the stake in the 1741 trials. · **When and where:** New York City, tried and burned May 1741.
Quack was enslaved by a painter named Roosevelt. Cuffee was enslaved by Frederick Philipse. The undersheriff put Cuffee in a cell with a white prisoner, Arthur Price, and gave them "a tankard of punch now and then". Price then swore that Cuffee had talked about the fires. At their trial the prosecution called eleven witnesses. Quack and Cuffee had no lawyer, so they defended themselves. They called ten witnesses, most of whom said the men were somewhere else, and they said they were innocent. The jury found them guilty. Judge Horsmanden sentenced them to be "chained to a stake, and burnt to death." On 30 May 1741, before a large crowd, the sheriff brought them to the stake. With the wood piled around them, both men confessed and named other people. The deputy secretary asked the sheriff to wait while the governor was asked about a pardon. The sheriff said he could not take them back through the crowd without a strong guard. The burning went ahead. Their confessions led to more arrests (Horsmanden, *A Journal of the Proceedings*, 1744, and Douglas Linder, *Famous Trials*).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1790 Pennsylvania lawmakers turned the Walnut Street Jail in Philadelphia into the first state prison in the United States. Until then, a jail held people who owed money and people waiting for their trial or their punishment. The punishment itself was a fine, a whipping, a branding or a hanging. The Philadelphia reformers wanted convicts locked in cells for years, working and alone, so that they would feel sorry and change. In 1791 Americans added the rights of accused people to the Constitution in the Bill of Rights. Some killers were never tried at all. In 1763 a band of Pennsylvania settlers killed twenty Conestoga people, and no one was ever charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiting was a hanging crime" -->
Colonial paper money carried the warning "To Counterfeit is Death." New York judges convicted Owen Sullivan, an Irish-born engraver who ran gangs passing fake bills from New England to New York, and he was hanged on 10 May 1756.
Shared with: `money` (lead: Sullivan and colonial paper money).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Conestoga killings, 1763" -->
On 14 December 1763, 57 armed settlers from Paxton Township and nearby rode to Conestoga, a small Native town near Lancaster, Pennsylvania, on a reserve that the Penn family had promised to protect (Benjamin Franklin, 1764, and the *Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia*). They were angry about Native attacks farther west in Pontiac's War, and they said the Conestoga were helping the enemy. The historian Michael Goode calls that charge "a fabrication." Twenty people lived in the town. The men found six at home, three men, two women and a boy. They shot them, stabbed them and killed them with hatchets. Then they scalped them, which means they cut away the skin and hair from the top of the head, and burned the houses. Shehaes, an old man who had been at William Penn's treaty in 1701, was killed in his bed.
- Lancaster's magistrates put the other fourteen in the town workhouse to protect them. On 27 December about fifty of the same men broke open the workhouse door. Franklin wrote that the families knelt, with the children holding on to their parents, and said they were innocent. The men killed all fourteen, children and babies too, with guns and hatchets. Will Soc was shot at the door.
- Franklin's pamphlet names people from the town, among them Shehaes, his daughter Peggy, her husband John Smith (a Cayuga) and their child of about three, Betty and her son Peter, and Sally, whose own name was Wyanjoy.
- Governor John Penn offered a reward for the killers' names. No one came forward, and no one was ever arrested or tried (*Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia*). A company of British soldiers stationed nearby did nothing to stop the second attack.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two trials in Boston, 1770" -->
In 1770 Boston juries tried men accused of killing Bostonians, in a town where most people wanted them hanged.
- On 22 February 1770 Ebenezer Richardson, who was paid by customs officials to report smugglers, fired a gun from his house into a crowd of boys and killed Christopher Seider, who was 10 or 11. A jury found Richardson guilty of murder on 21 April. The judges thought he was innocent and put off sentencing him.
- On 5 March British soldiers fired into a crowd and killed five men: Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray, James Caldwell, Samuel Maverick (17) and Patrick Carr. Captain Thomas Preston and eight soldiers were jailed. No lawyer in Boston would defend them until John Adams agreed, with Josiah Quincy. Adams later wrote that a lawyer should be "the very last thing that [an] accused Person should want in a free Country."
- The jury found Preston not guilty on 30 October. In December a jury acquitted six soldiers and found Hugh Montgomery and Mathew Kilroy guilty of manslaughter, which means killing without planning to. Manslaughter could carry death. The two used an old English rule called benefit of clergy, which let a first-time offender who could read escape hanging once. Instead, on the court's order, each man's right thumb was branded in the courtroom. A red-hot iron was pressed onto the thumb and burned a mark that stayed for life. The sources used here do not name who held the iron (National Park Service). Then the two soldiers were let go.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rights for the accused, 1791" -->
On 15 December 1791 enough states approved the first ten amendments to the Constitution, the Bill of Rights. Four of them protect people accused of crimes. Officers need a warrant, a judge's written order, to search a home, and a judge may give one only for good reason (Fourth). No one can be forced to be a witness against himself (Fifth). An accused person gets a "speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury" and a lawyer (Sixth). No one may be given "cruel and unusual punishments" or bail set too high (Eighth). At first these rules bound only the federal government and its courts. State courts did not have to follow them.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From the street gang to the cell, Philadelphia" -->
Pennsylvania's lawmakers tried two new punishments in these years.
- In 1786 they sentenced convicts to "continued hard labour publicly and disgracefully imposed." People called the prisoners "wheelbarrow men." Ann Warder, a visitor, wrote in her diary on 30 March 1787 that the convicts cleaned the streets with "an iron collar around their neck and waist to which a long chain is fashioned and at the end a heavy ball." Their heads were shaved and a guard went with each gang. Thirty of them escaped in one night in October 1788, and the lawmakers ended the law by 1790.
- In 1787 a group of 37 Philadelphians, among them the doctor Benjamin Rush and Benjamin Franklin, formed a society to reform the prisons. Philadelphia's old jail held men, women and children together, and the sheriff took money from the prisoners, sold them liquor and held back their food.
- In April 1790 the society's members got the law they had asked for. Under it the Walnut Street Jail became a state prison for convicts from all of Pennsylvania. The lawmakers ordered a separate building of cells for "the more hardened and atrocious offenders," each cell six feet wide, eight feet long and nine feet high. Thirty-six were built. A prisoner in one of them was kept alone. Between 1795 and 1800, 748 people were sent to the prison, and judges sentenced 29 of them to time alone in the cells (David Shapiro, *Harvard Law Review*, 2019).
- A prisoner who refused to work was kept in a cell "some weeks, without labour, bed, or furniture, of any kind, except a vessel to hold his drink, and another his mush, and a blanket" (Caleb Lownes, one of the prison's inspectors, 1799).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="patrick-lyon" name="Patrick Lyon" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Patrick Lyon
> **Who:** a Philadelphia blacksmith held three months in the Walnut Street Prison for a bank robbery he did not commit. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, September 1798 to January 1799.
Lyon was born in Scotland and came to Philadelphia in 1793. In August 1798 he fitted new locks on the vault doors of the Bank of Pennsylvania. He told the bank's carpenter the doors and locks were not safe. Then he fled a yellow fever outbreak by sailing to Lewes, Delaware, where his 19-year-old apprentice died of the fever. On the night of 31 August, while he was away, someone took $162,821 from the vault without breaking in. Lyon heard he was the main suspect and walked back to clear his name. "I had come 150 miles to surrender myself," he wrote. High Constable John Haines, who wanted the $2,000 reward, and the bank's officers decided he had made a spare key. A magistrate sent him to the Walnut Street Prison with bail set at $150,000. He called the prison "cold, damp, unwholesome and solitary," and he caught yellow fever there and lived.
The robbers were Isaac Davis, a carpenter, and the bank porter, Thomas Cunningham, who died of the fever. Davis confessed and gave back the money, and the governor promised him a pardon. Lyon still stayed in prison until a grand jury refused to charge him in January 1799. He wrote a book about it and sued the bank's officers and the constable. In 1805 a jury awarded him $12,000. The men he sued appealed, and in 1807 they paid him $9,000 to settle. The painter John Neagle later painted him as *Pat Lyon at the Forge* (Carpenters' Company of Philadelphia, drawing on Lyon's 1799 *Narrative*).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850 the biggest American cities hired their first full-time police, and states built large prisons where convicts spent years in cells. Boston hired a small day police in 1838 to work beside its night watchmen. New York replaced its night watch with a Municipal Police in 1845. Prison keepers in New York and Pennsylvania ran two rival kinds of prison, and both kept prisoners silent. Pennsylvania lawmakers moved hangings inside jail walls in 1834, and Michigan lawmakers ended the death penalty for murder in 1846. In the South, slave patrols and county courts with no jury still decided what happened to enslaved people. Mobs also killed people with no trial at all. In St. Louis in 1836 a white mob burned Francis McIntosh to death, and no one was charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police take over from the night watch" -->
Before the 1830s, American towns kept order at night with a watch, men who walked the streets after dark.
- In 1838 Boston hired a day police of a few officers who worked full time under the city marshal. The crime historian Gary Potter calls it the first publicly paid, full-time police force in the country (TIME, 2017). In 1854 Boston's city officials replaced the watch and the day police with one Police Department of 250 officers (City of Boston).
- In 1845 New York had about 320,000 people. They were guarded by a night watch, 100 city marshals, 31 constables and 51 municipal police officers. New York's state lawmakers passed a Municipal Police Act on 7 May 1844 that ended the night watch. The mayor and the city council argued over who would choose the officers, so Mayor William Havemeyer and the council put the law into force only on 23 May 1845. Accounts give the new force as 800 men, or as up to 1,200 officers allowed by the law. The officers first wore a navy blue uniform in 1853.
Shared with: `city-building` (how cities grew).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two kinds of prison: Auburn and Eastern State" -->
Prison keepers argued over how to make a convict change his ways. Both sides kept prisoners from talking to each other.
- At Auburn Prison in New York, prisoners worked together in shops by day and slept alone in small cells at night. They were not allowed to speak at any time. Elam Lynds, the head keeper from 1821, made them march in a line with arms locked, called the lockstep, and dressed them in striped uniforms. He had prisoners flogged for small rule-breaking. Flogging means beating a person's bare back with a whip. The whip cuts the skin and leaves welts and scars. New York officials used the same plan at Sing Sing prison. By 1829 Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maryland and Washington, D.C., used the Auburn plan, and officials in most other states chose it later.
- In Philadelphia, Pennsylvania's officials opened Eastern State Penitentiary in October 1829. The architect John Haviland built seven rows of cells around a center hall, so guards could watch every row. Each prisoner lived and worked alone in a cell about 8 feet wide and 12 feet long, with a tiny walled yard. A guard put a hood over a prisoner's head whenever he left his cell, so no other prisoner could see his face. Prisoners who broke the rules got cold showers, and some were put in the iron gag. An iron piece was clamped over the tongue and chained to the prisoner's hands, pulled up behind his back.
- The English writer Charles Dickens visited Eastern State in 1842. He wrote that he believed the system "in its effects, to be cruel and wrong."
- Pennsylvania's prison officials gave up keeping prisoners alone in 1913. Built for 250 people, Eastern State held about 1,700 in the mid-1920s.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-williams" name="Charles Williams" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Williams
> **Who:** the first prisoner at Eastern State Penitentiary. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, October 1829 to October 1831.
Charles Williams was an 18-year-old Black farmer. A court convicted him of breaking into a house and stealing a silver watch, a gold seal and a gold key worth $25 in all. The judge sentenced him to two years alone and in silence, with work. In October 1829 guards took him into the new penitentiary with a hood over his head. From then on the guards called him by a number, Prisoner Number 1, and not by his name. He lived in a cell twelve feet by eight feet. He spoke to the guards through a small hole in the door that his food came through, and he worked alone in the cell. He was let out in October 1831, two years to the day after he went in (Jane Brox, *Public Domain Review*, and the *Smithsonian* magazine).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-williams" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hangings move inside the jail" -->
Until the 1830s, hangings took place outdoors in front of large crowds. In 1834 Pennsylvania's lawmakers became the first state lawmakers to ban public hangings (Death Penalty Information Center). After that, hangings took place inside the county prisons. On 18 May 1846 Michigan's lawmakers voted to end the death penalty for murder. The radio station Michigan Public calls Michigan the first English-speaking government in the world to do so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="After Nat Turner's revolt, 1831" -->
In August 1831 Nat Turner led enslaved men in a revolt in Southampton County, Virginia, and they killed 57 white people. Afterward, white men with guns killed enslaved people across the county with no trial. The Library of Virginia says they killed hundreds and gives no exact count. The county court tried the accused rebels. It was a court of oyer and terminer, which in Virginia meant local justices judged an enslaved person with no jury. By one count the justices heard 53 cases, sentenced 18 people to hang, sold 12 out of the state and let 21 go. Turner hid for two months and gave himself up on 30 October. The justices tried him on 5 November and ordered him "hanged by the neck until he be dead" on 11 November. The same court record set his value, as property, at $375.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Francis McIntosh, St. Louis, 1836" -->
On 28 April 1836 a white mob in St. Louis, Missouri, burned Francis McIntosh alive. He was a free Black man, 26 years old, from Pittsburgh, who worked as a porter and cook on the steamboat *Flora*. Deputy Constable William Mull arrested him that day for getting in the way of officers who were arresting two sailors. McIntosh was told he could get five years in prison. He pulled a knife and stabbed Mull and Deputy Sheriff George Hammond. Hammond bled to death. Accounts differ on whether Mull died too.
- A crowd of several hundred white people gathered, and some of them broke into the jail and took McIntosh out. They chained him to a locust tree near Seventh and Chestnut Streets, piled wood around his legs up to the knees and set it on fire. He burned for about twenty minutes before he died. Accounts say he begged to be shot. A city alderman, a member of the city council, was in the crowd.
- Judge Luke Lawless then spoke to the grand jury, the group of citizens who decide whether a person should be charged with a crime. He told them that if the killing was the act of a whole crowd acting together, it was beyond the reach of the law. The grand jury charged no one.
- Elijah Lovejoy, a St. Louis newspaper editor, wrote against the killing and the judge. The next year a mob in Alton, Illinois, killed Lovejoy.
- In January 1838 a young lawyer named Abraham Lincoln spoke about the killing in Springfield, Illinois. He said McIntosh had been "chained to a tree, and actually burned to death."

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee Nation's police, 1808" -->
On 11 September 1808 the Cherokee national council, led by the principal chief Black Fox, passed a written law that set up mounted police. The council called them regulators. Each company had six men and was paid by the Nation. The regulators went after horse thieves and robbers and protected the property of widows and orphans. A man found guilty of stealing a horse got "one hundred stripes on the bare back," which means one hundred blows with a whip. If a regulator killed a man who fought back with a weapon, the dead man's clan could not take revenge. In 1810 the clans agreed that a person who killed someone by accident would no longer be killed in return. The regulators were later called the Lighthorse.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1850 to 1900 Americans were judged in two ways. City police, detectives and courts arrested and tried people. At the same time, white mobs killed thousands of people without any trial, most of them Black men in the South, and the killers were almost never punished. The Equal Justice Initiative counts 4,075 Black people lynched in twelve Southern states between 1877 and 1950. Mobs in the West also killed Chinese immigrants. Private detectives, such as Allan Pinkerton's men, worked for railroads and factory owners. After the Civil War, Southern officials rented prisoners, nearly all of them Black, to mining companies and planters. In New York, a committee of state senators found in 1894 that police officers took millions of dollars a year in payments from gambling houses and brothels.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vigilance committees in San Francisco, 1851 and 1856" -->
A vigilance committee was a group of private citizens who arrested, tried and punished people themselves, outside the courts. In June 1851 about 700 San Francisco men formed one. On 10 June they held their own trial and hanged John Jenkins, a man from Sydney, Australia, for stealing a safe. That year they hanged four men, whipped one and forced dozens of others to leave. In 1856, after James Casey shot the newspaper editor James King of William, the committee formed again with about 6,000 members and hanged four more men, among them Casey. The two committees hanged eight people in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dakota trials, 1862" -->
After the war between the United States and the Dakota in Minnesota in 1862, Colonel Henry Sibley set up a court of army officers on 28 September to try Dakota prisoners. The officers tried 392 men, and some trials lasted less than five minutes. The prisoners had no lawyers, and the trials were held in a language that was foreign to them. The officers sentenced 303 men to death. President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers read all 303 trial records and approved 39 hangings. One man was spared at the last minute. Under Lincoln's order, 38 Dakota men were hanged together on one gallows at Mankato on 26 December 1862, with about 4,000 people watching. Two of them were hanged by mistake, and one of those two, Wasicuŋ, had been found not guilty. The bodies were put in a shallow grave on a sandbar. Before morning, doctors dug up most of them to use for medical study (Minnesota Historical Society).

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police against the draft riots, 1863" -->
From 13 to 16 July 1863 mobs in New York City fought the new Civil War draft. The state militia had been sent to Gettysburg, so the Metropolitan Police were the only force on hand at first. On the first day rioters beat Police Superintendent John Kennedy until he was nearly unconscious and cut his hand with a knife. The rioters burned two police station houses and the Colored Orphan Asylum. Police held back the mob long enough for the more than 200 children inside to get out alive. Rioters beat, tortured and killed Black New Yorkers. On the first evening William Jones, a Black cart driver who had gone out to buy a loaf of bread, was chased and beaten by a mob on Clarkson Street. The men hanged him and set him on fire. The official count of the dead was 119 or 120. Soldiers sent from Gettysburg ended the riot.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs that killed Chinese immigrants" -->
In the West, white mobs killed Chinese immigrants, and courts punished almost no one.
- In Los Angeles on 24 October 1871, a white man named Robert Thompson was shot during a gunfight between two Chinese groups. A crowd of about 500 people then attacked the Chinese part of town and killed 18 or 19 Chinese men and boys, among them a doctor, Gene Tong. They hanged at least ten of them from the crossbar of a wagon shop and from the side of a freight wagon. A grand jury charged 25 men with murder. Only ten were tried, and seven or eight of them were found guilty of manslaughter, which means killing without planning to. The California Supreme Court threw out the verdicts on a technical point of law, and the men were never tried again (Los Angeles Public Library). A ruling of the California Supreme Court from 1854 said that Chinese people could not be witnesses against white people in court.
- In Rock Springs, Wyoming Territory, on 2 September 1885, 100 to 150 armed white miners and townspeople killed 28 Chinese coal miners, wounded 15 and burned all 79 of their homes. Sixteen white men were arrested and let out on bail. The killing had taken place in daylight, but no one would swear to having seen it. No charges were filed (WyoHistory.org).

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Haymarket trial, 1886" -->
On 4 May 1886 someone threw a bomb at police at a workers' meeting in Haymarket Square, Chicago. Seven officers died. No one ever found out who threw it. Prosecutors charged eight anarchists, people who wanted to do away with all government, and said their speeches had led to the bombing. A jury found all eight guilty in three hours. On 11 November 1887 Albert Parsons, August Spies, George Engel and Adolph Fischer were hanged. A fifth man, Louis Lingg, had killed himself in his cell with a smuggled explosive. In 1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld of Illinois pardoned the three men still in prison and wrote that the trial had been unfair.

### Allan Pinkerton
> **Who:** a Scottish barrel maker who started the best-known private detective agency in the United States. · **When and where:** Chicago and across the country, 1850 to 1884.
Pinkerton was born in Glasgow, Scotland, in 1819 and settled in Dundee, Illinois, in 1843. His house there was a hiding place for people escaping slavery. While cutting wood he found a gang of counterfeiters and told the sheriff, who arrested them. In 1850 he started a detective agency in Chicago with the lawyer Edward Rucker. It became the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. Its sign was an open eye and the words "We never sleep." Express companies, which carried money and packages by train, and the railroads hired his detectives to catch robbers. His men kept photographs of known criminals and joined gangs in disguise. In February 1861 Pinkerton guarded Abraham Lincoln on his way to Washington, against what he said was a plan to kill him in Baltimore.
Pinkerton's men were private detectives, not police officers, paid by the companies that hired them. In January 1875 his detectives and local helpers raided the Missouri farm of the outlaw Jesse James's family. They threw a fire bomb into the house. It exploded and killed Jesse's eight-year-old half-brother, Archie, and badly wounded his mother, Zerelda Samuel. Jesse was not there. After the Civil War, factory owners also hired Pinkerton men as armed guards against strikers. Pinkerton died in Chicago in 1884 (PBS *American Experience*).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="allan-pinkerton" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police who took payments: New York, 1894" -->
In 1894 a New York State Senate committee led by Senator Clarence Lexow investigated the New York City police. The committee's report ran to 10,576 pages. It estimated that officers took more than $10 million a year from people breaking the law. Brothels paid police captains $30,000 a year and gambling houses paid $15 to $300 a month to stay open. Officers paid to be promoted. Captain Timothy Creedon told the committee he paid $15,000 to become a captain. The chief of police, Thomas Byrnes, was said to be worth $350,000. In 1895 Mayor William Strong put Theodore Roosevelt on the city's police board. Roosevelt brought in written tests for new officers, walked the streets at midnight to check that officers were on duty, and started police patrols on bicycles.
Shared with: `city-building` (Roosevelt and Jacob Riis) · `government-politics` (Tammany Hall).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first electric chair, 1890" -->
On 6 August 1890, at Auburn Prison in New York, William Kemmler became the first person put to death in an electric chair. He had killed Matilda Ziegler with a hatchet in Buffalo in 1889. Electrocution means killing a person by sending a strong electric current through the body. The current stops the heart and burns the skin where the metal touches it. Kemmler's chair had two metal plates covered with wet sponges, one on his head and one on his back. Warden Charles Durston ordered the current turned on. The first shock lasted 17 seconds and did not kill him, and the witnesses smelled burning cloth and flesh. A second shock went on for about two minutes, until smoke rose from his head. Doctors found that the plate on his back had burned through to his spine. His lawyers had argued that the electric chair was a cruel and unusual punishment, which the Constitution forbids, and the US Supreme Court had turned them down (HISTORY.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A court for children, Chicago, 1899" -->
Police arrested children and held them in jails with adult prisoners. In 1882 the Cook County jail in Chicago held more than 250 children aged 14 and under, at least 20 of them younger than 11. A 10-year-old boy who stole a pair of shoes waited almost two weeks in the county jail for a grand jury to hear his case. Julia Lathrop, who worked at Hull House, visited Illinois jails and wrote down what she found. Lucy Flower proposed a separate court for children. In 1899 Illinois lawmakers passed a law that set one up in Chicago. The Chicago radio station WBEZ calls it the first juvenile court in the world. A juvenile court handles children accused of crimes and tries to help the child instead of only punishing him.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prisoners rented out: convict leasing" -->
After the Civil War, Southern state and county officials rented prisoners to private companies. This was called convict leasing. The companies worked the prisoners in coal mines, on farms and on railroads, and paid the state or county, not the prisoners. In Alabama, officials leased prisoners from 1875 to 1928. More than 95 percent of county prisoners and 90 percent of state prisoners there were Black. Many county prisoners had been convicted of small crimes and had to stay longer to work off their court costs. The companies' bosses whipped prisoners who tried to escape or talked back, and whipped others to make them work harder. In one count from 1896, 181 of 745 county prisoners in the mines had died in two years (Mary Ellen Curtin, *Encyclopedia of Alabama*).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-smith" name="Henry Smith" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Henry Smith
> **Who:** a Black man burned to death by a white mob in front of thousands of people, without a trial. · **When and where:** Paris, Texas, 1 February 1893.
In January 1893 Myrtle Vance, a white girl of three or four, was found dead in the woods near Paris, Texas. Her father, Henry Vance, was a Paris police officer who had recently arrested Henry Smith for being drunk and had beaten him. White people in Paris accused Smith of killing her. Smith fled to Arkansas. A Black constable named Robinson arrested him near Hope. Texas Governor James Hogg telegraphed Sheriff D. S. Hammond to hold Smith for a trial and ordered that he not be taken to Paris. The sheriff and the county attorney answered that they were "helpless."
On 1 February 1893 men brought Smith to Paris by train. The mayor closed the schools. Trains brought people from Dallas, Fort Worth and other towns. Between 5,000 and 15,000 people gathered. Men paraded Smith through town on a float and tied him on a wooden platform ten feet high. For fifty minutes Henry Vance and men of his family burned Smith with red-hot irons, starting at his feet. They burned out his eyes and pushed a hot iron down his throat. Then they poured oil on the platform and set it on fire. Smith rolled out of the fire, and people in the crowd threw a rope around him and pulled him back in. People took pieces of the burned wood home (*New York Sun*, 2 February 1893, reprinted by Ida B. Wells in *The Red Record*, 1895).
Smith never had a trial. The *New York Sun* reported that he had confessed at Hope. The Reverend King, a Black minister in Paris who had known him for years, said Smith was mentally ill and that he had tried two years earlier to get him put in a hospital. For speaking against the lynching, King was carried out of Paris on a rail and forced to leave. A few days later a mob hanged Smith's stepson, William Butler, and shot his body. Governor Hogg asked for the killers to be arrested. No one was ever arrested for killing Henry Smith or William Butler (Terry Anne Scott, BlackPast).

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1908 the US attorney general hired 34 federal agents, and by the 1930s their Bureau, later named the FBI, kept the nation's fingerprint files and ran a crime laboratory. Federal agents chased bank robbers across state lines and bootleggers who sold alcohol while Prohibition banned it. In the South, sheriffs and deputies still let mobs take Black prisoners from their jails, and some deputies whipped Black suspects until they confessed. Three times in the 1930s the US Supreme Court threw out verdicts because of how Black defendants had been treated. In Tulsa in 1921, white men, some of them sworn in as deputies by the police, burned the Black district of Greenwood and killed as many as 300 people. No white person went to prison for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ed-johnson" name="Ed Johnson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ed Johnson
> **Who:** a Black man wrongly convicted of rape and sentenced to death, lynched after the US Supreme Court ordered that he be kept safe. · **When and where:** Chattanooga, Tennessee, January to March 1906.
On 23 January 1906 a man attacked Nevada Taylor, a young white woman, from behind as she walked home from a streetcar stop, and raped her. She said she remembered little except that the man was Black and came from behind. Sheriff Joseph Shipp arrested Ed Johnson, a Black man who worked as a porter at a saloon. Johnson's lawyers said that a white man who claimed he had seen Johnson near the stop wanted a $375 reward. At the trial thirteen witnesses swore that Johnson was at the saloon at the time. An all-white jury found him guilty on 11 February, and the judge sentenced him to hang.
Two Black lawyers, Noah Parden and Styles Hutchins, appealed. Parden went to Washington and met Justice John Marshall Harlan of the US Supreme Court. The Court ordered a stay, which stopped the hanging until the justices could hear the case, and told Sheriff Shipp to keep Johnson safe. The order was in Chattanooga's evening newspapers on 19 March. That evening Shipp sent the usual guards away and left only the night jailer, Jeremiah Gibson. A mob broke into the jail and took Johnson to the county bridge over the Tennessee River. The men put a rope around his neck and hanged him from a beam. The rope broke, and they hanged him again and shot him many times. Shipp came to the jail during the break-in and did nothing to stop it. Johnson's gravestone gives his last words: "God Bless you all. I AM A Innocent Man."
For the first and only time, the US Supreme Court held a criminal trial itself. The charge was contempt of court, the crime of defying a court's order. In 1909 the justices found Shipp and five other men guilty. Shipp and two others got 90 days in jail and three got 60 days. In February 2000 a Hamilton County judge, Doug Meyer, set aside Johnson's conviction because of the all-white jury and because the trial had not been moved out of Chattanooga. A memorial to him opened near the bridge in 2021 (*United States v. Shipp*, 1909, and the Death Penalty Information Center).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ed-johnson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A federal police force, 1908 to 1935" -->
On 26 July 1908 Attorney General Charles Bonaparte put 34 agents to work investigating federal crimes for the Department of Justice. The group became the Bureau of Investigation, and later the Federal Bureau of Investigation, or FBI. In 1924 Attorney General Harlan Fiske Stone made J. Edgar Hoover, then 29, its director. Hoover fired agents he thought were unfit and required new agents to pass background checks and physical tests. On 1 July 1924 he set up a division to hold the country's fingerprint files. A fingerprint is the pattern of ridges on a fingertip, and no two people have the same one. Police anywhere could send in prints and ask whether they matched a known criminal. The Bureau opened its crime laboratory in 1932. In July 1934 agents led by Melvin Purvis found the bank robber John Dillinger in Chicago and shot him dead as he left a movie theater (FBI).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa, 1921: no one convicted" -->
On 30 May 1921 Tulsa police arrested Dick Rowland, a 19-year-old Black man, after a white man accused him of attacking Sarah Page, the white woman who ran the elevator he used. Page later said he had done nothing and would not press charges. A newspaper story brought a white crowd to the courthouse to lynch him. Black men from Greenwood, Tulsa's Black district, came armed to protect him. When a shot was fired, fighting broke out. Tulsa police then swore in hundreds of white men as special deputies, many of them from the lynch mob and many of them drunk. Police officers helped organize these men for the attack on Greenwood. From that night into the afternoon of 1 June, as many as 10,000 white Tulsans burned 35 city blocks and killed Black residents. Estimates of the dead run from 50 to 300. One of them was the surgeon Dr. A. C. Jackson, shot by a young white man as he came out of his house with his hands up. National Guard soldiers took Black residents to guarded camps. They held no white residents there (US Department of Justice, 2025).
- A state grand jury heard almost 200 witnesses. It charged about 70 men, many of them Greenwood's Black leaders, with starting a "riot." On 26 June 1921 its report blamed the armed Black men who had gone to the courthouse. Most of the cases were later dropped, including the one against Dick Rowland. Some white men were charged with looting, and none of them went to prison.
- The grand jury also charged Police Chief John Gustafson with failing to do his duty and with earlier corruption. He was convicted and removed from his job.
- In February 2001 an Oklahoma state commission published a report on the massacre and recommended that the survivors be paid. In January 2025 the US Department of Justice published its own review. It found that police and white residents had worked together in the attack, and that under today's civil rights laws the attackers could have been convicted. By 2025 every attacker was dead, so no one can be tried.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Osage murders, 1921 to 1929" -->
In the early 1920s the Osage Nation in Oklahoma earned more than $30 million a year from oil under its land. Osage families held headrights, shares of that money, and a non-Osage relative could inherit one. The cattleman William Hale set out to get the headrights of Mollie Burkhart's family. Her sister Anna Brown was shot in May 1921. Her mother, Lizzie Q, died soon after, and people suspected poison. Her cousin Henry Roan was shot in 1923. In March 1923 men blew up the house of her sister Rita Smith, killing Rita, her husband Bill and Nettie Brookshire, who worked in their house. Counts of the Osage people murdered in these years run from about 24 to more than 60. In April 1923 the Osage Tribal Council asked the federal government to send detectives, and the Bureau of Investigation sent agents. Tom White led four agents who worked undercover as a cattle buyer, an insurance salesman, an oil prospector and an herb doctor. Hale's nephew Ernest Burkhart, Mollie's husband, pleaded guilty in 1926. A jury convicted Hale in January 1929, and he was sent to prison for life (FBI and the Oklahoma Historical Society).

### Eliot Ness and Al Capone
> **Who:** the Chicago gang boss who sold illegal beer and liquor during Prohibition, and the federal agent whose team raided his breweries. · **When and where:** Chicago, 1925 to 1932.
From 1920 to 1933 the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution made it illegal to make or sell alcohol. The ban was called Prohibition. Al Capone became the head of a Chicago gang in 1925. His men made and sold beer and liquor, and he paid police and officials to leave the business alone. In 1929 federal officials hired Eliot Ness, who was 26, to run the Prohibition agents in Chicago. Ness picked a team of about ten agents he trusted. From March 1931 they raided Capone's stills and breweries. Gangsters offered them bribes, and the agents said no, so a Chicago newspaper called them "untouchables."
Capone went to prison for not paying his taxes. Treasury Department agents built that case. On 18 October 1931 a jury found him guilty of tax evasion, which means not paying taxes that are owed. A judge sentenced him to eleven years in federal prison and a $50,000 fine. He served part of it at Alcatraz, the federal prison on an island in San Francisco Bay. He was released in November 1939 with brain damage from syphilis. Ness died in 1957. A book he wrote, a TV series and a 1987 film made him famous, and the film changes many of the facts (FBI and *TIME*).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliot-ness-al-capone" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beaten into confessing: the third degree" -->
The "third degree" meant police using cruelty to force a prisoner to confess. In 1931 a federal commission set up by President Herbert Hoover reported that "physical brutality, protracted questioning, threats, prolonged illegal detention" by police were "widespread," in a study of 15 cities.
- In Mississippi on 30 March 1934, a man named Raymond Stewart was killed. That night Deputy Sheriff Dial and a group of white men seized Arthur Ellington, a Black man, and hanged him by a rope from a tree limb. They let him down and hanged him again. When he still said he was innocent, they tied him to the tree and whipped him. Days later the deputy whipped him again until he agreed to confess. On 1 April the same deputy made Ed Brown and Henry Shields take off their clothes, bent them over chairs and cut their backs open with a leather strap that had buckles on it, until they confessed in the words he wanted. The three were charged on 4 April, tried the next day and sentenced to death. In court the deputy admitted the whippings.
- In 1936, in *Brown v. Mississippi*, the US Supreme Court threw out the convictions. Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote: "The rack and torture chamber may not be substituted for the witness stand."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Scottsboro cases, 1931" -->
On 25 March 1931 nine Black teenagers and young men, aged 13 to 20, were taken off a freight train in Alabama and arrested. Two white women on the train, Victoria Price and Ruby Bates, accused them of rape. They were Olen Montgomery, Clarence Norris, Haywood Patterson, Ozie Powell, Willie Roberson, Charlie Weems, Eugene Williams, and the brothers Andy and Roy Wright. Eight of them were sentenced to death. In 1932 the US Supreme Court ordered new trials because they had not had real lawyers. Ruby Bates later said the story was false and testified for the defense. In 1935 the Court threw out the convictions again because Black people had been kept off the county's jury lists. New trials went on for seven more years. The last one left prison in 1976, and in 2013 Alabama's governor signed a law that led to pardons for all nine (Daren Salter, *Encyclopedia of Alabama*).

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of convict leasing in Alabama, 1928" -->
Alabama's officials kept renting county prisoners to coal companies into the 1920s. James Knox, a prisoner from Mobile, died at the Flat Top mine in 1924. His death was first reported as a suicide by swallowing poison. An autopsy, a doctor's examination of the body, showed that he had been whipped with a steel wire and put into hot water, and that he died of heart failure. The poison had been put into his body after he died. Birmingham's city history of the case does not name who whipped him. On 30 June 1928 Alabama became the last state to end convict leasing (City of Birmingham, and Mary Ellen Curtin, *Encyclopedia of Alabama*).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="crime-justice" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1961 and 1968 the justices of the US Supreme Court set new rules for police and courts. Police could not use evidence from an illegal search. A poor person charged with a serious crime had to get a lawyer. Police had to warn people of their rights before questioning them. In 1972 the justices struck down the death-penalty laws then in use, and in 1976 they allowed new ones. From the 1970s on, lawmakers in Congress and the states wrote long prison terms that judges had to give, including for drug crimes. The number of people in state and federal prisons grew from about 330,000 in 1980 to about 1.38 million in 2000. At the end of 2000, Black Americans were about 46 out of every 100 people serving prison sentences (Bureau of Justice Statistics). Some trials of white men and police officers ended with no one convicted, among them the 1955 trial for the murder of Emmett Till and the 1992 state trial of the officers who beat Rodney King.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emmett-till" name="Emmett Till" movie="Till (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emmett Till
> **Who:** a 14-year-old Black boy from Chicago, kidnapped and murdered by two white men who were then acquitted by an all-white jury. · **When and where:** Money and Sumner, Mississippi, August and September 1955.
In August 1955 Emmett Till, who was 14, was visiting relatives near Money, Mississippi. On 24 August he went into a grocery store owned by a white couple, Roy Bryant and his wife, Carolyn Bryant, to buy candy. As she left, he whistled at her (US Department of Justice). Between 2 and 3:30 in the morning of 28 August, Roy Bryant and his half-brother J. W. Milam came to the house of Till's great-uncle, Mose Wright. Milam carried a pistol and a flashlight. The two men took the boy away, with at least one other person.
Three days later two boys fishing in the Tallahatchie River found his body. He had been beaten and shot above the right ear, and one eye had been forced from its socket. The men had tied a cotton gin fan of about 70 to 75 pounds around his neck with barbed wire to sink him. His mother, Mamie Till Bradley, had his coffin left open at his funeral in Chicago so that people could see what had been done to him.
Bryant and Milam were tried for murder in Sumner in September 1955. Mississippi kept women and Black people off the jury. Mose Wright stood up in court and pointed to Milam as one of the men. On 23 September the twelve white men on the jury found both men not guilty after 67 minutes. In November another grand jury refused to charge them with kidnapping, although they had admitted taking him. In January 1956 they told a writer for *Look* magazine that they had killed him, and the magazine paid them between $3,600 and $4,000. The law did not allow them to be tried again for the murder. The US Department of Justice reopened the case in 2004 and again in 2017, and in 2021 closed it with no one charged. In 2023 President Joe Biden made places connected to Till and his mother a national monument.

### Clarence Earl Gideon
> **Who:** a poor man in Florida who was refused a lawyer, wrote to the US Supreme Court from prison in pencil, and won the right to a lawyer for everyone charged with a serious crime. · **When and where:** Panama City, Florida, 1961 to 1963.
On 3 June 1961 someone broke into the Bay Harbor Pool Room in Panama City, Florida, and took coins from the jukebox and the cigarette machine. A 22-year-old man named Henry Cook told police he had seen Clarence Earl Gideon leave with his pockets full of coins. Gideon, then 50, had no money for a lawyer. Florida gave free lawyers only to people facing death sentences, so Judge Robert McCrary Jr. refused him one. Gideon questioned the witnesses himself. The jury convicted him, and the judge sentenced him to five years in prison.
From the state prison at Raiford, Gideon wrote a petition to the US Supreme Court in pencil. The justices gave him a well-known Washington lawyer, Abe Fortas. On 18 March 1963 all nine justices agreed that a state must give a lawyer to a person charged with a serious crime who cannot pay for one. At Gideon's second trial on 5 August 1963, a local lawyer, W. Fred Turner, showed that Cook had lied at the first trial about his own criminal record. The jury found Gideon not guilty after an hour. The actor Henry Fonda played him in a 1980 television film (*Gideon v. Wainwright*).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarence-earl-gideon" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rules for the police station, 1961 to 1968" -->
The justices wrote several more rules for police and courts in these years.
- In 1957 Cleveland police forced their way into the home of Dollree Mapp and searched it. They showed her a paper they called a search warrant, a judge's written permission to search. No such paper was ever shown in court. In 1961, in *Mapp v. Ohio*, the Supreme Court ruled that evidence police get from an illegal search cannot be used in a state trial.
- In 1963 Phoenix police officers Carroll Cooley and Wilfred Young questioned Ernesto Miranda for two hours about a kidnapping and rape. He confessed. No one had told him he could stay silent or have a lawyer. On 13 June 1966, in *Miranda v. Arizona*, five of the nine justices ruled that police must tell a person in custody these rights before questioning. Police departments across the country printed "Miranda warning" cards for officers to read aloud. Arizona tried Miranda again without the confession, and a jury convicted him again in 1967.
- In June 1964 an Arizona sheriff locked up Gerald Gault, 15, without telling his parents, after a neighbor complained of a phone call full of dirty words. A judge sent him to a state reform school until he turned 21. In 1967 the Supreme Court ruled that children in juvenile court have the right to a lawyer, to hear the charges and to stay silent.
- In 1968, in *Terry v. Ohio*, the justices allowed police to stop a person they reasonably suspect is about to commit a crime and pat the outside of the person's clothes for weapons. This is called a stop and frisk.
Shared with: `government-politics` (the rulings as law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Executions stop and start again, 1972 to 1999" -->
On 29 June 1972, in *Furman v. Georgia*, the Supreme Court struck down the death-penalty laws then in use. More than 600 people were on death row. Justice Potter Stewart wrote that the sentences were given out as randomly as being "struck by lightning." In the next four years 35 states and the federal government wrote new death-penalty laws, and on 2 July 1976 the Court allowed them. On 17 January 1977 a Utah firing squad shot Gary Gilmore, who had murdered two young men in robberies the year before. A firing squad is a group of shooters who fire rifles at a prisoner's heart. He was the first person executed under the new laws. Texas carried out the first execution by lethal injection on 7 December 1982. In most states this meant three drugs put into the prisoner's body: one to put the person to sleep, one to stop the muscles moving, and one to stop the heart. Executions rose to 98 in 1999, the most in one year under the new laws (Death Penalty Information Center).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attica, September 1971" -->
On 9 September 1971 about 1,281 of the roughly 2,200 men held at Attica, a state prison in New York, took over the prison and held 42 guards and workers as hostages. They had asked in July for better food and medical care, and for fairer parole. A guard, William Quinn, was beaten and died. On 13 September Governor Nelson Rockefeller, who had refused to come to the prison, ordered state police and guards to retake it. They dropped tear gas into the yard and fired into it, some with shotguns. They killed at least 39 people: 29 prisoners and 10 of the hostages. State officials first said the prisoners had cut the hostages' throats. The medical examiners found that the hostages had died from the officers' bullets. Officers then tortured many of the prisoners who survived. Prosecutors charged 62 prisoners and one state trooper. New York paid $8 million to the prisoners and their families in 2000 and $12 million to the workers' families in 2005.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wounded Knee, 1973: the marshals and the trial" -->
From 27 February 1973, members of the American Indian Movement and Oglala Lakota people held the village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, for 71 days. US marshals and FBI agents surrounded it with armored vehicles and automatic weapons. A marshal, Lloyd Grimm, was shot and could no longer walk. Frank Clearwater, a Cherokee man, was shot in the head during a gunfight with federal officers and died. A federal sniper shot and killed Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont, an Oglala man. In 1974 a federal judge, Fred Nichol, threw out the charges against the movement's leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means. He wrote that the government's misconduct in the case was so serious that the case had to end.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drug laws and long sentences, 1973 to 1994" -->
Lawmakers wrote the sentences that filled the prisons. A mandatory minimum is the shortest sentence a law allows. The judge cannot give less.
- In 1973 New York lawmakers passed laws that Governor Nelson Rockefeller asked for. Selling two ounces of heroin or cocaine meant at least 15 years to life in prison.
- In June 1986 the basketball player Len Bias died from cocaine. House Speaker Tip O'Neill had Democratic leaders write a new drug law, and President Ronald Reagan signed it on 27 October 1986. It set five years in federal prison for 5 grams of crack cocaine, and the same five years for 500 grams of powder cocaine. The two are the same drug in different forms.
- In 1992, 91 out of every 100 people sentenced in federal court for crack were Black. In 2004, 53 out of every 100 people who entered treatment for crack were Black and 38 were white (US Sentencing Commission).
- In 1994 Senator Joe Biden wrote the Senate version of the largest crime law in the country's history, and President Bill Clinton signed it. It paid for 100,000 new police officers and $9.7 billion of prisons, and it added 60 new federal crimes that could be punished by death.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kemba-smith" name="Kemba Smith" movie="Kemba (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kemba Smith
> **Who:** a college student sentenced to 24 and a half years in federal prison for her boyfriend's crack cocaine ring, though she never sold drugs. · **When and where:** Virginia and a federal prison in Danbury, Connecticut, 1994 to 2000.
Kemba Smith grew up near Richmond, Virginia, and went to Hampton University. There she met Peter Hall, who ran a crack cocaine ring and who beat her. She did jobs for the ring, which she later said she did out of fear of him. While pregnant, she turned herself in to federal agents on 1 September 1994. She pleaded guilty to conspiracy, which means agreeing with others to commit a crime, and to lying to federal agents. She had never sold or used drugs (NAACP Legal Defense Fund).
Federal judge Richard B. Kellam sentenced her to 24 years and 6 months, with no parole. Her son was born while she waited in jail, and she had two days with him before her parents took him home. President Bill Clinton cut her sentence short on 22 December 2000, after about six and a half years. She finished college, worked to restore the vote to people with felony records, and in 2025 President Joe Biden pardoned her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kemba-smith" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many people were in prison" -->
The Bureau of Justice Statistics has counted prisoners every year since 1925. In 1950 state and federal prisons held about 166,000 people with sentences. In 1972 they held about 196,000. The count began to climb in 1974. It reached about 330,000 in 1980, about 771,000 in 1990 and about 1.38 million in 2000. Counting local jails, about 2.07 million people were locked up at the end of 2000. That year almost 10 of every 100 Black men aged 25 to 29 were in prison, against about 1 in 100 white men that age.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rodney King, 1991 and 1992" -->
Early on 3 March 1991, after a car chase, Los Angeles police officers Laurence Powell, Timothy Wind and Theodore Briseno and Sergeant Stacey Koon shocked Rodney King with a Taser and hit him with batons 53 to 56 times, by the police chief's own count. He had a broken bone in his face and a broken ankle. George Holliday filmed it from his apartment. The trial was moved to Simi Valley, and on 29 April 1992 a jury with ten white members acquitted the four officers of assault. Fighting, burning and looting spread across Los Angeles for five days. Counts of the dead are 55 and 63. Police or National Guard soldiers shot 10 or 11 of them. In 1993 a federal jury convicted Koon and Powell of violating King's civil rights, and each got 30 months in prison.

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="41 shots: Amadou Diallo, 1999" -->
On 4 February 1999 four New York City police officers, Sean Carroll, Richard Murphy, Edward McMellon and Kenneth Boss, fired 41 shots at Amadou Diallo, a 23-year-old immigrant from Guinea, outside his building in the Bronx. Nineteen hit him. He had no gun. He had taken out his wallet. A jury acquitted all four officers of murder in February 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wrong man: DNA and the Central Park case" -->
DNA is the chemical code inside the cells of a body. Cells left at a crime can be tested to show whether they came from a certain person. In 1985 a Maryland jury sentenced Kirk Bloodsworth to death for the rape and murder of Dawn Hamilton, a nine-year-old girl, because five witnesses said they had seen him with her. In 1993 a DNA test showed that the attacker's cells found on her underwear were not his. He was freed after nine years in prison, the first person on death row cleared by DNA. In 2003 the same kind of test matched Kimberly Shay Ruffner, who had been in a cell one floor below him.
- On 19 April 1989 Trisha Meili was attacked and raped while running in Central Park in New York City. Police questioned five teenagers for at least seven hours: Antron McCray, 15, Kevin Richardson, 14, Yusef Salaam, 15, Raymond Santana, 14, and Korey Wise, 16. Four of them confessed on video and later said the police had lied to them and pressured them. The DNA from the attack matched none of them. Juries convicted all five in 1990. What happened to them after 2000 is in the next era.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
State and federal prisons held about 1.62 million people at the end of 2009, the highest count ever. By the end of 2023 they held about 1.25 million (Bureau of Justice Statistics). Black Americans were still sent to prison at almost five times the rate of white Americans. DNA tests and new evidence cleared more than 3,700 people of crimes between 1989 and early 2026, by the count of the National Registry of Exonerations. Phones and police body cameras recorded police killings, and some officers were charged and convicted. Others were never charged, or juries acquitted them. Violent crime rose in 2020 and then fell. In 2025 the murder rate was 4.1 for every 100,000 people, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest the FBI has recorded. The figures here are the latest published by September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fewer prisoners, the same gap" -->
The prison count fell in 2010 for the first time since 1972. Senator Dick Durbin wrote the Fair Sentencing Act, and President Barack Obama signed it on 3 August 2010. It cut the gap between crack and powder cocaine sentences from 100 to 1 to 18 to 1. In December 2018 President Donald Trump signed the First Step Act, which applied that change to people already in prison. In its first year more than 2,000 federal prisoners got shorter sentences under it. New York ended the Rockefeller minimum sentences in 2009.
- At the end of 2023 about 1,254,000 people were in state and federal prisons, and about 657,000 more were in local jails in mid-2024. For every 100,000 Black Americans, 929 were serving prison sentences. For every 100,000 white Americans, 190 were. In 2013 the rates had been 1,348 and 237 (Bureau of Justice Statistics).
- In 2024 about 4 million Americans could not vote because of a felony conviction, including 1 in every 22 Black adults (The Sentencing Project).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="exonerated-five" name="The Exonerated Five" movie="When They See Us (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Exonerated Five
> **Who:** five New York City teenagers convicted of a 1989 rape in Central Park, cleared in 2002 when another man confessed and his DNA matched. · **When and where:** New York City, 1989 to 2014.
On 19 April 1989 Trisha Meili was beaten and raped while running in Central Park. Police questioned five teenagers for at least seven hours: Antron McCray, 15, Kevin Richardson, 14, Yusef Salaam, 15, Raymond Santana, 14, and Korey Wise, 16. Four of them made videotaped statements and took them back within weeks, saying police had lied to them and scared them into it. The DNA found at the scene matched none of them. Juries convicted all five in 1990. The four younger boys got 5 to 10 years. Wise was tried as an adult, found not guilty of rape but guilty of sexual abuse, assault and riot, and got 5 to 15 years in an adult prison.
In 2001 a prisoner named Matias Reyes, who was serving a life sentence for other rapes, met Wise at Auburn prison in New York. In 2002 Reyes told officials that he had raped Meili and that he had acted alone. A DNA test matched him. District Attorney Robert Morgenthau's office looked at the case again and asked the court to throw out the convictions. On 19 December 2002 Justice Charles Tejada vacated all of them, which means he cancelled them. By then all five had served their time. Wise, released in August 2002, had spent about 13 years locked up. A panel chosen by the police commissioner said in 2003 that the five had most likely also taken part. The court and the prosecutors did not accept that. In 2014 New York City paid the five $41 million to settle their lawsuit. The 1989 attacker was never charged, because the time limit for charging the crime had run out.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="exonerated-five" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Proving convictions wrong" -->
An exoneration happens when a court or a governor clears a person of a crime after a conviction, because new evidence shows the person did not do it. The National Registry of Exonerations counted 3,767 exonerations from 1989 through 2025. The Innocence Project counts more than 600 in which DNA tests helped. In 2025 alone 97 people were cleared, and on average each had spent 14.2 years in prison. Fifty-nine of the 97 were Black. In at least 70 of the cases, police, prosecutors or other officials had broken the rules (the Registry calls this official misconduct). States and cities have paid more than $5.5 billion to people cleared since 1989.
- In 1944 South Carolina officials tried, convicted and sentenced George Stinney Jr., a 14-year-old Black boy, in one day for killing two white girls, Betty June Binnicker, 11, and Mary Emma Thames, 8. The all-white jury took less than ten minutes. On 16 June 1944 state prison officials killed him in the electric chair. On 16 December 2014 Judge Carmen Mullen threw out his conviction because he had not had a real defense and his confession had probably been forced. No court has named anyone else as the killer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The death penalty, 2000 to 2026" -->
Executions fell from 85 in 2000 to 11 in 2021, then rose to 47 in 2025, 19 of them in Florida. In 2026, 27 people had been executed by 18 September. At least 202 or 203 people sentenced to death since 1973 have been cleared and freed (the Death Penalty Information Center prints both numbers). In 2002 the Supreme Court ruled that states may not execute people with intellectual disabilities, and in 2005 that they may not execute people for crimes committed before age 18. By 2026, 23 states had ended the death penalty. The Death Penalty Information Center, a research group, lists 21 people executed between 1989 and 2024 who may have been innocent, among them Cameron Todd Willingham in Texas in 2004 and Troy Davis in Georgia in 2011. The group's list records no court or governor declaring any of them innocent. Courts do not usually hear a claim of innocence once the person is dead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police killings and what happened in court" -->
No government agency keeps a full count of people killed by police. A group called Campaign Zero counted at least 1,383 in 2024 and 1,314 in 2025. By its count, Black Americans were killed by police at 2.6 times the rate of white Americans in 2025. In 2020, 62 of every 100 local police departments used body cameras, cameras worn on an officer's uniform (Bureau of Justice Statistics).
- **Michael Brown**, 18, Ferguson, Missouri, 9 August 2014: shot dead by officer Darren Wilson. A county grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to charge someone, did not charge Wilson. The US Department of Justice did not charge him in 2015.
- **Eric Garner**, 43, Staten Island, New York, 17 July 2014: officer Daniel Pantaleo held him around the neck in a chokehold, which police rules banned, while officers pinned him face down. Garner said "I can't breathe" 11 times and died. A grand jury did not charge Pantaleo. The police commissioner fired him in 2019.
- **Tamir Rice**, 12, Cleveland, 22 November 2014: he was holding a toy gun in a park. The caller to 911 said it was "probably fake," but the dispatcher did not pass that on. Officer Timothy Loehmann shot him almost as soon as the police car stopped. A grand jury did not charge him.
- **Breonna Taylor**, 26, Louisville, Kentucky, 13 March 2020: officers forced open her door with a warrant to search for drugs. Her boyfriend fired once, thinking they were intruders. Officers fired 32 shots, and she was killed. No officer was charged with her death. In 2024 a federal jury convicted Brett Hankison, whose shots hit no one, of using excessive force, and he got 33 months.
- **George Floyd**, 46, Minneapolis, 25 May 2020: officer Derek Chauvin knelt on his neck for more than nine minutes. On 20 April 2021 a jury convicted Chauvin of murder and manslaughter, and on 25 June the judge sentenced him to 22 and a half years. In 2022 a federal judge added 21 years, served at the same time. The three other officers got 2 and a half to 3 and a half years.
- **Tyre Nichols**, 29, Memphis, Tennessee, 7 January 2023: five officers pulled him from his car, sprayed and shocked him, and then punched, kicked and beat him with a baton near his mother's house. He died three days later. Two officers pleaded guilty. A federal jury convicted the other three of lesser crimes in 2024, and a state jury acquitted them of murder in May 2025. In August 2025 a federal judge ordered a new federal trial for the three.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crime goes up, then down" -->
Murders rose from 16,669 in 2019 to 21,570 in 2020, up 29 percent, the biggest one-year rise in more than a century (Pew Research Center, from FBI data). Then crime fell. From 2024 to 2025 the violent crime rate dropped 9.3 percent, the largest drop since the FBI began making national estimates in 1936. Murders fell about 18 percent. The 2025 murder rate of 4.1 for every 100,000 people matched 1955 and 1956 as the lowest the FBI has recorded (FBI, August 2026).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaders in court" -->
In May 2024 a Manhattan jury convicted former President Donald Trump of 34 felonies, serious crimes, for falsifying business records to hide a payment to an adult film actress. He was the first president convicted of a felony. After he won the 2024 election, Judge Juan Merchan sentenced him on 10 January 2025 to no jail, fine or probation. He appealed. In July 2024 a federal jury convicted Senator Bob Menendez of New Jersey of taking bribes and of acting as an agent of Egypt. Agents had found gold bars and $480,000 in cash in his home. In January 2025 a judge sentenced him to 11 years. On 20 January 2025 President Trump pardoned everyone convicted over the attack on the US Capitol on 6 January 2021, except 14 people whose sentences he cut to the time they had already served.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose courts: McGirt, 2020" -->
On 9 July 2020, in *McGirt v. Oklahoma*, five of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation that Congress set aside in the 1800s had never been ended. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that Oklahoma therefore could not prosecute Native people for major crimes there. Federal and tribal courts took those cases. Oklahoma's highest criminal court then applied the same rule to other nations' lands, so most of eastern Oklahoma, including much of Tulsa, counts as Indian country for criminal cases. In 2022 the justices ruled that state prosecutors may still charge people who are not Native for crimes against Native victims there.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa's survivors, 2020 to 2026" -->
In 2020 the last known survivors of the 1921 Tulsa massacre sued the city, the county and the state. A county judge dismissed the case in July 2023, and on 12 June 2024 the Oklahoma Supreme Court agreed with her. Viola Ford Fletcher, who was 7 in 1921, told Congress in 2021, "I still see Black men being shot, Black bodies lying in the street." She died on 24 November 2025, aged 111. Lessie Benningfield Randle, born in November 1914, was the last known living survivor in May 2026. On 1 June 2025 Tulsa's mayor, Monroe Nichols, announced a private fund with a goal of $105 million by June 2026 for housing, old Greenwood buildings, land and scholarships. It included no direct payments to the survivors. No total raised had been published by September 2026.
