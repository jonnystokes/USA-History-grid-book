## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Games here were old, organized, and sometimes played between nations to settle disputes instead of fighting. Children played too, and what survives of their play is mostly objects and accounts recorded later from the nations themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

Stickball and lacrosse, with hundreds of players and fields sometimes miles long; running as both training and competition; games of chance with rules and stakes; children's games and toys.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A thin era: newcomers here were mostly soldiers and missionaries, and their play is barely recorded. Native nations' games and children's play continued through the century; what the Spanish wrote down was about soldiering and missions, not about what anyone did for fun.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish games and mission restrictions" -->
Spanish colonists' horse racing and card games; missions restricting Native ceremonies and games.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In some colonies play is suspicious; in others it is the point of a holiday. Most children worked, and their play fit around the work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puritan limits and tavern games" -->
Puritan limits on games and on the Sabbath; bowling, quoits, and tavern games; horse racing beginning in Virginia; children's games carried from England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what a child's day held" -->

- What enslaved children and Native children in the colonies did with any free time, and how much there was — find the documentation, and where it is silent say so [VERIFY]

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial sport is mostly betting on animals and on each other. Nothing children play is organized by adults into clubs, teams, or leagues; that is more than a century away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="racetracks, blood sports, and fairs" -->
Horse racing and the first racetracks; tavern games; blood sports; wrestling and footraces at fairs and musters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="sports-play" label="1750 to 1800" state="thin" progress="seed" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Play and duty mix: the muster, the fair, and the tavern are where people compete. Organized sport does not exist yet — no leagues, no teams, no fixed rules, no spectators paying to watch. Children's play is household-made and outdoors, and the record of it is thin.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="musters and early ball games" -->
Militia musters as social occasions with contests; early bat-and-ball games with local rules; horse racing continuing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Sports are worth billions, and the biggest arguments are about safety, fairness, and money. Most American teenagers now carry a phone, much of what children play is online and with people they never meet, and organized youth sport costs money to join. How much that change matters, and to what, is something researchers are still measuring and still arguing about.
