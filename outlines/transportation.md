## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Everything and everyone moved on foot or on water. There were no wheeled vehicles and no horses — the horses of the Americas had died out after the ice age. Historians' explanation for the missing wheel is plain: with no large animal left to pull a cart, a wheel had no work to do, and the trail-and-river network already moved people and goods across the whole continent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the trail network" -->
- Named long-distance systems, not random paths: the Great Trail, a footpath network of Algonquian- and Iroquoian-speaking peoples linking New England and the mid-Atlantic to the Great Lakes; the path later called the Natchez Trace, begun as bison paths to salt licks and widened by the Natchez, Choctaw, and Chickasaw for trade and travel — in use for some 8,000 years (NPS; Mississippi Encyclopedia).
- Trade goods moved enormous distances on these trails and rivers — seashells and native copper traveled between the Great Lakes and the Gulf Coast as early as 6000 BC (Library of Congress).
- Many of these routes became colonial roads, then highways; parts of the book's later roads sit on them. Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations whose trails these were) · `migration` (the moving peoples).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="canoes, portages, and the travois" -->
- Two main watercraft: the dugout — a whole tree hollowed by fire and tools, used for thousands of years (canoes as old as Egypt's pyramids have been found in Midwest lakes — Smithsonian Magazine) — and the birchbark canoe of the Northeast and Great Lakes, light enough for one or two people to carry between waters (portaging), quick to repair with forest materials.
- On the Plains, loads moved on the travois — two poles dragged behind a dog, carrying the load between them. Dogs were the only pack animal (Encyclopedia of the Great Plains).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The horse comes back, the first wheels roll, and the first permanent port opens. For nearly everyone living here, though, travel in the 1500s still means foot, canoe, and trail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="horses, carts, and a port" -->
- Spanish ships carried horses to the Caribbean from 1493 and onto the mainland with Coronado's expedition, 1540–42 (verified in `research/research-migration.md`). How the horse then spread among Native nations, and what it changed, is `native-nations`' story.
- St. Augustine, founded September 8, 1565, by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, became the first permanent European port in the future United States — a supply and defense stop for Spanish ships (City of St. Augustine; EBSCO).
- In 1598, Oñate's colony column rolled north into New Mexico with 83 wagons and carts — the first documented wheeled vehicles in the future United States (the journey is `migration`'s story; the wheels are ours).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="transportation" label="The 1600s" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the colonies the river is the road, and the first paid rides in the future United States begin — a rowed ferry, then a monthly post rider.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="ferries and the first long road" -->
- Boston's Charlestown ferry, authorized June 14, 1631 — two pence a person — is among the first chartered public transportation in North America; in 1640 the colony gave the ferry's income to the new Harvard College (Massachusetts Court of Assistants records; West End Museum).
- January 1673: the first post rider leaves New York for Boston, about 250 miles in roughly two weeks. The mail is `news-communication`'s story; the path he rode, worn into the Boston Post Road, is ours (Connecticut History / CTHumanities).
- Nearly all freight still moves by water: canoes, shallops, and coastal ships between colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="edward-converse" name="Edward Converse" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Edward Converse
> **Who:** Puritan settler who ran the first ferry between Charlestown and Boston. · **When and where:** Boston harbor, authorized June 14, 1631.
The court record survives: he could charge two pence for a single passenger, one penny each for two or more. A rowed boat across a harbor was the era's whole public transit system (Massachusetts Court of Assistants record, 1631; Charlestown Historical Society).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edward-converse" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Roads good enough for wheels finally appear, and Pennsylvania builders shape a freight wagon for American conditions. Most long trips are still miserable — travelers ford rivers and sleep where they can.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Conestoga wagon" -->
The first record of the name is December 31, 1717 — James Logan's account book lists a "Conestogoe Waggon." German and Swiss wagon builders near the Conestoga River in Lancaster County made it: a bed that curved down toward the middle so barrels would not shift, a white cloth cover on hoops, four to six horses, built to haul farm loads the 60-plus miles to Philadelphia (explorepahistory.com; Lancaster historical sources). It becomes the main freight vehicle east of the Appalachians for over a century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="stage lines, corduroy roads, and the Great Wagon Road" -->
- First stage services: a Burlington–Perth Amboy line patented in New Jersey in 1706; irregular New York–Philadelphia coach service from 1732 (New Jersey postal-history records; Colonial Williamsburg research).
- Where roads crossed swamps, builders laid logs side by side — the corduroy road, named for the ribbed cloth. Bone-rattling but passable.
- The Great Wagon Road, heavily used from the 1720s, runs about 730 miles from Philadelphia down the Shenandoah Valley toward Georgia — the road is ours; the families moving down it are `migration`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-kemble-knight-transportation" name="Sarah Kemble Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Kemble Knight
> **Who:** Boston businesswoman who rode from Boston toward New Haven and New York with post riders as her guides, and wrote down what the road was actually like. · **When and where:** October 1704, on the future Boston Post Road; journal published 1825.
Her journal records the road itself: rivers crossed by canoe in the dark, horses swimming beside, bridges she feared, guides hired town to town. It is one of the best surviving eyewitness accounts of early American road travel. Her nights in the households along the way are `home-family`'s story (their slug: sarah-kemble-knight).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sarah-kemble-knight-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="transportation" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Private companies begin building roads and charging to use them, and the first American steamboat carries paying passengers — twenty years before the steamboat everyone remembers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the turnpike era opens" -->
The Philadelphia and Lancaster Turnpike: ground broken 1792, in use by 1794–95 (sources give both years for the opening), 62 miles of engineered stone-surfaced road with toll gates — the country's first long-distance built road, and the model for a turnpike boom that followed (FHWA highway history; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="steam on the water, before Fulton" -->
On August 22, 1787, John Fitch ran a steamboat on the Delaware in front of Constitutional Convention delegates. By summer 1790 his boat ran scheduled passenger service between Philadelphia and New Jersey towns — the first steamboat service anywhere in the world to carry paying passengers on a schedule. Too few people rode; the business failed (Pennsylvania Heritage; Encyclopedia.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-fitch" name="John Fitch" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Fitch
> **Who:** Built and ran America's first working steamboats, and could not make them pay. · **When and where:** the Delaware River, 1787–1790; died 1798.
He proved the machine worked and ran a real scheduled service in 1790, with steam-driven oars for propulsion. Mechanical breakdowns, thin ridership, and lost backers ended it. Fulton succeeded seventeen years later where Fitch had been first — the honest order of events, stated plainly.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-fitch" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="transportation" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Three new ways to move arrive inside forty years — the steamboat, the canal, and the railroad — and each one cuts travel time to a fraction of what it was.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the steamboat age" -->
- August 17, 1807: Fulton's North River Steamboat (later called the Clermont) leaves New York for Albany — 150 miles in 32 hours, against about four days for a sailing sloop. Regular service starts at once (Hudson River Valley Institute; History.com).
- Later retellings say doubters called the boat "Fulton's Folly." The nickname is a staple of the story; documentation from 1807 itself is thin — say so.
- Steamboats then take over the western rivers; Mississippi boats carry the cotton economy. Their boilers also explode, killing thousands over the era — the wrecks and the safety laws that followed are `disasters`' story.
- The National Road: begun at Cumberland, Maryland, 1811, the first highway built with federal money; reaches Wheeling on the Ohio River 1818, pushes to Vandalia, Illinois, by the 1830s (NPS; FHWA).
- Covered wagons and ox teams — the machines of the overland trails — are ours; the journeys and the people making them are `migration`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-fulton" name="Robert Fulton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Fulton
> **Who:** Made the steamboat a working business. · **When and where:** New York's Hudson River, August 17, 1807.
John Fitch ran a steamboat 17 years earlier. Fulton was the first to put an engine, a hull, money from his partner Robert Livingston and a paying route together into a business that lasted. In 1811 and 1812 his and Livingston's boat New Orleans ran from Pittsburgh down the Ohio and Mississippi to New Orleans, the first steamboat on the western rivers. New Orleans recorded 21 steamboat arrivals in 1814 and 2,770 in 1846 (Heinz History Center, American Battlefield Trust).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-fulton" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Erie Canal" -->
- Dug 1817–1825, mostly by hand: 363 miles from the Hudson to Lake Erie, opened October 26, 1825, cost about $7.1 million. Mockers called it "Clinton's Ditch" while it was being dug; tolls repaid the whole cost in about nine years (Library of Congress business-history guide; EBSCO).
- What it did to prices: hauling a ton from Buffalo to New York City fell from about $100 by wagon to under $10 by canal — some sources say about $6 (Library of Congress; EBSCO). Farm goods, people, and towns followed the water. The boom towns are `city-building`'s; the trade is `economy`'s.
- Who worked it: hoggees — often boys around ten years old — walked the towpath driving the mules, about $8 to $10 a month, 18 to 20 miles a day (Erie Canal Discovery / Lockport Union-Sun columns; Erie Canal Museum).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" name="DeWitt Clinton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DeWitt Clinton
> **Who:** New York governor who pushed the Erie Canal through years of mockery. · **When and where:** New York State, 1817–1825.
He championed the canal when critics called it "Clinton's Ditch" and "Clinton's Folly," and poured lake water into the ocean at the 1825 opening ceremony. The canal's numbers answered the mockery: cost repaid in about nine years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-garfield-transportation" name="James Garfield, canal boy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Garfield, canal boy
> **Who:** A sixteen-year-old who took the bottom job on an Ohio canal boat — decades before he was president. · **When and where:** the Ohio & Erie Canal, 1848, aboard the Evening Star.
Six weeks hauling coal, copper ore, and wood; he could not swim, fell overboard at night, and caught malaria that sent him home. Campaign biographies later inflated the falls to fourteen — the documented record is thinner, and the story should say so (NPS James A. Garfield National Historic Site; Eno Center for Transportation).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-garfield-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="who dug the canals, and whose land they crossed" -->
- The Erie Canal ran through the homelands of the Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations. New York officials got the land through state treaties and purchases. The Oneida Nation counts 27 treaties it calls illegal. In 1811 DeWitt Clinton, then a canal commissioner, predicted in a speech that "not a single Iroquois will be seen in this state" within a generation (Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor, Oneida Indian Nation).
- Local farmers and Irish immigrants dug the Erie. Recruiting ads in Ireland promised 8 to 12 dollars a month. Many men worked for 75 cents to $1.50 a day, and got no pay on rainy days. In the Montezuma Marshes in 1819, the canal commissioners reported that about 1,000 workers fell sick with malaria between July and October. Many died. No one kept a count of the dead (National Park Service study by George J. Svejda, 1969).
- In the South, owners hired out enslaved men to dig canals and kept their pay. Enslaved workers dug most of the Dismal Swamp Canal between Virginia and North Carolina, 1793 to 1805 (North Carolina History Project).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="moses-grandy" name="Moses Grandy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Moses Grandy
> **Who:** An enslaved canal-boat captain in the Dismal Swamp who bought his own freedom three times, and described the men who dug the canals. · **When and where:** Camden County, North Carolina, and the canals to Norfolk, Virginia, from the early 1800s. His narrative was published in London in 1843.
Grandy was born into slavery around 1786. He ran boats carrying shingles and boards through the swamp canals to Norfolk. He paid $600 for his freedom to his owner, James Grandy, who kept the money and sold him. He paid a second owner $600 more and was claimed again for that man's debts. A ship captain, Edward Minner, lent him $650 for the third price, and Grandy repaid him over three years. While Grandy worked on the canal, his wife was sold and marched past him in a group of enslaved people. The buyer, Mr. Rogerson, pointed a pistol at him to keep him away. He never saw her again. Grandy also described the canal diggers. They worked up to their waists in mud and slept in open huts with no bedding. An overseer named Wiley M'Pherson flogged men who had not finished the day's digging. Flogging means whipping a person's bare back. M'Pherson had salt brine poured into the cuts, and he left the men tied up all day with their feet barely touching the ground. Grandy wrote that M'Pherson whipped some men "till their entrails were visible." Grandy wrote that he saw men dead when they were untied, and that M'Pherson "never was called to account in any way for it." After he was free, Grandy kept working on boats and saved enough to buy his wife and children out of slavery (*Narrative of the Life of Moses Grandy*, 1843, Documenting the American South, UNC).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="moses-grandy" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first railroads" -->
- The Baltimore & Ohio lays its first stone July 4, 1828; its first 13 miles open in 1830 — the first American railroad to carry passengers and freight on a schedule (B&O Railroad Museum).
- The famous Tom Thumb story — Peter Cooper's little engine racing a horse near Baltimore, August 28, 1830, and losing when a belt slipped — rests almost entirely on a lecture John H. B. Latrobe gave in 1868, thirty-eight years later. No contemporary record confirms the race. Tell it as the legend it is, labeled (B&O Railroad Museum; History.com).
- August 9, 1831: the DeWitt Clinton locomotive pulls New York's first steam passenger train, Albany to Schenectady, 16 miles in about an hour (The Henry Ford; transportationhistory.org).
- Southern railroad companies bought or rented enslaved men to clear, grade and lay track. Historian Theodore Kornweibel Jr. found enslaved workers on 85 of the 113 railroads in the Confederate states, and about 15,000 people held by railroad companies in 1860. In 1855 the president of the Mississippi Central Railroad told his stockholders that "the slave is preferable to free labor" for building railroads (William G. Thomas, University of Nebraska railroad history project, and the National Railroad Hall of Fame).
- November 14, 1832: the first horse-drawn streetcar on rails, the John Mason, rolls in Manhattan — the beginning of American city mass transit (Hagley Museum; History.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="transportation" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The railroad becomes the way nearly everything moves. About 9,000 miles of track in 1850; roughly 193,000 to 215,000 by 1900, depending on the count (Library of Congress; Census historical statistics). At the era's end, cities are switching to electric streetcars and the whole country is on bicycles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the transcontinental railroad" -->
- Union Pacific built west, Central Pacific east; the rails met at Promontory Summit, Utah, May 10, 1869 — the golden spike. A coast-to-coast trip fell from about four months to about a week (Library of Congress).
- The Central Pacific hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers, 1865 to 1869, most from Guangdong province. They were about 9 in 10 of its workers. The company kept no record of workers who died. Avalanches, blasting with black powder and nitroglycerin, and smallpox killed them. Guesses printed in 1870 ran from about 50 to about 1,200 dead (PBS American Experience, Stanford Chinese Railroad Workers Project, Sacramento Union and Sacramento Reporter of June 30, 1870).
- Chinese workers were paid about $35 a month and bought their own food and housing. White workers got about $42 a month plus housing (Library of Congress).
- On July 1, 1862, Lincoln signed the Pacific Railroad Act. It gave each company 6,400 acres of public land and up to $48,000 in government bonds for every mile of track. The act ordered federal officials to end Native title to the land "as rapidly as may be." From 1850 to 1871, Congress gave railroads about 130 million acres (PBS, National Archives).
- The two lines took land from, or cut through the hunting grounds of, the Cheyenne, Lakota (Sioux), Arapaho, Shoshone and Paiute. Some Paiute men worked for the Central Pacific. The Union Pacific and the Army hired hundreds of Pawnee men to guard the work crews (Utah Division of State History, Spike 150). On November 29, 1864, soldiers under Colonel John Chivington killed about 150 Cheyenne and Arapaho people at Sand Creek, most of them women and children. Cheyenne, Arapaho and Lakota fighters struck back along the route. On August 27, 1867, Cheyenne men pulled up track at Plum Creek, Nebraska, wrecked a work train and killed its crew. The wars are `native-nations`' story (PBS American Experience timeline).
- June 25, 1867: several thousand Chinese workers (estimates run 2,000–5,000) struck for eight days for equal pay and shorter days; the company cut off food to the mountain camps and the strike ended without formal concessions — the largest labor action in the country to that date. The strike as a labor fight is `work-workers`'; the line is ours (Library of Congress; PBS).
- April 28, 1869: crews laid 10 miles and 56 feet of track in one day — still the record for hand-laid track. Three of the eight men who carried the last rails — Ging Cui, Wong Fook, Lee Shao — rode in the 50th-anniversary parade in 1919 (Chinese Railroad Workers Project; CPRR Museum).
- Shared with: `migration` (settlers carried) · `economy` (prices) · `big-business` (land grants) · `immigration` (the workers' arrival) · `native-nations` (whose land the rails crossed — the bison collapse telling is `land-environment`'s).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="hung-wah" name="Hung Wah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hung Wah
> **Who:** The leading Chinese headman on the Central Pacific — hiring, paying, and speaking for hundreds, at times thousands, of workers. · **When and where:** Sierra Nevada and Utah, 1864–1869.
He appears in the railroad's January 1864 payroll supervising its first small Chinese crew, and rose to manage the largest Chinese workforce on the line. Individual laborers' names are mostly lost — payrolls often listed only headmen — so his name has to stand for thousands, and the book should say exactly that (Stanford Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project). Topic documentary: *Transcontinental Railroad* (PBS American Experience, 2003) — about the railroad, not him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hung-wah" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" name="Cornelius Vanderbilt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cornelius Vanderbilt
> **Who:** Ferry hand who built first a steamboat empire, then a railroad one. · **When and where:** New York, 1810s–1877.
Moved his money from steamships into railroads in the 1860s, took control of the New York Central in 1867, merged his lines in 1869, and opened Grand Central Depot in Manhattan in 1871. Left about $100 million at his death in 1877 — the largest American fortune of its day (Britannica). The corporate-power side of railroads — land grants, rate fights, regulation — is `big-business`'s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="mark-twain-transportation" name="Mark Twain, steamboat pilot" movie="Mark Twain (2001)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mark Twain, steamboat pilot
> **Who:** Samuel Clemens, licensed Mississippi steamboat pilot before he was a writer. · **When and where:** the lower Mississippi; license April 9, 1859. · **Movie:** Mark Twain (2001) — Ken Burns's PBS documentary about his whole life.
Licensed at 23, he piloted until the Civil War closed the river in 1861. His pen name is a riverman's depth call — "mark twain," two fathoms, just enough water to pass. *Life on the Mississippi* (1883) is the pilot's-eye record of the steamboat age. His books belong to `art`; the wheelhouse is ours (twainquotes.com steamboat records; History.com).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mark-twain-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what the railroad did to the clock, the night, and the body" -->
- November 18, 1883 — the "day of two noons": the railroads split the continent into standard time zones and reset every station clock. The country's clocks followed the trains; Congress did not make it law until 1918 (Library of Congress).
- George Pullman's Pioneer sleeping car (1865) made long trips livable for those who could pay: folding berths, carpets, porters. The porters' story is told next era; their union fight is `work-workers`' (Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine).
- In 1889, about 8.5 of every 1,000 American trainmen were killed on the job in a single year, twice the British rate (historian Mark Aldrich, from Interstate Commerce Commission counts).
- Railroads also set where Black passengers could sit. In 1884, a train crew in Tennessee dragged Ida B. Wells out of the first-class car she had paid for. In 1890, Louisiana legislators passed a law requiring separate railroad cars by race. Homer Plessy sat in a white car on the East Louisiana Railroad on June 7, 1892, to test it. On May 18, 1896, seven Supreme Court justices ruled against him in *Plessy v. Ferguson*. The court fights are `rights-movements`' story (National Park Service, National Archives).
- The deadliest job in America: brakemen coupling cars by hand with link-and-pin couplers, losing fingers, hands, and lives. The Janney automatic coupler (patented 1873) and the air brake existed for decades before the 1893 Safety Appliance Act forced them onto all trains — coupling accidents fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902 (ASME; Railroad Safety Appliance Act records). Shared with: `work-workers` (the job itself).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the electric streetcar and the bicycle craze" -->
- February 2, 1888: Frank Sprague's Richmond Union Passenger Railway begins regular electric streetcar service — the first successful large-scale system; within about two years some 110 electric railways were built or under contract on his equipment. What streetcars did to city shape is `city-building`'s (IEEE Milestone; National Inventors Hall of Fame).
- The safety bicycle — two equal wheels, chain drive, air tires — sets off the 1890s craze. Susan B. Anthony told Nellie Bly in the New York World, February 2, 1896, that bicycling had "done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world" — a documented interview, quoted exactly (New York World, 1896).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-sprague" name="Frank Sprague" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frank Sprague
> **Who:** Engineer whose Richmond streetcar system electrified city transit. · **When and where:** Richmond, Virginia, regular service from February 2, 1888.
Ten cars at the start, about 12 miles of track and 23 cars by May; the hills of Richmond were the proof that electric traction worked. Horsecars began disappearing from American cities within a decade (IEEE Milestone "Richmond Union Passenger Railway, 1888").
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-sprague" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The car takes over the ground and the airplane goes from a stunt to a schedule, while the railroad reaches its peak and starts to slip. In 1900 almost nobody owned a car; by the late 1920s there were about 15 million Model Ts alone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Model T and what driving did" -->
- Introduced October 1, 1908, at $825; assembly-line production cut the price to $260 by 1925; the 15 millionth rolled out May 26, 1927 (Ford; Britannica). The assembly line itself is `technology`'s story (their slug: henry-ford-technology); ours is what driving did to the country.
- What it did: the Lincoln Highway (1913), the first named coast-to-coast auto route, organized by private boosters; then the numbered U.S. route system (1926) — including Route 66, designated November 11, 1926, Chicago to Santa Monica, 2,448 miles (FHWA). The Dust Bowl families who drove it west are `migration`'s story.
- Trucks, buses, and diesel locomotives spread through the 1930s–40s; paved-road mileage multiplies.
- Motor vehicle crashes killed about 4,100 to 4,200 people in 1913. In 1937 they killed about 37,800 to 39,600, about 30 of every 100,000 Americans, the highest rate on record. The two counts differ because the National Safety Council and the federal tables count crashes differently.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-transportation" name="The Wright brothers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers
> **Who:** Their four flights on December 17, 1903, opened flight as a way to travel. · **When and where:** Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina.
The machine and its invention belong to `technology` (their slug: wright-brothers-technology, with the film). This chapter's line is what happened to travel afterward: within eleven years of Kitty Hawk, an airplane was carrying paying passengers on a schedule.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="flight becomes transport" -->
- January 1, 1914: the St. Petersburg–Tampa Airboat Line opens the world's first scheduled airline — pilot Tony Jannus, a Benoist flying boat, 23 minutes across Tampa Bay. The first ticket went at auction for $400 to former St. Petersburg mayor Abram Pheil (IATA airline-history pages; Tampa Bay History journal, USF).
- May 15, 1918: the first regular airmail route, Washington–Philadelphia–New York, flown at first by Army pilots (National Postal Museum). A 1925 law then moved the mail to private companies, and today's airlines grew out of those mail contracts.
- May 1927: Lindbergh flies the Atlantic alone — the flight itself is `exploration`'s story (their slug: charles-lindbergh). What it did to transport is ours: U.S. airline passengers jumped from 5,782 in 1926 to about 173,000 in 1928 (FAA historical chronology; Cradle of Aviation).
- The Douglas DC-3 (1936) was, per the Smithsonian, the first airliner that could make money carrying passengers alone — flying stopped depending on mail contracts (National Air and Space Museum).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-knight" name="Jack Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Knight
> **Who:** Airmail pilot whose all-night flight through a snowstorm saved the airmail service. · **When and where:** North Platte, Nebraska, to Chicago, night of February 22–23, 1921.
The Post Office staged a day-and-night transcontinental relay to prove airmail could beat the trains. When the next pilot refused to fly into the storm, Knight — who had never flown the Omaha–Chicago leg — went on through snow and fog by compass and bonfires. The relay made it: 2,629 miles in 33 hours 20 minutes, and Congress funded the airmail (Smithsonian National Postal Museum).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-knight" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the railroad's peak and the war" -->
- Pullman porters — Black men working the sleeping cars — made the long-distance train a rolling hotel; the Pullman Company was for years the largest single employer of Black Americans. The union fight and A. Philip Randolph belong to `work-workers` (their slug: a-philip-randolph-work-workers).
- World War II moved on rails: about 43.7 million service members traveled by train, 1941–45 — over 97 percent of organized troop movement — with half the country's Pullman sleepers in war service (Association of American Railroads; American-Rails).
- The Jeep: about 640,000 built (Willys MB and Ford GPW together), the war's go-anywhere vehicle and the ancestor of the civilian SUV (Kaiser Willys archives; Jeep company history).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" name="The porters of Miles of Smiles" movie="Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle (1982)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The porters of Miles of Smiles
> **Who:** Retired Pullman porters who told their own working lives on camera — the men who made the sleeping car run. · **When and where:** Pullman cars nationwide, 1910s–1940s; filmed 1982. · **Movie:** Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle (1982) — the documentary IS the source; it is about them.
Eight retired porters and Rosina Tucker, a porter's wife and organizer, describe the job from inside: the smiles required, the tips lived on, the nights without sleep, being called "George" instead of their names. Our angle is the work of moving passengers; the union they built is `work-workers`' story (Folkstreams transcript; NMAAHC).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="irene-morgan" name="Irene Morgan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Irene Morgan
> **Who:** A Baltimore war worker who refused to give up her bus seat in Virginia and won in the Supreme Court. · **When and where:** A Greyhound bus near Saluda, Virginia, July 16, 1944. Decision June 3, 1946.
Morgan was 27 and built B-26 bombers in a wartime factory. She was riding a Greyhound bus home to Baltimore after visiting her mother. Near Saluda, two white passengers got on the crowded bus, and the driver told her to move to the back. Virginia law gave bus drivers the power of police officers to decide a rider's race and move or arrest the rider. Morgan refused to move. The driver drove to the jail. When the sheriff handed her a warrant, she tore it up and threw it out the window. She kicked him when he grabbed her, and the sheriff and a deputy pulled her off the bus. She paid a $100 fine for resisting arrest but refused to pay the $10 fine for breaking the seating law. NAACP lawyers Spottswood Robinson, Thurgood Marshall and William Hastie took her case. On June 3, 1946, the Supreme Court ruled that Virginia could not make passengers traveling between states sit apart by race. The Afro-American newspaper reported that bus company rules did not change right away (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Morgan v. Virginia").

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Three systems built in the same few years — the interstate, the jet, and the shipping container — make distance cheap. Passenger trains fade into a government rescue.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Interstate System" -->
- June 29, 1956: Eisenhower signs the Federal-Aid Highway Act — 41,000 planned miles, the largest public-works program in American history to that date; today about 48,900 miles (National Archives; FHWA). Congress added Eisenhower's name to the system in 1990.
- What it did is told by the neighbors: freeways through city neighborhoods (`city-building`), the Sun Belt shift (`migration`), the commute (`home-family`). Ours is the road: limited access, no stoplights, engineered grades — and the slow bypass-death of Route 66, removed from the U.S. route system in 1985 (FHWA).
- The new highways went through people's homes. The U.S. Department of Transportation estimates that federal road building forced more than 475,000 households, more than a million people, out of their homes. Most of the neighborhoods chosen were Black and poor. In Alabama, highway director Sam Engelhardt, who printed "I Stand for White Supremacy and Segregation" on his business cards, routed Interstate 85 through Montgomery's only middle-class Black neighborhood, where civil rights leader Ralph Abernathy lived. About 356 homes were torn down. Owners got an average of $3,300 a house. City-building tells what happened to the neighborhoods (History.com citing DOT, Kinder Institute at Rice University reporting Rebecca Retzlaff's 2019 study).
- Topic documentary: *Divided Highways* (PBS, 1997) — about the interstates, not any one person.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" name="Dwight Eisenhower" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Dwight Eisenhower
> **Who:** The president behind the Interstate System — convinced by a miserable 1919 road trip. · **When and where:** the 1919 convoy coast to coast; the law June 29, 1956.
As a young officer he volunteered for the Army's first transcontinental motor convoy: 81 vehicles, 3,251 miles, 62 days of mud, breakdowns, and failed bridges. Seeing Germany's autobahns in 1945 finished the argument for him. His war is `war`'s story; the highway law is ours (Eisenhower Presidential Library; U.S. Army).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the jet age" -->
- October 26, 1958: Pan Am flies the Boeing 707 New York to Paris — 111 passengers, about eight hours. Jets roughly double the speed and capacity of the piston airliners they replace (EBSCO; Smithsonian Air & Space).
- October 24, 1978: the Airline Deregulation Act ends government control of routes and fares. Fares fell — a Brookings study puts them about 22 percent lower than they would have been under regulation, and real average fares dropped roughly 40 percent over the following decades; flying became a bus, not a luxury (Brookings/Winston & Morrison; GAO).
- On June 30, 1956, a TWA airliner and a United airliner collided over the Grand Canyon. All 128 people on the two planes died. It was the deadliest air crash in the world up to then. Congress created the Federal Aviation Agency in 1958 to control the crowded skies (FAA).
- In May 1961, Black and white Freedom Riders rode buses into Alabama to test a court ruling against segregated bus stations. On May 14, a white mob at Anniston firebombed their Greyhound bus and beat the riders as they escaped. The same day in Birmingham, Klansmen beat riders at the bus station. Police commissioner Bull Connor had promised the Klan 15 minutes before police would come. On November 1, 1961, a federal rule banned separate waiting rooms and other facilities by race in bus and train stations. The movement is `rights-movements`' story (Encyclopedia of Alabama).
- Car crashes killed 54,589 people in 1972, the most in any year before or since.
- May 1, 1971: Amtrak takes over most intercity passenger trains after private railroads, losing money to jets and interstates for two decades, give them up (Rail Passenger Service Act of 1970; Eno Center).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-dusty-roads" name="Barbara 'Dusty' Roads" movie="Fly With Me (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara "Dusty" Roads
> **Who:** American Airlines stewardess through the piston-to-jet change who fought the rule firing women at 32. · **When and where:** flying from 1950; the fight through the 1960s; died 2023. · **Movie:** Fly With Me (2024) — PBS American Experience documentary about flight attendants; she is featured in it.
Her working life spans the jet age from inside the cabin. Airlines fired stewardesses for turning 32 or marrying; Roads lobbied Congress on her own Washington flights and dared reporters to pick the 32-year-olds out of a lineup. The discrimination-law fight belongs with `rights-movements` and `work-workers`; the cabin is ours (Wikipedia/obituaries; PBS American Experience).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-dusty-roads" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the box that remade trade" -->
- April 26, 1956: the Ideal X, a converted tanker, sails Newark to Houston with 58 truck-trailer-sized metal boxes — the first container ship. Loading cargo by hand cost about $5.86 a ton; by container, about 16 cents (Port Houston; Levinson's research, widely cited).
- The container empties the old finger piers, builds giant new ports, and quietly fills every store with goods from anywhere. The trade economics are `economy`'s; the box and the ships are ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="malcom-mclean" name="Malcom McLean" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Malcom McLean
> **Who:** North Carolina trucker who invented container shipping by asking why cargo was loaded twice. · **When and where:** Port Newark, April 26, 1956.
He started with one used truck in 1934, built a trucking company, then sold it to bet everything on putting whole truck bodies on ships. The standardized box remade world trade within a generation (Maritime Executive; American Business History Center).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="malcom-mclean" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the driveway changes" -->
The minivan (first Dodge Caravan built November 2, 1983) replaces the station wagon; SUVs grow through the 1990s from the Jeep's descendants. Two-car households become normal (Stellantis/Chrysler archives).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The vehicle changes faster than the road: satellite navigation, phone-hailed rides, electric power, and rented scooters arrive on a network designed in 1956. Current through 2026; revisit yearly.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the screen takes the wheel" -->
- May 2000: the government switches off "selective availability," making civilian GPS roughly ten times more accurate overnight — about 100 meters to about 20 (gps.gov). Within a decade turn-by-turn navigation is in every phone; paper road maps become souvenirs.
- Ride-hailing (journalism, labeled): Uber's app service began in San Francisco in July 2010 (founded 2009 as UberCab); Lyft followed in June 2012. Millions now drive their own cars for fares set by an app — the gig-work fight is `work-workers`' (Britannica; company records; press coverage).
- The companies paid drivers as independent contractors, so drivers paid for their own cars, gas and tolls. In November 2020, California voters approved Proposition 22, 58.6 percent to 41.4 percent. It kept app drivers as contractors and set a pay floor of 120 percent of minimum wage for time spent on a trip, plus 30 cents a mile. Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart and Postmates put $205.7 million into the Yes campaign, the most ever spent on a California ballot measure to that date. The justices of the California Supreme Court upheld the law on July 25, 2024. In June 2024, Uber and Lyft settled a lawsuit brought by Massachusetts attorneys general. They paid $175 million and agreed to pay drivers there at least $32.50 an hour while driving to a pickup or carrying a rider (Ballotpedia, certified results and finance filings, and WBUR, June 27, 2024).
- SUVs and pickups finish taking over: light trucks outsold cars for the first time in 2001 and kept widening the gap (industry sales data via trade press — journalism).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="electric, again" -->
- Electric cars return a century after they lost to gasoline. Tesla delivered its first Model S sedans June 22, 2012, in Fremont, California (company records; press coverage — journalism). State it plainly: the company is named for inventor Nikola Tesla (died 1943), who had no connection to it. The battery chemistry is `technology`'s story; the grid is `energy`'s.
- Americans bought about 1.3 million electric cars in both 2024 and 2025, about 8 of every 100 new cars. After federal tax credits for electric cars ended in October 2025, sales in the last three months of the year fell 46 percent from the three months before (Kelley Blue Book, January 2026).
- E-bikes spread; rentable electric scooters appear when Bird scatters them across Santa Monica in September 2017, and cities scramble to regulate (journalism: local and tech press).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the strained network" -->
- The 2021 supply-chain jam made the invisible system visible: by January 9, 2022, a record 109 container ships waited off Los Angeles/Long Beach (Marine Exchange of Southern California, via press — journalism). Store shelves showed what the box normally does.
- High-speed rail, honestly: California voters approved $9.95 billion in bonds in 2008 for a San Francisco–Anaheim system then estimated at about $33.6 billion. Ground broke in Fresno in January 2015; work today targets a 171-mile Merced–Bakersfield first segment; full-system estimates have passed $100 billion, and no passengers have ridden yet (CA High-Speed Rail Authority plans; Eno Center; press — journalism). The country that built the transcontinental in six years has not yet built one true high-speed line.
- In July 2025, the U.S. Transportation Department cancelled about $4 billion in federal grants for California's line. State officials sued, then dropped the suit in December 2025. California lawmakers set aside $1 billion a year for the project through 2045. Cost estimates were above $100 billion, and no passenger had ridden it as of 2026 (Associated Press, December 27, 2025).
- In 2025, federal inspectors rated 41,677 of the nation's 624,167 bridges in poor condition, about 7 in 100. Drivers crossed those poor bridges 163 million times a day (Federal Highway Administration bridge data, as counted by the road builders' trade group ARTBA, 2025).
- Traffic deaths rose to 43,230 in 2021, then fell four years in a row. Federal officials estimate that about 36,640 people died in crashes in 2025, the lowest death rate per mile since 2014 (NHTSA early estimate, April 2026).
- On March 26, 2024, the container ship Dali lost power and hit the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore. The bridge fell into the Patapsco River. Six men filling potholes on the bridge died: Dorlian Ronial Castillo Cabrera, Carlos Daniel Hernandez Estrella, Alejandro Hernandez Fuentes, Miguel Angel Luna Gonzalez, Jose Mynor Lopez and Maynor Yassir Suazo Sandoval. Federal investigators traced the power loss to a loose wire. In May 2026, federal prosecutors charged the ship's operating companies and one of their managers with causing the six deaths (NTSB, Maryland Matters).
- On January 29, 2025, an Army Black Hawk helicopter flew into an American Eagle passenger jet landing at Reagan National Airport near Washington. All 67 people on the two aircraft died. In January 2026, the head of the National Transportation Safety Board, Jennifer Homendy, called the crash "100% preventable." Her board blamed FAA officials for helicopter routes that ran close to the runway and for ignoring years of near misses (CNN).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-berwick" name="Barbara Ann Berwick" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara Ann Berwick
> **Who:** San Francisco Uber driver who asked the state of California whether she was Uber's employee, and won. · **When and where:** San Francisco, July to September 2014. Decision June 3, 2015.
She drove for Uber in her own car for about eight weeks in 2014 and quit on September 18. Uber called her an independent contractor, so it paid nothing toward her car costs. She took her claim to the state Labor Commissioner and argued it herself at a hearing on March 10, 2015. Uber sent two lawyers and a product manager. The hearing officer, Stephanie Barrett, listed the rules Uber set for drivers. The car could be no more than 10 years old. Drivers had to keep a rating of 4.6 stars out of 5, or Uber shut off their app. On June 3, 2015, Barrett ruled that Berwick was an employee. She ordered Uber to pay $4,152.20: 56 cents a mile for 6,468 miles, $256 in bridge tolls, and interest. Her claim for unpaid wages was dismissed, because she agreed Uber had paid her. Uber appealed and said the ruling applied only to her. Uber kept treating its other drivers as contractors. Shares with `work-workers` (gig work as a job) (Labor Commissioner decision, Case No. 11-46739 EK, June 3, 2015, with TIME and TechCrunch, June 17, 2015).
