## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were planned cities on this land centuries before any European saw it. The biggest sat by the Mississippi River, and in the Southwest people built stone towns, some of which are still lived in today. Nobody's name survives from these places — the builders are known by what they built.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia — a planned city" -->
Cahokia (near present-day St. Louis), built up fast from about 1050, held roughly 10,000–20,000 people at its peak around 1100 — about the size of London then. It was laid out on purpose: Monks Mound, the largest prehistoric earthen structure in the Americas, raised in stages to about 100 feet with a base of roughly 14 acres; below it the Grand Plaza, about 50 acres deliberately leveled flat for gatherings and games; five circles of red cedar posts ("Woodhenge") lined up with the sunrise on the solstices and equinoxes — a calendar built into the city plan; a two-mile log wall around the center, needing 15,000–20,000 logs, rebuilt three times. The city dispersed by about 1350. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — the nation and why it rose and emptied; ours is the urban form: plazas, mounds, the plan).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone towns of the Southwest" -->
- Chaco Canyon (New Mexico): the great-house era ran about 900–1180 — "great houses" of shaped stone, Pueblo Bonito alone with over 650 rooms — connected by engineered roads averaging about 30 feet wide and running straight for miles.
- Mesa Verde (Colorado): Cliff Palace, the largest cliff dwelling, 150 rooms and 23 kivas, built 1190–1280, home to about 100 people.
- Two dates, two different things — always say which is which: people **began leaving** Chaco in the 1140s, and the great-house era ran to about 1180. Mesa Verde was left by the 1280s. Then people regathered into the Pueblo towns along the Rio Grande — town-living never stopped in the Southwest.
- No individual builder's name survives at story level; the book does not invent people. The era carries the wide view and the spans.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
European town-building on this land begins with one town on the Florida coast — and, eight years later, with a written set of royal orders for laying out all the others. Thin era, honestly: one lasting town from this century, and no documented town-builder's life at story level.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine and the Laws of the Indies" -->
Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine in September 1565 — the oldest continuously occupied European-founded city in the continental United States. In 1573 King Philip II of Spain issued the Laws of the Indies: written rules for laying out new towns — a central plaza set to the compass points, its length one and a half times its width, a street grid growing out from it, lots assigned for church and government. St. Augustine's Plaza de la Constitución was laid out under those 1573 ordinances and is the oldest public square in the country. Spanish town-builders across what is now the Southwest kept laying towns out to that plaza-and-grid pattern. (Two specifics cut 2026-09-07 as unsourced: "a printed rulebook" — the 1573 document was royal ordinances, and the printed *Recopilación* came only in 1681 — and "for the next 250 years", a span no source in the bank carries. Do not restore either without a citation; see the research bank, era 02.)

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first English and Dutch towns were small, wooden, and improvised — a fort, a wall, a huddle of houses by the water. Some grew streets with no plan at all; one, at the century's end, got the most influential city plan in American history.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort towns, walls, and watchmen" -->
- Jamestown began in 1607 as a three-sided fort on the James River; in the 1620s surveyor William Claiborne laid out "New Towne" east of the fort — row houses replacing a stockade.
- Santa Fe, founded about 1610 by Governor Pedro de Peralta, was planned around a central plaza; its Palace of the Governors is the oldest continuously occupied public building in the United States.
- Boston (1630) grew with no plan — houses went up near the waterfront and streets formed around hills, brooks, and marshes. (The story that its crooked streets were cow paths is a legend, traced to a line Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in 1860.) Boston's leaders set a night watch to patrol after dark starting in 1631.
- Director General Petrus Stuyvesant and the court of New Amsterdam ordered a wall across the top of the town on March 13, 1653, against an English attack. Africans held as property by the Dutch West India Company dug the ditch from the East River to the North River and stood the plank wall in it, working alongside soldiers, Company servants and townsmen the same order conscripted. Some of those Africans had "half-freedom", granted February 25, 1644: they could hold land, but their children stayed the Company's property and the Company could call them to work whenever it wanted. The street along the wall became Wall Street. New Amsterdam's "rattle watch" of August 12, 1658 — paid patrolmen with wooden noisemakers to raise alarms — was among the first paid watches in America. (Rewritten 2026-09-07: this line read "New Amsterdam built a wooden wall", which personified a town and left the enslaved workers out altogether.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Penn's plan" -->
In 1682 William Penn had his surveyor Thomas Holme lay out Philadelphia in advance of settlement — the grid arrives as an idea, not an accident. Shared with: `religion` (Penn's Quaker colony) · `government-politics` (Penn's frame of government).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-penn" name="William Penn" movie="Penn of Pennsylvania (1941)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Penn
> **Who:** Quaker founder of Pennsylvania who planned Philadelphia on paper before it existed on the ground. · **When and where:** the plan laid out December 1682 by his surveyor-general Thomas Holme; Holme's map published 1683. · **Movie:** *Penn of Pennsylvania* (1941; released in the U.S. as *The Courageous Mr. Penn*, 1942).
Penn wanted "a greene Country Towne which will never be burnt, and always be wholesome" — he had lived through London's 1666 fire and 1665 plague. Holme drew a straight grid between two rivers: two wide main streets (Broad and High, now Market), a big central square, and four smaller public squares, one per quarter — all five still there today (Logan, Franklin, Washington, Rittenhouse, and City Hall on the center square). It was the pattern American towns copied for two centuries: when in doubt, draw a grid.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonial ports grew into real little cities — and hit real city problems: fire first. Around 1750 Philadelphia held about 13,000 people, Boston about 12,000, New York about 11,000, and Charleston about 8,000 — big for the colonies, small next to London's hundreds of thousands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire — the city killer" -->
Wooden towns burned street by street, and no government would fight the fires for you. Boston organized "mutual fire societies" whose members protected each other's houses only. The step forward came in Philadelphia: a volunteer company that would fight anyone's fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-fire-company-city-building" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** printer and civic organizer; co-founder of the colonies' first volunteer fire company that served the whole town. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, December 7, 1736.
After arguing in his *Pennsylvania Gazette* that the city needed organized firefighting, Franklin and about 25 others signed the Union Fire Company's articles: each member kept six leather buckets and two strong linen bags (for hauling water and rescuing goods) and answered every alarm — anybody's house, not just members'. Other companies copied it, and volunteer fire companies became a fixture of American town life for the next century. (His lightning rod and other inventions are `technology`'s story; the almanac and press are `news-communication`'s.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-fire-company-city-building" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The men running a brand-new country chose ground for a capital and had a whole city drawn for it before it was built — but the ground was not empty. George Washington picked it; Andrew Ellicott's crew measured it; Pierre L'Enfant drew the city. And after yellow fever killed about a tenth of Philadelphia, the men governing that city decided to build the country's first steam-driven public waterworks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A capital on land that was already somebody's" -->
The Residence Act of July 16, 1790 set the capital on the Potomac in an area "not exceeding ten miles square" and let President Washington fix the exact spot; he chose the ground at and next to Georgetown and told Congress on January 24, 1791. What stood inside that square: three Native towns had been there, the largest called Nacotchtank, whose people had been reduced to about a quarter of their number within forty years of English contact in 1608; Alexandria, Virginia, a tobacco port since 1749; Georgetown, Maryland, a tobacco port since 1751 — Banneker's own survey camp at Jones Point sits in Alexandria; and, on the ground the city itself took, tobacco farms worked by enslaved people. The five men who owned the plots that became the Mall — Daniel Carroll of Duddington, David Burnes, Notley Young, Benjamin Oden, Samuel Davidson — were paid for the land and became "the Original Proprietors". The 1790 census counted 245 people enslaved on Notley Young's 800-acre tobacco plantation alone (another account says 260). Then the plan: from a French-born engineer, with the boundary survey run by Andrew Ellicott and its astronomy done by Benjamin Banneker, a free Black farmer. (Rewritten 2026-09-07: this line read "it designed its capital city from nothing, on an empty riverbank" — personification plus the "empty land" erasure `control/hard-subjects-policy.md` forbids. Full sourcing: research bank, era 05, "The land the capital was built on".) Shared with: `government-politics` (why the capital moved) · `landmarks` (the Capitol and White House as buildings) · `slavery-freedom` (who was held on that ground).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-lenfant" name="Pierre L'Enfant" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pierre L'Enfant
> **Who:** French-born engineer who designed the plan of Washington, D.C. — and was fired before it was built. · **When and where:** the federal district, 1791–February 1792.
His 1791 plan crossed a street grid with grand diagonal avenues meeting at circles and squares — the reason Washington looks like no other American city. But L'Enfant refused to hand over his drawings for engraving and ignored the commissioners over him, and in February 1792 Washington dismissed him. Surveyor Andrew Ellicott redrew the plan from his own knowledge of it (straightening one avenue, trimming some squares), and that printed version built the city. L'Enfant died poor in 1825; in 1909 his body was moved to Arlington, overlooking the city built roughly to his design. Both truths stay in the telling: the vision was his, and he was genuinely impossible to work with.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pierre-lenfant" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-banneker" name="Benjamin Banneker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Banneker
> **Who:** free Black farmer, self-taught astronomer and almanac author who worked on the federal district's boundary survey. · **When and where:** the survey's base camp at Jones Point, Virginia, February–April 1791.
Hired as scientific assistant to surveyor Andrew Ellicott, Banneker made the astronomical observations and calculations that fixed the district's starting corner, maintaining the regulator clock and working the nights at the observation tent. He left the survey in spring 1791 (he was nearly 60 and in poor health) and published his first almanac the next year. Told honestly: a popular story says Banneker redrew L'Enfant's whole city plan from memory after the firing — the timeline doesn't support it (Banneker had left months earlier; Ellicott reconstructed the plan), and the National Park Service and historians credit Banneker with the boundary astronomy, which was achievement enough. Shared with: `science` (his astronomy and almanacs) · `slavery-freedom` (his letter to Jefferson, 1791).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-banneker" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Water after the fevers" -->
Yellow fever killed about 5,000 people in Philadelphia between August 1 and November 9, 1793 — roughly one person in ten in a city of about 50,000 — and about 20,000 more fled. It came back in 1798 (no Philadelphia death toll found for that year: give no number). Say what it did to a person: fever, then in the severe stage the liver fails and the skin and eyes turn yellow, bleeding starts from mouth, nose, eyes and stomach, and blood mixed with stomach acid comes up black. Then the men governing the city decided to build the country's first steam-powered public waterworks. Construction began 1799 on Benjamin Latrobe's Centre Square engine house, and on January 21, 1801 its steam pumps began pushing Schuylkill River water through the streets — the first American city to do it. Shared with: `health` (the epidemics themselves) · `technology` (the steam engines).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Canals and rivers made boomtowns — places that went from crossroads to city inside a decade — and the crowding brought cholera and fire, which forced the first great city water projects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boomtowns" -->
- Rochester, New York — often called America's first boomtown — grew from about 1,500 people (1820) to about 9,000 by 1830 (some counts run to 12,600 by the mid-1830s) after the Erie Canal opened through it in 1825; by 1838 it milled more flour than any city in the country. Shared with: `transportation` (the canal itself) · `economy` (the flour trade).
- Chicago incorporated as a town in 1833 with about 350 people, and as a city in 1837 with 4,170 — built on low, wet ground where a short river met Lake Michigan. What that mud cost comes in the next era.
- San Francisco went from about 200 people (1846) to about 36,000 (1852) in the Gold Rush — a city improvised out of tents, ships' timbers, and repeated fires. Shared with: `migration` (lead — the rush itself) · `economy` (the gold).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1811 grid" -->
New York's Commissioners' Plan of 1811 ruled lines across all of Manhattan above the existing town: 12 numbered avenues, 155 numbered cross streets, rectangles all the way up — surveyed by John Randel Jr., who started the job at age 20. It took about 60 years of digging and leveling to build reality up to the map. The plan skimped on parks (the commissioners thought the rivers gave air enough) — the fix for that comes in the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cholera, fire, and the Croton" -->
New York learned the price of dirty water and no water: cholera killed about 3,500 New Yorkers in 1832 (of roughly 250,000), and the Great Fire of December 1835 burned about 17 blocks and 600-plus buildings while firefighters watched wells and cisterns run dry. The answer was the Croton Aqueduct — 41 miles of stone tunnel carrying a dammed river to the city by gravity. Water arrived June 22, 1842; on October 14, 1842 the city threw a seven-mile parade while the new City Hall Park fountain jetted 50 feet. Shared with: `health` (lead — cholera as disease) · `disasters` (lead — the 1835 fire).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-templeton-strong" name="George Templeton Strong" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Templeton Strong
> **Who:** a 22-year-old New York lawyer whose private diary — 2,250 pages kept over 40 years — records what piped water actually meant to one household. · **When and where:** New York City, 1842.
As the aqueduct neared his neighborhood he wrote, "Croton Water is slowly flowing towards the city," adding that New York might finally "stand a chance of being cleaned." When it reached his house he could do a thing almost no American city dweller had ever done: turn a tap and bathe at home, daily. Before Croton, water meant wells, street pumps, peddlers' carts, and rain barrels. One diary, one bathtub — the whole revolution of city water in miniature.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-templeton-strong" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="city-building" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The industrial city arrived — and it grew in three directions at once: up (elevators and steel frames), down (sewers and water tunnels), and out (streetcars). It also packed poor families into tenements tighter than Americans had ever lived, and Chicago passed a million people in 1890, second only to New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chicago lifts itself out of the mud" -->
Chicago sat too low to drain, so after engineer Ellis Chesbrough's 1856 plan the city laid its sewers on top of the streets, buried them under new fill — and jacked the buildings up to match. In January 1858 a four-story brick block was raised 6 feet 2 inches on 200 jackscrews without cracking. In 1860, contractors including George Pullman lifted half a city block on Lake Street — about 35,000 tons of buildings, 320 feet long — 4 feet 8 inches in five days, using 600 men at 6,000 jackscrews, while shoppers and clerks went on working inside. A whole downtown, raised by hand.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Up: the elevator and the steel frame" -->
- Elisha Otis showed his safety elevator at New York's Crystal Palace exhibition in 1854 — hoisted up on a platform, he had the rope cut with an axe, and the catch held. The first passenger elevator went into the Haughwout store at 488 Broadway on March 23, 1857. Buildings could now charge the most rent for the top floors instead of the bottom. (The machine is `technology`'s story; what it did to building height is ours.)
- Chicago's Home Insurance Building (1885, William Le Baron Jenney) — ten stories, 138 feet, metal frame carrying the walls' weight — is usually called the first skyscraper. Told honestly: historians dispute it. It wasn't all steel, it wasn't the first with a metal frame, and the "first skyscraper" title owes a lot to a later publicity campaign by Chicago boosters. What is certain: in 1880s Chicago and New York, frame construction plus the elevator let buildings triple in height within a generation. The 1893 World's Columbian Exposition's "White City" then showed millions of visitors what a planned, matched, electric-lit cityscape could look like — plaster, but persuasive.
- Native continuity, on the steel itself: starting in 1886 at a Canadian bridge job, Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake and Akwesasne began "booming out" to high-steel work, and Mohawk crews helped raise most of New York's famous towers over the next century (Smithsonian NMAI, "Booming Out"). More in the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement" -->
New York's answer to millions of arrivals was the tenement: a 25-by-100-foot lot, four to six floors, four families to a floor. An 1879 law required a window in every room, which produced the "dumbbell" tenement — pinch-waisted buildings around an air shaft so narrow it worked mainly as a garbage chute and a fire flue. The 1901 law that finally forced real light, air, and indoor toilets lands in the next era. Shared with: `home-family` (lead on life inside the rooms; the building and the housing laws are ours) · `immigration` (who the families were).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jacob-riis" name="Jacob Riis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jacob Riis
> **Who:** Danish immigrant, police reporter, and the first American to use flash photography to show how the city's poor lived. · **When and where:** New York's tenement districts; *How the Other Half Lives* published 1890.
Riis (born 1849 in Ribe, Denmark; arrived 1870 nearly penniless) walked the night beat for years, then carried the brand-new magnesium flash into airless rooms and cellar lodgings — pictures of what no reader could pretend not to know. His 1890 book pushed New York into its first serious tenement reforms and made photography a tool for changing cities. Theodore Roosevelt, then police commissioner, became his ally. (The reform politics are `rights-movements`' and `government-politics`' stories; the photographs as city evidence are ours.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-riis" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bridget-joseph-moore" name="Bridget and Joseph Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bridget and Joseph Moore
> **Who:** an Irish immigrant waiter and his wife — one documented tenement family among thousands. · **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, Lower East Side, New York, 1869.
The Moores moved into 97 Orchard Street in 1869 with their daughters — one Irish family in a mostly German building that would house about 7,000 people from over 20 countries in its 72 years. That April their five-month-old daughter Agnes Mary died of marasmus — starvation-level malnutrition, in an era when city "swill milk" from distillery-fed cows sickened infants and the building's water was a backyard pump shared with the outhouses. Their rooms still exist: the Tenement Museum documented the family through church, census, and death records and rebuilt their apartment. Of the Moores' eight children, four lived to adulthood. Shared with: `home-family` (lead on the household's inside life — the museum's other families, like the Baldizzis, are parked for them) · `health` (infant mortality).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bridget-joseph-moore" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Central Park — and what stood there first" -->
New York bought the middle of Manhattan and built the first great landscaped public park, 1858 onward, to the "Greensward" design of Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux. Told honestly: the land was not empty. Seneca Village — founded 1825 when Andrew Williams, a 25-year-old Black shoeshiner, bought the first three lots for $125 — had grown to about 225 people (two-thirds Black, plus Irish and German families), with three churches and a school. The city took it by eminent domain and cleared it in 1857 with everything else on the park site. The park is real and so is what it displaced; both go in.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-law-olmsted" name="Frederick Law Olmsted" movie="Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Law Olmsted
> **Who:** the inventor of American landscape architecture — Central Park first, then park systems across the country. · **When and where:** New York from 1858; parks and campuses nationwide to the 1890s. · **Movie:** *Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America* (PBS, 2014).
A farmer and journalist who had never designed anything, Olmsted won the Central Park competition with Vaux in 1858 and ran the build. His idea — that a crowded industrial city owes every resident, rich or poor, a piece of made countryside — spread to Brooklyn's Prospect Park, Boston's "Emerald Necklace," and dozens of cities, and he did the site planning for the 1893 White City. Cities have been required to think about green space ever since.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-law-olmsted" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Out: streetcars stretch the city" -->
Frank Sprague's electric streetcar system in Richmond, Virginia — the first big one to work, running February 1888 with about 12 miles of track — meant a ten-minute ride covered what had been a thirty-minute walk. Within two years about 110 cities were building electric lines, and "streetcar suburbs" grew along the tracks: the city's first big stretch outward. (The vehicles are `transportation`'s; the new city shape is ours.) Cleaner streets came the same decade: New York's 1895 street-cleaning commissioner George Waring put 2,000 sweepers in white uniforms — the "White Wings" — on 450 miles of streets daily; Riis wrote that Waring's broom "saved more lives in the crowded tenements than a squad of doctors."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Instant cities, one more time" -->
At noon on April 22, 1889, the Oklahoma land run started on land the United States had taken from Native nations — NOT "empty prairie", which was the phrase here until 2026-09-07 and is the erasure `control/hard-subjects-policy.md` forbids; by nightfall Guthrie was a tent city of about 10,000 people, and by morning it had a provisional government, a land office, a newspaper, and three banks in tents. Shared with: `migration` (lead — the run itself) · `native-nations` (whose land was opened).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="city-building" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Cities swelled with immigrants and Great Migration arrivals, raced each other into the sky, and — for the first time — wrote laws about what could be built where and drew maps deciding who could borrow money where. And for the atomic bomb programme, the Army took farmland by eminent domain and built whole secret cities on it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The river turned around" -->
On January 2, 1900, Chicago finished reversing its own river. The 28-mile Sanitary and Ship Canal made the Chicago River flow backward, away from Lake Michigan — the city's drinking water — and toward the Mississippi. Workers blasted the last barrier at dawn on New Year's Day, partly to beat a threatened court injunction from downstream neighbors. One study estimates the reversal cut the city's overall death rate about 4 percent in 1900 alone. San Francisco solved its water another way: after the 1906 earthquake and fire exposed the city's weakness, Congress (Raker Act, December 19, 1913) let it dam Hetch Hetchy Valley inside Yosemite National Park — dam finished 1923, water flowing 1934. Shared with: `health` (sanitation and typhoid) · `land-environment` (lead on the Hetch Hetchy conservation fight) · `disasters` (the 1906 earthquake).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Zoning — the city writes rules for itself" -->
On July 25, 1916, New York adopted the country's first citywide zoning law, sorting the city into districts and forcing tall buildings to step back from the street as they rose — the "wedding cake" skyscraper shape comes straight from that formula. The 40-story Equitable Building (1915), rising sheer from the sidewalk and shadowing whole blocks, was the argument made visible. The Supreme Court upheld zoning in *Euclid v. Ambler* (1926), and nearly every American city adopted it. Zoning could protect light and air — and it could also quietly wall off neighborhoods; both uses date from the start. Shared with: `government-politics` (the law as law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-burnham" name="Daniel Burnham" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Burnham
> **Who:** Chicago architect who ran the 1893 White City and turned it into the "City Beautiful" planning movement. · **When and where:** Chicago; the *Plan of Chicago* published 1909.
Burnham's 1909 *Plan of Chicago* (with Edward Bennett) was the first comprehensive plan for an American big city: a lakefront kept open and public, ringed parks, widened streets — much of Chicago's open lakefront today traces to it. His plans also reshaped central Washington and other cities. Told honestly: the line he is famous for — "make no little plans" — was attributed to him after his death; no verbatim record of him saying it survives. The plans themselves are record enough.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="daniel-burnham" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The race to the sky" -->
New York, 1929–1931: the Chrysler Building's architect assembled a 125-foot spire in secret inside the building and jacked it through the roof in October 1929 to beat 40 Wall Street to 1,046 feet; the Empire State Building then passed them both — 102 stories, 1,250 feet, built in 410 days, opened May 1, 1931 into a Depression so deep it stood half-empty for years. Mohawk ironworker crews from Kahnawake and Akwesasne worked the steel on the era's towers, a trade passed down in those communities ever since. Shared with: `landmarks` (lead — the buildings as monuments; the race and the skyline as city form are ours) · `work-workers` (the building trades).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the Great Migration settled" -->
About 6 million Black Americans left the South between 1910 and 1970; `migration` tells the journey — the city they arrived into is ours. Chicago's Black population rose 148 percent and Detroit's 611 percent just in 1910–1920, but white property owners' "restrictive covenants" — contract clauses barring sale or rental to Black families — penned arrivals into narrow districts like Chicago's South Side "Black Belt" (renamed Bronzeville by its residents) and Harlem. Landlords chopped apartments into one-room "kitchenettes" — Bronzeville alone held over 80,000 of them — and charged more per square foot for crowding than white neighborhoods paid for space. Shared with: `migration` (lead — the journey) · `home-family` (life inside) · `rights-movements` (the fight against covenants).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The maps with red lines" -->
From 1935 to 1940 a federal agency — the Home Owners' Loan Corporation — graded neighborhoods in 239 cities for mortgage "security": A green, B blue, C yellow, D red, "hazardous." Neighborhoods where Black families lived were almost always drawn red, whatever their houses looked like; the maps trained a generation of lenders not to lend there. The lines outlived the agency: about three-quarters of the redlined areas are low-to-moderate income today (NCRC; the University of Richmond's Mapping Inequality project has digitized the maps). The maps are ours; what they did to families is `home-family`'s; the injustice and the fight are `rights-movements`'. New Deal building went on at the same time — WPA crews laid roughly 24,000 miles of sewer lines and 19,700 miles of water mains and built about 500 water-treatment plants and thousands of schools (figures vary by source).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Secret cities on taken farmland" -->
The ridgeland was not empty. About 1,000 families farmed it or lived in its crossroads communities — Wheat, Elza, Scarboro, Robertsville — and in October 1942 the Army Corps of Engineers filed a declaration of taking and took about 59,000 acres at an average of $46.86 an acre. Some families learned of it from notices nailed to their fence posts and had a few weeks to go; the clearance was done within a year. Accounts differ on how many people that was: the Tennessee Encyclopedia says roughly 4,000, others more than 3,000 — state the range. (Corrected 2026-09-07: this span read "Secret cities from nothing" and "on empty ridgeland" — the third "empty land" erasure found in this chapter. ⚠ The same false phrase is still in `manuscript/city-building/part3-1900s-and-today.md`.) On that ground, from 1942, workers for the Manhattan Project built Oak Ridge, Tennessee — planned for 13,000 people, holding about 75,000 by 1945, briefly Tennessee's fifth-largest city, with prefab "alphabet houses," schools, buses, a hospital, a symphony — behind fences and gates, on no public map. Los Alamos, New Mexico and Hanford/Richland, Washington were its siblings. Shared with: `war` (lead — the weapons program) · `elements` (what was made and found there).

### Colleen Black
> **Who:** a teenager from Nashville who moved with her family to a city that officially did not exist. · **When and where:** Oak Ridge, Tennessee, from 1944; she stayed the rest of her life (died 2015).
At 19, Colleen Rowan checked welded pipes for invisible leaks with a helium probe at the K-25 plant — miles of pipe, purpose never explained, questions not allowed. Her whole family had come for war work and lived in the government's instant housing among mud streets, boardwalks, and gate passes. Only after Hiroshima did she learn what the pipes were for. She married there, stayed, and became one of Oak Ridge's best-recorded voices — oral histories with the Atomic Heritage Foundation and a central figure in the book *The Girls of Atomic City* (2013).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The American city turned inside out. Money, people, and jobs moved to the suburban edge; highways and "urban renewal" went through what was left; and the fastest-growing cities were suddenly in the hot South and West. By century's end some old downtowns had lost more than half their people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Urban renewal" -->
The Housing Act of 1949 paid cities to condemn "blighted" neighborhoods, level them, and hand the land to new development. Between 1949 and 1974 the program demolished about 2,000 neighborhoods and displaced at least 300,000 families — likely over a million people. Black Americans were about 13 percent of the population and at least 55 percent of the displaced; James Baldwin said in 1963 that urban renewal "means Negro removal." Shared with: `rights-movements` (the injustice and the organizing) · `government-politics` (the law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Highways through neighborhoods" -->
The Interstate Highway Act (June 29, 1956) funded 41,000 miles of freeways — and inside cities, engineers routinely routed them through Black and poor neighborhoods, where land was cheapest to take and resistance easiest to ignore. St. Paul, Minnesota's Rondo neighborhood — home to about 80 percent of the city's Black residents — lost roughly 600–700 homes and about 300 businesses to Interstate 94 between 1956 and 1968; one in eight Black St. Paul residents lost their home. Shared with: `transportation` (lead — the road system itself) · `migration` (where the displaced went).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="marvin-roger-anderson" name="Marvin Roger Anderson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Marvin Roger Anderson
> **Who:** a Rondo kid who watched the freeway take his neighborhood — and spent his retirement working to bridge it back together. · **When and where:** Rondo, St. Paul, Minnesota; the news came in 1958, when he was 8.
His father had built two-story apartment buildings in Rondo in 1948; when I-94 came through, buildings were jacked onto flatbed trucks and hauled away — four of his father's still stand on a university campus. Anderson grew up to be Minnesota's State Law Librarian, co-founded the Rondo Days festival (1983) so the neighborhood would not be forgotten, and now leads ReConnect Rondo, a plan to cap the freeway with a 21-acre land bridge carrying housing and businesses where Rondo's blocks used to be. His story is documented in oral-history projects and press interviews (Sahan Journal and others — journalism, labeled).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marvin-roger-anderson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sprawl and the emptying middle" -->
- The suburbs: Levittown, New York opened 1947 — 17,000-plus mass-produced houses at $7,990 by 1951. `home-family` leads on the house and who was allowed to buy it; ours is the pattern it set — single-use subdivisions, everything reached by car, repeated at the edge of every metro for fifty years. "Sprawl" got its name.
- The cities left behind: Detroit fell from 1.85 million people (1950) to 951,000 (2000); St. Louis fell from 857,000 to 348,000. Downtown stores followed the customers to the malls (`marketplace`'s story).
- White flight was the engine: as Black families moved into city neighborhoods, white families and their tax dollars moved out — pushed by blockbusting realtors, pulled by federally insured suburban mortgages that redlining denied to the neighborhoods being left. Shared with: `home-family` (the mortgage) · `rights-movements` (the discrimination) · `migration` (the moves).

### Jane Jacobs and Robert Moses
> **Who:** the writer who said cities are their streets and the builder who never stopped clearing them — the century's defining argument about what a city is for. · **When and where:** New York City, 1950s–1968. · **Movie:** *Citizen Jane: Battle for the City* (documentary, 2016).
Robert Moses, who held as many as a dozen unelected posts at once, built 13 bridges, two tunnels, 658 playgrounds, and the expressways that cut through boroughs — the Cross-Bronx displacing thousands of families. Jane Jacobs, a Greenwich Village writer with no planning degree, argued in *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) that dense, mixed, "eyes on the street" neighborhoods were the city working, not the city failing. Her side won three real fights: the road through Washington Square Park (killed 1958), the plan to bulldoze her West Village as a slum (beaten 1961–62), and Moses's ten-lane Lower Manhattan Expressway (dead by decade's end; Jacobs was arrested at a 1968 hearing, accused of inciting a riot). Robert Caro's *The Power Broker* (1974) fixed Moses's reputation as the cautionary tale. Planning schools have taught Jacobs ever since — and cities still do both things, build big and preserve blocks, arguing her argument.

### Carl Stokes
> **Who:** the first Black mayor of a major American city — elected to run a city in the middle of everything this era did to cities. · **When and where:** Cleveland, Ohio; elected November 7, 1967 (the same night Richard Hatcher won in smaller Gary, Indiana); served 1968–1971.
Grandson of a slave, raised by a single mother in Cleveland public housing, Stokes won city hall in a city hemorrhaging jobs and people. He campaigned on housing, pollution, and neglected neighborhoods; when the Cuyahoga River caught fire on June 22, 1969 — at least the thirteenth fire on that industrial river since 1868 — he held a riverbank press conference the next morning demanding state and federal action, helping push the laws that followed (`land-environment` leads the river-to-EPA story). His election proved Black political power in the very cities white flight was abandoning; dozens of Black mayors followed in the 1970s and 80s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-stokes" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sun Belt boom" -->
While the old industrial cities shrank, air conditioning, cheap land, and annexation built a new kind of city in the South and West: Phoenix grew from about 107,000 people (1950) to 1.32 million (2000); Houston from 596,000 to 1.95 million, the country's fourth-largest city by 1990 — cities of freeways and subdivisions, built car-first from the start. Shared with: `migration` (lead — who moved and why) · `energy`/`technology` (air conditioning).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Downtowns filled again in some cities and kept emptying in others — and then the pandemic scrambled the question entirely. The era's city arguments are about who can afford to stay, what to do with empty office towers, and how to build for heat and rising water. Current through August 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Downtowns refill — then empty — then change jobs" -->
The 2000s brought a real downtown revival — young workers and empty-nesters moving back to city centers. Then COVID-19 (2020) made remote work ordinary, and the offices never refilled: U.S. office vacancy hit about 20 percent in 2024, a 30-year high, with over 900 million square feet empty (Moody's, via press reports — journalism, labeled). Cities began converting towers to housing — a record 55,000-plus apartments were being carved out of office buildings in 2024, four times the 2021 figure, with Washington, D.C. leading (RentCafe/Time — journalism, labeled). The skyline is being re-plumbed for a second life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gentrification — the return of money, and who it pushes out" -->
The clearest documented case is Washington, D.C.: 71 percent Black in 1970 — "Chocolate City" — and below 50 percent by 2011 (Census), as rising rents and renovation moved long-time Black residents out of neighborhoods like Shaw and U Street. Gentrification's mechanics are city-form mechanics: the same blocks once redlined became the cheapest close-in housing, so reinvestment landed there first and hardest. Shared with: `home-family` (rents and ownership) · `rights-movements` (displacement as an equity fight) · `economy` (the money). Coverage is journalism and Census — labeled in the bank.

### Virginia Ali
> **Who:** co-founder of Ben's Chili Bowl, who has watched one American city block change for almost seventy years from behind the same counter. · **When and where:** U Street, Washington, D.C., August 22, 1958 to today.
She and her husband Ben opened the diner with a $5,000 loan on the U Street of "Black Broadway" — Black-owned theaters, doctors, lawyers, everything. From the counter she saw the 1968 riots burn the corridor, the drug years empty it, Metro construction (1986–1991) tear up the street, and then gentrification remake it — condos and newcomers, most of the old Black businesses gone, her block now some of the most expensive land in the city. Ben's stayed, and the family talks openly about being one of the last anchors of the neighborhood that was. (Her account: interviews with WETA, the Washington Post, NPR, and the shop's own published history — journalism and public media, labeled.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="virginia-ali" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building for heat and water" -->
- Cities run hotter than the land around them — pavement and buildings hold heat: daytime urban air 1–6°F warmer than nearby rural areas, nights up to 22°F warmer, surfaces 50–90°F hotter than the air (EPA). Heat now shapes city design: white roofs, street trees, shade rules.
- Miami Beach, flooding on sunny days at high tide, began physically raising streets and installing pump stations in 2014 — a program that grew from about $400 million to $600 million — and the raised blocks stayed dry when the King Tides came. An American city lifting itself above the water, again (Chicago did it first, by jackscrew).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tallest buildings moved abroad" -->
Said plainly: the world's tallest building has not stood in America since 1998, when Malaysia's Petronas Towers took the title from Chicago's Sears Tower (by a 1996 rules decision counting spires). Dubai's Burj Khalifa (2010, 2,717 feet) stands more than 900 feet taller than anything in the U.S. [corrected 2026-09-07: this line read "more than doubled", which is false against 1WTC's 1,776 ft - 2,717 is 1.53x. A verifier caught it in the prose; the error originated HERE and had already propagated once.] One World Trade Center (2014, a symbolic 1,776 feet, its steel topped out by Mohawk ironworkers like the towers before it) is the tallest in the Western Hemisphere — but the sky-race this chapter followed since 1885 now runs through Asia and the Gulf. Shared with: `landmarks` (the buildings themselves) · `america-world` (what the shift says about the world).
