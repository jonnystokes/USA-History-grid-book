## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There was no money in this land before 1500 — no coins, no bills, no banks, and no prices. People traded without it. Goods moved through barter, gift exchange, and obligation, between neighbors and between nations, and shells from the Gulf coast, copper from Lake Superior, and obsidian from the West traveled hundreds of miles along rivers and trails.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum — records and diplomacy, not coins" -->
- Wampum is beads made from shell: white beads from whelk shells, purple from the quahog clam, strung or woven into belts (Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections; LOC).
- Its uses were records, diplomacy, ceremony, and gifts — not buying and selling. Belts recorded treaties and events and were presented and read in councils (Clements Library; LOC "Shell Money").
- Wampum only began passing as currency after Europeans arrived, in trade between colonists and Native nations — that episode is told in the 1600s section. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — wampum's meaning, the Two Row belt, the Covenant Chain) · `news-communication` (belts as message and record).
- The trade networks themselves are `economy`'s and `native-nations`' subject; no named individual is documented from this chapter's angle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There was still no American money in the 1500s and no mint anywhere in this land. The century's great money event happened far to the south: Spain found silver at Potosí in 1545, and Spanish mints in the Americas began striking the silver coin called the piece of eight. It became the first money used around the whole world. Almost none of it circulated here yet.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The piece of eight" -->
- The 8-real coin — the Spanish dollar, the "piece of eight" — was minted from American silver and moved through Europe, across the Pacific to Asia, and through every ocean trade route. Historians of money call it the first global currency (University of London Press, *New World Objects of Knowledge*; CoinsWeekly).
- What Spanish coin existed in this land sat in garrison towns like St. Augustine (founded 1565), paid from Spain's colonial funds — a foreign system passing through.
- The coin's American future, for later sections: the U.S. dollar was modeled on it, it stayed legal tender in the United States until 1857, and "two bits" for a quarter survives from its eight parts (NMAH; Coinage Act of 1857).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies were short of coin from the start. England sent little money over, and what coin the colonists earned flowed back across the ocean to pay for imported goods. So the colonies improvised: they paid each other in tobacco, corn, beaver skins, and wampum, they minted an illegal coin of their own, and in 1690 Massachusetts printed the first government paper money in the Western world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Commodity money" -->
- Massachusetts made wampum receivable as small change in 1637 (6 beads a penny) and legal tender up to 40 shillings in 1650; the law was repealed in 1661 (Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections). What wampum meant to the nations that made it is `native-nations`' telling — to them it was never small change.
- Virginia made tobacco legal tender in 1642; Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina used tobacco as money for most of the colonial period. Beaver pelts and corn also passed as money in the north (Encyclopedia.com; Cleveland Fed). Shared with: `economy` (tobacco as the crop that made the Chesapeake's living).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pine-tree shilling, 1652" -->
- Massachusetts opened a mint in Boston in 1652 without asking England — coining money was the king's right, but in 1652 England had no king (CoinWeek; Smithsonian NMAH).
- Pine-tree shillings were struck from about 1667 to 1682, but nearly all carry the date 1652. Numismatic historians read the frozen date as cover: after Charles II took the throne in 1660, coins dated 1652 could pass as leftovers from the kingless years (CoinWeek; ANS).
- The mint closed in 1682, and the crown listed it among the complaints when it revoked the Massachusetts charter in 1684.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-hull" name="John Hull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Hull
> **Who:** the Boston silversmith who ran the illegal colonial mint and struck the pine-tree shilling. · **When and where:** Boston, mintmaster 1652–1682.
Hull came to Boston from England as a boy in 1635 and trained as a silversmith. In 1652, at 28, the colony made him mintmaster; his contract paid him a cut of every coin struck, and the mint made him one of New England's richest men and later the colony's treasurer. The tale that he gave his daughter Hannah her weight in pine-tree shillings as a wedding dowry is a legend — Nathaniel Hawthorne retold and embroidered it two centuries later (New England Historical Society; CoinWeek).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-hull" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1690 — the first government paper money" -->
- In 1690 Massachusetts sent soldiers against French Quebec, lost, and had no coin to pay them when they came home. The colony printed paper "bills of credit" instead — a written promise the treasurer would accept the paper as money (New England Historical Society; ANS).
- These bills are the first paper money issued by any government in the Western world. China had used paper money centuries earlier.
- The paper worked. Massachusetts had printed 240,000 pounds of it by 1714, and by 1718 the other New England colonies had followed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s every colony printed paper money. Where colonies printed carefully — Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey — the paper held its value. Where they printed heavily — New England and the Carolinas — prices rose and the bills bought less each year. London merchants, paid in shrinking colonial paper, complained to Parliament, and the argument over paper money became one of the running quarrels between the colonies and Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper that held and paper that didn't" -->
- The pattern, verified across the colonies: moderate issues held value, heavy issues did not (Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, Economic Commentary 2015-06).
- Virginia's tobacco money grew a paper layer: government warehouses inspected and stored the crop and issued receipts — "tobacco notes" — that passed from hand to hand as money (system built out in the 1710s–1730s; Encyclopedia.com; the receipts-as-money studies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-money" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** the printer who argued for paper money and then printed it — with leaves, so it could not be faked. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1729.
> **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
At 23, Franklin published *A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of a Paper-Currency* (1729), arguing Pennsylvania needed more money in circulation — and his print shop won the contract to print it. His firm printed money for Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware. Against counterfeiters he printed impressions of real leaves on the bills: no engraver could copy a leaf's vein pattern by hand. A 2023 study of 600 of his notes found the leaf prints, mica flakes in the paper, and colored fibers — security features on money three centuries before watermark strips (Library of Congress Franklin exhibition; The Conversation on the 2023 PNAS study). Colonial bills carried the plain warning "To Counterfeit is Death." The documentary is about his whole life, not the money alone.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Parliament banned New England's paper money in 1751 and every colony's in 1764, and the shortage of money joined the list of American grievances. Then the Revolution was paid for with paper that collapsed: the Continental dollar lost nearly all its value by 1781. The new nation answered by building a money system on purpose — a decimal dollar in 1785, a national bank in 1791, and a mint in 1792.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Currency Acts" -->
- Currency Act of 1751: Parliament barred the New England colonies from issuing new legal-tender paper. Currency Act of April 19, 1764: the ban extended to all thirteen colonies (Colonial Williamsburg; American History Central). Shared with: `government-politics` (the grievance list toward revolution).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Not worth a Continental" -->
- Congress could not tax, so from June 1775 it printed Continental dollars — $241.5 million of them in all. The British counterfeited the bills on purpose to speed the collapse (Harvard CURIOSity; NJ State Library).
- The slide, measured in Philadelphia: about 4 paper dollars to 1 silver dollar by the end of 1777, 8 to 1 by the end of 1778, and 100 to 1 by 1781, when the paper stopped circulating. In North Carolina the ratio reached 210 to 1 (Harvard CURIOSity; NY Fed).
- The collapse erased roughly $226 million in face value, and the loss fell on whoever was holding the bills — soldiers, farmers, shopkeepers. "Not worth a Continental" survived as a phrase for anything worthless. The war debt itself is `economy`'s story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the dollar" -->
- Thomas Jefferson's 1784 notes on coinage proposed the Spanish dollar as the unit, split into tenths and hundredths instead of English shillings and pence. On July 6, 1785 Congress adopted the dollar with decimal parts — the first nation to put decimal money in law (Monticello; LOC Jefferson Papers).
- First Bank of the United States, chartered February 25, 1791 for twenty years: the government's banker, proposed by Hamilton, opposed by Jefferson and Madison as unconstitutional, signed by Washington (Federal Reserve History).
- Coinage Act of April 2, 1792: created the U.S. Mint at Philadelphia — the first federal building raised under the Constitution — and defined the dollar in gold, silver, and copper coins, from eagles down to half-cents (U.S. Mint; the act's text).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-money" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton
> **Who:** the first Treasury Secretary, who designed the dollar, the Mint, and the national bank. · **When and where:** New York and Philadelphia, 1789–1795.
> **Movie:** Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)
Hamilton's reports to Congress laid out the machinery piece by piece: a national bank (December 1790) to hold the government's money and issue trusted notes, and a mint (January 1791) with the dollar defined in both silver and gold. Congress built both. The bank was chartered in 1791, the Mint in 1792, and the United States had what the Revolution had lacked — money people could trust (Founders Online; Federal Reserve History). His debt plan and the dinner-table bargain that passed it are told in `economy`; his arrival as an immigrant, and the musical, in `immigration`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-money" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Twice the United States built a national bank, and twice it let the bank die — the First Bank's charter lapsed in 1811, and Andrew Jackson killed the Second Bank in the 1830s. After 1836 the country had no national currency at all. Money was gold and silver coin plus paper notes printed by hundreds of state banks, each bank's notes worth less the farther they traveled from home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bank War" -->
- The Second Bank of the United States (chartered 1816, Philadelphia) held the government's money and, under Nicholas Biddle from 1823, policed the state banks by demanding coin for their notes (Federal Reserve History; Britannica).
- Jackson's case: a private corporation with public power, unaccountable to voters, serving the connected rich. Biddle's case: the Bank kept the currency sound and the state banks honest. Both cases had evidence — tell both (Richmond Fed Econ Focus 2023Q2).
- Congress renewed the charter early, in July 1832; Jackson vetoed it on July 10, 1832, calling the Bank dangerous to liberty, and won reelection that fall. In 1833 he moved the government's deposits to chosen state banks; the Senate censured him for it in 1834. The charter ran out in 1836, and the Bank failed as a private company in 1841 (Britannica; Richmond Fed). Shared with: `government-politics` (the veto, the censure, presidential power) · `big-business` (a private corporation with public power).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-money" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson
> **Who:** the president who killed the national bank. · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1829–1837.
> **Movie:** Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)
Jackson distrusted banks and paper money all his life — a land deal gone bad in the 1790s left him debts he spent years paying. He vetoed the Second Bank's recharter in 1832 and pulled the government's money out in 1833. The Bank died; nothing replaced it; and for the next thirty years American money was whatever paper the nearest bank printed. Whether that was liberation or wreckage was argued then and is argued still (Britannica; Richmond Fed). The documentary covers his whole presidency, including Indian removal — this chapter's slice is the Bank.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-jackson-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="State banknotes and counterfeit detectors" -->
- From 1836 to 1863, paper money meant state banknotes — every bank its own designs. Notes traded at a discount that grew with distance from the issuing bank, and merchants checked weekly pamphlets — "bank note reporters" and "counterfeit detectors," published from the 1820s to the 1860s — to learn which notes were good and which were fake (Dwyer, Atlanta Fed 1996; ANS on Dillistin's bibliography).
- "Wildcat banks" — banks set up under loose state laws, some in spots so remote (the joke went) that only wildcats lived there, so no one could bring the notes home to redeem. Modern historians find most free-era banks were not frauds and losses were smaller than the legend — state the legend and the correction together (Dwyer 1996; Richmond Fed 2018Q1).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837 — the runs" -->
- On May 10, 1837, New York City's banks stopped paying gold and silver for their notes and deposits, and banks across the country followed. People in the lines got paper promises or nothing (research bank; Federal Reserve History).
- What caused the crash and the six hard years after is `economy`'s telling. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the downturn) — the split: they tell why the crash came; this chapter tells what a bank run is and what the money was worth after.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1860 more than 10,000 different kinds of banknotes circulated in the United States, and an estimated one-third or more of the paper money was counterfeit. The Civil War swept that world away: the Union printed a national paper currency in 1862 and taxed the state banknotes out of existence. Then the country spent the rest of the century fighting over what should stand behind the new money — gold alone, or gold and silver together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Greenbacks" -->
- Legal Tender Act, February 25, 1862: to pay for the war, Congress created United States Notes — paper backed by no metal, money because the law said so. Green ink on the back gave the name: greenbacks. $150 million authorized at first, $450 million by war's end (U.S. Capitol Visitor Center; Federal Reserve History). Shared with: `war` (paying for the war) · `economy` (war finance and prices).
- Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase managed the new money and put his own face on the first $1 bill (Smithsonian NMAH).
- National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864 created national banks issuing uniform notes; an 1865 law taxed state banknotes 10 percent, and state paper vanished — $143 million circulating in 1865, $4 million by 1867 (Federal Reserve History).
- "In God We Trust" entered the coinage in 1864 on the two-cent piece, after a Pennsylvania minister, M. R. Watkinson, wrote to Chase in 1861 urging it; the letter survives in Treasury records. It reached paper money in 1957 (Treasury history; U.S. Mint).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiters and the Secret Service" -->
- The Secret Service was founded July 5, 1865 as a Treasury unit with one job: catching counterfeiters. Estimates at the time put one-third to one-half of the paper money in circulation as fake — state it as an estimate. Guarding presidents was added to its duties only after President McKinley was shot in 1901 (secretservice.gov; U.S. Marshals history). Shared with: `crime-justice` (federal law enforcement).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="emanuel-ninger" name="Emanuel Ninger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emanuel Ninger
> **Who:** the sign painter who drew money by hand — and was caught by a wet bar top. · **When and where:** New Jersey and Manhattan, 1883–1896.
Ninger, a German-born sign painter, drew fifty- and hundred-dollar bills with pen, pencil, and camel's-hair brush, tracing a real note against glass, colored inks for the seals, weeks of work on a single bill. For thirteen years the fakes passed. On March 28, 1896 he laid a bill on a saloon bar in Manhattan; the bar was wet, the fresh ink smudged, and he was followed and arrested. He pleaded guilty and served six years. The newspapers' verdict, retold ever since: each bill had cost him more labor than honest work would have paid (Smithsonian *Sidedoor*, "Jim the Penman"; *American Heritage*, 1984).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emanuel-ninger" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold against silver" -->
- Explained simply: with the dollar tied to gold, money grew slowly and prices FELL for thirty years after 1865. Falling prices crushed borrowers — a farmer's crop brought fewer dollars every year while the mortgage payment stayed the same. Coining silver too would have made more money and raised prices. So indebted farmers and silver-mining states wanted silver; bankers and lenders wanted gold only (Fed history; EBSCO).
- The Coinage Act of February 12, 1873 dropped the silver dollar from the coinage — "the Crime of '73" to the silver side, passed just as western mines began pouring out silver. Partial silver-purchase laws followed (Bland-Allison 1878; Sherman 1890, repealed 1893), satisfying no one (U.S. Mint history; Encyclopedia.com). Shared with: `economy` (farm debt and the 1890s depression) · `elements` (silver the metal; Comstock).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-jennings-bryan" name="William Jennings Bryan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Jennings Bryan
> **Who:** the 36-year-old who made money the whole question of a presidential election. · **When and where:** Democratic convention, Chicago, July 9, 1896.
Bryan, a young ex-congressman from Nebraska, closed the convention's currency debate with a speech his hearers never forgot, ending: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns; you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The hall erupted; the next day the convention nominated him for president on the fifth ballot. He carried the farm South and West and lost to William McKinley, whose backers stood for gold — and lost again in 1900. The gold men won the argument; the Gold Standard Act of 1900 put their victory in law (Britannica; History Matters/GMU).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-jennings-bryan" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Gold Standard Act of March 14, 1900 made gold the sole backing of the dollar. Within eight years a panic showed that the gold-backed system still had no defense against bank runs, and the country built one: the Federal Reserve in 1913, and deposit insurance in 1933 — the same year the government took the dollar off gold at home. By 1945, money meant Federal Reserve notes, insured bank accounts, and, in millions of family drawers, war bonds.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1907" -->
- In October 1907 a failed stock scheme wrecked the banks behind it, and fear spread to New York's trust companies. A run closed the Knickerbocker Trust — one of the city's largest — on October 22; depositors lined up around the block at the next trusts in line (Federal Reserve History; LOC).
- A bank run, explained simply: a bank lends out most of the money people deposit, so no bank can pay everyone back at once. If enough depositors demand their money on the same day, a sound bank fails like a broken one. Panic of 1837, Panic of 1907, 1933 — the mechanism is the same.
- The United States had no central bank, so one private banker organized the defense (Morgan story below). Congress drew the obvious lesson and set up a commission to design something permanent. The downturn the panic caused is `economy`'s. Shared with: `economy` (the 1907–08 recession).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan-money" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan
> **Who:** the banker who stopped the Panic of 1907 from his own library — the country's central bank, in one man. · **When and where:** New York City, October–November 1907.
Morgan, 70, ran the rescue from his private library on Madison Avenue: his examiners decided which trusts could be saved, the U.S. Treasury deposited $25 million with his syndicate, and he pressed bank presidents into money pools. On the night of November 2–3 he gathered the trust-company presidents in the library and locked the doors until, near five in the morning, they signed a $25 million rescue of the weakest trusts. The locked-door night is documented — the Morgan Library itself tells it (Morgan Library & Museum exhibition guide; Federal Reserve History). The rescue worked, and it alarmed the country: the money system had depended on one aging private citizen. His railroad and steel empire is `big-business`'s story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve, 1913" -->
- November 1910: Senator Nelson Aldrich and five banking and Treasury men traveled in secret, posing as a duck-hunting party, to the Jekyll Island Club off Georgia and drafted a central-bank plan. The participants admitted the meeting only in the 1930s. It reads like conspiracy fiction and is documented fact — the Federal Reserve's own history tells it (Federal Reserve History, "The Meeting at Jekyll Island").
- Their bill failed, but the Federal Reserve Act — signed December 23, 1913 — built on its machinery: twelve regional Reserve Banks under a Washington board. Explained simply: a bank for banks. It holds banks' reserves and can lend to a sound bank facing a run, so a scare need not become a collapse (Federal Reserve History). Shared with: `government-politics` (the law and the fight over it).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1930–1933: the runs, the holiday, and insurance" -->
- The Federal Reserve did not stop the Depression's bank runs. About 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, and roughly nine million savings accounts were wiped out — estimates, stated as such (FDR Library; ushistory.org). The Bank of United States in New York (December 11, 1930) was the largest failure yet: 400,000 depositors, many of them immigrant garment workers. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the Depression itself).
- March 6, 1933: Roosevelt, two days in office, closed every bank in the country — the "bank holiday." Banks reopened March 13–15 after federal review. In his first fireside chat (March 12, 60 million listeners) he explained banking in plain words and said money was safer in a reopened bank than under the mattress. When the doors opened, more money was deposited than withdrawn (LOC; Federal Reserve History; FDR Library).
- April 5, 1933: Americans were ordered to turn in their gold coins and gold certificates at $20.67 an ounce; the Gold Reserve Act (January 30, 1934) repriced gold at $35. From then on, a citizen's dollar was paper, not a claim on gold (Federal Reserve History; National Archives).
- The FDIC, created June 1933, began insuring deposits January 1, 1934, up to $2,500. Explained simply: if an insured bank fails, the government pays the depositors back — so there is no reason to run. Bank runs, a fixture of American life for a century, mostly stopped (FDIC histories).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lydia-lobsiger" name="Lydia Lobsiger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lydia Lobsiger
> **Who:** the first depositor in American history paid back by federal deposit insurance. · **When and where:** East Peoria, Illinois, 1934.
When the Fond du Lac State Bank of East Peoria failed in the spring of 1934 — the first insured bank to go under — Lobsiger did not lose her savings. She received the first federal deposit insurance check, $1,250, dated July 3, 1934, and newspapers across the country carried the story: a bank had failed and a small depositor had been made whole. Every generation before hers had known bank failures as ruin (FDIC, *The First Fifty Years*; FDIC 90th-anniversary timeline).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lydia-lobsiger" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War bonds" -->
- By the end of World War II about 85 million Americans — half the population — had bought war bonds, raising about $185.7 billion. The Series E bond cost $18.75 and paid $25 ten years later; children bought 10-cent stamps toward bonds (Army Historical Foundation; Duke Ad*Access). Shared with: `war` (the home front) · `economy` (war finance).
- Schools at War, 1942–1945: about 200,000 schools and 30 million students bought stamps and bonds toward goals — over $2 billion in all, enough that schools "sponsored" at least 90,000 jeeps and thousands of airplanes, tracked in classroom scrapbooks (Treasury/Office of Education program records; Eleanor Roosevelt's 1942 radio address). No single verified family account found yet — the program numbers carry the span; do not invent a family.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000 Americans started carrying money as plastic cards, drawing cash from machines, and moving it as electronic signals. In 1971 the dollar's last tie to gold was cut. And in between, the worst peacetime inflation in the country's history ate through paychecks and savings for fifteen years, until the Federal Reserve broke it with interest rates near 20 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic: the charge card and the credit card" -->
- Diners Club, 1950: the first charge card good at many businesses — cardboard, first used at a New York restaurant in February 1950, the bill paid in full each month (Smithsonian NMAH; Diners Club history). The founding legend — Frank McNamara forgetting his wallet at dinner — was invented for publicity, by the admission of the company's own first PR man, Matty Simmons. Tell it only as the legend it is (payment-history journalism, labeled).
- The Fresno Drop, September 1958: Bank of America mailed about 60,000 ready-to-use BankAmericards — the first true credit card, with balances that could revolve — to Fresno, California residents who had never asked for them. Delinquency ran about 22 percent against 4 projected, and fraud followed; the bank lost millions before the card caught on. Licensed nationwide, it became Visa in 1976. Congress banned mailing unsolicited cards in 1970 (KVPR; 99% Invisible; Nocera — journalism, labeled). Shared with: `marketplace` (lead — buying on credit at the counter) · `home-family` (household debt).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ATM" -->
- September 2, 1969, Rockville Centre, New York: Chemical Bank switched on the first American ATM, built by Docutel and conceived by Don Wetzel. The ad: "On September 2, our banks will open at 9:00 and never close again" (History.com; Long Island Herald — journalism, labeled).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="August 15, 1971 — the last tie to gold" -->
- Since 1934, only foreign governments could trade dollars for U.S. gold, at $35 an ounce. By 1971 the dollars held abroad far outnumbered the gold, and governments were lining up to redeem — a bank run on the United States itself.
- On Sunday night, August 15, 1971, after a secret Camp David weekend, Nixon announced the window was closed. It was called temporary. It never reopened (Federal Reserve History; IMF history blog).
- Since that night the dollar has been backed by no metal at all. It is money because the law says so and because people trust it — which is what the greenback's critics had feared in 1862, and what every dollar has been ever since. Shared with: `economy` (what floating money did to trade and prices) · `america-world` (the end of the postwar currency system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Inflation" -->
- From the late 1960s to 1982, prices rose faster nearly every year: about 11 percent in 1974, 13.5 percent in 1980. President Ford handed out "Whip Inflation Now" buttons in 1974; inflation did not notice (Federal Reserve History; BLS; Ford Library).
- What it meant at home, in numbers: food prices rose over 20 percent in late 1973; a fixed pension or a savings account lost a third of its buying power between 1965 and 1982; by October 1981 a home mortgage averaged 18.63 percent — the highest ever recorded (BLS; Freddie Mac survey).

### Paul Volcker
> **Who:** the Federal Reserve chairman who broke the Great Inflation, at the price of the worst recession since the 1930s. · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1979–1987.
Volcker — six foot seven, famous for cheap cigars — took over the Fed in August 1979 with inflation heading past 13 percent. On Saturday night, October 6, 1979, he announced the Fed would clamp down on the supply of money and let interest rates go where they must. They went to 20 percent. The recession of 1981–82 pushed unemployment to 10.8 percent; indebted farmers drove tractors around the Fed's building, and builders mailed him sawed-off two-by-fours from houses nobody could afford to finance. Inflation fell from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.2 percent in 1983 and stayed low for a generation (Federal Reserve History; St. Louis Fed Review 2021). The recession's job losses are `economy`'s and `work-workers`' telling.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most American money is now numbers in bank computers, moved by card and phone; in 2024 only 14 percent of payments used cash. The old bank run returned in 2008 wearing new clothes, and was stopped the 1933 way — with a government guarantee. And since 2009 some money is issued by nobody at all. This section is current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008 — the run comes back" -->
- By 2008 millions of people and businesses kept cash in money-market funds — accounts treated like bank deposits but carrying no insurance. On September 16, 2008, the day after the Lehman Brothers investment bank failed, the $62.5 billion Reserve Primary Fund — holding $785 million of Lehman's debt — "broke the buck": its shares fell below the fixed $1. Depositors did what depositors did in 1837, 1907, and 1933 — they ran. Over $140 billion left money-market funds in a single day (Yale Journal of Financial Crises).
- On September 19 the Treasury announced a temporary guarantee of money-market balances — deposit insurance, improvised in a week — and the run stopped. The crisis around it (housing, the crash, the recession) is `economy`'s lead. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the crisis) · `big-business` (the firms) · `home-family` (the foreclosed households).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cash fades" -->
- Federal Reserve payment diaries: in 2016 cash was still the single most-used way to pay; by 2024 it was third, used in 14 percent of payments, behind credit cards (35 percent) and debit cards (30 percent). Adults 18 to 24 paid by phone 45 percent of the time. Over 90 percent of consumers still keep and use some cash (Federal Reserve, Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, 2024–2025). The shopping side — online carts, tap-to-pay at the counter — is `marketplace`'s.
- The penny: in fiscal 2024 each one-cent coin cost 3.69 cents to make. Production was ordered ended in 2025, and the last circulating pennies were struck at the Philadelphia Mint on November 12, 2025 — 232 years after the first federal cents of 1793. Pennies already made stay legal (U.S. Mint; news coverage — journalism, labeled).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money issued by nobody" -->
- In October 2008 a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a design for Bitcoin, and on January 3, 2009 started the system; the first block's code carries that day's London Times headline about bank bailouts. Nakamoto's identity is still unknown (technical record; journalism, labeled).
- Stated plainly: Bitcoin is money issued by no government and no bank — a public ledger kept by computers checking one another. Its dollar price has swung violently. Some people hold it as an investment, some use it to pay, and economists disagree about whether it is money, an asset, or a speculation. State the disagreement; no hype either way.
- The 2021–2023 inflation, from this chapter's side: prices rose 9.1 percent in the year to June 2022, the most since 1981, and the Federal Reserve raised rates at the fastest pace since Volcker; economists still dispute how much blame belongs to supply chains, relief spending, and energy prices (BLS; CRS R47273). `economy` tells the household side with the same honest dispute.

### Laszlo Hanyecz
> **Who:** the programmer who made the first documented purchase with bitcoin — two pizzas for 10,000 coins. · **When and where:** Jacksonville, Florida, May 22, 2010.
Hanyecz, an early Bitcoin volunteer, posted an offer on the project's forum: 10,000 bitcoins to anyone who would get two large pizzas delivered to him. A user took the deal; the pizzas cost about $25, and the bitcoins were then worth about $41. It was the first recorded time the new money bought a real thing. At later prices those 10,000 coins would be worth hundreds of millions of dollars; Hanyecz has said he does not regret it — someone had to show the money worked. The date is marked yearly as "Bitcoin Pizza Day" (Bitcointalk forum record; Forbes, Bitcoin Magazine — journalism, labeled).
