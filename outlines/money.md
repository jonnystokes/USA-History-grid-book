## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There was no money in this land before 1500. There were no coins, no bills, no banks and no prices. People traded goods through barter, gifts and obligations, between neighbors and between nations. Shells from the Gulf coast, copper from Lake Superior and obsidian from the West traveled hundreds of miles along rivers and trails.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum: records and diplomacy, not coins" -->
- Wampum is beads made from shell: white beads from whelk shells, purple from the quahog clam, strung or woven into belts (Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections; LOC).
- Its uses were records, diplomacy, ceremony, and gifts — not buying and selling. Belts recorded treaties and events and were presented and read in councils (Clements Library; LOC "Shell Money").
- Wampum only began passing as currency after Europeans arrived, in trade between colonists and Native nations — that episode is told in the 1600s section. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — wampum's meaning, the Two Row belt, the Covenant Chain) · `news-communication` (belts as message and record).
- The trade networks themselves are `economy`'s and `native-nations`' subject; no named individual is documented from this chapter's angle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There was still no American money in the 1500s and no mint anywhere in this land. The century's great money event happened far to the south: Spain found silver at Potosí in 1545, and Spanish mints in the Americas began striking the silver coin called the piece of eight. It became the first money used around the whole world. Almost none of it circulated here yet.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The piece of eight" -->
- The 8-real coin — the Spanish dollar, the "piece of eight" — was minted from American silver and moved through Europe, across the Pacific to Asia, and through every ocean trade route. Historians of money call it the first global currency (University of London Press, *New World Objects of Knowledge*; CoinsWeekly).
- What Spanish coin existed in this land sat in garrison towns like St. Augustine (founded 1565), paid from Spain's colonial funds — a foreign system passing through.
- The coin's American future, for later sections: the U.S. dollar was modeled on it, it stayed legal tender in the United States until 1857, and "two bits" for a quarter survives from its eight parts (NMAH; Coinage Act of 1857).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies were short of coin from the start. England sent little money over, and what coin the colonists earned flowed back across the ocean to pay for imported goods. So the colonies improvised: they paid each other in tobacco, corn, beaver skins, and wampum, they minted an illegal coin of their own, and in 1690 Massachusetts printed the first government paper money in the Western world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Commodity money" -->
- Massachusetts made wampum receivable as small change in 1637 (6 beads a penny) and legal tender up to 40 shillings in 1650; the law was repealed in 1661 (Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections). What wampum meant to the nations that made it is `native-nations`' telling — to them it was never small change.
- Virginia made tobacco legal tender in 1642; Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina used tobacco as money for most of the colonial period. Beaver pelts and corn also passed as money in the north (Encyclopedia.com; Cleveland Fed). Shared with: `economy` (tobacco as the crop that made the Chesapeake's living).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pine-tree shilling, 1652" -->
- Massachusetts opened a mint in Boston in 1652 without asking England — coining money was the king's right, but in 1652 England had no king (CoinWeek; Smithsonian NMAH).
- Pine-tree shillings were struck from about 1667 to 1682, but nearly all carry the date 1652. Numismatic historians read the frozen date as cover: after Charles II took the throne in 1660, coins dated 1652 could pass as leftovers from the kingless years (CoinWeek; ANS).
- The mint closed in 1682, and the crown listed it among the complaints when it revoked the Massachusetts charter in 1684.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-hull" name="John Hull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Hull
> **Who:** the Boston silversmith who ran the illegal colonial mint and struck the pine-tree shilling. · **When and where:** Boston, mintmaster 1652–1682.
Hull came to Boston from England as a boy in 1635 and trained as a silversmith. In 1652, at 28, the colony's leaders made him mintmaster. His contract paid him a cut of every coin struck, and the mint made him one of New England's richest men and later the colony's treasurer. A story says he gave his daughter Hannah her weight in pine-tree shillings as a wedding gift. That story is a legend, which Nathaniel Hawthorne retold and added to two centuries later (New England Historical Society, CoinWeek).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-hull" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1690: the first government paper money" -->
- In 1690 Massachusetts sent soldiers against French Quebec, lost, and had no coin to pay them when they came home. The colony printed paper "bills of credit" instead — a written promise the treasurer would accept the paper as money (New England Historical Society; ANS).
- These bills are the first paper money issued by any government in the Western world. China had used paper money centuries earlier.
- The paper worked. Massachusetts had printed 240,000 pounds of it by 1714, and by 1718 the other New England colonies had followed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s every colony printed paper money. Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey printed moderate amounts, and their paper held its value. New England and the Carolinas printed large amounts, and their bills bought less each year as prices rose. London merchants who were paid in this shrinking paper complained to Parliament. Paper money became one of the running arguments between the colonies and Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper that held and paper that didn't" -->
- The pattern, verified across the colonies: moderate issues held value, heavy issues did not (Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, Economic Commentary 2015-06).
- Virginia's tobacco money grew a paper layer: government warehouses inspected and stored the crop and issued receipts — "tobacco notes" — that passed from hand to hand as money (system built out in the 1710s–1730s; Encyclopedia.com; the receipts-as-money studies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-money" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** the printer who argued for paper money, then printed it with leaf patterns so it could not be faked. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1729.
> **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
At 23, Franklin published *A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of a Paper-Currency* (1729), arguing that Pennsylvania needed more money in circulation. His print shop then won the contract to print it. His firm printed money for Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware. To stop counterfeiters he printed copies of real leaves on the bills, because no engraver could copy a leaf's vein pattern by hand. A 2023 study of 600 of his notes found the leaf prints, flakes of the mineral mica in the paper, and colored fibers (Library of Congress Franklin exhibition, The Conversation on the 2023 PNAS study). Colonial bills carried the warning "To Counterfeit is Death." The documentary is about his whole life, not the money alone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper money lent against land and people" -->
- South Carolina's government printed paper money from 1703. At first 1.5 South Carolina pounds equaled one English pound. By the late 1720s it took about 7 (Charleston County Public Library, 2024).
- Colonial South Carolina had no banks. People borrowed against land and against enslaved people, recorded in the law as movable property. A slave owner could pledge the people he held to a slave broker for a loan, buy more people with it, and pay the debt from their forced labor. Thousands of these mortgages survive in the South Carolina state archives (Charleston County Public Library). Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (lead, people as property).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Parliament banned New England's paper money in 1751 and every colony's in 1764, and colonists added the money shortage to their complaints against Britain. Congress paid for the Revolution with paper money that collapsed, and the Continental dollar had lost nearly all its value by 1781. After the war the new government's leaders built a money system on purpose: a decimal dollar in 1785, a national bank in 1791 and a mint in 1792.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Currency Acts" -->
- Currency Act of 1751: Parliament barred the New England colonies from issuing new legal-tender paper. Currency Act of April 19, 1764: the ban extended to all thirteen colonies (Colonial Williamsburg; American History Central). Shared with: `government-politics` (the grievance list toward revolution).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Not worth a Continental" -->
- Congress could not tax, so from June 1775 it printed Continental dollars — $241.5 million of them in all. The British counterfeited the bills on purpose to speed the collapse (Harvard CURIOSity; NJ State Library).
- The slide, measured in Philadelphia: about 4 paper dollars to 1 silver dollar by the end of 1777, 8 to 1 by the end of 1778, and 100 to 1 by 1781, when the paper stopped circulating. In North Carolina the ratio reached 210 to 1 (Harvard CURIOSity; NY Fed).
- The collapse erased roughly $226 million in face value, and the loss fell on whoever was holding the bills — soldiers, farmers, shopkeepers. "Not worth a Continental" survived as a phrase for anything worthless. The war debt itself is `economy`'s story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiting was a hanging crime" -->
- Colonial bills printed "To Counterfeit is Death," and courts carried it out. Owen Sullivan, an Irish-born engraver who ran counterfeiting gangs from New England to New York, was convicted in New York of faking that colony's bills and hanged on May 10, 1756 (1756 pamphlet of his life, University of Michigan catalog record). A period newspaper quoted in secondary sources says that earlier he had parts of both ears cut off and the letter C burned into both cheeks with a hot iron for counterfeiting in another colony. That detail still needs checking against the original. Shared with: `crime-justice` (punishment).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the dollar" -->
- Thomas Jefferson's 1784 notes on coinage proposed the Spanish dollar as the unit, split into tenths and hundredths instead of English shillings and pence. On July 6, 1785 Congress adopted the dollar with decimal parts — the first nation to put decimal money in law (Monticello; LOC Jefferson Papers).
- First Bank of the United States, chartered February 25, 1791 for twenty years: the government's banker, proposed by Hamilton, opposed by Jefferson and Madison as unconstitutional, signed by Washington (Federal Reserve History).
- Coinage Act of April 2, 1792: created the U.S. Mint at Philadelphia — the first federal building raised under the Constitution — and defined the dollar in gold, silver, and copper coins, from eagles down to half-cents (U.S. Mint; the act's text).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-money" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton
> **Who:** the first Treasury Secretary, who designed the dollar, the Mint, and the national bank. · **When and where:** New York and Philadelphia, 1789–1795.
> **Movie:** Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)
Hamilton's reports to Congress laid out the plan piece by piece. A national bank (December 1790) would hold the government's money and issue trusted notes. A mint (January 1791) would make coins, with the dollar defined in both silver and gold. Congress chartered the bank in 1791 and created the Mint in 1792 (Founders Online, Federal Reserve History). His debt plan and the dinner-table bargain that passed it are told in `economy`. His arrival as an immigrant, and the musical, are in `immigration`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-money" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Congress created a national bank twice and let each charter end. Congress let the First Bank's charter lapse in 1811, and President Andrew Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's renewal in 1832. After 1836 the country had no national currency. Money was gold and silver coin plus paper notes printed by hundreds of state banks, and each bank's notes were worth less the farther they traveled from home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banknotes lent against enslaved people" -->
- By the 1830s Louisiana planters had set up banks that lent their own paper money against mortgages on land and on enslaved people. At the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana, an enslaver could borrow bank notes worth up to half the appraised value of the people and land he pledged. Louisiana's legislature backed the banks' bonds, and Baring Brothers of London and Hope and Company of Amsterdam sold them to investors in Europe. Each $1,000 bond was about the price of one field worker (Edward E. Baptist, Common-place, 2010).
- When cotton prices fell in 1837, planters could not pay. The people they had mortgaged were sold to raise cash. Mississippi and Florida refused to pay the state-backed bonds. In 2005 JPMorgan Chase reported that two of the Louisiana banks it grew out of had taken about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral and came to own about 1,250 of them. Shared with: `economy` (lead, these banks as the South's capital) · `slavery-freedom` (people as property).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bank War" -->
- The Second Bank of the United States (chartered 1816, Philadelphia) held the government's money and, under Nicholas Biddle from 1823, policed the state banks by demanding coin for their notes (Federal Reserve History; Britannica).
- Jackson's case: a private corporation with public power, unaccountable to voters, serving the connected rich. Biddle's case: the Bank kept the currency sound and the state banks honest. Both cases had evidence — tell both (Richmond Fed Econ Focus 2023Q2).
- Congress renewed the charter early, in July 1832; Jackson vetoed it on July 10, 1832, calling the Bank dangerous to liberty, and won reelection that fall. In 1833 he moved the government's deposits to chosen state banks; the Senate censured him for it in 1834. The charter ran out in 1836, and the Bank failed as a private company in 1841 (Britannica; Richmond Fed). Shared with: `government-politics` (the veto, the censure, presidential power) · `big-business` (a private corporation with public power).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-money" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson
> **Who:** the president who killed the national bank. · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1829–1837.
> **Movie:** Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)
Jackson distrusted banks and paper money all his life. In 1795 he sold land for another man's written promises to pay. That man went bankrupt in 1797, and Jackson spent about 15 years paying off the debts. As president he vetoed the Second Bank's recharter in 1832 and moved the government's money out of it in 1833. No national bank replaced it, and for the next thirty years American paper money was whatever the nearest bank printed. People argued then, and historians argue now, over whether ending the Bank protected ordinary Americans or left the money system without a brake (Britannica, Richmond Fed, *American Heritage* 2001). The documentary covers his whole presidency, including Indian removal. This chapter's slice is the Bank.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-jackson-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="State banknotes and counterfeit detectors" -->
- From 1836 to 1863, paper money meant state banknotes — every bank its own designs. Notes traded at a discount that grew with distance from the issuing bank, and merchants checked weekly pamphlets — "bank note reporters" and "counterfeit detectors," published from the 1820s to the 1860s — to learn which notes were good and which were fake (Dwyer, Atlanta Fed 1996; ANS on Dillistin's bibliography).
- Michigan's wildcat banks lent against land in the southeast of the state. The Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Odawa and Wyandot nations had signed that land over to the United States in the Treaty of Detroit on November 17, 1807, about 8 million acres for roughly 1.2 cents an acre (Nottawaseppi Huron Band of the Potawatomi).
- "Wildcat banks" — banks set up under loose state laws, some in spots so remote (the joke went) that only wildcats lived there, so no one could bring the notes home to redeem. Modern historians find most free-era banks were not frauds and losses were smaller than the legend — state the legend and the correction together (Dwyer 1996; Richmond Fed 2018Q1).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="alpheus-felch-money" name="Alpheus Felch" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Alpheus Felch
> **Who:** the Michigan bank inspector who opened the boxes of coin and found nails and glass. · **When and where:** Michigan, 1838 to 1839.
Michigan passed a free-banking law on March 15, 1837, and the number of banks in the state went from 9 to 40 within months. Felch, a lawyer from Monroe, had voted against the law. In 1838 he became one of three state bank commissioners, the officials who checked that each bank really held the gold and silver coin that stood behind its paper notes. In his own later account, one bank had no assets at all. Another showed him boxes of coin that were filled with nails and glass under a top layer of coins. At three banks in a row, the bankers showed the commissioners the same coins, carried ahead from one bank to the next. The commissioners closed banks and reported the frauds to the legislature. By September 1839 only 9 banks were left, and Michigan notes lost an estimated 60 percent of their face value. Economic historians count the losses from the true wildcat banks alone at about $350,000, and point out that there were perhaps a dozen of them. Felch resigned in 1839 and later served as governor and U.S. senator (Felch, *Michigan Pioneer Collections*, 1880, as summarized in Dove, Pecquet and Thies 2014; Ann Arbor District Library).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alpheus-felch-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837: the runs" -->
- On May 10, 1837, New York City's banks stopped paying gold and silver for their notes and deposits, and banks across the country followed. People in the lines got paper promises or nothing (research bank; Federal Reserve History).
- What caused the crash and the six hard years after is `economy`'s telling. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the downturn) — the split: they tell why the crash came; this chapter tells what a bank run is and what the money was worth after.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1860 more than 10,000 different kinds of banknotes circulated in the United States, and an estimated one-third or more of the paper money was counterfeit. During the Civil War the Union government printed a national paper currency in 1862, and Congress taxed the state banknotes until banks stopped issuing them. For the rest of the century Americans argued over what should stand behind the new money: gold alone, or gold and silver together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Greenbacks" -->
- Legal Tender Act, February 25, 1862: to pay for the war, Congress created United States Notes — paper backed by no metal, money because the law said so. Green ink on the back gave the name: greenbacks. $150 million authorized at first, $450 million by war's end (U.S. Capitol Visitor Center; Federal Reserve History). Shared with: `war` (paying for the war) · `economy` (war finance and prices).
- Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase managed the new money and put his own face on the first $1 bill (Smithsonian NMAH).
- National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864 created national banks issuing uniform notes; an 1865 law taxed state banknotes 10 percent, and state paper vanished — $143 million circulating in 1865, $4 million by 1867 (Federal Reserve History).
- "In God We Trust" entered the coinage in 1864 on the two-cent piece, after a Pennsylvania minister, M. R. Watkinson, wrote to Chase in 1861 urging it; the letter survives in Treasury records. It reached paper money in 1957 (Treasury history; U.S. Mint).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiters and the Secret Service" -->
- The Secret Service was founded July 5, 1865 as a Treasury unit with one job: catching counterfeiters. Estimates at the time put one-third to one-half of the paper money in circulation as fake — state it as an estimate. Guarding presidents was added to its duties only after President McKinley was shot in 1901 (secretservice.gov; U.S. Marshals history). Shared with: `crime-justice` (federal law enforcement).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="emanuel-ninger" name="Emanuel Ninger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emanuel Ninger
> **Who:** the sign painter who drew money by hand and was caught when his ink ran on a wet bar top. · **When and where:** New Jersey and Manhattan, 1883 to 1896.
Ninger, a German-born sign painter, drew fifty- and hundred-dollar bills with pen, pencil and a camel's-hair brush. He traced a real note against glass and used colored inks for the seals, and a single bill took him weeks. For thirteen years the fakes passed. On March 28, 1896 he laid a bill on a wet saloon bar in Manhattan. The fresh ink smudged, and he was followed and arrested. He pleaded guilty and served six years. Newspapers reported that each bill had cost him more work than honest sign painting would have paid (Smithsonian *Sidedoor*, "Jim the Penman", *American Heritage*, 1984).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emanuel-ninger" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Freedman's Savings Bank, 1865 to 1874" -->
- President Lincoln signed the charter of the Freedman's Savings and Trust Company on March 3, 1865, to hold the savings of people freed from slavery. By 1871 it had about 37 branches in 17 states and Washington, D.C. About 70,000 people opened accounts, most of them holding $5 to $50 (National Archives, Prologue 1997; OCC).
- In 1870 Congress changed the charter so the bank could make real-estate loans instead of holding only safe government bonds. Henry D. Cooke, a board member, used the bank's money to benefit his own family's banking business, according to the Comptroller of the Currency's history. The Panic of 1873 hit its loans.
- Frederick Douglass became president in March 1874 and put in $10,000 of his own money. The bank closed on June 29, 1874. About 61,000 depositors were owed nearly $3 million. Between 1875 and 1883 they got back 62 percent, and about 31,000 of them never collected anything. The bank's signature books survive and list each depositor's name, age, job and family. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (what freedom looked like).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bank runs of 1873 and 1893" -->
- At least 100 banks failed in the Panic of 1873, and the New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days starting September 20, 1873, the first closing in its history. In 1893 more than 100 banks suspended in June and 340 more between mid-July and mid-August. Other counts put 1893's failures at about 500 (Federal Reserve History, Banking Panics of the Gilded Age). No one counted how many depositors lost savings, or how much. There was no deposit insurance. Shared with: `economy` (lead, the depressions).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold against silver" -->
- Explained simply: with the dollar tied to gold, money grew slowly and prices FELL for thirty years after 1865. Falling prices crushed borrowers — a farmer's crop brought fewer dollars every year while the mortgage payment stayed the same. Coining silver too would have made more money and raised prices. So indebted farmers and silver-mining states wanted silver; bankers and lenders wanted gold only (Fed history; EBSCO).
- The Coinage Act of February 12, 1873 dropped the silver dollar from the coinage — "the Crime of '73" to the silver side, passed just as western mines began pouring out silver. Partial silver-purchase laws followed (Bland-Allison 1878; Sherman 1890, repealed 1893), satisfying no one (U.S. Mint history; Encyclopedia.com). Shared with: `economy` (farm debt and the 1890s depression) · `elements` (silver the metal; Comstock).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-jennings-bryan" name="William Jennings Bryan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Jennings Bryan
> **Who:** the 36-year-old who made money the whole question of a presidential election. · **When and where:** Democratic convention, Chicago, July 9, 1896.
Bryan, a 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska, gave the last speech in the convention's debate over money. He ended by telling the supporters of gold, "you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The next day the delegates nominated him for president on the fifth ballot. He won most of the South, the rural Midwest and the Rocky Mountain states. He lost to William McKinley, whose party stood for gold, 271 electoral votes to 176, and lost to him again in 1900. The Gold Standard Act of 1900 made gold the only backing of the dollar (Britannica, History Matters/GMU, 1896 official returns).

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Gold Standard Act of March 14, 1900 made gold the only backing of the dollar. The Panic of 1907 showed that the gold-backed system had no defense against bank runs. Congress created the Federal Reserve in 1913 and deposit insurance in 1933. In 1933 President Roosevelt also ordered Americans to turn in their gold coins. By 1945, money meant Federal Reserve notes, insured bank accounts and, for about 85 million Americans, war bonds.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1907" -->
- In October 1907 a failed stock scheme wrecked the banks behind it, and fear spread to New York's trust companies. A run closed the Knickerbocker Trust — one of the city's largest — on October 22; depositors lined up around the block at the next trusts in line (Federal Reserve History; LOC).
- A bank run, explained simply: a bank lends out most of the money people deposit, so no bank can pay everyone back at once. If enough depositors demand their money on the same day, a sound bank fails like a broken one. Panic of 1837, Panic of 1907, 1933 — the mechanism is the same.
- The United States had no central bank, so one private banker organized the defense (Morgan story below). Congress drew the obvious lesson and set up a commission to design something permanent. The downturn the panic caused is `economy`'s. Shared with: `economy` (the 1907–08 recession).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan-money" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan
> **Who:** the banker who ran the rescue in the Panic of 1907 from his own library, doing the job a central bank would later do. · **When and where:** New York City, October and November 1907.
Morgan, 70, ran the rescue from his private library on Madison Avenue. His examiners decided which trust companies could be saved, the U.S. Treasury deposited $25 million with his group of banks, and he pressed bank presidents to put money into shared rescue funds. On the night of November 2 to 3 he gathered the trust-company presidents in the library and locked the doors. Near five in the morning they signed a $25 million rescue of the weakest trusts. The Morgan Library tells this story in its own exhibition (Morgan Library & Museum exhibition guide, Federal Reserve History). The rescue held. It also showed members of Congress that the money system had depended on one 70-year-old private banker. His railroad and steel companies are `big-business`'s story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan-money" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve, 1913" -->
- November 1910: Senator Nelson Aldrich and five banking and Treasury men traveled in secret, posing as a duck-hunting party, to the Jekyll Island Club off Georgia and drafted a central-bank plan. The participants admitted the meeting only in the 1930s. It reads like conspiracy fiction and is documented fact — the Federal Reserve's own history tells it (Federal Reserve History, "The Meeting at Jekyll Island").
- Their bill failed, but the Federal Reserve Act — signed December 23, 1913 — built on its machinery: twelve regional Reserve Banks under a Washington board. Explained simply: a bank for banks. It holds banks' reserves and can lend to a sound bank facing a run, so a scare need not become a collapse (Federal Reserve History). Shared with: `government-politics` (the law and the fight over it).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1930–1933: the runs, the holiday, and insurance" -->
- The Federal Reserve did not stop the Depression's bank runs. About 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, and roughly nine million savings accounts were wiped out — estimates, stated as such (FDR Library; ushistory.org). The Bank of United States in New York (December 11, 1930) was the largest failure yet: 400,000 depositors, many of them immigrant garment workers. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the Depression itself).
- The FDIC's own count: 9,096 banks suspended in 1930 to 1933, and depositors lost about $1.34 billion (1,350 banks and $237 million in 1930, 2,293 and $390 million in 1931, 1,453 and $168 million in 1932, 4,000 and $540 million in 1933). The Federal Reserve did little to help, and its officials felt no responsibility for most of the banks that failed in 1930 because those banks were not members (FDIC, A Brief History of Deposit Insurance, 1998).
- March 6, 1933: Roosevelt, two days in office, closed every bank in the country — the "bank holiday." Banks reopened March 13–15 after federal review. In his first fireside chat (March 12, 60 million listeners) he explained banking in plain words and said money was safer in a reopened bank than under the mattress. When the doors opened, more money was deposited than withdrawn (LOC; Federal Reserve History; FDR Library).
- April 5, 1933: Americans were ordered to turn in their gold coins and gold certificates at $20.67 an ounce; the Gold Reserve Act (January 30, 1934) repriced gold at $35. From then on, a citizen's dollar was paper, not a claim on gold (Federal Reserve History; National Archives).
- The FDIC, created June 1933, began insuring deposits January 1, 1934, up to $2,500. Explained simply: if an insured bank fails, the government pays the depositors back — so there is no reason to run. Bank runs, a fixture of American life for a century, mostly stopped (FDIC histories).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black-owned banks, and Greenwood's losses" -->
- Many banks would not serve Black customers, so Black Americans opened their own. Maggie Lena Walker opened the St. Luke Penny Savings Bank in Richmond, Virginia, in November 1903, the first bank founded by a Black woman in the United States. It took in more than $8,000 on its first day. In the Depression she merged it into the Consolidated Bank and Trust Company, which lasted into the 21st century (Richmond Fed, 2022). Sources disagree on whether she was the first woman of any race to charter a U.S. bank.
- Jesse Binga opened a bank for Black Chicagoans in 1908 and the Binga State Bank in 1921. His home in a white neighborhood and his offices were bombed at least five times. The records do not say who set the bombs. Illinois officials closed his bank in 1930, and many depositors lost their savings.
- On May 31 and June 1, 1921, a white mob, including men deputized by Tulsa police, and National Guard units burned Greenwood, Tulsa's Black district. More than 1,000 homes and businesses were destroyed, and credible estimates of the dead run from 50 to 300 (Oklahoma Historical Society). Residents filed more than $1.8 million in damage claims with the city, and officials denied all but one. Insurance companies refused to pay by calling the attack a "riot," which their policies excluded. No city, state or federal rebuilding money came. Greenwood's residents rebuilt, and by the 1940s the district had 242 Black-owned businesses (Brookings, 2021). Shared with: `crime-justice` · `rights-movements`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lydia-lobsiger" name="Lydia Lobsiger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lydia Lobsiger
> **Who:** the first depositor in American history paid back by federal deposit insurance. · **When and where:** East Peoria, Illinois, 1934.
The Fond du Lac State Bank of East Peoria failed in the spring of 1934, the first insured bank to fail. Lobsiger did not lose her savings. She received the first federal deposit insurance check, $1,250, dated July 3, 1934, and newspapers across the country reported that a bank had failed and a small depositor had been paid in full. From 1930 to 1933, before insurance, depositors in failed banks had lost about $1.34 billion (FDIC, *A Brief History of Deposit Insurance*, *The First Fifty Years*, FDIC 90th-anniversary timeline).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lydia-lobsiger" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War bonds" -->
- By the end of World War II about 85 million Americans — half the population — had bought war bonds, raising about $185.7 billion. The Series E bond cost $18.75 and paid $25 ten years later; children bought 10-cent stamps toward bonds (Army Historical Foundation; Duke Ad*Access). Shared with: `war` (the home front) · `economy` (war finance).
- Schools at War, 1942–1945: about 200,000 schools and 30 million students bought stamps and bonds toward goals — over $2 billion in all, enough that schools "sponsored" at least 90,000 jeeps and thousands of airplanes, tracked in classroom scrapbooks (Treasury/Office of Education program records; Eleanor Roosevelt's 1942 radio address). No single verified family account found yet — the program numbers carry the span; do not invent a family.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000 Americans started carrying money as plastic cards, drawing cash from machines, and moving it as electronic signals. In 1971 the dollar's last tie to gold was cut. And in between, the worst peacetime inflation in the country's history ate through paychecks and savings for fifteen years, until the Federal Reserve broke it with interest rates near 20 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic: the charge card and the credit card" -->
- Diners Club, 1950: the first charge card good at many businesses — cardboard, first used at a New York restaurant in February 1950, the bill paid in full each month (Smithsonian NMAH; Diners Club history). The founding legend — Frank McNamara forgetting his wallet at dinner — was invented for publicity, by the admission of the company's own first PR man, Matty Simmons. Tell it only as the legend it is (payment-history journalism, labeled).
- The Fresno Drop, September 1958: Bank of America mailed about 60,000 ready-to-use BankAmericards — the first true credit card, with balances that could revolve — to Fresno, California residents who had never asked for them. Delinquency ran about 22 percent against 4 projected, and fraud followed; the bank lost millions before the card caught on. Licensed nationwide, it became Visa in 1976. Congress banned mailing unsolicited cards in 1970 (KVPR; 99% Invisible; Nocera — journalism, labeled). Shared with: `marketplace` (lead — buying on credit at the counter) · `home-family` (household debt).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ATM" -->
- September 2, 1969, Rockville Centre, New York: Chemical Bank switched on the first American ATM, built by Docutel and conceived by Don Wetzel. The ad: "On September 2, our banks will open at 9:00 and never close again" (History.com; Long Island Herald — journalism, labeled).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="August 15, 1971: the last tie to gold" -->
- Since 1934, only foreign governments could trade dollars for U.S. gold, at $35 an ounce. By 1971 the dollars held abroad far outnumbered the gold, and governments were lining up to redeem — a bank run on the United States itself.
- On Sunday night, August 15, 1971, after a secret Camp David weekend, Nixon announced the window was closed. It was called temporary. It never reopened (Federal Reserve History; IMF history blog).
- Since that night the dollar has been backed by no metal at all. It is money because the law says so and because people trust it — which is what the greenback's critics had feared in 1862, and what every dollar has been ever since. Shared with: `economy` (what floating money did to trade and prices) · `america-world` (the end of the postwar currency system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Inflation" -->
- From the late 1960s to 1982, prices rose faster nearly every year: about 11 percent in 1974, 13.5 percent in 1980. President Ford handed out "Whip Inflation Now" buttons in 1974; inflation did not notice (Federal Reserve History; BLS; Ford Library).
- What it meant at home, in numbers: food prices rose over 20 percent in late 1973; a fixed pension or a savings account lost a third of its buying power between 1965 and 1982; by October 1981 a home mortgage averaged 18.63 percent — the highest ever recorded (BLS; Freddie Mac survey).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="paul-volcker" name="Paul Volcker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Paul Volcker
> **Who:** the Federal Reserve chairman who broke the Great Inflation, at the price of the worst recession since the 1930s. · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1979–1987.
Volcker, six foot seven and known for cheap cigars, became Fed chairman in August 1979. Prices were then rising more than 13 percent a year. On Saturday night, October 6, 1979, he announced that the Fed would limit the supply of money and let interest rates rise as high as they went. The federal funds rate passed 20 percent in 1981. In the recession of 1981 to 1982 unemployment reached 10.8 percent. Farmers in debt drove tractors around the Fed's building, and builders mailed him sawed-off pieces of lumber from houses nobody could afford to finance. Inflation fell from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.2 percent in 1983 and stayed low for about twenty years (Federal Reserve History, St. Louis Fed Review 2021). The recession's job losses are `economy`'s and `work-workers`' telling.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paul-volcker" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The savings and loan crisis" -->
- Savings and loans took in savings and made long-term home loans at fixed rates. When interest rates climbed toward 20 percent around 1980, they had to pay savers more than their old loans earned. By 1983 closing the broken ones would have cost about $25 billion, and their insurance fund held $6 billion. In 1982 Congress let them make riskier loans (Federal Reserve History).
- In 1989 Congress set up the Resolution Trust Corporation to shut them down. By the end of 1995 it had closed 747 savings and loans with over $407 billion in assets. Insured savers were paid in full, and taxpayers paid up to $124 billion (Federal Reserve History). Shared with: `economy` · `home-family`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bruce-bent-henry-brown" name="Bruce Bent and Henry Brown" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bruce Bent and Henry Brown
> **Who:** two New York money managers who built a way for small savers to earn more than the legal cap on bank interest. · **When and where:** New York, 1969 to 1973.
Since 1933 a federal rule called Regulation Q had set the most interest a bank could pay on a savings account. In July 1973 the cap on a bank passbook account was 5 percent. In 1974 prices rose about 11 percent, so money in a passbook account lost about 6 percent of its buying power that year. Large investors could get higher rates by buying short-term government and business debt, but that took far more than most families had. In August 1969 Bent proposed a fund that would pool many small savers' money and buy that debt for them. He and Brown set up the Reserve Fund with a $1,000 minimum. Sources date its opening to 1971 or 1972. After a *New York Times* article in January 1973, it held $100 million by the end of that year. Other firms copied it, and by 1980 there were 90 such funds. The same fund, grown to about $62 billion, was the one that "broke the buck" in September 2008 (Federal Reserve History; Richmond Fed 1978; *New York Times* and *Wall Street Journal* obituaries of Brown, 2008).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most American money is now numbers in bank computers, moved by card and phone. In 2025 only 14 percent of payments used cash. In 2008 savers ran from money-market funds, and the Treasury stopped the run with a government guarantee, as Roosevelt had in 1933. In 2023 depositors ran from Silicon Valley Bank, and federal officials again guaranteed every deposit. Since 2009 some money, such as bitcoin, has been issued by no government and no bank. This section is current through September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008: the run comes back" -->
- By 2008 millions of people and businesses kept cash in money-market funds — accounts treated like bank deposits but carrying no insurance. On September 16, 2008, the day after the Lehman Brothers investment bank failed, the $62.5 billion Reserve Primary Fund — holding $785 million of Lehman's debt — "broke the buck": its shares fell below the fixed $1. Depositors did what depositors did in 1837, 1907, and 1933 — they ran. Over $140 billion left money-market funds in a single day (Yale Journal of Financial Crises).
- Banks failed too. From 2008 through 2013, 489 insured banks failed. Washington Mutual, with $307 billion in assets, failed on September 25, 2008, the largest bank failure in U.S. history. In October 2008 Congress raised deposit insurance from $100,000 to $250,000, and in 2010 made it permanent (FDIC, Crisis and Response, 2017).
- On September 19 the Treasury announced a temporary guarantee of money-market balances — deposit insurance, improvised in a week — and the run stopped. The crisis around it (housing, the crash, the recession) is `economy`'s lead. Shared with: `economy` (lead — the crisis) · `big-business` (the firms) · `home-family` (the foreclosed households).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2023: the run by phone" -->
- On March 9, 2023, depositors pulled $42 billion out of Silicon Valley Bank in California in one day. About 88 percent of its deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit. State officials closed it on March 10. New York officials closed Signature Bank on March 12 (FDIC, statement to Congress, March 27, 2023).
- On March 12 Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen, after consulting the President, used an emergency power to protect every depositor at both banks, insured or not. The cost, about $22.5 billion, was charged to other banks. California officials closed First Republic Bank on May 1, 2023, and JPMorgan Chase took its deposits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cash fades" -->
- Federal Reserve payment diaries: in 2016 cash was still the single most-used way to pay; by 2024 it was third, used in 14 percent of payments, behind credit cards (35 percent) and debit cards (30 percent). Adults 18 to 24 paid by phone 45 percent of the time. Over 90 percent of consumers still keep and use some cash (Federal Reserve, Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, 2024–2025). The shopping side — online carts, tap-to-pay at the counter — is `marketplace`'s.
- The 2025 figures, released in May 2026, barely moved: cash was 14 percent of payments for the sixth year in third place, and 90 percent of people planned to keep using it. People 55 and older paid cash about 10 times a month, people 18 to 24 about twice (Federal Reserve, 2026 Diary of Consumer Payment Choice).
- The penny: in fiscal 2024 each one-cent coin cost 3.69 cents to make. Production was ordered ended in 2025, and the last circulating pennies were struck at the Philadelphia Mint on November 12, 2025 — 232 years after the first federal cents of 1793. In 2026 Congress was working on the Common Cents Act, which would end circulating pennies in law and round cash totals to the nearest nickel (CoinWeek, September 2026). Pennies already made stay legal (U.S. Mint; news coverage — journalism, labeled).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money issued by nobody" -->
- In October 2008 a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a design for Bitcoin, and on January 3, 2009 started the system; the first block's code carries that day's London Times headline about bank bailouts. Nakamoto's identity is still unknown (technical record; journalism, labeled).
- Stated plainly: Bitcoin is money issued by no government and no bank — a public ledger kept by computers checking one another. Its dollar price has swung violently. Some people hold it as an investment, some use it to pay, and economists disagree about whether it is money, an asset, or a speculation. State the disagreement; no hype either way.
- In November 2022 the crypto exchange FTX went bankrupt. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, had used customer money for real estate, investments and political donations. A jury convicted him of fraud in November 2023, and on March 28, 2024 Judge Lewis Kaplan sentenced him to 25 years in prison. By July 2026 the bankruptcy had paid customer claims back at 105 percent of their value in the bankruptcy (Washington Post; FTX, 2026).
- On July 18, 2025 President Trump signed the GENIUS Act, a federal law setting rules for stablecoins. A stablecoin is a digital token that its issuer promises to trade for exactly one dollar. The law requires one dollar of cash or safe government debt behind every coin. The coins carry no federal insurance (Congressional Research Service).
- The 2021–2023 inflation, from this chapter's side: prices rose 9.1 percent in the year to June 2022, the most since 1981, and the Federal Reserve raised rates at the fastest pace since Volcker; economists still dispute how much blame belongs to supply chains, relief spending, and energy prices (BLS; CRS R47273). `economy` tells the household side with the same honest dispute.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="laszlo-hanyecz" name="Laszlo Hanyecz" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Laszlo Hanyecz
> **Who:** the programmer who made the first documented purchase with bitcoin, two pizzas for 10,000 coins. · **When and where:** Florida, May 22, 2010.
Hanyecz, a programmer who had worked on Bitcoin's code, posted an offer on the project's forum: 10,000 bitcoins to anyone who would get two large pizzas delivered to him. On May 22 a forum user named jercos took the deal. The pizzas cost about $25, and the bitcoins were then worth about $41. It was the first recorded time bitcoin was traded for a real thing. At the May 2025 price those 10,000 coins would have been worth about $1.1 billion. In a 2019 interview Hanyecz said, "A trade happens because both parties think they're getting a good deal." May 22 is now marked each year as "Bitcoin Pizza Day" (bitcoin.it wiki, Gulf News, CoinDesk 2025, journalism, labeled).
