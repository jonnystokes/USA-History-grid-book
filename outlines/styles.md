## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, people in North America made clothes and ornaments from plants, animals and minerals near home, and from goods they got by trade. Deer hide, rabbit fur, turkey feathers, yucca and cotton were clothing. Shell from the Gulf of Mexico and copper from the Lake Superior country were ornaments. Colors came from plants and from minerals such as red ochre. No written record names a single dressmaker, weaver or wearer from these centuries. What is known comes from cloth, feathers, beads and copper that archaeologists have dug up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Feathers, fiber and cotton in the Southwest" -->
- Ancestral Puebloan people of the Four Corners made clothing from deer hide, rabbit skin and fiber from the yucca plant (Grand Canyon Conservancy, "Weavers").
- A feather blanket made in the 1200s is held at Edge of the Cedars State Park Museum in southeastern Utah. Its maker wrapped about 11,550 turkey feathers around more than 180 meters (590 feet) of cord twisted from yucca fiber. The feathers came from 4 to 10 turkeys, and the makers probably pulled them from live birds, which grow them back. Feather blankets had replaced blankets made from strips of rabbit fur in this region centuries earlier (Bill Lipe and Shannon Tushingham, Washington State University, *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*, November 2020, reported by Sci.News).
- Cotton came north by trade from Mexico. The earliest farmed cotton found in Arizona's Tonto Basin dates to about the year 250. Cotton reached the Four Corners around 600. By about 900, Ancestral Puebloan men were weaving large cloths and blankets on upright looms. Men grew the cotton, spun it and wove it (Grand Canyon Conservancy, "Weavers". American Southwest Virtual Museum, Tonto National Monument).
- The Salado people of the Tonto Basin (about 1250 to 1450) wove plain cotton cloth, cloth with open geometric patterns, belts, and a head strap for carrying loads dyed in bright colors. They wove sandals from agave and yucca fiber. By about 1300 the Tonto Basin was one of the leading cotton growing areas of the Southwest (American Southwest Virtual Museum, Tonto National Monument, National Park Service).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations themselves) · `food-farming` (cotton as a crop). This chapter has what the cloth and feathers looked like and what they were made of.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell and copper along the Mississippi, 1050 to 1400" -->
- At Cahokia, a Mississippian city across the river from today's St. Louis, people traded for copper from the Lake Superior region and for sea shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Metalworkers heated copper, hammered it while it was soft, and repeated the steps (a process called annealing). They made beads, thin plates, hair ornaments and headdress pieces, some with bird-man figures pushed out from the back. Mississippian copper plates were made between about 800 and 1600 and have been found from Florida and Georgia to Oklahoma and Wisconsin (research bank: Mississippian copper plates, Etowah plates, Wulfing cache).
- Archaeologists found a copper workshop just north of Mound 34 at Cahokia, a building about 5.5 by 4.4 meters holding raw copper nuggets, scraps of worked copper and stone tools. Gregory Perino first found signs of it in 1956, and excavators confirmed its location in 1999 (research bank: Wikipedia, "Mound 34").
- In the 1000s, people at Cahokia buried a man and a woman on a platform covered with marine-shell disc beads laid out in the shape of a bird. The count is given as 20,000 beads by the magazine *Archaeology* and as about 10,000 in other published descriptions (Wikipedia, "Mound 72", summarizing Fowler). Melvin Fowler dug the burial in Mound 72 between 1967 and 1971 and first described both central bodies as men. A team led by Thomas Emerson of the Illinois State Archaeological Survey re-examined the bones in 2016 and found a man and a woman (*Archaeology*, August 5, 2016).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (lead: Cahokia) · `elements` (copper as a metal). This chapter has copper and shell as things people wore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors from plants and earth" -->
- Red ochre is iron oxide, a mineral dug from the ground and ground into powder. Mixed with fat or another binder, it made paint. The Karankawa of the Texas coast painted symbols on faces with ochre, according to early written accounts. At the Mitchell Ridge burial site, red ochre stains the bones of 18 people (Texas Beyond History, "Ochre").
- Women across the Northeast woodlands, the Great Lakes and the northern Plains decorated clothing with porcupine quills. They flattened the quills and boiled them in plant dyes. Tamarack bark, spruce cones and berries gave red. Walnuts and wild grapes gave black. Sunflower, pine bark and willow root gave yellow. Blueberries and larkspur gave blue. The quills were sewn onto moccasins, pouches, cradleboards and clothing (Penn Museum, "The Art of Quillwork"). Quillwork was being done before Europeans arrived (McCord Museum, Quillwork scholarship). The quilled pieces in museum collections are much younger. Penn Museum names pieces George Catlin collected between 1832 and 1839 among its oldest, so the dye plants listed here were written down long after 1500.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish, French and English expeditions met Native nations whose clothes were made of deerskin, bark fiber, feathers and cotton, and whose ornaments were made of pearls, shell and copper. The newcomers wrote down what they saw. In 1540 Hernando de Soto's soldiers dug pearls out of Native graves. By 1540, glass beads from a failed Spanish colony had already reached the town of Cofitachequi. In 1598 Spanish colonists drove churro sheep into New Mexico, and wool from those sheep went into Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for about 300 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth and pearls at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
- In May 1540 Hernando de Soto's army reached Cofitachequi, a town on a river in what is now South Carolina or Georgia. A Portuguese soldier in the army, known only as the Gentleman of Elvas, described what the people there wore and kept. The woman who ruled the town arrived by canoe and gave de Soto mantles and deerskins. She took a long string of pearls from her own neck and put it around his (Elvas, *Narrative*, 1557, Hakluyt translation).
- Elvas wrote that the town's storehouses held mantles woven from yarn made of tree bark and mantles made of feathers colored white, green, red and yellow, worn in winter. They held deerskins painted with designs, and some deerskins were made into leggings, stockings and shoes. He wrote that everyone in the town wore shoes and clothes (Elvas).
- According to Elvas, the ruler told de Soto to search the town's graves for pearls. De Soto's men dug up the graves and took the pearls, along with small figures of babies and birds made from pearls. The accounts disagree on how much. Translations of Elvas give 14 arrobas, a Spanish weight of about 25 pounds each, so about 350 pounds. One English edition gives 392 pounds. Other accounts give about 200 pounds (research bank).
- De Soto then took the ruler with him as a prisoner, guarded by enslaved women. On the march she told her guards she needed to relieve herself, walked into the woods and escaped. She carried off a cane box of undrilled pearls (Elvas). No record gives her name. Spanish writers called her the Lady of Cofitachequi.
- The same town already held a dagger and glass beads from Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón's failed Spanish colony of 1526 (Elvas).
- Shared with: `health` (lead: the empty towns and the sickness that emptied them) · `native-nations` (the nation) · `exploration` (de Soto's route). This chapter has what people wore and the pearls taken from the graves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Secotan people wore, 1585" -->
- John White painted the Algonquian people of the Carolina coast in 1585, and Thomas Hariot wrote captions for the pictures. One painting shows a leader wearing a fringed apron of animal skin with a tail hanging down behind, and a quiver of arrows tied to the tail. His hair is decorated with feathers and shaved on one side. Hariot wrote that the shaving kept the hair from tangling in the bowstring (Encyclopedia Virginia, "The manner of their attire", from Hariot's *Briefe and True Report*, 1590). His body is painted for a special occasion rather than tattooed, and he wears copper beads or pearls at his neck and wrist (Encyclopedia Virginia, search result, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Shared with: `art` (lead: John White as a painter).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish clothes in St. Augustine, 1565 to 1600" -->
- Spanish soldiers and settlers founded St. Augustine in 1565 in Timucua land. Archaeologists from the Florida Museum have dug up what the colonists wore. Rich residents wore imported cloth trimmed with metal lace, buttons, beads and metal ornaments. Most people tied their plain clothes shut with laces tipped with small metal ends called aglets. Buttons were mostly worn by soldiers and the rich (Florida Museum, "Life on the Remote Frontier").
- Luxury cloth reached the town from Mexico, China and Europe. Juan de Cevadilla, the official who collected the soldiers' pay in Mexico, died in 1590. He left his widow a carved and gilded bed with a canopy of embroidered Chinese taffeta, a smooth silk cloth, and a large amount of fine cloth and clothing (Florida Museum).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Churro sheep and red from Mexico" -->
- Juan de Oñate brought churro sheep (a long-haired Spanish breed) up the road from Mexico into New Mexico in 1598. By the 1700s the churros in the Rio Grande valley numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Their wool became the base of Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for the next 300 years (National Park Service, "New Mexico Fiber Arts Traditions"). One sheep breeders' history puts Oñate's flock at close to 5,000 (research bank, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Before this, Pueblo weavers worked in cotton on upright looms. The Spanish brought a floor loom set flat, which weavers later used for Rio Grande blankets and serapes (NPS).
- Far to the south, Indigenous farmers in Oaxaca, Puebla and Tlaxcala in Mexico raised cochineal, a small insect that lives on prickly-pear cactus. Crushed, it gives a strong red dye. After the Spanish conquest, Spanish merchants traded it around the world. The work and the knowledge stayed with Indigenous Mexicans, who were never credited for it (Harvard Museums of Science and Culture, "Cochineal: Mexico's Red").
- Shared with: `food-farming` (sheep as livestock) · `migration` (lead: Oñate's column).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the English colonies of the 1600s, lawmakers in Virginia and Massachusetts wrote laws about clothing. In Massachusetts, a person worth less than 200 pounds was not allowed to wear silk hoods, gold or silver lace, or big boots, and county courts fined people who did. Many people today picture Puritan colonists dressed only in black. The lists of their belongings made when they died record red, blue, violet, grey and green clothes. In New Mexico, Spanish colonists made Pueblo households hand over woven cotton blankets or hides every year as tribute, a payment demanded from a conquered people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia taxes clothing, 1619" -->
- At its first meeting, on July 30, 1619, the General Assembly of Virginia passed a law "against excesse in apparell." The members voted that every man would pay his share of church and public costs according to his clothes. An unmarried man was assessed on his own clothes, and a married man on his own and his wife's (John Pory's record of the 1619 Assembly, Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation transcription and Online Library of Liberty).
- In 1621 Virginia's leaders resolved again to "supress excess in cloaths" and to keep men who held no office from wearing gold in their clothes (Ink Mendelsohn, "We Were What We Wore", *American Heritage*, December 1988).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts dress laws, 1634 to 1682" -->
- The General Court of Massachusetts Bay (the colony's elected lawmakers and magistrates) passed its first clothing law in 1634. It banned "new fashions, or long hair," silver and gold hatbands and belts, cloth woven with gold thread, lace, beaver hats, and sleeves with more than two slashes. A wearer caught in them had to give them up (research bank: Atlas Obscura, and the University of Colorado Denver *Historical Studies Journal* 2025, citing Shurtleff, *Records of the Governor*). A 1639 law banned more lace and short sleeves.
- In 1651 the General Court declared its "utter detestation and dislike that men or women of mean condition" should dress like gentlemen, "by the wearing of gold or silver lace, or buttons, or points at their knees, to walk in great boots," or that women of that rank should wear "silk or tiffany hoods or scarves." Anyone worth less than 200 pounds who did so owed a fine of 10 shillings for each offense. Magistrates, officers, soldiers, their families, and people who had once been rich were exempt. Town selectmen, the elected men who ran each town, were told to tax people who dressed above their rank (Shurtleff, *Records*, vol. 3, cited by the *Historical Studies Journal*. James Russell Trumbull, *History of Northampton*, vol. 1, 1898).
- County courts enforced it. In 1653 an Essex County court heard 18 cases and let 12 people go because they were worth more than 200 pounds (*Historical Studies Journal*, citing *Records and Files of the Quarterly Courts*). In Hampshire County in 1673, the court charged 7 married women of Northampton with wearing silk and acquitted all but one. The same court charged 9 married women of Springfield, 9 of Hadley, 7 of Hatfield and 3 of Westfield (Trumbull).
- In 1663 the General Court extended the law to children and servants and barred tailors from making the forbidden clothes (Trumbull).
- In 1682 the Hampshire County court charged the five towns of Northampton, Hadley, Hatfield, Westfield and Springfield for failing to tax residents who wore silk "beyond their ranks." Trumbull found no later attempt to enforce the law there.

### Hannah Lyman
> **Who:** a teenager fined for wearing a silk hood, who wore it again to court. · **When and where:** Northampton, Massachusetts, 1676.
- During King Philip's War, the Northampton minister Solomon Stoddard asked the colony's leaders to act against "that intolerable pride in clothes and hair." In November 1675 the General Court passed a law listing the colony's sins and their punishments (Trumbull, *History of Northampton*, vol. 1, 1898).
- Northampton stood on Nonotuck land. On September 24, 1653, John Pynchon of Springfield got a deed to it from seven Nonotuck men, including Chickwalloppe, for 100 fathoms of wampum (strings of shell beads) and 10 wool coats. The historian Margaret Bruchac writes that the Nonotuck signers probably understood it as an agreement to share the land, keeping their own rights to hunt, fish and plant there (Bruchac, "From Nonotuck to Northampton", Historic Northampton).
- On March 27, 1676, the Hampshire County court at Northampton charged 23 people "for wearing silk in a flaunting manner and for long hair and other extravagances." Six were married women, six unmarried women, and the rest young men. The court warned them and made each pay court fees of 2 shillings 6 pence. The wife of Samuel Holton was fined 10 shillings because she had been charged before (Trumbull).
- At a later session the court charged Hannah Lyman of Northampton with "wearing silk in a flaunting manner, in an offensive way and garb, not only before but when she stood presented." She had worn the silk to the court where she was on trial. The court fined her 10 shillings. She was about 16, and her father, Richard Lyman, was dead (Trumbull).
- *American Heritage* counts her among 36 young women of Northampton charged that year, and *Albion's Seed* repeats the count (research bank). Trumbull gives the numbers above. Atlas Obscura places the case in Connecticut, which is wrong: Northampton was in Massachusetts.
- In March 1676 the county court also fined 45 people from Springfield, Westfield, Hadley and Hatfield for silk and long hair: 11 married women, 15 unmarried women and 19 young men (Trumbull).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hannah-lyman-1676" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors in a Puritan closet" -->
- Puritan New Englanders wore colors they called "sad," meaning dark or muted. A list from 1638 names liver color, tawny, russet, purple, French green, orange and "deer colour." Black cloth was worn by ministers, elders and the governing families. Governor John Winthrop's portrait of about 1629 shows him in black velvet (David Hackett Fischer, *Albion's Seed*, 1989).
- Winthrop's niece Mary Downing was reprimanded for wearing a little lace (Fischer).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Mary Ring
> **Who:** a widow of Plymouth Colony whose estate list records every piece of her clothing by color. · **When and where:** Leiden in Holland, then Plymouth, 1629 to 1631.
- Mary Ring was the widow of William Ring, who died in Leiden, Holland, in the 1620s. She came to Plymouth in 1629 or 1630 with her children Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. She died on July 15 or 19, 1631, and her will was proved on October 28, 1633 (Caleb Johnson, notes to the inventory, Plymouth Colony Archive Project).
- When a colonist died, neighbors listed and valued everything the person owned. This list is called a probate inventory. Mary Ring's inventory lists a red petticoat worth 16 shillings, a violet petticoat, two violet waistcoats, a "mingled coloured" waistcoat, pieces of blue cloth, blue stockings and three blue aprons, a black kirtle (a long dress), a piece of grey kersey (a rough wool), a murrey (dark purplish red) apron, white Irish stockings, six ruffs, seven smocks, a hat, and two pairs of shoes. Her will also left green cloth "to make a coat" (Plymouth Colony Archive Project, "Probate Inventory for Estate of Mary Ring, 1633").
- She could write, owned books, and was owed money by Governor William Bradford and by Edward Winslow (Plymouth Colony Archive Project).
- The English built Plymouth in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. Before the English arrived, a sickness had killed most of Patuxet's people, and the few who lived joined other Wampanoag towns (Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Patuxet").
- Shared with: `home-family` (lead: slug `mary-ring`, her household goods). This chapter has her clothes and their colors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-ring-styles" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blankets taken as tribute in New Mexico" -->
- After 1598 the Spanish governors of New Mexico gave colonists called encomenderos the right to collect tribute from Pueblo households. By the 1660s each household owed one fanega (a Spanish measure of grain) of corn in the spring and one woven cotton blanket, called a manta, or one hide in the fall. The Spanish king allowed no more than 35 encomenderos (research bank: Beyond Origins of NM Families, a genealogy research site summarizing published studies).
- Ginés de Herrera Horta, a Spanish legal officer in Oñate's colony, testified in 1601 that soldiers demanded a blanket each year from each household, and that when people said they had none, "the said soldiers took them from the women, who were left naked" (unconfirmed: search summary only, New Mexico Historical Review).
- Pueblo leaders drove the Spanish out of New Mexico in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. The labor, ground corn and cloth the colonists had taken as tribute were among the causes (Wikipedia, "Pueblo Revolt").

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, rich men in the English colonies shaved their heads and wore wigs. A traveler in Maryland wrote that most other men wore caps. Enslavers gave enslaved people coarse cloth, and in 1740 the South Carolina assembly made it the law. Its members listed the only fabrics enslaved people were allowed to wear and let any constable or other person take away finer clothes. In the same years South Carolina planters began growing indigo, the plant that gives blue dye, and enslaved people did the work of turning the leaves into dye.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs and caps" -->
- A wig, called a peruke, was built from rows of hair sewn onto a cap of net and ribbon. Wigmakers used human hair and hair from horses, cows and goats. Men who wore wigs had their heads shaved so the wig would fit (Thomas K. Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*, Colonial Williamsburg, 1958).
- A traveler in Maryland in the 1740s wrote that only a few men of "the very elevated Sort" wore wigs. Ordinary men wore caps of wool or yarn, and better-off men wore caps of white linen or cotton, even on 50-mile journeys (Ford, quoting the traveler).
- Some rich planters, including Councillor Robert Carter of Virginia, had enslaved men trained to shave them and dress their hair. Some enslaved coachmen and house servants of rich planters were dressed in white wigs as part of their masters' display (Ford).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1740 clothing law in South Carolina" -->
- The South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act on May 10, 1740, after the Stono Rebellion of 1739. Section 40 began: "whereas, many of the slaves in this Province wear clothes much above the condition of slaves, for the procuring whereof they use sinister and evil methods." It barred any owner from letting an enslaved person "have or wear any sort of apparel whatsoever, finer, other, or greater value than Negro cloth, duffels, kerseys, osnabrigs, blue linen, check linen or coarse garlix, or calicoes, checked cottons, or Scotch plaids." Servants in livery (a uniform chosen by the master) were exempt (Negro Act of 1740, section XL, in David J. McCord, ed., *Statutes at Large of South Carolina*, vol. 7, 1840, p. 397).
- The fabrics named were rough and cheap. "Negro cloth" was coarse cloth made for enslaved people's clothes. Osnaburg was a coarse cloth first woven from flax in the German city of Osnabrück. Kersey was a thick, coarse wool, and duffel a heavy wool used for blankets and coats (research bank: Wikipedia, "Negro cloth", "Osnaburg", "Kersey", "Duffel").
- The law told "all and every constable and other persons" to seize finer clothes from any enslaved person wearing them and to keep the clothes "to his or their own use." An owner who objected could take the matter to a justice of the peace (Negro Act, section XL).
- An earlier South Carolina act of 1735 had already named cheap cloth for enslaved people's clothing (research bank: Wikipedia, "Negro cloth").

### Jack
> **Who:** a young man taken from Gambia, enslaved in Virginia, who ran away within weeks of his sale. · **When and where:** Hanover County, Virginia, 1745.
- The only record of Jack is an advertisement his owner, Margaret Arbuthnott of Hanover County, placed in the *Virginia Gazette* in October 1745 to get him back (*Virginia Gazette*, Williamsburg, October 3 to 10, 1745, transcribed by the Geography of Slavery in Virginia project, University of Virginia).
- Slave traders had brought Jack and another man from Gambia, in West Africa, on the ship *Ranger* and sold them at Newcastle, Virginia, on September 5, 1745. Neither man spoke English. Both were nearly 6 feet tall. Arbuthnott described Jack as having a scar over his right eyebrow. The other man, whose name the advertisement does not give, had three small lines cut on each side of his face.
- They ran away wearing the clothes Arbuthnott had given them: new jackets and breeches of napped cotton "without either Buttons or Button-holes," new shirts of osnaburg, and new felt hats. Arbuthnott accused them of taking a damask tablecloth, fine Scottish linen, checked cloth, a white linen shirt and a silk handkerchief.
- The record does not say whether Jack was caught.
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (runaway advertisements as a record of resistance). This chapter has what enslavers dressed him in.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-gambia-1745" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo blue, 1739 to 1750" -->
- Indigo seeds came to South Carolina with the first English settlers in 1670. From 1739 Eliza Lucas tested indigo on her father's plantation on Wappoo Creek near Charleston, and in 1744 her workers made a successful crop of dye. She was one of several planters doing this work (Charleston County Public Library, "Indigo in the Fabric of Early South Carolina").
- South Carolina's lawmakers paid planters a bounty (a reward from the government) of 1 shilling for each pound of indigo in 1744. The British Parliament set a bounty of 6 pence a pound in 1748, starting in 1749. Planters began shipping large amounts of indigo to England in 1747 (Charleston County Public Library). Exports rose from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 to 1746 to about 130,000 pounds by 1748 (`economy` research bank).
- Enslaved people of African descent did almost all the work: planting, weeding and cutting the plants, building the vats, and making the dye. They soaked cut leaves in vats of water, beat the liquid with paddles or bottomless buckets to change its chemistry, added lime so the blue settled out, then scooped the blue mud into bags, dried it, and cut it into cubes. Charles Woodmason wrote in 1755 that 15 enslaved workers could grow 50 acres of indigo and that 25 skilled workers were needed to turn that crop into dye (Charleston County Public Library).
- Almost all of South Carolina's indigo went to England. South Carolinians bought most of their cloth from England already dyed (Charleston County Public Library).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1765 to 1776, colonists who opposed British taxes refused to buy British cloth. They wore cloth spun and woven in America to show that they backed the boycott. Women held public spinning meetings, and Harvard's graduating class of 1768 voted not to wear imported British clothes. In Spanish New Orleans in 1786, the governor ordered free women of color to stop wearing feathers and jewels in their hair and to cover it with a cloth. In 1789 George Washington took the oath as the first president in a suit of brown wool woven in Connecticut. Furniture makers in the new country copied a style of straight lines, thin legs and inlaid eagles now called the Federal style.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homespun as a protest, 1765 to 1776" -->
- Homespun means cloth spun and woven at home or in America instead of bought from Britain. During the boycotts of British goods in the 1760s, Boston merchants reopened spinning schools. In Hartford, a society offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of wool cloth in a year. Newspapers printed reports of spinning meetings from Massachusetts to Rhode Island (Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, *The Age of Homespun*, 2001, excerpt published by the Museum of the American Revolution).
- In August 1769, women met at their minister's house in Brookfield, Massachusetts, at 5 in the morning and spun until 7 at night. The minister reported that the best young spinner at the linen wheel spun 70 knots of yarn. One married woman did her morning housework and made cheese, rode more than 2 miles carrying her own spinning wheel, spun 53 knots from 9 to 7, and then went home to milk (Ulrich).
- In the autumn of 1767, Harvard's senior class, in protest against the Townshend taxes, voted not to drink tea or wear imported British clothes at their commencement. The printed program of the 1768 commencement was on paper made in New England. The next year's seniors did the same (Colonial Society of Massachusetts, "Harvard College on the Eve of the American Revolution").

### Charity Clarke
> **Who:** a young New Yorker who wrote to her cousin in London that American women would spin and weave their own clothes rather than buy British ones. · **When and where:** her father's Chelsea farm, Manhattan, 1768 to 1774.
- Charity Clarke was born in New York on June 28, 1747, and grew up on her father's estate, called Chelsea, in Manhattan. Between 1768 and 1774 she wrote letters to her cousin Joseph Jekyll, a lawyer in London. Columbia University holds the letters (Alan J. Singer, *New York Almanack*, July 3, 2026).
- In a letter of June 1769, when she was 21, she wrote that if the English would not give Americans the liberty they asked for, she would gather "a fighting army of Amazons ... armed with spinning wheels." The women would "learn to weave and keep sheep" and would be "clothed with the work of our hands." She wrote that they would be "no more slave to fashion and ceremony" (Singer, quoting the letter).
- In 1774 she wrote that "this body is not clad with silken garments" (Singer).
- She married the Rev. Benjamin Moore of Trinity Church in 1778. Their son, Clement Clarke Moore, wrote the poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (Singer).
- Shared with: `marketplace` (the boycott) · `rights-movements` (women's political writing). This chapter has her plan to dress in homespun.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charity-clarke" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs in Williamsburg, 1769 to 1775" -->
- Edward Charlton was the leading wigmaker in Williamsburg, Virginia. His account book for 1769 to about 1775 shows he sold sixty brown "dress bob" wigs in one year at 43 shillings each. Many men paid him about 2 pounds 3 shillings a year to shave them and dress their wigs (Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*, from Charlton's account book). Wigs were going out of fashion in England, and Charlton retired shortly before the Revolution (Ford).
- A Petersburg man advertised that he would pay one shilling an ounce for human hair of any color to sell to wigmakers (Ford).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo's peak and end" -->
- South Carolina shipped more than one million pounds of dried indigo cakes to England in 1775, made by enslaved workers. The British bounty ended in 1777. Some planters grew indigo again after the war, but by the 1790s planters had stopped, and many turned to cotton (Charleston County Public Library, "Indigo in the Fabric of Early South Carolina").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tignon order in New Orleans, 1786" -->
- New Orleans was a Spanish colony in 1786. On June 2, 1786, Governor Esteban Rodríguez Miró issued a proclamation called the *bando de buen gobierno* (order of good government). One clause said that free women of African descent could "no longer have feathers nor jewelry in their hair." They had to wear their hair plain or covered with a *pañuelo*, a handkerchief or head cloth, the kind of head covering enslaved women wore. The cloth was later called a tignon (Wikipedia, "Tignon law", citing the proclamation and historian Virginia Meacham Gould).
- Gould writes that Miró aimed the rule at women who dressed "too elegantly" or "competed too freely with white women for status." She found no written evidence that officials enforced it or punished anyone under it (Gould, via Wikipedia).
- Portraits from the early 1800s show free women of color in New Orleans wearing head wraps of fine, bright cloth tied in many styles (Wikipedia, "Tignon law").

### George Washington
> **Who:** the first president, who took his oath in a suit made from American wool. · **When and where:** Mount Vernon, Virginia, and New York City, January to April 1789.
- In January 1789 a writer who signed himself "a Philadelphia mechanic" urged American leaders in *The Federal Gazette* to wear clothing made in America. Washington decided to be sworn in wearing American cloth (Mount Vernon, "Recreating Washington's Inaugural Suit").
- The Hartford Woolen Manufactory in Connecticut had just begun weaving fine wool. Washington's merchant in Philadelphia failed to get the cloth, so Henry Knox, Washington's friend and the secretary of war, wrote to the Hartford factory himself. The cloth reached Alexandria, Virginia, in early April, days before Washington left for New York (Mount Vernon).
- Cavan Boa, a tailor bound to work for Washington for a set number of years (an indentured servant), is believed to have made the coat, waistcoat and breeches at Mount Vernon. Mount Vernon's costume historian Kathrin Breitt Brown calls the coat "well-made but hastily finished." Washington asked for large engraved buttons, and they were sewn on after the coat was made (Mount Vernon).
- The color was called London brown in England. Americans called it Congress brown (Mount Vernon).
- On April 8, 1789, Washington wrote: "I shall allways take a peculiar pleasure in giving every proper encouragement in my power to the manufactures of my Country." He wore the suit to take the oath in New York on April 30, 1789. The coat came to Mount Vernon's collection in 1933 (Mount Vernon).
- Washington did not wear a wig. He powdered and curled his own hair (Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Federal style, 1780 to 1820" -->
- After independence, American cabinetmakers made furniture in a style now called Federal. It had straight tapered legs, sharp edges, thin veneers of contrasting wood, and inlaid pictures of eagles, urns and bellflowers. The main wood was mahogany. Makers copied English pattern books by George Hepplewhite (1788) and Thomas Sheraton (1793). Well-known makers included John and Thomas Seymour in Boston and Duncan Phyfe in New York (Society of American Period Furniture Makers, "Federal Furniture, 1780 to 1820").
- The eagle on the Great Seal of the United States was one of the designs cabinetmakers inlaid on desks, tables and chairs (research bank: Metropolitan Museum of Art essay, search summary only, unconfirmed).
