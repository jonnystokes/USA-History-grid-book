## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, people in North America made clothes and ornaments from plants, animals and minerals near home, and from goods they got by trade. Deer hide, rabbit fur, turkey feathers, yucca and cotton were clothing. Shell from the Gulf of Mexico and copper from the Lake Superior country were ornaments. Colors came from plants and from minerals such as red ochre. No written record names a single dressmaker, weaver or wearer from these centuries. What is known comes from cloth, feathers, beads and copper that archaeologists have dug up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Feathers, fiber and cotton in the Southwest" -->
- Ancestral Puebloan people of the Four Corners made clothing from deer hide, rabbit skin and fiber from the yucca plant (Grand Canyon Conservancy, "Weavers").
- A feather blanket made in the 1200s is held at Edge of the Cedars State Park Museum in southeastern Utah. Its maker wrapped about 11,550 turkey feathers around more than 180 meters (590 feet) of cord twisted from yucca fiber. The feathers came from 4 to 10 turkeys, and the makers probably pulled them from live birds, which grow them back. Feather blankets had replaced blankets made from strips of rabbit fur in this region centuries earlier (Bill Lipe and Shannon Tushingham, Washington State University, *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*, November 2020, reported by Sci.News).
- Cotton came north by trade from Mexico. The earliest farmed cotton found in Arizona's Tonto Basin dates to about the year 250. Cotton reached the Four Corners around 600. By about 900, Ancestral Puebloan men were weaving large cloths and blankets on upright looms. Men grew the cotton, spun it and wove it (Grand Canyon Conservancy, "Weavers". American Southwest Virtual Museum, Tonto National Monument).
- The Salado people of the Tonto Basin (about 1250 to 1450) wove plain cotton cloth, cloth with open geometric patterns, belts, and a head strap for carrying loads dyed in bright colors. They wove sandals from agave and yucca fiber. By about 1300 the Tonto Basin was one of the leading cotton growing areas of the Southwest (American Southwest Virtual Museum, Tonto National Monument, National Park Service).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations themselves) · `food-farming` (cotton as a crop). This chapter has what the cloth and feathers looked like and what they were made of.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell and copper along the Mississippi, 1050 to 1400" -->
- At Cahokia, a Mississippian city across the river from today's St. Louis, people traded for copper from the Lake Superior region and for sea shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Metalworkers heated copper, hammered it while it was soft, and repeated the steps (a process called annealing). They made beads, thin plates, hair ornaments and headdress pieces, some with bird-man figures pushed out from the back. Mississippian copper plates were made between about 800 and 1600 and have been found from Florida and Georgia to Oklahoma and Wisconsin (research bank: Mississippian copper plates, Etowah plates, Wulfing cache).
- Archaeologists found a copper workshop just north of Mound 34 at Cahokia, a building about 5.5 by 4.4 meters holding raw copper nuggets, scraps of worked copper and stone tools. Gregory Perino first found signs of it in 1956, and excavators confirmed its location in 1999 (research bank: Wikipedia, "Mound 34").
- In the 1000s, people at Cahokia buried a man and a woman on a platform covered with marine-shell disc beads laid out in the shape of a bird. The count is given as 20,000 beads by the magazine *Archaeology* and as about 10,000 in other published descriptions (Wikipedia, "Mound 72", summarizing Fowler). Melvin Fowler dug the burial in Mound 72 between 1967 and 1971 and first described both central bodies as men. A team led by Thomas Emerson of the Illinois State Archaeological Survey re-examined the bones in 2016 and found a man and a woman (*Archaeology*, August 5, 2016).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (lead: Cahokia) · `elements` (copper as a metal). This chapter has copper and shell as things people wore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors from plants and earth" -->
- Red ochre is iron oxide, a mineral dug from the ground and ground into powder. Mixed with fat or another binder, it made paint. The Karankawa of the Texas coast painted symbols on faces with ochre, according to early written accounts. At the Mitchell Ridge burial site, red ochre stains the bones of 18 people (Texas Beyond History, "Ochre").
- Women across the Northeast woodlands, the Great Lakes and the northern Plains decorated clothing with porcupine quills. They flattened the quills and boiled them in plant dyes. Tamarack bark, spruce cones and berries gave red. Walnuts and wild grapes gave black. Sunflower, pine bark and willow root gave yellow. Blueberries and larkspur gave blue. The quills were sewn onto moccasins, pouches, cradleboards and clothing (Penn Museum, "The Art of Quillwork"). Quillwork was being done before Europeans arrived (McCord Museum, Quillwork scholarship). The quilled pieces in museum collections are much younger. Penn Museum names pieces George Catlin collected between 1832 and 1839 among its oldest, so the dye plants listed here were written down long after 1500.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish, French and English expeditions met Native nations whose clothes were made of deerskin, bark fiber, feathers and cotton, and whose ornaments were made of pearls, shell and copper. The newcomers wrote down what they saw. In 1540 Hernando de Soto's soldiers dug pearls out of Native graves. By 1540, glass beads from a failed Spanish colony had already reached the town of Cofitachequi. In 1598 Spanish colonists drove churro sheep into New Mexico, and wool from those sheep went into Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for about 300 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth and pearls at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
- In May 1540 Hernando de Soto's army reached Cofitachequi, a town on a river in what is now South Carolina or Georgia. A Portuguese soldier in the army, known only as the Gentleman of Elvas, described what the people there wore and kept. The woman who ruled the town arrived by canoe and gave de Soto mantles and deerskins. She took a long string of pearls from her own neck and put it around his (Elvas, *Narrative*, 1557, Hakluyt translation).
- Elvas wrote that the town's storehouses held mantles woven from yarn made of tree bark and mantles made of feathers colored white, green, red and yellow, worn in winter. They held deerskins painted with designs, and some deerskins were made into leggings, stockings and shoes. He wrote that everyone in the town wore shoes and clothes (Elvas).
- According to Elvas, the ruler told de Soto to search the town's graves for pearls. De Soto's men dug up the graves and took the pearls, along with small figures of babies and birds made from pearls. The accounts disagree on how much. Translations of Elvas give 14 arrobas, a Spanish weight of about 25 pounds each, so about 350 pounds. One English edition gives 392 pounds. Other accounts give about 200 pounds (research bank).
- De Soto then took the ruler with him as a prisoner, guarded by enslaved women. On the march she told her guards she needed to relieve herself, walked into the woods and escaped. She carried off a cane box of undrilled pearls (Elvas). No record gives her name. Spanish writers called her the Lady of Cofitachequi.
- The same town already held a dagger and glass beads from Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón's failed Spanish colony of 1526 (Elvas).
- Shared with: `health` (lead: the empty towns and the sickness that emptied them) · `native-nations` (the nation) · `exploration` (de Soto's route). This chapter has what people wore and the pearls taken from the graves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Secotan people wore, 1585" -->
- John White painted the Algonquian people of the Carolina coast in 1585, and Thomas Hariot wrote captions for the pictures. One painting shows a leader wearing a fringed apron of animal skin with a tail hanging down behind, and a quiver of arrows tied to the tail. His hair is decorated with feathers and shaved on one side. Hariot wrote that the shaving kept the hair from tangling in the bowstring (Encyclopedia Virginia, "The manner of their attire", from Hariot's *Briefe and True Report*, 1590). His body is painted for a special occasion rather than tattooed, and he wears copper beads or pearls at his neck and wrist (Encyclopedia Virginia, search result, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Shared with: `art` (lead: John White as a painter).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish clothes in St. Augustine, 1565 to 1600" -->
- Spanish soldiers and settlers founded St. Augustine in 1565 in Timucua land. Archaeologists from the Florida Museum have dug up what the colonists wore. Rich residents wore imported cloth trimmed with metal lace, buttons, beads and metal ornaments. Most people tied their plain clothes shut with laces tipped with small metal ends called aglets. Buttons were mostly worn by soldiers and the rich (Florida Museum, "Life on the Remote Frontier").
- Luxury cloth reached the town from Mexico, China and Europe. Juan de Cevadilla, the official who collected the soldiers' pay in Mexico, died in 1590. He left his widow a carved and gilded bed with a canopy of embroidered Chinese taffeta, a smooth silk cloth, and a large amount of fine cloth and clothing (Florida Museum).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Churro sheep and red from Mexico" -->
- Juan de Oñate brought churro sheep (a long-haired Spanish breed) up the road from Mexico into New Mexico in 1598. By the 1700s the churros in the Rio Grande valley numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Their wool became the base of Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for the next 300 years (National Park Service, "New Mexico Fiber Arts Traditions"). One sheep breeders' history puts Oñate's flock at close to 5,000 (research bank, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Before this, Pueblo weavers worked in cotton on upright looms. The Spanish brought a floor loom set flat, which weavers later used for Rio Grande blankets and serapes (NPS).
- Far to the south, Indigenous farmers in Oaxaca, Puebla and Tlaxcala in Mexico raised cochineal, a small insect that lives on prickly-pear cactus. Crushed, it gives a strong red dye. After the Spanish conquest, Spanish merchants traded it around the world. The work and the knowledge stayed with Indigenous Mexicans, who were never credited for it (Harvard Museums of Science and Culture, "Cochineal: Mexico's Red").
- Shared with: `food-farming` (sheep as livestock) · `migration` (lead: Oñate's column).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the English colonies of the 1600s, lawmakers in Virginia and Massachusetts wrote laws about clothing. In Massachusetts, a person worth less than 200 pounds was not allowed to wear silk hoods, gold or silver lace, or big boots, and county courts fined people who did. Many people today picture Puritan colonists dressed only in black. The lists of their belongings made when they died record red, blue, violet, grey and green clothes. In New Mexico, Spanish colonists made Pueblo households hand over woven cotton blankets or hides every year as tribute, a payment demanded from a conquered people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia taxes clothing, 1619" -->
- At its first meeting, on July 30, 1619, the General Assembly of Virginia passed a law "against excesse in apparell." The members voted that every man would pay his share of church and public costs according to his clothes. An unmarried man was assessed on his own clothes, and a married man on his own and his wife's (John Pory's record of the 1619 Assembly, Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation transcription and Online Library of Liberty).
- In 1621 Virginia's leaders resolved again to "supress excess in cloaths" and to keep men who held no office from wearing gold in their clothes (Ink Mendelsohn, "We Were What We Wore", *American Heritage*, December 1988).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts dress laws, 1634 to 1682" -->
- The General Court of Massachusetts Bay (the colony's elected lawmakers and magistrates) passed its first clothing law in 1634. It banned "new fashions, or long hair," silver and gold hatbands and belts, cloth woven with gold thread, lace, beaver hats, and sleeves with more than two slashes. A wearer caught in them had to give them up (research bank: Atlas Obscura, and the University of Colorado Denver *Historical Studies Journal* 2025, citing Shurtleff, *Records of the Governor*). A 1639 law banned more lace and short sleeves.
- In 1651 the General Court declared its "utter detestation and dislike that men or women of mean condition" should dress like gentlemen, "by the wearing of gold or silver lace, or buttons, or points at their knees, to walk in great boots," or that women of that rank should wear "silk or tiffany hoods or scarves." Anyone worth less than 200 pounds who did so owed a fine of 10 shillings for each offense. Magistrates, officers, soldiers, their families, and people who had once been rich were exempt. Town selectmen, the elected men who ran each town, were told to tax people who dressed above their rank (Shurtleff, *Records*, vol. 3, cited by the *Historical Studies Journal*. James Russell Trumbull, *History of Northampton*, vol. 1, 1898).
- County courts enforced it. In 1653 an Essex County court heard 18 cases and let 12 people go because they were worth more than 200 pounds (*Historical Studies Journal*, citing *Records and Files of the Quarterly Courts*). In Hampshire County in 1673, the court charged 7 married women of Northampton with wearing silk and acquitted all but one. The same court charged 9 married women of Springfield, 9 of Hadley, 7 of Hatfield and 3 of Westfield (Trumbull).
- In 1663 the General Court extended the law to children and servants and barred tailors from making the forbidden clothes (Trumbull).
- In 1682 the Hampshire County court charged the five towns of Northampton, Hadley, Hatfield, Westfield and Springfield for failing to tax residents who wore silk "beyond their ranks." Trumbull found no later attempt to enforce the law there.

### Hannah Lyman
> **Who:** a teenager fined for wearing a silk hood, who wore it again to court. · **When and where:** Northampton, Massachusetts, 1676.
- During King Philip's War, the Northampton minister Solomon Stoddard asked the colony's leaders to act against "that intolerable pride in clothes and hair." In November 1675 the General Court passed a law listing the colony's sins and their punishments (Trumbull, *History of Northampton*, vol. 1, 1898).
- Northampton stood on Nonotuck land. On September 24, 1653, John Pynchon of Springfield got a deed to it from seven Nonotuck men, including Chickwalloppe, for 100 fathoms of wampum (strings of shell beads) and 10 wool coats. The historian Margaret Bruchac writes that the Nonotuck signers probably understood it as an agreement to share the land, keeping their own rights to hunt, fish and plant there (Bruchac, "From Nonotuck to Northampton", Historic Northampton).
- On March 27, 1676, the Hampshire County court at Northampton charged 23 people "for wearing silk in a flaunting manner and for long hair and other extravagances." Six were married women, six unmarried women, and the rest young men. The court warned them and made each pay court fees of 2 shillings 6 pence. The wife of Samuel Holton was fined 10 shillings because she had been charged before (Trumbull).
- At a later session the court charged Hannah Lyman of Northampton with "wearing silk in a flaunting manner, in an offensive way and garb, not only before but when she stood presented." She had worn the silk to the court where she was on trial. The court fined her 10 shillings. She was about 16, and her father, Richard Lyman, was dead (Trumbull).
- *American Heritage* counts her among 36 young women of Northampton charged that year, and *Albion's Seed* repeats the count (research bank). Trumbull gives the numbers above. Atlas Obscura places the case in Connecticut, which is wrong: Northampton was in Massachusetts.
- In March 1676 the county court also fined 45 people from Springfield, Westfield, Hadley and Hatfield for silk and long hair: 11 married women, 15 unmarried women and 19 young men (Trumbull).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hannah-lyman-1676" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors in a Puritan closet" -->
- Puritan New Englanders wore colors they called "sad," meaning dark or muted. A list from 1638 names liver color, tawny, russet, purple, French green, orange and "deer colour." Black cloth was worn by ministers, elders and the governing families. Governor John Winthrop's portrait of about 1629 shows him in black velvet (David Hackett Fischer, *Albion's Seed*, 1989).
- Winthrop's niece Mary Downing was reprimanded for wearing a little lace (Fischer).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Mary Ring
> **Who:** a widow of Plymouth Colony whose estate list records every piece of her clothing by color. · **When and where:** Leiden in Holland, then Plymouth, 1629 to 1631.
- Mary Ring was the widow of William Ring, who died in Leiden, Holland, in the 1620s. She came to Plymouth in 1629 or 1630 with her children Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. She died on July 15 or 19, 1631, and her will was proved on October 28, 1633 (Caleb Johnson, notes to the inventory, Plymouth Colony Archive Project).
- When a colonist died, neighbors listed and valued everything the person owned. This list is called a probate inventory. Mary Ring's inventory lists a red petticoat worth 16 shillings, a violet petticoat, two violet waistcoats, a "mingled coloured" waistcoat, pieces of blue cloth, blue stockings and three blue aprons, a black kirtle (a long dress), a piece of grey kersey (a rough wool), a murrey (dark purplish red) apron, white Irish stockings, six ruffs, seven smocks, a hat, and two pairs of shoes. Her will also left green cloth "to make a coat" (Plymouth Colony Archive Project, "Probate Inventory for Estate of Mary Ring, 1633").
- She could write, owned books, and was owed money by Governor William Bradford and by Edward Winslow (Plymouth Colony Archive Project).
- The English built Plymouth in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. Before the English arrived, a sickness had killed most of Patuxet's people, and the few who lived joined other Wampanoag towns (Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Patuxet").
- Shared with: `home-family` (lead: slug `mary-ring`, her household goods). This chapter has her clothes and their colors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-ring-styles" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blankets taken as tribute in New Mexico" -->
- After 1598 the Spanish governors of New Mexico gave colonists called encomenderos the right to collect tribute from Pueblo households. By the 1660s each household owed one fanega (a Spanish measure of grain) of corn in the spring and one woven cotton blanket, called a manta, or one hide in the fall. The Spanish king allowed no more than 35 encomenderos (research bank: Beyond Origins of NM Families, a genealogy research site summarizing published studies).
- Ginés de Herrera Horta, a Spanish legal officer in Oñate's colony, testified in 1601 that soldiers demanded a blanket each year from each household, and that when people said they had none, "the said soldiers took them from the women, who were left naked" (unconfirmed: search summary only, New Mexico Historical Review).
- Pueblo leaders drove the Spanish out of New Mexico in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. The labor, ground corn and cloth the colonists had taken as tribute were among the causes (Wikipedia, "Pueblo Revolt").

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, rich men in the English colonies shaved their heads and wore wigs. A traveler in Maryland wrote that most other men wore caps. Enslavers gave enslaved people coarse cloth, and in 1740 the South Carolina assembly made it the law. Its members listed the only fabrics enslaved people were allowed to wear and let any constable or other person take away finer clothes. In the same years South Carolina planters began growing indigo, the plant that gives blue dye, and enslaved people did the work of turning the leaves into dye.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs and caps" -->
- A wig, called a peruke, was built from rows of hair sewn onto a cap of net and ribbon. Wigmakers used human hair and hair from horses, cows and goats. Men who wore wigs had their heads shaved so the wig would fit (Thomas K. Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*, Colonial Williamsburg, 1958).
- A traveler in Maryland in the 1740s wrote that only a few men of "the very elevated Sort" wore wigs. Ordinary men wore caps of wool or yarn, and better-off men wore caps of white linen or cotton, even on 50-mile journeys (Ford, quoting the traveler).
- Some rich planters, including Councillor Robert Carter of Virginia, had enslaved men trained to shave them and dress their hair. Some enslaved coachmen and house servants of rich planters were dressed in white wigs as part of their masters' display (Ford).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1740 clothing law in South Carolina" -->
- The South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act on May 10, 1740, after the Stono Rebellion of 1739. Section 40 began: "whereas, many of the slaves in this Province wear clothes much above the condition of slaves, for the procuring whereof they use sinister and evil methods." It barred any owner from letting an enslaved person "have or wear any sort of apparel whatsoever, finer, other, or greater value than Negro cloth, duffels, kerseys, osnabrigs, blue linen, check linen or coarse garlix, or calicoes, checked cottons, or Scotch plaids." Servants in livery (a uniform chosen by the master) were exempt (Negro Act of 1740, section XL, in David J. McCord, ed., *Statutes at Large of South Carolina*, vol. 7, 1840, p. 397).
- The fabrics named were rough and cheap. "Negro cloth" was coarse cloth made for enslaved people's clothes. Osnaburg was a coarse cloth first woven from flax in the German city of Osnabrück. Kersey was a thick, coarse wool, and duffel a heavy wool used for blankets and coats (research bank: Wikipedia, "Negro cloth", "Osnaburg", "Kersey", "Duffel").
- The law told "all and every constable and other persons" to seize finer clothes from any enslaved person wearing them and to keep the clothes "to his or their own use." An owner who objected could take the matter to a justice of the peace (Negro Act, section XL).
- An earlier South Carolina act of 1735 had already named cheap cloth for enslaved people's clothing (research bank: Wikipedia, "Negro cloth").

### Jack
> **Who:** a young man taken from Gambia, enslaved in Virginia, who ran away within weeks of his sale. · **When and where:** Hanover County, Virginia, 1745.
- The only record of Jack is an advertisement his owner, Margaret Arbuthnott of Hanover County, placed in the *Virginia Gazette* in October 1745 to get him back (*Virginia Gazette*, Williamsburg, October 3 to 10, 1745, transcribed by the Geography of Slavery in Virginia project, University of Virginia).
- Slave traders had brought Jack and another man from Gambia, in West Africa, on the ship *Ranger* and sold them at Newcastle, Virginia, on September 5, 1745. Neither man spoke English. Both were nearly 6 feet tall. Arbuthnott described Jack as having a scar over his right eyebrow. The other man, whose name the advertisement does not give, had three small lines cut on each side of his face.
- They ran away wearing the clothes Arbuthnott had given them: new jackets and breeches of napped cotton "without either Buttons or Button-holes," new shirts of osnaburg, and new felt hats. Arbuthnott accused them of taking a damask tablecloth, fine Scottish linen, checked cloth, a white linen shirt and a silk handkerchief.
- The record does not say whether Jack was caught.
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (runaway advertisements as a record of resistance). This chapter has what enslavers dressed him in.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-gambia-1745" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo blue, 1739 to 1750" -->
- Indigo seeds came to South Carolina with the first English settlers in 1670. From 1739 Eliza Lucas tested indigo on her father's plantation on Wappoo Creek near Charleston, and in 1744 her workers made a successful crop of dye. She was one of several planters doing this work (Charleston County Public Library, "Indigo in the Fabric of Early South Carolina").
- South Carolina's lawmakers paid planters a bounty (a reward from the government) of 1 shilling for each pound of indigo in 1744. The British Parliament set a bounty of 6 pence a pound in 1748, starting in 1749. Planters began shipping large amounts of indigo to England in 1747 (Charleston County Public Library). Exports rose from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 to 1746 to about 130,000 pounds by 1748 (`economy` research bank).
- Enslaved people of African descent did almost all the work: planting, weeding and cutting the plants, building the vats, and making the dye. They soaked cut leaves in vats of water, beat the liquid with paddles or bottomless buckets to change its chemistry, added lime so the blue settled out, then scooped the blue mud into bags, dried it, and cut it into cubes. Charles Woodmason wrote in 1755 that 15 enslaved workers could grow 50 acres of indigo and that 25 skilled workers were needed to turn that crop into dye (Charleston County Public Library).
- Almost all of South Carolina's indigo went to England. South Carolinians bought most of their cloth from England already dyed (Charleston County Public Library).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1765 to 1776, colonists who opposed British taxes refused to buy British cloth. They wore cloth spun and woven in America to show that they backed the boycott. Women held public spinning meetings, and Harvard's graduating class of 1768 voted not to wear imported British clothes. In Spanish New Orleans in 1786, the governor ordered free women of color to stop wearing feathers and jewels in their hair and to cover it with a cloth. In 1789 George Washington took the oath as the first president in a suit of brown wool woven in Connecticut. Furniture makers in the new country copied a style of straight lines, thin legs and inlaid eagles now called the Federal style.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homespun as a protest, 1765 to 1776" -->
- Homespun means cloth spun and woven at home or in America instead of bought from Britain. During the boycotts of British goods in the 1760s, Boston merchants reopened spinning schools. In Hartford, a society offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of wool cloth in a year. Newspapers printed reports of spinning meetings from Massachusetts to Rhode Island (Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, *The Age of Homespun*, 2001, excerpt published by the Museum of the American Revolution).
- In August 1769, women met at their minister's house in Brookfield, Massachusetts, at 5 in the morning and spun until 7 at night. The minister reported that the best young spinner at the linen wheel spun 70 knots of yarn. One married woman did her morning housework and made cheese, rode more than 2 miles carrying her own spinning wheel, spun 53 knots from 9 to 7, and then went home to milk (Ulrich).
- In the autumn of 1767, Harvard's senior class, in protest against the Townshend taxes, voted not to drink tea or wear imported British clothes at their commencement. The printed program of the 1768 commencement was on paper made in New England. The next year's seniors did the same (Colonial Society of Massachusetts, "Harvard College on the Eve of the American Revolution").

### Charity Clarke
> **Who:** a young New Yorker who wrote to her cousin in London that American women would spin and weave their own clothes rather than buy British ones. · **When and where:** her father's Chelsea farm, Manhattan, 1768 to 1774.
- Charity Clarke was born in New York on June 28, 1747, and grew up on her father's estate, called Chelsea, in Manhattan. Between 1768 and 1774 she wrote letters to her cousin Joseph Jekyll, a lawyer in London. Columbia University holds the letters (Alan J. Singer, *New York Almanack*, July 3, 2026).
- In a letter of June 1769, when she was 21, she wrote that if the English would not give Americans the liberty they asked for, she would gather "a fighting army of Amazons ... armed with spinning wheels." The women would "learn to weave and keep sheep" and would be "clothed with the work of our hands." She wrote that they would be "no more slave to fashion and ceremony" (Singer, quoting the letter).
- In 1774 she wrote that "this body is not clad with silken garments" (Singer).
- She married the Rev. Benjamin Moore of Trinity Church in 1778. Their son, Clement Clarke Moore, wrote the poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (Singer).
- Shared with: `marketplace` (the boycott) · `rights-movements` (women's political writing). This chapter has her plan to dress in homespun.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs in Williamsburg, 1769 to 1775" -->
- Edward Charlton was the leading wigmaker in Williamsburg, Virginia. His account book for 1769 to about 1775 shows he sold sixty brown "dress bob" wigs in one year at 43 shillings each. Many men paid him about 2 pounds 3 shillings a year to shave them and dress their wigs (Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*, from Charlton's account book). Wigs were going out of fashion in England, and Charlton retired shortly before the Revolution (Ford).
- A Petersburg man advertised that he would pay one shilling an ounce for human hair of any color to sell to wigmakers (Ford).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo's peak and end" -->
- South Carolina shipped more than one million pounds of dried indigo cakes to England in 1775, made by enslaved workers. The British bounty ended in 1777. Some planters grew indigo again after the war, but by the 1790s planters had stopped, and many turned to cotton (Charleston County Public Library, "Indigo in the Fabric of Early South Carolina").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tignon order in New Orleans, 1786" -->
- New Orleans was a Spanish colony in 1786. On June 2, 1786, Governor Esteban Rodríguez Miró issued a proclamation called the *bando de buen gobierno* (order of good government). One clause said that free women of African descent could "no longer have feathers nor jewelry in their hair." They had to wear their hair plain or covered with a *pañuelo*, a handkerchief or head cloth, the kind of head covering enslaved women wore. The cloth was later called a tignon (Wikipedia, "Tignon law", citing the proclamation and historian Virginia Meacham Gould).
- Gould writes that Miró aimed the rule at women who dressed "too elegantly" or "competed too freely with white women for status." She found no written evidence that officials enforced it or punished anyone under it (Gould, via Wikipedia).
- Portraits from the early 1800s show free women of color in New Orleans wearing head wraps of fine, bright cloth tied in many styles (Wikipedia, "Tignon law").

### George Washington
> **Who:** the first president, who took his oath in a suit made from American wool. · **When and where:** Mount Vernon, Virginia, and New York City, January to April 1789.
- In January 1789 a writer who signed himself "a Philadelphia mechanic" urged American leaders in *The Federal Gazette* to wear clothing made in America. Washington decided to be sworn in wearing American cloth (Mount Vernon, "Recreating Washington's Inaugural Suit").
- The Hartford Woolen Manufactory in Connecticut had just begun weaving fine wool. Washington's merchant in Philadelphia failed to get the cloth, so Henry Knox, Washington's friend and the secretary of war, wrote to the Hartford factory himself. The cloth reached Alexandria, Virginia, in early April, days before Washington left for New York (Mount Vernon).
- Cavan Boa, a tailor bound to work for Washington for a set number of years (an indentured servant), is believed to have made the coat, waistcoat and breeches at Mount Vernon. Mount Vernon's costume historian Kathrin Breitt Brown calls the coat "well-made but hastily finished." Washington asked for large engraved buttons, and they were sewn on after the coat was made (Mount Vernon).
- The color was called London brown in England. Americans called it Congress brown (Mount Vernon).
- On April 8, 1789, Washington wrote: "I shall allways take a peculiar pleasure in giving every proper encouragement in my power to the manufactures of my Country." He wore the suit to take the oath in New York on April 30, 1789. The coat came to Mount Vernon's collection in 1933 (Mount Vernon).
- Washington did not wear a wig. He powdered and curled his own hair (Ford, *The Wigmaker in Eighteenth-Century Williamsburg*).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, the cotton that American mills spun came from Southern plantations, where enslaved people grew and picked it. Mills in New England spun and wove that cotton into cloth. Some mills in Rhode Island and Lowell, Massachusetts, also wove a rough cheap cloth that planters bought to dress the people they enslaved. Fashionable women wore high-waisted white cotton dresses in the 1800s, then tight corsets and wider skirts held out by layers of petticoats. Men gave up knee breeches and powdered hair for long trousers and short hair. Most clothes were still sewn at home or by a tailor. The first shops that sold clothes already made stood near the docks and sold to sailors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What fashionable people wore, 1800 to 1850" -->
- In the 1800s the fashionable dress for women was high-waisted, with the waistline just under the bust, made of thin white cotton called muslin. White dresses were hard and costly to keep clean. Printed cottons and patterned silks were worn every day, and wool in cold weather (Harper Franklin, "1800-1809", Fashion History Timeline, Fashion Institute of Technology).
- Men in the same years stopped powdering their hair and cut it short. Plain dark wool coats replaced the embroidered silk of the 1700s. Knee breeches were still worn, and so were long tight pants called pantaloons (Franklin, "1800-1809").
- In the early 1830s women's sleeves puffed out wide from the shoulder, a shape called the gigot or leg-of-mutton sleeve. Skirts grew wide and women wore more and more petticoats under them, with a long corset laced tight underneath (Franklin, "1830-1839", Fashion Institute of Technology).
- In the 1840s a fashionable woman's corset ran from her breasts to her hips, stiffened with cords and whalebone and a flat piece of steel, wood or whalebone down the front, so she could not bend at the waist. Sleeves were cut so tight that she often could not lift her arms higher than her shoulders. By the end of the decade some women wore six or seven petticoats. Men wore long frock coats, narrow trousers and bright patterned silk vests (Franklin, "1840-1849", Fashion Institute of Technology).
- From 1830 *Godey's Lady's Book*, a Philadelphia magazine, opened each issue with a fashion plate, a picture of the latest dresses colored by hand. Its publisher, Louis Godey, said coloring the plates cost $8,000 in 1859. Sarah Josepha Hale edited it from 1837 to 1877. By 1860 it had about 150,000 subscribers (Wikipedia, "Godey's Lady's Book"). Shared with: `holidays` (lead: Sarah Josepha Hale's story) · `news-communication` (magazines).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton picked in the South, woven in the North" -->
- New England mill owners and mill workers depended on cotton grown in the South. Their demand for raw cotton pushed planters to grow more of it and to enslave more people to do the work (National Park Service, "Lowell's Southern Connection"). Charles Sumner, a senator from Massachusetts, called the tie between the planters and the New England mill owners an "unholy union ... between the cotton planters and fleshmongers of Louisiana and Mississippi and the cotton spinners and traffickers of New England" (NPS).
- The Merrimack Company in Lowell printed calico, cotton cloth printed with colored patterns. It hired skilled printers from England. The head printer it hired in 1825 was paid more than the company's treasurer (NPS, "Products of the Mills").
- Solomon Northup was a free Black man from New York. Kidnappers seized him in Washington in 1841 and sold him into slavery in Louisiana, where he was held until January 1853. He described cotton picking on Edwin Epps's plantation on Bayou Boeuf. Pickers had to be in the field as soon as it was light and stayed until it was too dark to see, with 10 or 15 minutes at noon to eat cold bacon. At night each picker's basket was weighed. An ordinary day's work was 200 pounds. Anyone who brought in less was whipped, a punishment Northup counted in lashes, each lash one blow of the whip on the bare body (Solomon Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*, 1853).
- Northup wrote that a woman named Patsey was the fastest picker on Bayou Boeuf and often picked 500 pounds a day. Epps set each picker's daily amount by what that person could pick. Northup wrote that Patsey "would surely have been beaten if she failed to produce twice as much" as the 200 pounds he was held to (Northup).

<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth made for enslaved people's clothes" -->
- Planters called the rough fabric they bought for enslaved people's clothes "negro cloth" or "slave cloth." The names covered several coarse fabrics: osnaburg (a rough unbleached cotton or linen), linsey-woolsey (a cheap mix of wool and cotton or linen), kersey (a thick wool) and jeans (a cotton and wool twill) (Madelyn Shaw, "Slave Cloth and Clothing Slaves", *MESDA Journal*, 2012).
- The Hazard family's woolen mill in Peace Dale, Rhode Island, made negro cloth for Southern planters. In the 1820s Isaac Peace Hazard traveled the South taking orders from plantation owners. By 1845, 17 of Rhode Island's 40 textile mills specialized in negro cloth. Lowell's mills made it too (Shaw. Rhode Island Historical Society, "Slave Cloth". NPS, "Lowell's Southern Connection").
- Southern buyers told the Hazards what they wanted. One wrote in 1824 that the cloth "would not suit servants unless it was blue." Enslaved people showed a visitor clothes "as thin as baize, threads not beat close together, others split all to pieces" (Shaw, quoting the Hazard papers).
- Enslavers gave out clothing on set days, once a year on some plantations and in a summer share and a winter share on others (Douglass. Shaw). On Stephen Duncan's Carlisle plantation in Mississippi in the 1850s, each man got enough cloth for three shirts, two pairs of summer pants and one pair of winter pants, plus a coat and two pairs of shoes. Each child got four short loose dresses called slips. John Potter bought 1,800 yards of cloth a year for the 400 people enslaved on his South Carolina rice plantation, about 4.5 yards each (Shaw).
- Harriet Jacobs was enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in the household of Dr. James Norcom, whom her book calls Dr. Flint. She wrote: "I have a vivid recollection of the linsey-woolsey dress given me every winter by Mrs. Flint. How I hated it! It was one of the badges of slavery." Her grandmother's work paid for the rest of her clothes (Harriet Jacobs, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl*, 1861. Wikipedia, "Harriet Jacobs").
- Slave traders dressed people in new clothes before selling them. In the New Orleans slave pen of Theophilus Freeman in 1841, Northup wrote, the traders made the captives wash and the men shave, then gave each person "a new suit each, cheap, but clean." Men got a hat, coat, shirt, pants and shoes. Women got calico dresses and handkerchiefs to tie around their heads (Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave*).

### Frederick Douglass
> **Who:** a man enslaved in Maryland who described the clothes he was given as a child and escaped to freedom dressed as a sailor. · **When and where:** Talbot County and Baltimore, Maryland, 1820s to 1838.
- Frederick Douglass was born enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland. As a child he lived on the home plantation of Colonel Edward Lloyd, where enslaved people from Lloyd's other farms came for their food and their yearly clothing (Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*, 1845).
- Each enslaved adult got two coarse linen shirts, one pair of linen trousers, one jacket, one pair of winter trousers "made of coarse negro cloth," one pair of stockings and one pair of shoes a year. Douglass wrote that all of it "could not have cost more than seven dollars." Children too young for the fields got only two coarse linen shirts a year. "When these failed them, they went naked until the next allowance-day" (Douglass, *Narrative*).
- Douglass wrote that as a boy he wore "nothing on but a coarse tow linen shirt, reaching only to my knees," summer and winter, with no shoes, stockings, jacket or trousers. On the coldest nights he slept inside a corn sack on the clay floor. His feet cracked open from the frost (Douglass, *Narrative*).
- When he was about seven or eight, his owners sent him to Baltimore. Lucretia Auld, the daughter of his old master, told him he could have a pair of trousers if he scrubbed the dirt off his skin first. He wrote: "The thought of owning a pair of trousers was great indeed!" (Douglass, *Narrative*).
- On September 3, 1838, Douglass escaped from Baltimore by train. He wrote: "I had on a red shirt and a tarpaulin hat, and a black cravat tied in sailor fashion carelessly and loosely about my neck." He carried a friend's sailor's protection, a paper that described a free American sailor, in place of free papers (Frederick Douglass, "My Escape from Slavery", *The Century Magazine*, November 1881). A tarpaulin hat was a sailor's waterproofed hat.
- Anna Murray, a free Black woman in Baltimore who later married him, helped. Accounts differ on the clothes. Some say she sewed the sailor's outfit. Others say she got it through her work as a laundress. She also gave him part of her savings and sold a feather bed to add to it (Wikipedia, "Anna Murray Douglass", citing *The Frederick Douglass Encyclopedia*, 2010. Search summaries of Britannica and Smithsonian say she sewed it, unconfirmed: search summary only).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first ready-made clothes" -->
- The first clothes sold ready to wear in America were sold at "slop shops" on the streets next to the wharves of Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore and smaller whaling and fishing towns in the early 1800s. Their buyers were sailors (Sofi Thanhauser, *Worn: A People's History of Clothing*, 2022, excerpt in *Literary Hub*).
- Between 1840 and 1860 making cheap clothes for enslaved people grew into its own industry. Planters who bought ready-made clothes did not have to take enslaved women away from other work to sew them (Thanhauser).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1850 to 1900, machines and chemistry changed what Americans wore and what colors they could buy. Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851, and from 1856 his partner Edward Clark let families pay for one a few dollars a month. In 1856 an English chemistry student made the first man-made dye to be sold, a purple, from chemicals in coal tar. Chemists soon made other new colors. In 1851 a few women's rights leaders wore short skirts over trousers and were mocked for it. In 1873 a Reno tailor and a San Francisco merchant got a patent for work pants with metal rivets, the pants later called blue jeans. Other Americans used clothes and hair to push people into line. San Francisco's leaders passed a law in 1876 that let the sheriff cut off Chinese prisoners' long braids, and the staff at Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania cut Native children's hair and took their clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bloomer costume, 1851 to 1854" -->
- In February 1851, Elizabeth Smith Miller wore a short dress over loose trousers gathered at the ankle, sometimes called the Turkish dress, when she visited Amelia Bloomer in Seneca Falls, New York. Bloomer adopted it and printed articles and pictures of it in her newspaper, *The Lily*. Elizabeth Cady Stanton wore it too. People named it the Bloomer after her (Wikipedia, "Bloomers (clothing)". NPS, Women's Rights National Historical Park, "Amelia Bloomer").
- Bloomer wrote that "letters came pouring in upon me by the hundreds from women all over the country" asking about the dress. The number of copies of *The Lily* sent out each month rose from 500 to 4,000, by NPS's count, or to 3,000 by another count (NPS. Wikipedia).
- Men mocked women who wore it and called them "Amazons." Some churches refused women who wore it as members. By about 1854 Stanton and other women's rights leaders had gone back to long skirts and corsets, because they felt the mockery took attention away from their fight for the vote (Wikipedia, "Bloomers (clothing)").
- Shared with: `rights-movements` (lead: Stanton, Bloomer and the fight for the vote). This chapter has the clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoops, sewing machines and paper patterns" -->
- In 1856 the cage crinoline came into use, a frame of steel hoops that held a skirt out wide. Before it, women wore up to seven petticoats to get the same shape (Fashion Institute of Technology, Fashion History Timeline, "1850-1859").
- Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851, using the lockstitch Elias Howe had invented. Sewing a man's frock coat took almost 17 hours by hand and 2 and a half by machine (FIT, "1850-1859").
- A Singer machine cost about $100 or more, when a family might earn about $500 a year. In September 1856 Edward Clark, Singer's partner, began selling machines on installments, a little each month. Accounts of the terms differ: $5 a month (Peter Lyon, "Isaac Singer and His Wonderful Sewing Machine", *American Heritage*, 1958) or $5 down and $3 a month (search summaries, unconfirmed: search summary only). Clark also took in old machines of other makes as part payment (Lyon).
- In 1863 Ebenezer Butterick and his wife, Ellen Augusta Pollard Butterick, of Sterling, Massachusetts, began selling sewing patterns cut from tissue paper in a range of standard sizes. Before that, a pattern came in one size. Family members cut and folded the first ones at home. They sold patterns for men's and boys' clothes first and added women's dresses in 1866 (Wikipedia, "Ebenezer Butterick").

### Elizabeth Keckley
> **Who:** a dressmaker, born enslaved, who bought her freedom with her sewing and made Mary Todd Lincoln's dresses. · **When and where:** Virginia, St. Louis and Washington, D.C., 1818 to 1868.
- Elizabeth Keckley was born enslaved in February 1818 at Dinwiddie Court House, Virginia. Her mother, Agnes, made clothes for 82 people: 12 members of the Burwell family who enslaved them and the 70 people the Burwells enslaved. Keckley learned sewing by helping her (Wikipedia, "Elizabeth Keckley").
- When she was 18, in Hillsboro, North Carolina, a schoolmaster named Bingham whipped her because Mrs. Burwell, in whose house she was enslaved, wanted her "stubborn pride" broken. When she asked why, he hit her with a chair and knocked her to the floor. She wrote that for four years after that a white man, whom she refused to name, forced himself on her, and that she gave birth to his son, George (Elizabeth Keckley, *Behind the Scenes, or, Thirty Years a Slave, and Four Years in the White House*, 1868).
- In St. Louis her owners, the Garland family, lived on her sewing. She wrote: "With my needle I kept bread in the mouths of seventeen persons for two years and five months." The richest women in the city became her customers (Keckley).
- Her owner agreed to free her and George for $1,200. One of her customers, Mrs. Le Bourgeois, collected the money from other customers as a loan. Keckley was freed on November 15, 1855, and paid back every cent from her sewing (Keckley. Wikipedia).
- She moved to Washington in 1860 and later employed 20 seamstresses. On the Tuesday after Abraham Lincoln took office in March 1861, she went to the White House. Three other dressmakers were waiting, and Mary Todd Lincoln chose her. She wrote that she made "fifteen or sixteen dresses" for Mrs. Lincoln that spring and summer. Her customers also included Varina Davis, the wife of Jefferson Davis (Keckley. Wikipedia).
- In August 1862 she started the Contraband Relief Association to help Black people who had escaped slavery and come to Washington. In its first year it handed out 5,150 pieces of clothing (Wikipedia).
- She wrote *Behind the Scenes*, published in 1868. The Smithsonian holds a purple velvet gown she made for Mrs. Lincoln (Wikipedia). She died in Washington in May 1907.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New colors from coal tar, 1856 on" -->
- Before 1856 every dye came from plants, insects, shellfish or minerals, like the indigo and cochineal of earlier centuries. In 1856 William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old student in London, was trying to make quinine, a malaria drug, from chemicals found in coal tar. He made a purple dye instead and called it mauveine. It was the first man-made dye sold for use. He opened a factory near London in 1857 (Science History Institute, "William Henry Perkin").
- By 1859 chemists had made more coal-tar colors, such as fuchsine, a deep crimson-pink (FIT, "1850-1859"). These are called aniline dyes, after aniline, one of the coal-tar chemicals used to make them.

### Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis
> **Who:** a Reno tailor who put metal rivets on work pants and the San Francisco merchant who paid for the patent with him. · **When and where:** Reno, Nevada, and San Francisco, California, 1870 to 1873.
- Jacob Davis was born Jacob Youphes in Riga, in the Russian Empire, on May 14, 1831. He came to New York in 1854 and changed his name. By 1869 he ran a tailor shop in Reno, Nevada (Wikipedia, "Jacob W. Davis").
- A woman asked Davis to make strong work pants for her husband, a woodcutter. Davis had been using copper rivets on horse blankets. He put rivets at the corners of the pockets and the bottom of the fly, where the seams tore. Davis said in a court statement that this was in January 1871. Wikipedia dates it to December 1870 (National Archives, *Prologue* blog, "Forever in Blue Jeans ... and in Court", 2010. Wikipedia).
- Levi Strauss was born in Buttenheim, Bavaria, on February 26, 1829. He came to the United States in 1847 and reached San Francisco in 1854, where he sold cloth and clothing wholesale as Levi Strauss & Co. (Wikipedia, "Levi Strauss"). Davis could not pay the patent fee, so in 1872 he wrote to Strauss and offered to share the patent if Strauss paid (search summaries of History.com and NARA, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- On May 20, 1873, the U.S. Patent Office granted patent number 139,121 for an "improvement in fastening pocket-openings": "a metal rivet or eyelet at each edge of the pocket opening to prevent the ripping of the seam." Davis and Levi Strauss & Co. shared it (NARA *Prologue*. Wikipedia).
- In January 1874 Davis and Strauss sued a company that copied the rivets. The court decided for them on February 10, 1875, and awarded $2,000 of the $20,000 they asked (NARA *Prologue*).
- Davis ran the San Francisco factory that made the riveted pants. He died in 1908. Strauss died on September 26, 1902 (Wikipedia).
- Shared with: `work-workers` (work clothes) · `marketplace` (the brand). This chapter has the pants.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cutting Chinese men's braids in San Francisco, 1876 to 1879" -->
- Chinese men in the 1800s shaved the front of the head and wore the rest of their hair in one long braid called a queue. Losing it was a disgrace, and many believed it brought "misfortune and suffering after death" (Justice Stephen Field, *Ho Ah Kow v. Nunan*, U.S. Circuit Court, California, July 7, 1879).
- In 1873 San Francisco's Board of Supervisors passed an order to cut prisoners' hair, and Mayor William Alvord vetoed the hair-cutting part (San Francisco Sheriff's Office history, "Sheriff Matthew Nunan and the Chinese Queues").
- In April 1876 California's lawmakers made it a crime to sleep in a room with less than 500 cubic feet of air per person. The law was used against Chinese people living in crowded boardinghouses (Federal Judicial Center, "Ho Ah Kow v. Nunan"). On June 14, 1876, the Board of Supervisors ordered that every man jailed in the county have his hair cut to one inch from the scalp. Field found that the order was "directed against the Chinese only" (*Ho Ah Kow v. Nunan*).
- In April 1878 Ho Ah Kow was convicted under the lodging law and fined $10. He could not pay, so he was jailed for five days. Sheriff Matthew Nunan cut off his queue. Nunan and his deputies cut off other men's queues too. The records do not say how many (*Ho Ah Kow v. Nunan*. San Francisco Sheriff's Office history).
- Ho Ah Kow sued. On July 7, 1879, Justice Field ruled that the hair order broke the Fourteenth Amendment, because it punished Chinese prisoners more harshly than others (*Ho Ah Kow v. Nunan*). Search summaries say Ho Ah Kow was awarded $10,000 (unconfirmed: search summary only).

The pairs set students in the clothes they arrived in beside the same students in school uniforms (Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center, Dickinson College, "Before and After Photographs"). In the "after" pictures boys had short hair and girls had their hair tied back (search summary of the Digital Resource Center's teaching page, unconfirmed: search summary only).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shirtwaists and the Gibson Girl, 1890s" -->
- In the 1890s women wore a separate skirt and a shirtwaist, a blouse cut like a man's shirt. Women who worked in offices and stores wore it every day. Sleeves puffed out wide again, biggest around 1895. Most men wore the sack suit, a loose jacket and pants (FIT, "1890-1899").
- About 50,000 people rode bicycles in 1885 and about 10 million in 1896, by one estimate. Some women rode in bloomers, but most wore shorter skirts (FIT, "1890-1899").
- Charles Dana Gibson (1867 to 1944) drew for *Life*, *Collier's Weekly*, *Harper's Weekly* and other magazines. In the 1890s he drew a tall, slim young woman who played sports and went to college, called the Gibson Girl. The Library of Congress says she "set the standard for beauty, fashion, and manners" from the 1890s until World War I (Library of Congress, "The Gibson Girl's America").

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, new man-made materials changed what Americans wore and handled every day. Leo Baekeland made Bakelite, a hard plastic, in 1907, and chemists at the DuPont company made nylon in 1935. In the 1920s many women cut their hair short and wore shorter, looser dresses. Black women built companies that sold hair products to Black customers. Madam C. J. Walker and Annie Malone each grew rich from it, with fortunes reported at or above a million dollars. Government officials also used clothes and hair as tools of control. In 1902 the U.S. commissioner of Indian affairs told reservation officials to make Native men cut their hair. In 1942 the War Production Board limited how much cloth a dress or suit could use. In June 1943 servicemen in Los Angeles beat Mexican American young men and tore off their zoot suits, and the police arrested the young men.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The order to cut Native men's hair, 1902" -->
- On January 11, 1902, William A. Jones, the U.S. commissioner of Indian affairs, sent a letter to the superintendents of the federal reservations and agencies. He wrote: "The wearing of long hair by the male population of your agency is not in keeping with the advancement they are making, or will soon be expected to make, in civilization." He called short hair "a great step in advance" (*ICT*, "Native History: The Astonishing 1902 Order to Cut Native Hair!", quoting the letter. Rebecca Onion, *Slate*, 2013, from the National Archives copy).
- Jones wrote that "Indian costume and blanket" should be discouraged and that "Indian dances and so-called Indian feasts should be prohibited." He claimed that face paint caused most of the blindness among Native people. He told superintendents they could make men obey by holding back their rations, the food and supplies the government handed out on reservations, and their jobs. Superintendents had to report their progress by June 30, 1902 (*ICT*. *Slate*). Some accounts say the letter also suggested putting men who refused "in the guardhouse at hard labor" (search summary, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Charles Burton, the superintendent for the Hopi and Navajo, was reported to have used "whips, guns, and sheep shears" to enforce the order (Tisa Wenger, *We Have a Religion*, 2009, quoted by *ICT*).
- The letter made national news and drew criticism, including in *Harper's Weekly* in February 1902. After that and after reports of rough enforcement, the Bureau of Indian Affairs told its officials to use "persuasion and example, rather than force" (*Slate*. *ICT*).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (lead: the reservation system) · `religion` (the ban on dances). This chapter has the hair and clothing order.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shirtwaists, short hair and short skirts" -->
- The shirtwaist, a blouse cut like a man's shirt, stayed the everyday top for working women in the 1900s. Young immigrant women made them in New York factories. The Triangle Waist Company made shirtwaists on the top floors of a building near Washington Square. On March 25, 1911, a fire there killed 146 workers, most of them young immigrant women (`work-workers` research bank, which tells the fire and the strikes).
- In the 1920s many women cut their hair into a short bob. Dresses hung straight, with a low waist, in a slim shape often called the flapper look. Hemlines rose until 1926 and then began to fall. Many women gave up formal layers for lighter, looser clothes. Cheaper fabrics such as jersey and "artificial silk" let more women dress in the new styles (FIT, Fashion History Timeline, "1920-1929").
- Shared with: `work-workers` (lead: the shirtwaist strikes and the Triangle fire) · `disasters` (the fire). This chapter has the blouse and the 1920s look.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hair care built by Black women" -->
- Annie Turnbo was born on August 9, 1877, in Metropolis, Illinois, to parents who had been enslaved in Kentucky. She made a hair product she called Wonderful Hair Grower and sold it door to door. In 1902 she moved to St. Louis and opened a shop. She later named her company Poro. In 1918 she opened Poro College in St. Louis, a building with a factory, a store, classrooms, a dormitory and a chapel. About 200 people worked there, and about 75,000 women trained as Poro agents. She paid nearly $40,000 in taxes in 1924 (Wikipedia, "Annie Turnbo Malone").

### Madam C. J. Walker
> **Who:** a Black businesswoman, born to formerly enslaved parents, who built a hair-care company and trained thousands of women to sell her products. · **When and where:** St. Louis, Denver and Indianapolis, 1889 to 1919.
> **Movie:** Two Dollars and a Dream (1987)
- Madam C. J. Walker was born Sarah Breedlove on December 23, 1867, in Delta, Louisiana. Her parents had been enslaved. She was orphaned at 7, married at 14 and moved to St. Louis in 1889, where she worked as a laundress and cook (National Women's History Museum, "Madam C. J. Walker". Wikipedia).
- Skin disease, harsh soaps and hair products, poor food and homes without running water made her hair fall out. In 1904 she began using Annie Turnbo's hair product and became one of Turnbo's sales agents in St. Louis (Wikipedia. NWHM).
- In July 1905 she moved to Denver and began selling her own product, "Madam Walker's Wonderful Hair Grower." She married Charles Joseph Walker in 1906 and took his name. In 1910 she moved her company to Indianapolis and built a factory there (Wikipedia. NWHM).
- She trained women to sell her products and to do hair. By 1917 her company said it had trained nearly 20,000 women (Wikipedia). The National Women's History Museum says she employed 40,000 people.
- She gave money to the YWCA and $5,000 to the NAACP's work against lynching (NWHM).
- She died on May 25, 1919. Accounts of her wealth differ: between half a million and a million dollars (Wikipedia), or more than $1 million (NWHM).
- *Two Dollars and a Dream* (1987) is a documentary about her by Stanley Nelson. The Netflix series *Self Made* (2020) is a drama based on her life (Wikipedia).
- Shared with: `marketplace` (lead: selling) · `rights-movements` (her anti-lynching giving). This chapter has the hair care.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastics and nylon" -->
- In 1907 the chemist Leo Baekeland made Bakelite from two chemicals, phenol and formaldehyde, heated under pressure. It was the first plastic made entirely from chemicals rather than from plants or animals. Makers used it for jewelry, telephones, radios and billiard balls, and in cars (Science History Institute, "Leo Hendrik Baekeland"). His patent was granted on December 7, 1909 (research bank, parked from `technology`).
- Nylon went on sale as women's stockings. DuPont sold the first 4,000 pairs to its employees' wives in Wilmington, Delaware, on October 24, 1939, and they sold out in three hours. On May 16, 1940, called Nylon Day, four million pairs went on sale in stores across the country at about $1.15 a pair and sold out in two days. Some sources give the date as May 15 (Emily Spivack, "Stocking Series, Part 1", *Smithsonian Magazine*, 2012).
- After the United States entered World War II in December 1941, DuPont's nylon went to parachutes, tire cords, ropes and other war goods instead of stockings. When stockings came back in 1945, women lined up by the thousands. In Pittsburgh 40,000 people lined up for more than a mile for 13,000 pairs (Spivack).

### Wallace Carothers
> **Who:** the chemist who led the DuPont team that made nylon. · **When and where:** DuPont's Experimental Station near Wilmington, Delaware, 1928 to 1937.
- Wallace Hume Carothers was born on April 27, 1896, in Burlington, Iowa. He started work at DuPont's research station near Wilmington, Delaware, on February 6, 1928 (Wikipedia, "Wallace Carothers").
- His laboratory made two of the most widely used man-made materials of the 1900s. In April 1930 his team made neoprene, a man-made rubber. On February 28, 1935, Gerard Berchet, working under Carothers, made the first half ounce of the nylon later used in stockings (Science History Institute, "Wallace Hume Carothers". Wikipedia).
- Carothers had depression, an illness that brings long periods of deep sadness and makes it hard to think and work. He wrote: "I suffer from neurotic spells of diminished capacity." In the summer of 1936 he was treated in a psychiatric hospital. His sister Isobel died in January 1937 (Wikipedia).
- On April 29, 1937, at age 41, Carothers killed himself by taking cyanide, a poison, in Philadelphia (Science History Institute. Wikipedia). DuPont announced nylon on October 27, 1938. His daughter, Jane, was born in November 1937, seven months after his death (Wikipedia).

### Ann Lowe
> **Who:** a Black dressmaker from Alabama who made gowns for the richest families in Tampa and New York while the wider public hardly knew her name. · **When and where:** Alabama, New York and Tampa, 1898 to 1950.
- Ann Lowe was born about 1898 in Clayton, Alabama, into a family of Black dressmakers. She learned the trade from her mother and her grandmother. Her great-grandmother had been enslaved (Winterthur Museum, "Ann Lowe: American Couturier", 2023. Wikipedia, "Ann Lowe").
- Her mother, Jane, and grandmother, Georgia, made dresses for rich families in Montgomery. Her mother died in 1914, when Ann was 16, and Ann took over the business (Wikipedia).
- In 1917 she went to the S. T. Taylor Design School in New York. The school made her sit in a classroom by herself, apart from the white students. She was ready to graduate after six months. She then made gowns in Tampa, Florida, where she worked for the heiress Josephine Lee and opened her own dress shop in 1920 (Wikipedia. Search summaries of the Winterthur exhibition coverage, unconfirmed: search summary only, for Josephine Lee).
- In 1928 she moved to New York with $20,000 she had saved. For years she made gowns on commission for stores such as Henri Bendel, Neiman Marcus and Saks Fifth Avenue. The dress Olivia de Havilland wore to accept her Academy Award in 1946 was credited to another dressmaker, "Sonia Rosenberg" (Wikipedia).
- The Winterthur Museum calls her work part of "a legacy of Black women's knowledge and skills that began as enslaved labor" (Winterthur).
- Her best-known dress, Jacqueline Bouvier's wedding gown of 1953, belongs to the next era. Filled the target "a dressmaker or department-store buyer".
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ann-lowe" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth rules in World War II, 1942 to 1946" -->
- On April 8, 1942, the War Production Board, the federal agency that controlled materials in wartime, issued General Limitation Order L-85. It set how long and how wide women's coats, dresses, suits and skirts could be. It banned hoods, capes and scarves attached to coats, belts and hems more than 2 inches wide, cuffs on long jacket sleeves and on slacks, and pleating, tucking or gathering all over a garment. Wedding gowns, maternity dresses, children's clothes, burial gowns and religious robes were exempt. The rules lasted until 1946 (Jennifer M. Mower, "Pretty and Patriotic: Women's Consumption of Apparel During World War II", PhD dissertation, Oregon State University, 2011, quoting *Women's Wear Daily*, April 8, 1942).
- Stanley Marcus, co-owner of the Neiman-Marcus department stores, headed the board's women's and children's clothing section from December 27, 1941, and wrote the rules with people from the clothing trade. Makers and stores that broke them could be fined or jailed (Mower, citing Marcus's memoir, *Minding the Store*, 1974).
- The same month the board limited elastic for girdles and bras. Nylon and rubber went to the military (Mower).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The zoot suit attacks in Los Angeles, June 1943" -->
- A zoot suit had high-waisted, very wide pants that narrowed at the ankle and a long coat with wide lapels. It began among Black men in Harlem. In Los Angeles in the early 1940s young Mexican American men wore it, and so did some Filipino American, Japanese American and Black young men and some Mexican American young women. The War Production Board had stopped the making of zoot suits because they used so much cloth (Stephanie Hinnershitz, "The Zoot Suit Riots and Wartime Los Angeles", National WWII Museum).
- From June 3 to June 8, 1943, sailors, soldiers and Marines, joined by civilians, went through Mexican American neighborhoods of Los Angeles hunting young men in zoot suits. They beat them and tore off their clothes, and burned the suits in street fires. The attacks began on June 3 with about 50 sailors. By the later days thousands of servicemen had joined (Hinnershitz. Wikipedia, "Zoot Suit Riots").
- More than 150 people were injured (Wikipedia). Los Angeles police arrested nearly 600 Mexican Americans, by the National WWII Museum's count, or more than 500 by another count. Most of them had been attacked. Police arrested far fewer servicemen. Wikipedia says no soldiers were arrested for the beatings (Hinnershitz. Wikipedia).
- On June 8, 1943, Navy and Marine Corps commanders declared Los Angeles off limits to servicemen, and the attacks stopped (Hinnershitz. Wikipedia).
- The Los Angeles City Council approved a resolution to make wearing a zoot suit in the city a crime. Sources disagree on whether it became law: Wikipedia says no ordinance was ever passed or signed. Other accounts say the council banned the suits with a 30-day jail term (Wikipedia. Search summaries, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Governor Earl Warren set up a committee to study the attacks. It reported that "most of the persons mistreated during the recent incidents in Los Angeles were either persons of Mexican descent or Negroes" and found racism a main cause (Hinnershitz. Wikipedia).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000, teenagers set more of what Americans wore, and plastics went into everyday clothes and furniture. In the 1950s young men copied the jeans, white T-shirts and leather jackets that film stars wore. The first suits made partly of polyester, a plastic fiber, went on sale in 1951. By the 1970s polyester was in so many clothes that people called the decade the "Polyester Decade." From 1950 the designers Charles and Ray Eames sold chairs molded from plastic and glass fiber. Clothes and hair also went to court. In 1969 seven of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled that students could wear black armbands to school to protest a war. Judges disagreed about boys' long hair, and in 1981 a federal judge allowed an airline to ban a Black woman's braids. In 1995 state and federal officers found 72 Thai workers held behind razor wire in El Monte, California, sewing clothes sold in American chain stores.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jeans, T-shirts and teenagers in the 1950s" -->
- In the early 1950s most women's clothes still followed Christian Dior's "New Look" of 1947: a tight waist and a full skirt. By the middle of the decade dresses grew straighter and slimmer, and clothing makers began copying what teenagers wore (FIT, Fashion History Timeline, "1950-1959").
- Young men in the United States took up a working-class look of jeans, a white T-shirt and a leather jacket, with greased-back hair. Marlon Brando wore it in the film *The Wild One* (1953) and James Dean in *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955). Older people often saw the look as a sign of trouble. By the end of the decade boys wore jeans to most places (FIT, "1950-1959").
- Shared with: `music` (the rock and roll it came with) · `storytelling-evolution` (the films). This chapter has the clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Polyester and wash-and-wear" -->
- Polyester is a plastic made into thread. Two British chemists, John Rex Whinfield and James Tennant Dickson, patented it in 1941. The DuPont company bought the American rights in 1946 and called its fiber Dacron (Wikipedia, "Polyester").
- On May 8, 1951, Hart, Schaffner & Marx of New York began selling men's suits made of cloth that was 55 percent Dacron and 45 percent wool. Buyers were told the cloth did not crease or wear through easily and was easy to clean. In 1953 DuPont opened a Dacron plant in Kinston, North Carolina (Hagley Museum and Library, 2023). Clothes made with it could be washed and worn without ironing, so they were sold as "wash and wear." Most of the ironing time they saved was women's (NCpedia, "Ardathy Spikes: Somewhere like DuPont").
- By the 1970s polyester was so common and cheap that the decade was nicknamed the "Polyester Decade." Men wore leisure suits, jacket-and-pants sets in pastel colors, often made of polyester. People dancing at disco clubs wore satin, sequins and stretchy Lycra. By the late 1970s sweatshirt fabric and leggings had moved from the gym into everyday clothes (FIT, "1970-1979").

### Charles and Ray Eames
> **Who:** a husband and wife who designed furniture from molded plywood, plastic and wire, meant to be sold cheaply to families in small postwar homes. · **When and where:** Los Angeles, 1941 to 1978.
> **Movie:** Eames: The Architect and the Painter (2011)
- Charles Eames was born on June 17, 1907, in St. Louis, Missouri, and studied architecture. Ray Kaiser was born on December 15, 1912, in Sacramento, California, and trained as a painter. They married in June 1941 and moved to Los Angeles (Wikipedia, "Charles and Ray Eames", "Charles Eames", "Ray Eames").
- During World War II they made leg splints of molded plywood for wounded servicemen, paid for by the Navy. They then used the same method to make plywood chairs in 1945 and 1946 (Wikipedia).
- In 1948 the Museum of Modern Art in New York held a contest for low-cost furniture for the small homes being built after the war. The Eameses entered a chair seat made in one piece of stamped steel and won second place. Managers at Herman Miller, the company that sold their furniture, would not pay $80,000 for a steel press. So the Eameses used fiberglass instead: plastic resin hardened around glass threads, a material the military had used in the war (Eames Office, "Molded Shell Chairs").
- Zenith Plastics of Los Angeles pressed the seats in metal molds, and Herman Miller began selling them in 1950. The first colors were greige (a gray-beige), elephant-hide gray and parchment. Buyers could put the same seat on metal legs, wooden legs or rockers (Wikipedia, "Eames Fiberglass Armchair"). The Eameses described their aim as "getting the most of the best to the greatest number of people for the least" (quoted in Wikipedia).
- In 1956 they designed an expensive leather lounge chair of molded rosewood plywood. Ray called it "comfortable and un-designy." Its first label said "designed by Charles Eames." Ray got less public credit than Charles while she was alive (Wikipedia, "Eames Lounge Chair", "Ray Eames").
- Charles died on August 21, 1978. Ray died on August 21, 1988, ten years later to the day (Wikipedia). *Eames: The Architect and the Painter* (2011) is a PBS American Masters documentary about them.
- Filled the target "a mid-century designer". Shared with: `art` (design as art) · `home-family` (the postwar house). This chapter has the furniture and what it was made of.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ann Lowe's wedding gown, 1953" -->
- In 1953 Janet Auchincloss hired Ann Lowe, a Black dressmaker in New York, to make the wedding dress for her daughter, Jacqueline Bouvier, and the bridesmaids' dresses. Jacqueline Bouvier married Senator John F. Kennedy that September. The dress used 50 yards of ivory silk taffeta and cost $500 (Wikipedia, "Ann Lowe").
- Ten days before the wedding, water flooded Lowe's workroom. She and her workers remade the ruined dresses, and Lowe paid the extra cost herself without telling the family. She received no public credit for the dress until after President Kennedy was killed in 1963 (Wikipedia).
- Lowe often charged her rich customers too little to make a profit. In 1962 she lost her New York shop for failing to pay taxes, and doctors removed her right eye because of glaucoma, a disease in which pressure inside the eye destroys the nerve that carries sight. She went bankrupt in 1963, opened a new shop on Madison Avenue in 1968, and retired in 1972 (Wikipedia). Her story is told in 1900 to 1950 (slug `ann-lowe`).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Armbands and long hair at school" -->
- School officials made rules about what students wore, and students took some of those rules to court. On December 14, 1965, the principals of the Des Moines, Iowa, schools agreed that any student who wore a black armband would be told to take it off and suspended if he refused. They had heard that students planned to wear armbands to protest the Vietnam War (*Tinker v. Des Moines*, 393 U.S. 503, 1969, text of the opinion).
- In 1969 seven of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled for the students (see the story below). Justice Abe Fortas wrote that the case was not about "the length of skirts or the type of clothing, to hair style." Rules about hair and clothes stayed open to argument (opinion).
- Boys with long hair lost some cases and won others. In September 1966 Phillip Ferrell, Stephen Webb and Paul Jarvis, who played in a Dallas band called Sounds Unlimited, were refused entry to W. W. Samuell High School because of their "Beatle" haircuts. In 1968 the judges of a federal appeals court sided with the school (*Ferrell v. Dallas Independent School District*, 392 F.2d 697). In 1970 Chesley Karr lost his case against an El Paso, Texas, rule that a boy's hair could not hang over his ears or his collar. In 1972 judges on a federal appeals court in the Midwest ruled for long-haired students (First Amendment Encyclopedia, "Hair Length and Style").
- The Supreme Court justices never agreed to hear a school hair case. In 1971 Justice Hugo Black wrote that he could not see why "anyone should think the Federal Constitution imposes on the United States courts the burden of supervising the length of hair that public school students should wear" (SCOTUSblog, 2025). Justice William O. Douglas disagreed each time the other justices turned such a case away (First Amendment Encyclopedia).
- One of those cases came from Pawnee boys in Oklahoma. They wore their hair parted in the middle with a long braid on each side, to follow "the old traditional ways." One said it was "one way of telling people that I am proud [to be an Indian]." On April 24, 1972, school officials at Pawnee Junior High School suspended them indefinitely under a rule that boys' hair could not reach the collar or the ears. The first name on the case is Norman New Rider. Federal appeals judges upheld the rule. On December 10, 1973, the Supreme Court justices refused to hear the case, with Douglas and Thurgood Marshall disagreeing (*New Rider v. Board of Education*, 414 U.S. 1097, Douglas dissent).

### Mary Beth Tinker
> **Who:** a 13-year-old junior high student who wore a black armband to school to protest the Vietnam War and was suspended, which led to a Supreme Court ruling on students' rights. · **When and where:** Des Moines, Iowa, December 1965 to February 1969.
- In December 1965 a group of adults and students met at the home of Christopher Eckhardt's family in Des Moines. They decided to wear black armbands through the holidays to object to the Vietnam War and to call for a truce (*Tinker v. Des Moines*, 393 U.S. 503, opinion).
- On December 16, 1965, Mary Beth Tinker, 13, wore an armband to Warren Harding Junior High School, and Christopher Eckhardt, 16, wore one to his high school. Her brother John, 15, wore his the next day. School officials sent all three home and suspended them. They did not go back until after New Year's Day (opinion. Wikipedia). Her younger brother Paul, 8, and sister Hope, 11, also wore armbands, but the rule did not cover elementary schools (Wikipedia).
- The three students' fathers sued the school district, with help from the Iowa Civil Liberties Union. The family received hate mail and death threats. A federal judge ruled for the school, and the appeals court split evenly (Wikipedia).
- On February 24, 1969, seven of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled for the students. Justice Abe Fortas wrote that students do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." He wrote that the armbands were a quiet way of stating an opinion and had caused no disorder. Justices Hugo Black and John Harlan disagreed (opinion. Wikipedia).
- Shared with: `rights-movements` (students and the antiwar movement) · `education` (students' rights at school). This chapter has the armband as something a student wore. Movie: none found.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Afro, braids and a court ruling" -->
- Before the 1960s many Black Americans straightened their hair. Relaxers, the creams used to straighten it, often contained lye, a chemical that could burn the scalp and ears. In the 1960s the "Black is beautiful" movement led many Black men and women to wear their hair natural and untreated. The Afro, hair combed out into a round shape, became a sign of Black pride. The activist Angela Davis wore one. An Afro could be kept up at home without paying a hairdresser (Wikipedia, "Afro").
- Renee Rogers was an airport operations agent for American Airlines in New York. The airline had a rule against an all-braided hairstyle for workers in her job, and it told her to wear a hairpiece over her braids at work. She sued. On December 1, 1981, federal Judge Abraham Sofaer ruled for the airline (*Rogers v. American Airlines*, 527 F. Supp. 229. Wikipedia).
- Judge Sofaer held that a person can change a hairstyle but cannot change hair texture, so the braid rule was not race or sex discrimination under the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Lawyers for the airline said the style had been made popular by a white actress, Bo Derek, in the 1979 film *10*. Rogers said braids had a long history for Black women (Wikipedia).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Slavery in a California sewing shop, 1995" -->
- At 5 in the morning on August 2, 1995, state and federal officers and El Monte police raided a group of seven apartments in El Monte, California, near Los Angeles. Inside they found 72 garment workers from Thailand (*Smithsonian Magazine*, 2020. PBS SoCal, 2015).
- Suni Manasurangkun, whom the workers called "Auntie," ran the shop with her sons. Recruiters in Thailand promised the workers jobs, gave them false passports and flew them to Los Angeles. The bosses took their passports and said each worker owed about $5,000 for the trip. A high fence and razor wire surrounded the apartments, and armed guards watched the workers. Guards showed them a picture of a man who had been beaten after he tried to escape (*Smithsonian Magazine*. Wikipedia).
- The workers sewed about 84 hours a week, from early morning to midnight, for about $1.60 an hour (PBS SoCal). Some had not been outside the fence for as long as seven years (PBS SoCal).
- The clothes carried "Made in the USA" labels and were sold in chain stores including Mervyn's, Montgomery Ward and Miller's Outpost. In 1997 the workers won back pay by settling lawsuits against stores and clothing companies that had sold the clothes: $4 million by one account, more than $4.5 million by another (*Smithsonian Magazine*. Wikipedia).
- In February 1996 Suni Manasurangkun, three of her sons and three other people pleaded guilty to federal crimes and were sentenced to up to seven years in prison. Two sons escaped the country and were never caught (*Smithsonian Magazine*. PBS SoCal).

### Rotchana Cheunchujit
> **Who:** a Thai garment worker held in the El Monte sewing shop until federal officers freed her in 1995. · **When and where:** El Monte, California, early 1990s to 1995.
- Rotchana Cheunchujit came from Thailand. She took a loan from a recruiter to pay about $4,800 for her plane ticket and fees, because she wanted a better life for herself and her children (Wikipedia, "El Monte Thai garment slavery case").
- In the El Monte shop she sewed blouses and other clothes. She was paid five to seven cents a piece, about $300 to $500 a month. The owners took half her pay first, then made the workers buy food and supplies from the owners' own store at high prices. She was told she owed about $5,000 (Erin Blakemore, *Smithsonian Magazine*, 2020).
- She was 24 and asleep when officers broke in on August 2, 1995. "We were in shock," she said. "We didn't know what was going on." Immigration officers held her and the other workers for nine days until Asian American groups paid their bail (*Smithsonian Magazine*).
- Later she became a US citizen, learned English and was reunited with her children. She married Steven Sussman and took his name. She spoke up for other workers and opened a vegetarian Thai restaurant in Arcadia, California (*Smithsonian Magazine*).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000 people around the world have bought more clothes and worn each piece fewer times. Worldwide, clothing sales more than doubled between 2000 and 2015. In 2018 Americans threw away 17 million tons of cloth, most of it clothing, and 11.3 million tons of it went to landfills. Almost all clothes sold in the United States are now sewn in other countries, many in Bangladesh. There, in 2012 and 2013, a factory fire and a building collapse killed more than 1,200 people, most of them garment workers. Some of the workers had been making clothes for American stores. In Los Angeles, garment workers were paid by the piece, often a few cents each, until California lawmakers banned piece pay starting in 2022. Hair stayed a fight. Starting with California in 2019, lawmakers in about 30 states passed laws against firing workers or punishing students for braids, locs, twists or Afros. Facts in this section are current to September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the clothes come from" -->
- About 97 percent of the clothes and shoes bought in the United States are made in other countries, by a 2024 count from the American Apparel & Footwear Association, a trade group (quoted in news reports, 2025).
- Bangladesh is one of the biggest makers. In 2021-22 its garment factories earned $42.6 billion, about 82 percent of everything the country sold abroad. About 4 million people worked in them. According to the International Labour Organization, the typical worker is a woman of 23 who moved from a farm area to the city (ILO, 2023).
- Shared with: `work-workers` (lead: workers) · `america-world` (trade) · `economy`. This chapter has where the clothes Americans wear are made.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tazreen fire and the Rana Plaza collapse" -->
- On November 24, 2012, a fire started in a ground-floor warehouse of the Tazreen Fashions factory near Dhaka, Bangladesh. All three staircases of the nine-story building led down through the ground floor. At least 112 people died, by the first count, and later counts run from 117 to 124. Twelve died jumping from windows. Police arrested three supervisors and accused them of padlocking the exits. One survivor, Mohammad Ripu, said a manager told workers, "The fire alarm had just gone out of order. Go back to work" (Wikipedia, "2012 Dhaka garment factory fire").
- Workers at Tazreen were sewing clothes sold by Walmart and other companies. Walmart officials said a supplier had sent the work there without permission, and they ended the relationship (Wikipedia).
- On April 23, 2013, a Bangladeshi TV channel showed cracks in Rana Plaza, a nine-story building near Dhaka with five garment factories on its upper floors. The owner, Sohel Rana, had added floors without a permit, and they were not built to hold factory machines. The shops and the bank on the lower floors closed. Sohel Rana told reporters the building was safe. Factory managers ordered workers back the next morning, and managers at one factory threatened to hold back a month's pay from anyone who stayed away (Wikipedia. Clean Clothes Campaign).
- The building fell at about 9 a.m. on April 24, 2013. Counts of the dead run from 1,129 to 1,136. The most common figure is 1,134 (Wikipedia. Clean Clothes Campaign. *The Business Standard*, Dhaka, 2026). About 2,500 people were hurt. Some trapped workers could be freed only by cutting off an arm or a leg (Clean Clothes Campaign).
- Researchers for the Clean Clothes Campaign, a workers' rights group, and its partners in Bangladesh found at least 29 brands that had recent orders with the factories, including two American companies, Cato Fashions and The Children's Place. News reports also linked Walmart. J.C. Penney officials said one factory had made a small amount of clothing for another brand that Penney sold (Clean Clothes Campaign. Wikipedia. *Dallas News*, 2015).
- After the collapse, officials of more than 200 mostly European clothing companies signed a legally binding safety agreement. Officials of Walmart and 14 other North American companies did not. In July 2013 leaders of seventeen North American store chains, including Walmart, Gap, Target and Macy's, announced their own safety plan, without a binding promise to pay for repairs (Wikipedia).
- By October 2015, companies, governments and others had paid more than $34 million to families of the dead and to injured workers (ILO). Some of the brands linked to the building never paid (Clean Clothes Campaign). A lawsuit by victims against Walmart, J.C. Penney and The Children's Place was thrown out in 2016 because it had been filed after Bangladesh's time limit. No court has found an American company responsible for the deaths (search summaries of the court record).
- Sohel Rana has been in jail since his arrest near the border with India on April 28 or 29, 2013. As of April 2026 his murder trial had heard only 145 of 594 listed witnesses and had not ended (*The Business Standard*, April 24, 2026).
- Shared with: `work-workers` (lead: garment workers and safety) · `disasters` (the collapse) · `america-world`. This chapter has the clothes and the American stores that sold them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paid by the piece in Los Angeles" -->
- Clothes are still sewn in Los Angeles, often in small shops that open and close quickly. In 2020 workers there were paid about 5 to 12 cents for each piece they sewed. They worked about 10 hours a day, five and a half days a week, for about $300 a week in cash, about $5.50 an hour, when the city's minimum wage was $15 (*CalMatters*, 2020).
- On September 27, 2021, Governor Gavin Newsom signed the Garment Worker Protection Act, written by State Senator María Elena Durazo. From January 1, 2022, garment shops in California had to pay at least the minimum wage by the hour. Workers could also claim unpaid wages from the brands and stores that sold the clothes (Durazo press release. Remake, 2023). About 45,000 garment workers in California were covered, most of them immigrant women (Garment Worker Center, in the Durazo release). A year later some workers were earning the minimum wage and others still reported unpaid wages (Remake).

### Francisco Tzul
> **Who:** a garment worker from Guatemala who sewed clothes in Los Angeles for three decades, often for a few cents a piece, and asked lawmakers to end piece pay. · **When and where:** Los Angeles and Sacramento, 1990s to 2021.
- Francisco Tzul came to Los Angeles from Guatemala and has sewed clothes there for decades without legal papers to live in the United States (Nigel Duara, *CalMatters*, 2020. Knock LA, 2021).
- In the 1990s he saw Kathie Lee Gifford's television talk show and recognized her name. It was on labels of clothes he sewed. He was paid a few cents a piece, about a dollar for each finished item. He shared a one-bedroom apartment with three friends (Knock LA).
- In 2020 he sewed face masks at the Los Angeles Apparel factory for $15 an hour. He caught COVID-19 there. "My respiratory system was completely blocked and I wasn't able to breathe at all," he said. He spent 10 days in the hospital. When he came out, his roommates told him to leave, and the Garment Worker Center found him a hotel room for two weeks. At least 375 workers at that factory caught the virus, and four died (*CalMatters*).
- In 2021 he went to Sacramento to tell lawmakers about his work. He called the day the Garment Worker Protection Act was signed the best day of his life (Knock LA).
- Filled the target "a garment worker or resale seller". A living person, told only from his published interviews. Shared with: `work-workers` (lead) · `immigration`. Movie: none found.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hair and the CROWN Act" -->
- On July 3, 2019, California became the first state with a CROWN Act. The name stands for "Creating a Respectful and Open World for Natural Hair." The law states that employers and public schools may not treat people worse because of hair texture or styles such as braids, locs (dreadlocks) and twists. State Senator Holly Mitchell wrote it with a group led by Dove, a soap company, and civil rights groups (Wikipedia, "CROWN Act of 2022". CROWN Coalition).
- New Jersey's governor, Phil Murphy, signed a CROWN Act on December 19, 2019, one year to the day after a referee made a high school wrestler cut his locs (see the story below) (Jackson Lewis, 2019). By July 2025, 27 states and Washington, D.C., had such laws. Pennsylvania's governor, Josh Shapiro, signed one on November 25, 2025. The coalition counted 30 states in 2026 (GovDocs, 2025. Jackson Lewis, 2025. CROWN Coalition website, read September 2026).
- Members of the House of Representatives passed a national CROWN Act in 2020 and again in 2022. Senators never voted on it. As of September 2026 there is no national law (Wikipedia).
- In 2020 guards at a Louisiana prison handcuffed Damon Landor to a chair and shaved off his dreadlocks. He had grown them for decades as part of his Rastafarian religion and had three weeks left in his sentence. He showed them a court ruling that protected his hair, and they threw it in the trash. On June 23, 2026, six of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled that he could not sue the guards for money under a federal religious-freedom law (JURIST, 2026).

### Andrew Johnson
> **Who:** a high school wrestler in New Jersey whose locs were cut beside the mat so he could compete, which led to a state law against hair discrimination. · **When and where:** Buena, New Jersey, December 2018 to December 2019.
- On December 19, 2018, Andrew Johnson, a junior at Buena Regional High School, was about to wrestle in a home match. The referee, Alan Maloney, said his hair broke the rules. Maloney rejected the cover Johnson wore over his locs and gave him 90 seconds to cut his hair or lose the match (NPR, 2018).
- The team's athletic trainer, a white staff member, cut Johnson's hair beside the mat while the crowd watched. Johnson won the match in overtime. A local sports reporter posted the video, and millions of people watched it (NPR. WHYY, 2019).
- Johnson's family said Maloney had arrived late, missed the weigh-ins where referees check each wrestler, and raised no problem with the hair there (family statement, NPR). In 2016 Maloney had been accused of calling a Black referee a racial slur at a party (NPR).
- In September 2019 the New Jersey Division on Civil Rights and the state's school sports association agreed that Maloney would be suspended for two seasons. All wrestling officials in the state would be taught that the rule is about the length of hair, not its style (WHYY).
- Governor Phil Murphy signed New Jersey's CROWN Act on December 19, 2019, one year after the match (Jackson Lewis, 2019).
- Shared with: `rights-movements` (lead: the law) · `sports-play` (school wrestling). This chapter has the hair. Movie: none found.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where old clothes go" -->
- Americans threw away about 1.8 million tons of cloth in 1960, 9.5 million tons in 2000 and 17 million tons in 2018, the latest year counted. Most of it was clothing. In 2018 about 13 percent of old clothes and shoes were recycled, 3.2 million tons of all cloth waste were burned to make power, and 11.3 million tons went to landfills (US Environmental Protection Agency, page updated March 2026).
- Researchers at the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, a British group that studies waste, reported in 2017 that one garbage truck of cloth is dumped or burned every second. Between 2000 and 2015 people bought more than twice as many clothes a year and wore each one fewer times (Waste Dive, 2017).
- More Americans now buy used clothes. In April 2026 ThredUp, a company that sells used clothing online, published a report stating that sales of used clothes in the United States grew nearly four times as fast as sales of new clothes in 2025. Its researchers expect them to reach $78.8 billion a year by 2030. About half of used-clothing buyers told its researchers they find items on social media (ThredUp, 2026 Resale Report, research by GlobalData).
- Until August 29, 2025, packages worth $800 or less could enter the United States without paying import taxes, which let online sellers mail cheap clothes straight to buyers. On that date federal officials ended that rule for packages from every country (US Customs and Border Protection, 2025).
