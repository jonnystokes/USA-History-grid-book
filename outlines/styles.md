<!-- hb-time:start id="before-1500" order="01" chapter="styles" label="Before 1500" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Clothing and adornment were made from what a place provided, and they said who you were, where you were from, and what you had done.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="materials and natural colors" -->
Hide, fur, plant fiber, and woven cotton in the Southwest; quillwork and shell; wood, hide, clay, stone, and copper as materials; natural colors — ochre and other iron oxides, berries, roots, and minerals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="styles" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="seed" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two wardrobes meet, and trade cloth begins changing what Native people wear.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="trade cloth and Spanish dress" -->
European wool and linen; iron tools and glass beads entering Native design; Spanish dress in the missions and presidios.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="styles" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial dress is homespun, imported, and — in New England — regulated by law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="homespun, sumptuary law, and plain dress" -->
Wool and linen; sumptuary laws telling people what their rank allowed them to wear [VERIFY]; indigo and other imported dyes; plain dress among Puritans and Quakers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1700-1750" order="04" chapter="styles" label="1700 to 1750" state="thin" progress="seed" -->
## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For those who could afford it, fashion means following London a year or two late.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Georgian style and more iron" -->
Wigs, buckles, and Georgian style; imported fabrics and dyes; cabinetmaking and the Georgian house's look (the house itself belongs to `home-family`); more iron in everyday objects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="styles" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Clothing turns political — first as a boycott, then as a national look.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="homespun protest and the Federal style" -->
Homespun as a statement during non-importation; the Federal style after independence; cotton beginning to replace linen; simpler dress after the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="styles" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Machines start making cloth, and for the first time people buy clothes instead of making them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="factory textiles and ready-made clothing" -->
Empire dresses and changing silhouettes; the first factory textiles from the Lowell mills; ready-made clothing beginning, first for sailors and enslaved workers [VERIFY]; natural dyes still standard.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1800-1850" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="styles" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="seed" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The sewing machine and the department store put fashion within reach of ordinary people — and chemistry gives them colors nobody had seen.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the sewing machine, blue jeans, and synthetic color" -->
Victorian dress and its layers; the Gibson Girl; the home sewing machine; blue jeans as work clothes from 1873 [VERIFY the Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis patent]; the first synthetic dye made abroad in 1856 and the bright colors that followed; steel and aluminum in everyday objects; celluloid as the first widely used plastic.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Young people become the ones who decide what is fashionable, and plastic becomes the material of everyday life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="youth fashion and synthetics" -->
Mid-century modern design; teenage fashion; hippie, disco, and punk; polyester and other synthetics; Formica and fluorescent color; brand logos becoming visible on the outside of clothes; sneakers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

Clothes are cheaper and are replaced faster than at any time in history, and trends are set on screens.

Fast fashion and its labor and waste; streetwear; trends set online; carbon fiber and new materials; LED color; recycled and "green" materials; vintage and resale. Current through 2026; state the cutoff.
