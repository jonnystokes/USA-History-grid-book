## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A shaped continent, not an untouched one. Native nations managed land deliberately and at scale — with fire, with planting, and by living alongside two animals that engineered the country: the bison and the beaver. When later Americans called this land "wilderness," they were looking at somebody's managed ground and not recognizing the work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire as a tool" -->
Regular low burns, repeated over thousands of years, kept eastern woods open, favored oak and other fire-adapted trees, cleared brush, and fed the game people hunted. Modern research (Penn State, 2019) finds Native burning shaped eastern forest makeup more than climate did. Colonists dismissed the practice; after about 1940 the government put out every fire — and that suppression changed the forests again (the bill arrives in the last era).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Planting and the animal engineers" -->
- The Three Sisters — corn, beans, squash grown together: beans feed the soil, squash shades and holds it, corn carries the beans. Farming that keeps its ground (the food itself is `food-farming`'s story).
- Beaver: estimates before the fur trade run as high as 400 million across the continent (estimates, not counts). Their dams ponded water, slowed floods, and built wet meadows — whole watersheds shaped by an animal.
- Bison: roughly 30 million on the Plains (the early-1800s baseline), grazing and churning the grasslands that grew around them.
- In southwest Florida the Calusa built shell-walled "watercourts" that penned live fish (1300–1400) — engineered water management (`native-nations` leads on the Calusa).
- No named individual survives from this era at story level, and the book does not invent people; the era carries the wide view and the spans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The land began changing before most Europeans ever settled on it. Ships brought animals, plants, worms, and disease, and each remade some part of the country on its own. This chapter's telling of the Columbian Exchange is the land's side; `food-farming` leads on what people ate, `health` on the disease.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New animals, new weeds, new worms" -->
- Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay in May 1539 with a herd of pigs — chronicles say about 13, grown to about 700 by his death in 1542. Escapees went wild; the Southeast's feral hogs descend from Spanish stock. Horses (back on the continent from 1493) and cattle followed.
- The northern forests had no earthworms — glaciers had scraped them away, and the woods that regrew ran on a thick spongy leaf-litter layer. European worms rode in ship-ballast soil and plant roots, then spread west with wagons and plows. Where they invade, they eat the litter layer that native wildflowers and seedlings need. The ground itself, changed by a stowaway.
- One research team (Koch et al., 2019) estimates that after epidemics emptied Native fields across the Americas, about 56 million hectares of farmland regrew into forest — enough regrowth to pull carbon from the air and slightly cool the planet. State as one study's estimate; the starting population is itself disputed.
- Thin era, honestly: big forces, no documented individual from this angle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="land-environment" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two ideas of land met on the coast. Native nations used land seasonally — planting grounds, fishing stations, hunting territories — without treating it as a thing one person owned forever. English colonists brought deeds, fences, and livestock: land as permanent, exclusive property. Within one generation the difference showed on the ground itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The beaver trade" -->
European hat-makers paid for beaver felt, and the fur trade ran the animal down watershed by watershed, moving inland as each region emptied. From an estimated population as high as 400 million, the long decline began here and ran until about 100,000 were left on the continent by 1900. When beaver vanish, their dams fail — ponds drain, wet meadows dry. The trade's money and routes belong to `economy` and `exploration`; the emptied streams are ours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fences, hogs, and the first limits" -->
- Colonial cattle and hogs ranged free and rooted through Native planting fields and clam banks — a running cause of conflict the treaties kept circling back to.
- Some colonies noticed the taking: Plymouth barred cutting timber off colony land without consent (1626); William Penn's rules for Pennsylvania (1681) told settlers to leave one acre of trees for every five cleared — widely ignored, but on the books.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="miantonomi" name="Miantonomi" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Miantonomi
> **Who:** Narragansett sachem who put the environmental change of colonization into recorded words a single generation after settlement began. · **When and where:** speech to the Montaukett on Long Island, 1642, as written down by the English soldier Lion Gardener; executed 1643 after capture by the Mohegans.
As Gardener recorded it: our fathers had plenty of deer, skins, and turkeys — but the English cut the grass with scythes and the trees with axes, their cows and horses eat the grass, their hogs spoil the clam banks, "and we shall all be starved." Caveat stated in the telling: the wording is Gardener's, written years later; historians treat the substance as credible, the exact words as filtered through an English pen.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="miantonomi" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1700-1750" order="04" chapter="land-environment" label="1700 to 1750" state="thin" progress="researched" -->
## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial economies settled into taking more from the land than it replaced — trees for the navy, soil for tobacco, whales for lamp oil — and cheap new land westward made the exhaustion a habit instead of a crisis.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The King's pines" -->
The tallest white pines were warship masts, and the Crown claimed them: the Massachusetts charter of 1691 reserved big pines (24 inches through and up), and the White Pine Act of 1722 made cutting any pine over 12 inches without license illegal in New Hampshire. Surveyors marked the King's trees with the Broad Arrow — three hatchet slashes. Colonists cut them anyway; enforcement stayed loose until the 1760s, and what happened when it tightened opens the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Worn-out fields and the first whale ships" -->
- A tobacco field gave three or four good crops, then was left to scrub while the planter cleared new ground. Land around Jamestown wore out early; planting kept moving up the rivers decade after decade.
- New Englanders had taken right whales from shore for decades; by tradition, Nantucket's deep-sea sperm whaling began in 1712 when Christopher Hussey's boat, blown offshore, killed a sperm whale. The story was first written down in 1835 and may be legend; what is certain is that by mid-century Nantucket ran a deep-ocean whale fishery.
- Thin era at story level: no single documented land-story individual found; said plainly rather than padded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="land-environment" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The era the land was measured. A new country, paying war debts with the one thing it had plenty of, invented a machine for turning land into property: the survey grid. Before most of the interior was even settled by newcomers, it was already squares on paper with prices attached.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The grid" -->
The Land Ordinance of May 20, 1785: public land would be surveyed before sale into townships six miles square, each cut into 36 sections of one square mile — 640 acres — with section 16 in every township reserved to support public schools. The rectangles marched across the continent for the next century, ignoring rivers, hills, and everyone already living there. From an airplane over the Midwest today, the 1785 grid is still the visible shape of the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Clearing unleashed" -->
Independence voided the King's tree reservations, and clearing accelerated as settlement crossed the Appalachians into the new survey squares. The settlers' journeys belong to `migration`; the axe-work belongs here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ebenezer-mudgett" name="Ebenezer Mudgett" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ebenezer Mudgett
> **Who:** a sawmill owner in Weare, New Hampshire, fined for cutting the King's white pines. · **When and where:** the Pine Tree Riot, Weare, April 14, 1772.
Sheriff Benjamin Whiting rode into South Weare on April 13 with a warrant; Mudgett promised to pay in the morning. At dawn he came back with 20 to 30 men, faces blackened with soot, who beat the sheriff and his deputy with rods and drove them out of town. The rioters got off with small fines, the mast law went unenforced afterward, and some historians credit the riot as a forerunner of the Boston Tea Party a year and a half later — an ordinary mill owner, a tree law, and one of the first open fights over who owns American trees.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ebenezer-mudgett" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="land-environment" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Rivers and forests were put to work at industrial scale — and the first American voices said out loud that something was being lost. Both things are true of this era at once: the East hit its barest, and the argument for keeping some land wild was born.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dams stop the fish" -->
Mill and factory dams walled off the rivers. The first dam across the Connecticut (Turners Falls, 1798) cut salmon off from their spawning water — by the War of 1812 no salmon entered the river. The Great Stone Dam at Lawrence (1847–48) blocked the Merrimack 30 miles from the sea. Atlantic salmon were gone from southern New England rivers by 1865, and the shad runs that had fed towns every spring collapsed with them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bare East" -->
By 1850 an estimated 60 to 80 percent of New England was cleared (estimates vary — some put the remaining forest as low as a quarter); one driver with a number was sheep — about 4 million in New England by 1840 eating pastures that had been woods. Eastern forest cover bottomed out around 1872 at roughly half its 1620 extent. Wood was still fuel, fencing, and building material; the forest was being spent like money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="First voices" -->
The painter George Catlin, watching the bison and the Plains nations from the upper Missouri in 1832, proposed "a nation's park" to keep both — forty years before anyone built one. Audubon painted the birds while they were still uncountable; Thoreau moved to a pond to see what was left of the wild close to home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-james-audubon" name="John James Audubon" movie="John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature (2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John James Audubon
> **Who:** painter and naturalist; *The Birds of America* — 435 life-size bird portraits, published 1827–1838, about 18 years of work. · **When and where:** the American frontier and Atlantic cities, 1785–1851. · **Movie:** *John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature* (PBS American Masters, 2007).
In 1813, riding toward Louisville, he passed under a single passenger-pigeon flock that darkened the sky for three days; his own arithmetic made it more than a billion birds. He painted species that readers of his book would outlive — his pigeon plate is one of the few windows on a bird no one alive has seen.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-james-audubon" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-david-thoreau" name="Henry David Thoreau" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry David Thoreau
> **Who:** writer, and a working land surveyor — the man who argued for wild land measured woodlots for a living. · **When and where:** Walden Pond, Concord, Massachusetts, July 4, 1845 to September 6, 1847 — two years, two months, two days in a cabin he built himself.
*Walden* (published 1854) made the case that a life could be measured by something other than what it cleared, built, and earned — and that the wild remnants close to town were worth keeping. Later conservationists from Muir onward read him first.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-david-thoreau" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1800-1850" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="land-environment" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The half-century of the biggest taking — the bison brought from tens of millions to hundreds, the passenger pigeon from billions toward zero — and, in the same decades, the invention of protection as public policy: the first national park, the first forest reserves, the first conservation club with lawyers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison slaughter" -->
Roughly 30 million bison in the early 1800s; fewer than 1,000 by 1900 — one 1884 count found 325. The transcontinental railroad (1869) carried hide hunters out and hides back; a good team killed dozens a day for dollar-or-two hides; the Army encouraged it because the herds fed the Plains nations' independence. What the collapse did to those nations is `native-nations`' telling; ours is the animal and the grassland — an ecosystem's keystone shot out from under it in one generation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pigeon collapse" -->
Peak population an estimated 3 to 5 billion — possibly four of every ten North American birds. The 1871 Wisconsin nesting held an estimated 136 million breeding birds across 850 square miles. At Petoskey, Michigan in 1878, professional netters worked one of the last great nestings at a reported 50,000 birds a day for months, shipping barrels to city markets by rail while the telegraph told hunters where every new nesting was. By the mid-1890s the wild flocks were effectively gone. The finish arrives in the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Protection invented" -->
- Yosemite Grant, June 30, 1864: Lincoln signed the first federal set-aside of land for preservation and public use (given to California to manage; national park 1890).
- Yellowstone, March 1, 1872: Grant signed the act making it the first national park — kept by the federal government "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people."
- George Perkins Marsh's *Man and Nature* (1864) argued civilizations fall when they destroy their forests and soils; it helped produce the Forest Reserve Act of 1891, the start of the national forests.
- The Sierra Club, founded May 28, 1892, John Muir its first president — citizens organized to defend land in courts and Congress.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-muir" name="John Muir" movie="John Muir in the New World (2011)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Muir
> **Who:** Scotland-born wanderer, writer, and campaigner; the loudest American voice for keeping wild land wild. · **When and where:** California's Sierra Nevada from 1868; Sierra Club president 1892–1914. · **Movie:** *John Muir in the New World* (PBS American Masters, 2011).
His magazine pieces turned Yosemite's valleys and sequoias into a national cause and helped make Yosemite a national park (1890). He believed wild places had worth beyond board-feet and acre-prices — the position that would lose its biggest fight, at Hetch Hetchy, in the next era.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-muir" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-mayer" name="Frank Mayer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank Mayer
> **Who:** a buffalo runner — one of the professional hide hunters — from 1872 to 1878. · **When and where:** the southern Plains; died Fairplay, Colorado, 1954, at a reported 104.
His memoir *The Buffalo Harvest* (told at about age 100, published 1958) lays out the trade's arithmetic — rifle, wagons, hired skinners, hides at a dollar or two — and ends in plain regret at what the runners did. Caveats told with the story: a memoir set down in extreme old age, the numbers his own. The other side of the slaughter, in the voice of a man who did it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-mayer" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="land-environment" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Conservation became federal work — a president who used it as presidential power, new agencies, a corps of young men planting trees — and the era also delivered two lessons in what failure costs: a bird extinct in a zoo cage, and the southern Plains blowing to the Atlantic.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The conservation presidency" -->
Theodore Roosevelt, 1901–1909: about 230 million acres put under federal protection — 150 national forests, 5 national parks, the first 18 national monuments, 51 federal bird reservations, 4 game preserves (counts vary slightly by source). Pelican Island, Florida (March 14, 1903), the first federal bird refuge. The U.S. Forest Service created in 1905 with Gifford Pinchot as first chief; the Antiquities Act (June 8, 1906) let presidents protect land by proclamation — Devils Tower first, that September.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-land-environment" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="The Roosevelts: An Intimate History (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt
> **Who:** the president who made protecting land a central use of presidential power. · **When and where:** the White House, 1901–1909; Yosemite, May 1903. · **Movie:** *The Roosevelts: An Intimate History* (PBS, Ken Burns, 2014) — a documentary about the family.
In May 1903 he slipped his handlers for three nights of camping with John Muir — under the Grizzly Giant sequoia, in a snowstorm near Sentinel Dome, in a meadow by Bridalveil Fall. A hunter who watched species vanishing, he treated the land as a national inheritance to be defended by law — and used every legal tool he had, sometimes ahead of Congress's wishes.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-land-environment" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hetch Hetchy — use it or keep it wild" -->
Conservation split into its two lasting camps over one valley. San Francisco, rebuilding after the 1906 earthquake and fire, wanted Hetch Hetchy Valley — inside Yosemite National Park — for a reservoir. Pinchot's camp said conservation means careful use for the greatest good; Muir's said a national park must mean never. Congress sided with the city (Raker Act, signed December 19, 1913); Muir died the next year; the O'Shaughnessy Dam finished in 1923, and the valley is under water today. The National Park Service (Organic Act, August 25, 1916) was created partly in reaction — a bureau whose whole job is the parks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="gifford-pinchot" name="Gifford Pinchot" movie="Seeking the Greatest Good (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gifford Pinchot
> **Who:** America's first professionally trained forester; first chief of the Forest Service (1905–1910); later twice governor of Pennsylvania. · **When and where:** Washington and the national forests, 1898–1910. · **Movie:** *Seeking the Greatest Good* (WVIA/PBS, 2013).
His rule for the public lands: "the greatest good to the greatest number for the longest time" — cut, graze, and dam, but carefully, forever. He and Muir were friends until Hetch Hetchy made their two conservations enemies; American land policy has argued their argument ever since.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gifford-pinchot" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Extinction, witnessed" -->
The passenger pigeon's end had names and dates: the last confirmed wild bird shot in Ohio in 1900, the last of the species dead at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914 — the first time Americans watched a species they had numbered in billions go to zero on a known day. Topic documentary: *From Billions to None* (2014).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="press-clay-southworth" name="Press Clay Southworth" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Press Clay Southworth
> **Who:** the 14-year-old Ohio farm boy who shot the last confirmed wild passenger pigeon — and the person through whom the bird's whole ending can be told. · **When and where:** Pike County, Ohio, March 24, 1900; the Cincinnati Zoo to September 1, 1914.
He saw a strange bird eating corn, asked his mother's permission, and used the family 12-gauge; nobody in the family recognized it until a neighbor did. Mounted with button eyes, "Buttons" is held by the Ohio History Connection; Southworth told the story himself decades later. Fourteen years after his shot, Martha — the species' last bird — died in her Cincinnati Zoo cage, about age 29; keeper Sol Stephan had saved her molted feathers in a cigar box (his accounts of where she hatched never stayed consistent — said plainly). Her body went to the Smithsonian frozen in a 300-pound block of ice. A boy with a shotgun, a zookeeper with a cigar box, and the space between them is the extinction.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="press-clay-southworth" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dust Bowl — the soil itself" -->
War wheat prices and tractors had plowed up tens of millions of acres of native shortgrass; drought came in 1931, and the sod that held the southern Plains was gone. By December 1934 about 35 million acres of farmland were essentially destroyed for crops and 100 million more had lost most of their topsoil; in 1935 alone an estimated 850 million tons blew away. Black Sunday, April 14, 1935: a black wall of soil moving 60 miles an hour. That spring, as soil scientist Hugh Hammond Bennett testified in Washington, Plains dust darkened the sky outside the hearing room — "This, gentlemen, is what I've been talking about" (accounts differ on the exact day). Congress declared soil erosion "a national menace"; the Soil Conservation Service began April 27, 1935 — contour plowing, shelterbelts, grass put back. The exodus west is `migration`'s story; the storms as calamity are `disasters`'; the plowing and the soil are ours. Topic documentary: *The Dust Bowl* (Ken Burns, 2012).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="caroline-henderson" name="Caroline Henderson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Caroline Henderson
> **Who:** a Mount Holyoke graduate who homesteaded the Oklahoma Panhandle in 1907, farmed it through the dust years — and stayed. · **When and where:** near Eva, Oklahoma; her letters ran 1908–1966.
Her "Letters from the Dust Bowl" in the *Atlantic Monthly* (May 1936 the best known) told the country what the dust actually did — fields drifted like snow, wheat dead, neighbors leaving one by one — from a kitchen table inside the storm. The Secretary of Agriculture wrote to thank her. Where `migration` follows the families who left, she is the documented voice of the ones who stayed with the soil.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="caroline-henderson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tree army and the fire rule" -->
- The Civilian Conservation Corps (1933–1942) put about 3 million young men to work; they planted between 2 and 3.5 billion trees (estimates vary; "about 3 billion" is the common figure), fought fires, terraced eroding farmland, and built the parks' trails and shelters.
- After the 1910 "Big Burn" (3 million acres, 78+ dead), the Forest Service set out to kill every fire; the 1935 "10 a.m. policy" ordered each one controlled by the morning after it was spotted, and CCC manpower made that real. Smokey Bear arrived in 1944 ("Only YOU can prevent forest fires," 1947; the living cub rescued from a New Mexico fire, 1950). The policy stood until 1978 — and a century of unburned fuel is part of era 10's megafires.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="aldo-leopold" name="Aldo Leopold" movie="Green Fire: Aldo Leopold and a Land Ethic for Our Time (2011)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aldo Leopold
> **Who:** Forest Service forester turned professor who gave American conservation its ethics. · **When and where:** the Southwest, then a worn-out Wisconsin sand-county farm; 1887–1948. · **Movie:** *Green Fire: Aldo Leopold and a Land Ethic for Our Time* (2011).
He pushed through the Gila Wilderness in New Mexico (1924) — the world's first designated wilderness area — then bought a spent farm and spent weekends planting it back to life. He wrote about watching the "green fire" die in a wolf's eyes and changing his mind about predators. He died of a heart attack fighting a neighbor's grass fire in April 1948; *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) appeared after his death: people as plain members of the land community, not its conquerors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aldo-leopold" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1900-1950" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="land-environment" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Pollution became visible — in eagle nests, on a burning river, in a schoolyard over a chemical dump — and then became law. In one decade the country built nearly its whole environmental legal system, and ordinary people, more than experts, forced the building.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The quiet poison" -->
DDT, sprayed everywhere from the mid-1940s, moved up food chains and thinned raptor eggshells until eggs broke under the parents. Bald eagles — the national symbol — were down to 417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 by 1963.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rachel-carson" name="Rachel Carson" movie="Rachel Carson (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rachel Carson
> **Who:** marine biologist and writer, formerly of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; author of *Silent Spring*. · **When and where:** Silver Spring, Maryland; the book September 1962; died April 14, 1964. · **Movie:** *Rachel Carson* (PBS American Experience, 2017).
*Silent Spring* laid out, with citations, what persistent pesticides were doing to birds, soil, water, and people, and asked whether a spring without birdsong was the price of convenience. The chemical industry attacked her as hysterical; she answered on national television (CBS Reports, April 3, 1963) already sick with the cancer that killed her seventeen months later. The DDT ban (1972) and much of the coming decade's law trace to her book.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rachel-carson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The river that burned" -->
On June 22, 1969, an oil slick on the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland caught fire — about twenty minutes, quickly controlled, and at least the thirteenth fire on that river since 1868 (the 1952 fire did $1.3 million in damage; the 1912 fire killed five men). What changed was the audience: *Time* magazine showed the burning river to the country, and Mayor Carl Stokes — the first Black mayor of a major U.S. city — stood at the riverbank the next day demanding action. The same year, a well blowout blackened Santa Barbara's beaches.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The environmental decade" -->
- Earth Day, April 22, 1970 — an estimated 20 million Americans, one in ten, turned out.
- The laws, in five years: NEPA (Jan 1, 1970) · the EPA opens (Dec 2, 1970) · Clean Air Act (1970) · DDT banned and the Clean Water Act (1972) · Endangered Species Act (Dec 28, 1973).
- The proof came with feathers: bald eagles climbed from 417 pairs (1963) to about 10,000 by 2007, when the bird left the endangered list.
- Wolves came back on purpose: gray wolves from Canada released in Yellowstone starting January 1995 — the first deliberate return of a top predator to reshape land the government had once paid to clear of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Love Canal and the buried waste" -->
Hooker Chemical buried about 21,000 tons of chemical waste in an abandoned canal in Niagara Falls, New York (1942–1953), then sold the covered site to the school board for one dollar. A school and hundreds of homes went up. By the 1970s chemicals were rising into yards and basements, and the neighborhood counted miscarriages, birth defects, and sick children. State and federal emergencies (1978, 1980) moved about 900 families out; Congress answered with Superfund (December 1980) — polluters pay for toxic cleanups.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="lois-gibbs" name="Lois Gibbs" movie="Lois Gibbs and the Love Canal (1982)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lois Gibbs
> **Who:** a 27-year-old housewife with no political experience whose son attended the school built on the dump. · **When and where:** Love Canal, Niagara Falls, New York, 1978–1980. · **Movie:** *Lois Gibbs and the Love Canal* (CBS television film, 1982).
Told there was no problem, she went door to door with a health survey, founded the Love Canal Homeowners Association (August 1978), and out-organized the state of New York and the federal government until her neighbors were moved to safety. She then spent the rest of her life teaching other neighborhoods to do the same (Center for Health, Environment & Justice; Goldman Prize, 1990). The era's proof that the person who forces the law can be anyone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lois-gibbs" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who lives next to the dump" -->
Warren County, North Carolina, September 1982: the state trucked PCB-laced soil to a landfill in Afton, in a mostly Black rural county; residents and civil-rights leaders lay down in front of the trucks; about 500 were arrested. The landfill was built anyway — and the fight named a pattern and a movement: environmental justice, the question of whose neighborhood gets the waste. It returns, with a victory, in the last era.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A warming climate reached into everything this chapter has covered — fire, water, soil, coasts, wildlife. 2024 was the warmest year ever measured, both globally and in the United States (NOAA and NASA agree; the U.S. ran 3.5°F above its 20th-century average). The era's fights are over how the land copes — and its clearest hope is that management has started coming full circle to the continent's first managers. Current through August 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire comes back bigger" -->
A century of put-out-every-fire left the woods loaded with fuel; heat and drought dried it. The Camp Fire (November 8, 2018) killed 85 people and burned the town of Paradise, California — about 19,000 buildings. The 2020 California season burned about 4.2 million acres, four percent of the state; the August Complex alone passed one million acres — the first "gigafire" of modern record. The reversal followed: agencies that once banned all burning now plan and set fires on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="margo-robbins" name="Margo Robbins" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Margo Robbins
> **Who:** Yurok tribal member who co-founded the Cultural Fire Management Council to bring traditional burning back to Yurok land. · **When and where:** the Klamath River country, northern California; first community burn 2012, council formed 2013.
She wanted hazel shoots that grow straight after a burn — for weaving the baskets that carry Yurok babies, starting with her own grandchildren. The council's burns brought back hazel and the deer, and its training exchanges now teach cultural burning to government fire crews — the practice this chapter opened with, outlawed and suppressed for a century, taught back to the people who banned it. Native continuity past 1900, on the ground.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="margo-robbins" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The shrinking river" -->
Tree rings say 2000–2021 was the driest 22-year stretch in the Southwest in at least 1,200 years, and a study in *Nature Climate Change* (2022) attributes about 42 percent of its severity to human-caused warming. Lake Mead and Lake Powell — the Colorado River's two great reservoirs, water for some 40 million people — hit record lows in 2021–22, Mead down to about a quarter full. In May 2023 Arizona, California, and Nevada agreed to cut about a tenth of the lower basin's use through 2026. The deals run out as this book is written; the river is not refilling.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poison, plastic, and justice" -->
- Environmental justice moved from protest to some victories: see Margie Richard, below.
- Plastic is now weather: a *Science* study (2020) measured more than 1,000 tons of microplastic — mostly clothing fibers — falling with rain and dust on western parks and wilderness every year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="margie-richard" name="Margie Richard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Margie Richard
> **Who:** a schoolteacher from the Old Diamond neighborhood of Norco, Louisiana — four generations of her family 25 feet from a Shell Chemical fence line. · **When and where:** Louisiana's petrochemical corridor; campaign to 2002; Goldman Environmental Prize, April 2004.
Her neighborhood, wedged between a chemical plant and a refinery, counted cancers, birth defects, and neighbors killed in plant accidents. She led a roughly 13-year campaign — bucket air samples, shareholder meetings, a trip to Shell's headquarters in the Netherlands — until Shell agreed in 2002 to fund the relocation of any Old Diamond family that wanted out, cut emissions 30 percent, and pay $5 million toward community development. First African American winner of the Goldman Prize. The Warren County question — who lives next to the plant — answered once, by one neighborhood that refused to stay put quietly.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="margie-richard" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Comebacks" -->
The recoveries are real and countable: bald eagles at roughly 71,400 nesting pairs; bison at about 420,000 in commercial herds and 20,500 in conservation herds — and the National Bison Range itself returned to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes (2020–2022); wolves at about 500 in the Yellowstone region and 1,600-plus in the northern Rockies, their legal status still fought over. None of it is the old abundance — 30 million bison are not coming back — but extinction stopped being the automatic ending.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land — Bears Ears" -->
Bears Ears, Utah — a monument proposed by five tribes (Hopi, Navajo, Ute Mountain Ute, Ute Indian Tribe, Zuni) — has become the live test of whether protection lasts: proclaimed at 1.35 million acres (December 28, 2016), cut about 85 percent (2017), restored (October 8, 2021), co-managed with the five tribes under a first-of-its-kind 2022 agreement — then cut again to about 121,000 acres, nine percent of its restored size, with the tribal commission terminated (July 13, 2026; the proclamation cites minerals; tribes and conservation groups have vowed suit — live news as this book is written, labeled as such). Protection, this era shows, is a decision each government can remake.
