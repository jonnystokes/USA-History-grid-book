## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were no stores in this land before 1500. There were no prices, no coins to pay them with, and no advertising. What there was instead was exchange. Goods moved from hand to hand across thousands of miles, at gathering places where nations met to fish, trade, and hold ceremonies. Nothing in that exchange carried a price, and nobody in it was a customer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dalles and Celilo Falls" -->
- The ten-mile stretch of the Columbia River between Celilo Falls and The Dalles, in what is now Oregon and Washington, was the largest trading place in the Northwest. People had lived and fished there for thousands of years; archaeologists date human use of the site back at least 10,000 years, and some estimates run to 15,000 (Oregon Historical Society, "The Columbia River Trade Network"; Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs materials).
- Traders came from every direction. Goods recorded at the site include coiled baskets, dried salmon, and berries from the north; obsidian and bows from the south; horses, camas root, and buffalo hides from the east; and wapato root, whale oil, and dentalium shells from the coast (Oregon Historical Society).
- When Meriwether Lewis and William Clark reached the stretch in October 1805 they wrote it down as the "Great Mart of all this Country." The trading was centuries older than their visit; theirs is simply the first written record (Clark's journal, October 1805, via Oregon Historical Society and lewis-clark.org).
- Dentalium shells passed so widely along the coast and inland that people used them to measure the worth of other goods. `money` tells shell and bead exchange as a money question. Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations who held the site and lost it to the Dalles Dam in 1957) · `economy` (trade as a living) · `food-farming` (the salmon fishery).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Goods that traveled without a store" -->
- Copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf of Mexico, obsidian from the West, and turquoise from the Southwest have all been found hundreds of miles from where they came from. No shop moved them. They passed between people, one exchange at a time, along rivers and trails (Illinois State Museum; Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site).
- Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, had open plazas where thousands gathered. `economy` leads Cahokia as a working economy and `native-nations` leads it as a city; this chapter's point is narrower. A person there could obtain goods from a thousand miles away without a market as later readers would know one.
- No individual trader is documented by name from this angle before 1500. Do not invent one.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were still no stores in the 1500s. What began instead was trade between Native nations and the crews of European fishing and trading ships along the northern coasts. Native people chose what they took in these trades, and archaeologists can see the choices in what they kept, cut up, and reworked.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Native customers chose" -->
- Copper and brass kettles were among the most wanted European goods in the Northeast and Great Lakes in the second half of the 1500s. Basque, French, and Dutch traders carried them because Native buyers asked for them (Ehrhardt, *Historical Archaeology*; Fitzgerald and others on Basque banded copper kettles).
- Many kettles were never used as pots. Archaeologists find them cut into sheets and reworked into beads, pendants, tubes, and arrow points, made in shapes people had long made from natural copper. The metal was wanted; the European object was not (Ehrhardt; Museum of Ontario Archaeology, metal artifacts guide).
- Fragments of Basque kettles from the late 1500s and early 1600s have been found from the Maritimes to the lower Great Lakes, which shows Native traders passing the metal on among themselves far beyond the coast (Fitzgerald, Turgeon, and others).
- Cloth, glass beads, iron knives, and axes were the other steady wants. Later, when the Hudson's Bay Company found Native customers rejecting poorly made guns, it sent gunsmiths to its posts to check quality and repair them. Said plainly: these were choosy customers, not people being fooled (Bill of Rights Institute, "The Fur Trade"; EH.net, "The Economic History of the Fur Trade").
- The first recorded exchange, in July 1534 at Chaleur Bay, is `economy`'s span. Shared with: `economy` (the fur trade as business) · `native-nations` (the nations' own terms) · `elements` (copper as a metal) · `styles` (beads and cloth as adornment).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists in the 1600s bought and sold with very little coin. Most trade ran on credit written in a book, on goods swapped for goods, and on work swapped for goods. The first fixed places to buy appeared in this century: trading posts on the rivers and market days in the town centers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Market days" -->
- Boston's market was ordered by Governor John Winthrop in 1634 and is the first officially recorded European-style public market in the English colonies (City of Boston, "Brief History of Farmers Markets in the United States").
- Others followed: Hartford in 1643, New York by 1686, Philadelphia in 1693 (City of Boston).
- A market day was a day, not a building. Farmers brought meat, dairy, and produce into town on set days because town people could not travel out to the farms for them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trading posts" -->
- Plymouth Colony built a trading post at Aptucxet on Cape Cod in 1627 to trade with the Wampanoag and with Dutch traders from New Amsterdam. The colonists traded for corn and beans; the Wampanoag name meant "little trap in the river" (Bourne Historical Society; Plymouth Archaeological Rediscovery Project).
- In the same year, Isaack de Rasieres, secretary to the Dutch colony, brought wampum into Plymouth's trade. What wampum meant to the nations who made it, and how it worked as money, is told in `native-nations` and `money`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ledger" -->
- A colonial merchant kept two books. The journal listed every sale and purchase in the order it happened. The ledger held one page per customer, with what they bought on the left and what they paid on the right (Accounting Historians Journal, "Accounting in Colonial America").
- Payment came in commodities, in labor, and sometimes in coin. Credit was usually extended for up to a year, often until a crop came in (EH.net, "Credit in the Colonial American Economy").
- These books are why historians can say what ordinary colonists actually bought. The names in them are real customers, listed item by item (Ian Brabner, "Daily Records," on manuscript account books; East Hampton Library, Robert Townsend account books).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s, British factory goods reached ordinary colonial houses in quantity for the first time. Historians call the change the consumer revolution. Colonists who had made their own cloth, tools, and dishes began buying them instead, and shops became fixtures in the port towns. Newspapers began carrying advertisements for the goods, and the advertisements paid the printers' bills.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The consumer revolution" -->
- More British goods reached the colonies as British manufacturing, shipping, and credit improved. Textiles, furniture, and ceramics became cheaper, and colonial incomes rose, so goods that had been luxuries became ordinary (Khan Academy, "The consumer revolution"; Lumen/OpenStax, *U.S. History*; Library of Congress research guide, "Colonies in America: Consumers").
- Tea came with a set of objects: pot, cups, sugar, and a table to put them on. Buying and using them correctly marked a family as respectable, which is one reason tea later made such a useful thing to refuse (Khan Academy; LOC guide).
- The plain version for a young reader: for the first time, large numbers of ordinary people could want a thing they did not need, and buy it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shops and shopkeepers in the port towns" -->
- Port-town shops sold imported cloth, ceramics, tea, sugar, and haberdashery, ordered from London on credit and sold on credit again to customers.
- Women ran a share of these shops. Elizabeth Murray arrived in Boston from Scotland in 1749, at 23, and opened a shop selling imported cloth and dry goods, traveling to London to pick her own stock (Boston Women's Heritage Trail; The Elizabeth Murray Project, California State University Long Beach). She reappears in the next section, because she set up other women in business and one of those shops became a target in the boycotts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspaper advertising begins to pay" -->
- Benjamin Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* on October 2, 1729. Under him, advertising became the paper's main source of income (American Antiquarian Society, *The News Media and the Making of America*; Library of Congress).
- He changed how the notices looked, using more white space and different type sizes so a reader's eye would stop on them. The paper grew from two pages to four partly to fit the notices (American Antiquarian Society).
- Shared with: `news-communication` (lead — the press itself, printers, and the post). This chapter's slice is the selling: who paid for the notice and what it was meant to make people do.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-marketplace" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** the printer who made advertising pay for a newspaper. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, from October 2, 1729.
> **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from his former employer Samuel Keimer in 1729 and turned it into the best-selling paper in the colonies. Much of the money came from advertisements for ships, land, books, cloth, runaway servants, and enslaved people. Franklin laid the notices out with white space around them and varied the type sizes, so that an advertisement no longer looked like the rest of the page. The Gazette grew from two pages to four in part to hold them all. Franklin retired from printing in 1748 at 42, and advertising revenue was a large part of why he could (American Antiquarian Society; Library of Congress; Franklin's *Autobiography*). The documentary covers his whole life, not the printing business alone. `news-communication` tells him as a printer and postmaster.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1765 and 1776, buying became a political act, and so did refusing to buy. Colonists organized boycotts of British goods, made cloth at home instead of importing it, and published the names of shopkeepers who kept selling. After the war, country stores spread inland and sold on credit against next year's crop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Non-importation" -->
- About sixty Boston merchants signed the Boston Non-Importation Agreement on August 1, 1768, promising not to import most British goods from January 1, 1769 to January 1, 1770. Within two weeks all but sixteen of the town's merchants had joined (Massachusetts Historical Society, "Non-consumption and Non-importation"; Avalon Project text of the agreement).
- The value of goods imported into Boston from Britain fell from about £420,000 in 1768 to about £208,000 in 1769, roughly by half (American History Central; Massachusetts Historical Society).
- Enforcement was public. Committees visited shops, and newspapers printed the names of merchants who would not sign.
- Shared with: `government-politics` (lead — the taxes and the protest) · `war` (the road to war) · `styles` (homespun cloth as a statement) · `rights-movements` (women organizing in public). This chapter leads the goods and the shop counter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spinning instead of buying" -->
- Boycotting cloth meant making cloth. Between 1768 and 1770, at least 46 public spinning meetings were held in New England, involving 1,644 women; ministers hosted 30 of them (Museum of the American Revolution, "Age of Homespun"; Daughters of Liberty scholarship summarized by the American Battlefield Trust).
- The meetings were public on purpose. Spinning in a churchyard in front of a crowd turned housework into a demonstration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="cuming-sisters" name="Ame and Elizabeth Cuming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ame and Elizabeth Cuming
> **Who:** two Boston shopkeepers who refused to join the boycott and were named in the newspaper for it. · **When and where:** Boston, 1765 to 1776.
The Cuming sisters were unmarried, in their thirties, and running their own shop in Boston by 1765, selling imported cloth, silk, and trimmings. Elizabeth Murray had helped set them up in business. In late 1769 a £300 shipment of silk and haberdashery arrived from London. In mid-November, men from the merchants' committee came to the shop and warned them of consequences if they sold it. The sisters refused, answering that they had never signed any agreement not to import, and describing themselves as two hard-working girls trying to earn their bread in an honest way. The *Boston Gazette* called them enemies to their country. On March 19, 1770 the Boston town meeting voted to write their names into the record so that later generations would know they had opposed their country. They kept the shop until March 1776, then left with the Loyalists for Halifax, Nova Scotia, where by 1780 they were doing better than they ever had in Boston (National Park Service, "Cuming Sisters: She-Merchants of Boston"). The boycott was a real weapon and it fell on real people who had not chosen a side.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cuming-sisters" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The country store and the crop ledger" -->
- Away from the ports, one store served a whole neighborhood. It carried salt, iron, cloth, sugar, tea, tools, and rum, and it took payment in crops, in labor, and in promises.
- The store's ledger ran the credit. A farm family bought through the year and settled after harvest. A bad harvest carried the debt into the next year, which gave the storekeeper considerable power over the neighborhood.
- Merchant account books from these years survive and name the customers. Robert Townsend's books from Oyster Bay and New York, 1773 to 1785, are one published example (East Hampton Library, Robert Townsend account books).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Roads, canals, and factories put machine-made goods within reach of inland families in the first half of the 1800s. Peddlers carried the goods on their backs and in wagons to houses that were nowhere near a store. In the cities, the first stores large enough to be called department stores opened. Sellers also found that an advertised claim could draw a paying crowd whether or not the claim was true, and P. T. Barnum built a business on exactly that.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The peddler's golden age" -->
- Peddlers walked or drove circuits through the countryside carrying tinware, clocks, pins, needles, combs, books, and patent medicines. Connecticut tin peddlers were the best known; the trade also carried booksellers and, later in the century, many recent immigrants who started with a pack (Journal of Antiques, "The Tin Peddler"; peddler-trade histories).
- For a farm family miles from any store, the peddler was the store. He also carried news, which is part of why he was welcomed and part of why he was distrusted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-guild" name="James Guild" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Guild
> **Who:** a Vermont farm boy who set out to peddle with a trunk of goods and wrote down what happened. · **When and where:** Tunbridge, Vermont, and the roads south, 1818 to 1824.
Guild left Tunbridge in 1818 with a small trunk of goods and peddled his way through New England and beyond. He kept a journal of the six years that followed, in which he moved from peddling to tinkering, to keeping a school, to painting portraits, and finally to London. The journal was published by the Vermont Historical Society and is one of the few first-person accounts of the peddling trade written by a peddler rather than about one. It records the ordinary work of the job: knocking on doors, being refused, sleeping where he could, and counting up what he had earned (Vermont Historical Society, *From Tunbridge, Vermont, to London, England: The Journal of James Guild, Peddler, Tinker, Schoolmaster, Portrait Painter, from 1818 to 1824*). Confirm exact earnings figures against the printed journal before quoting any.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-guild" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first department store" -->
- Alexander Turney Stewart opened his marble-fronted dry goods store at 280 Broadway in New York on September 21, 1846. Shoppers called it the Marble Palace (Village Preservation; Untapped New York; Smithsonian Libraries).
- Two things about it were new. Stewart sold from several floors instead of only the ground floor, and he put a set price on the goods so that customers did not have to bargain (Village Preservation; Visualizing 19th-Century New York).
- It is usually called the first American department store. Treat that as a claim, not a fact: what counts as a department store is defined differently by different historians, and Wanamaker's and Macy's are named as firsts too. The building still stands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling by spectacle" -->
- Phineas Taylor Barnum built his early business on paid publicity for exhibits that were not what he advertised. In 1835 he exhibited Joice Heth, an enslaved Black woman, advertising her as the 161-year-old former nurse of George Washington. After her death in 1836, a public autopsy concluded she had been no more than about 80 (hoaxes.org archive; Smithsonian Magazine).
- In 1842 he exhibited the Feejee Mermaid at his American Museum in New York, advertised with pictures of a beautiful creature. The object was a monkey's head and torso sewn to a fish tail (hoaxes.org; Live Science).
- Barnum called this work humbug and was proud of it. What he proved is that the advertisement, not the object, was what people were paying for.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="pt-barnum-marketplace" name="P.T. Barnum" movie="The Circus (American Experience, 2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### P.T. Barnum
> **Who:** the showman who sold tickets with advertising rather than with what was inside the tent. · **When and where:** New York City, from 1835.
> **Movie:** The Circus (American Experience, 2018)
Barnum's first paying exhibit, in 1835, was a human being. He bought the right to display Joice Heth, an enslaved and blind woman, and advertised her as George Washington's 161-year-old nurse. When the crowds thinned, he planted a rumor in the newspapers that she was actually a machine, and the crowds came back to check. She died in 1836, and the autopsy he sold tickets to concluded she was about 80. He went on to run the American Museum in New York from 1841, where the Feejee Mermaid of 1842 was a monkey sewn to a fish. Barnum used the word humbug for what he did and wrote a book defending it. Say the hard part plainly: he made his first money by exhibiting an enslaved woman and lying about her age. The documentary covers the American circus and includes Barnum. The 2017 musical *The Greatest Showman* invents most of its story and leaves out Joice Heth (hoaxes.org archive; Smithsonian Magazine; PBS American Experience).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two new ways of buying began between 1850 and 1900. In the cities, department store owners marked prices on the goods, displayed them behind plate glass, and let customers bring purchases back for a refund. In the countryside, mail-order companies sent catalogs that offered city prices to any mailbox. Chain stores, brand names, and advertising agencies all started in the same fifty years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The department store" -->
- Rowland Hussey Macy opened his dry goods store at Sixth Avenue and 14th Street in New York on October 28, 1858. First-day sales are usually given as $11.06, though some accounts say $11.08; first-year sales were about $85,000 (Macy's corporate history; CBS News; company histories — the two cent difference is unresolved, so give the range).
- John Wanamaker opened the Grand Depot in a former Pennsylvania Railroad freight building at Thirteenth and Market in Philadelphia in 1876.
- Wanamaker is credited with the one-price policy, the printed price tag on the goods, and the money-back guarantee. Sources say "credited with" and "one of the first," not "the first," and A.T. Stewart had set fixed prices in 1846. State it as a practice he made normal rather than a thing he alone invented (Wanamaker company and museum histories; Vintage Fashion Guild).
- What the guarantee changed for a customer: before it, a bad purchase was the buyer's problem. After it, the store carried the risk, and buying without inspecting became reasonable. That is what made mail order possible.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-wanamaker" name="John Wanamaker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Wanamaker
> **Who:** the Philadelphia merchant who made the price tag and the money-back guarantee normal. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1861; the Grand Depot from 1876.
Wanamaker opened a menswear shop with his brother-in-law Nathan Brown in Philadelphia in 1861, then converted a railroad freight hall into the Grand Depot in 1876. He marked the price on the goods so that every customer paid the same, and he promised money back on anything returned. He also bought full-page newspaper advertisements at a time when most stores bought a few lines. He served as U.S. Postmaster General from 1889 to 1893, and pushed for the rural free delivery that would later carry his competitors' catalogs to farm mailboxes (Wanamaker company histories; Philadelphia historical collections; USPS history). No documentary film about him was found; if one is claimed later, verify before adding it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-wanamaker" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mail order reaches the farm" -->
- Aaron Montgomery Ward mailed his first catalog on August 18, 1872. It was one sheet of paper listing 163 items. Late that year the Illinois Grange, a farmers' organization, named him its purchasing agent, which brought him his first steady customers (Chicago History Museum; Britannica).
- The 1875 catalog carried the promise "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back." The catalog reached 32 pages in 1874 and 152 pages with about 3,000 items in 1876 (Chicago History Museum; Encyclopedia.com).
- Richard Warren Sears was a railroad station agent at North Redwood, Minnesota, when he bought an unclaimed shipment of watches in 1886 and sold them at a profit to other agents. He ordered more, moved to Chicago, and with the watchmaker Alvah Roebuck founded the firm that became Sears, Roebuck and Company in 1893. That year's catalog ran 196 pages (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com).
- Rural free delivery began on October 1, 1896, on three routes out of Charles Town, Halltown, and Uvilla, West Virginia. Within a year 44 routes were running in 29 states (U.S. Postal Service history). Before it, a farm family collected mail from the nearest post office, sometimes miles away.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="aaron-montgomery-ward" name="Aaron Montgomery Ward" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aaron Montgomery Ward
> **Who:** the traveling salesman who worked out that a farm family could buy from a list instead of from a store. · **When and where:** Chicago, from August 18, 1872.
Ward had sold goods for wholesale houses and had seen how much a country store's prices rose above the city price by the time they reached a farm. His answer was to cut out the store: buy in quantity for cash, print what he had and what it cost, and mail it. The first catalog was a single sheet with 163 items. Country storekeepers said the goods would be junk and the company would take the money and vanish, so in 1875 Ward printed the promise that any customer could send anything back for a refund. By 1876 the sheet had become a 152-page book (Chicago History Museum; Britannica; Encyclopedia.com).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aaron-montgomery-ward" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-sears" name="Richard Sears" movie="Mr. Sears' Catalogue (American Experience, 1989)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Richard Sears
> **Who:** the station agent who became the country's loudest salesman in print. · **When and where:** North Redwood, Minnesota, 1886; Chicago from 1887.
> **Movie:** Mr. Sears' Catalogue (American Experience, 1989)
Sears was 22 and selling railroad tickets when a shipment of watches went unclaimed at his station. He bought the lot, sold them along the line at a profit, and ordered more. Within a year he had left the railroad. What made Sears, Roebuck bigger than Montgomery Ward was not the goods but the writing: Sears wrote much of the catalog copy himself, in a voice that talked directly to the reader and promised more than a careful man would. He retired in 1908 at 44 (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com; PBS American Experience). At its height the catalog ran to about 1,500 pages and listed roughly 100,000 items; the documentary gives a print run of about 20 million copies for the peak years. Treat the peak circulation figures as approximate, because the company's own counts and later retellings differ.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-sears" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chains, brands, and the advertising business" -->
- George Gilman and George Huntington Hartford formed the partnership in New York in 1859 that became the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, known as A&P. It had about 70 stores by 1878 and nearly 200 by 1900 (A&P company histories; Encyclopedia.com).
- Frank Winfield Woolworth opened a five-cent store in Utica, New York on February 22, 1879. It closed within weeks. He opened another in Lancaster, Pennsylvania on June 21, 1879, and that one worked. The idea was a fixed low price, goods out where customers could handle them, and no bargaining (Library of Congress, This Month in Business History; Woolworths Museum).
- Goods that had been scooped from a barrel began arriving in a printed package with a name on it. A brand name let a maker advertise to the whole country over the shopkeeper's head. `food-farming` carries Coca-Cola's origin; `health` carries what was actually in the medicines.
- Francis Wayland Ayer founded N. W. Ayer & Son in Philadelphia in 1869 at age 21, with $250 from his father. It is generally called the first American advertising agency, and it later put the agency on the client's side by publishing what the newspapers charged (N. W. Ayer records, Smithsonian National Museum of American History; Historical Society of Pennsylvania).
- Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound, sold from the mid-1870s, was advertised as a cure for almost any complaint a woman might have and was 18 to 20 percent alcohol. Her sons put her face in the newspaper advertisements, and she invited customers to write to her personally (Smithsonian National Museum of American History object records; Britannica; New England Historical Society). Shared with: `health` (lead — what the medicines did and the 1906 law) · `drugs-alcohol` (the alcohol content).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="persis-albee" name="Persis Foster Eames Albee" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Persis Foster Eames Albee
> **Who:** the first door-to-door saleswoman for the company that became Avon. · **When and where:** Winchester, New Hampshire, and across New England, from 1886.
David McConnell had sold books door to door and found that the perfume he gave away as a free gift sold better than the books. He started a perfume company and in 1886 hired Albee, who was 50, as his first traveling agent. She sold the Little Dot Perfume Set of five scents from house to house across Cheshire County and then the Northeast, and she recruited and trained other women to do the same. This was 34 years before American women could vote, and it gave women without shops or capital a way to earn their own money (Avon corporate history; *Keene Sentinel* local history — company and journalism sources, labeled). Verify any earnings figures separately before using them; the company's own retellings are promotional.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950 the chain store put the same goods at the same prices in thousands of towns, the grocery counter was replaced by shelves customers walked past themselves, and buying on installments spread from sewing machines to cars and radios. Sellers reached people inside their own houses, first by radio from 1922 and then by television from 1941. During the Second World War, a shopper needed government ration stamps as well as money to buy many everyday goods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Five-and-dime, at national scale" -->
- In 1911 Woolworth persuaded four rival chains to merge with his. The agreements were signed on November 12, 1911, and the combined F. W. Woolworth Co. opened with 596 stores and $65 million in capital (Library of Congress, This Month in Business History).
- Woolworth paid $13.5 million in cash for the Woolworth Building in New York, completed in 1913 and at 792 feet the tallest building in the world at the time. `landmarks` tells the building. This chapter's point is that a fortune made five and ten cents at a time paid for it in cash (Library of Congress; Woolworth Building histories).
- A&P grew to 1,600 stores by 1915 and to about 15,000 stores and $2.9 billion in sales by 1930, when it was the largest retailer in the world. It stayed the largest U.S. grocery retailer from 1915 to 1975 (A&P company histories; Encyclopedia.com). `big-business` leads what that size did to competitors and to the law.
- Woolworth lunch counters served customers seated. In the South they served white customers only, which is why they became the place the sit-in movement began in 1960. Shared with: `rights-movements` (lead — the Greensboro sit-in of February 1, 1960 and the campaign that followed).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-woolworth" name="F. W. Woolworth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### F. W. Woolworth
> **Who:** the storekeeper who built a national chain on fixed low prices and goods customers could pick up themselves. · **When and where:** Utica and Lancaster, 1879; New York, 1911 to 1919.
Woolworth's first five-cent store, opened in Utica, New York on February 22, 1879, did well for about two weeks and then failed. He tried again in Lancaster, Pennsylvania on June 21, 1879, and that store paid. His method was simple and it spread: one low fixed price, no haggling, and goods laid out on counters where customers could handle them instead of asking a clerk behind a counter to fetch them. By November 1911 he had merged with four rival chains into a company with 596 stores. He paid cash for the tallest building in the world in 1913 (Library of Congress, This Month in Business History; Woolworths Museum; Britannica). No documentary film about him was found.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-woolworth" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Self-service" -->
- Clarence Saunders opened the first Piggly Wiggly at 79 Jefferson Street in Memphis, Tennessee on September 6, 1916. He filed a patent for the "self-serving store" on October 21, 1916 and received U.S. Patent 1,242,872 on October 9, 1917 (Tennessee Encyclopedia; Smithsonian Magazine; the patent record).
- The design was deliberate. A turnstile let one customer in at a time, and a single fenced path led past every shelf in the store before reaching the checkout. Customers picked their own goods, which cut the number of clerks and let prices fall.
- King Kullen opened in Jamaica, Queens, New York on August 4, 1930, founded by Michael J. Cullen, a former Kroger employee whose supermarket proposal Kroger had ignored. The Smithsonian recognizes it as the first supermarket, on five tests: separate departments, self-service, discount pricing, chain marketing, and volume buying. Cullen's slogan was "Pile it high. Sell it low." (King Kullen corporate history citing the Smithsonian; Encyclopedia.com.) Shared with: `food-farming` (the food itself and frozen food from 1930) · `work-workers` (what self-service did to clerks' jobs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="clarence-saunders" name="Clarence Saunders" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clarence Saunders
> **Who:** the grocer who invented self-service shopping and then lost everything fighting Wall Street. · **When and where:** Memphis, Tennessee, 1916 to 1923.
Saunders opened the first Piggly Wiggly in Memphis on September 6, 1916 and patented the layout the next year. The chain grew fast and he listed it on the New York Stock Exchange. In early 1923 some franchised stores in New York failed, and traders began selling Piggly Wiggly stock short, betting the price would fall. Saunders borrowed about $10 million from Southern banks, added his own money, and bought until he held orders for 196,000 of the 200,000 shares in existence. The short sellers could not buy shares to deliver. Then the Exchange suspended trading in the stock and gave them extra time to settle. Saunders was ruined within months and lost the company he had built (Tennessee Encyclopedia; Smithsonian Magazine; financial histories of the 1923 corner). He kept inventing store systems for the rest of his life and none of them lasted.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarence-saunders" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on time" -->
- Installment buying, which had sold sewing machines and pianos in the 1800s, spread to cars, radios, phonographs, furniture, and appliances in the 1920s. By the end of the decade roughly 60 percent of cars and about 80 percent of radios were bought on installments (Encyclopedia.com; American RadioWorks, "The American Dream and Consumer Credit").
- Registered automobiles rose from about 9 million in 1920 to nearly 27 million by 1929, and financing is a large part of why (Encyclopedia.com).
- In 1924 the National Association of Finance Companies set standards of one-third down on a new car, two-fifths on a used one, and twelve months to pay (Encyclopedia.com).
- Said plainly for a young reader: people began buying things before they had the money, and paying afterward, a little at a time, with an extra charge for the privilege. Shared with: `money` (the credit instrument) · `economy` (what household debt did in 1929) · `home-family` (debt inside the household) · `transportation` (car ownership).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising comes into the house" -->
- The first paid radio advertisement was broadcast on WEAF in New York on August 28, 1922. The Queensboro Corporation paid $50 for about ten minutes promoting apartments in Jackson Heights (Saturday Evening Post; NPR; broadcast histories).
- The first legal television commercial ran on WNBT in New York on July 1, 1941, before a Brooklyn Dodgers game. It showed a Bulova watch face over a map of the United States for about ten seconds and cost the company $9, made up of $4 for air time and $5 in station charges (Television Academy; Slate; Guinness World Records).
- Shared with: `news-communication` (lead — radio and television as media). This chapter's slice is the pitch and what it cost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shopping under rationing" -->
- From 1942 the Office of Price Administration rationed sugar, coffee, meat, cheese, fats, canned goods, gasoline, tires, and shoes. Every person in the country, including babies, was issued a ration book (National WWII Museum; National Park Service).
- Processed foods went on rationing with War Ration Book Two from March 1, 1943. Blue stamps bought processed foods; red stamps bought meat, cheese, fats, and oils. Each person got 48 blue points and 64 red points a month (National WWII Museum; Oklahoma Historical Society).
- At the counter a shopper handed over both money and points, and the price could not go above the government ceiling. Point values changed often and were printed in newspapers and posted at the local ration board, so shopping meant checking a list before leaving the house (National WWII Museum; National Park Service).
- Shared with: `food-farming` (lead — rationing and victory gardens) · `home-family` (the kitchen) · `government-politics` (price control as policy) · `war` (the home front).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The catalog under Jim Crow" -->
- In the segregated South, a Black farm family usually had to buy on credit at a white-owned country store, often owned by their landlord, at prices the storekeeper set. Ordering from a catalog meant paying the same national price as anyone else, with no clerk deciding who was served first (Louis Hyman, Cornell University, argued this in a widely circulated 2018 account; reported by NPR, the *Washington Post*, and America's Black Holocaust Museum — journalism carrying a historian's argument, labeled).
- Hyman also reported that some Southern storekeepers burned catalogs to keep their customers. Treat the burnings as his claim rather than as a settled count, and say so.
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` and `rights-movements` (what segregation did at the store counter) · `economy` (sharecropping and the crop lien).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950 Americans shopped where they could drive. Enclosed malls drew customers away from downtown shopping streets, discount stores replaced the five-and-dime, and advertisers reached most of the country at once through television. A plastic card replaced the store's own credit book, and a laser reading a printed code replaced the clerk typing in the price.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The enclosed mall" -->
- Southdale Center opened in Edina, Minnesota on October 8, 1956, the first fully enclosed, climate-controlled shopping mall in the United States. It had 72 stores on two levels around a lighted central court, with two department stores at the ends (Minnesota Historical Society; Library of Congress, This Month in Business History; MNopedia).
- The Dayton family, Minneapolis department store owners, paid for it and hired Victor Gruen & Associates to design it. Enclosing it was a response to Minnesota weather: a controlled indoor climate meant shoppers came in January as well as June.
- Malls spread nationwide over the next thirty years, and the shops on downtown main streets lost the customers who moved to the suburbs. Shared with: `city-building` (lead — suburbs and what happened to downtowns) · `transportation` (the car and the parking lot) · `home-family` (suburban life).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="victor-gruen" name="Victor Gruen" movie="The Gruen Effect (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Victor Gruen
> **Who:** the architect who designed the first enclosed American mall and spent his later years rejecting what was built from it. · **When and where:** Vienna, then Los Angeles and Minnesota; Southdale opened October 8, 1956.
> **Movie:** The Gruen Effect (2009)
Gruen was an Austrian Jewish architect who fled Vienna after the German annexation in 1938 and reached New York with almost nothing. He designed shops, then shopping centers, and he argued that suburbs had no town centers and that a shopping center could be one, with a library, a school, a post office, and public space alongside the stores. Southdale, opened in 1956, was built without most of those parts. Developers copied the shopping half across the country and left out the civic half. In a speech in London in 1978, Gruen said he refused to pay alimony for what developers had made of his idea (Minnesota Historical Society; MNopedia; the 2009 documentary by Anette Baldauf and Katharina Weingartner). He returned to Vienna, where he then objected to a shopping center built on the city's edge.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-gruen" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic at the counter" -->
- Diners Club, from February 1950, was the first card good at many different businesses. The bill had to be paid in full each month (Smithsonian National Museum of American History; `money` leads the instrument itself).
- In September 1958, Bank of America mailed about 60,000 ready-to-use BankAmericards to people in Fresno, California who had not asked for them. Cardholders got $300 to $500 of instant credit, and merchants paid the bank 6 percent of each sale. About 22 percent of accounts went unpaid against the 4 percent the bank had projected. The card was licensed nationwide and renamed Visa in 1976 (KVPR, "Central Valley Roots"; 99% Invisible; Joe Nocera's 1994 history — journalism, labeled).
- Congress banned mailing unsolicited credit cards in 1970 (Public Law 91-508, amending the Truth in Lending Act).
- What changed at the counter: a customer no longer needed the storekeeper's trust or the store's own credit book. The bank carried the debt, and any store that took the card would sell to a stranger. Shared with: `money` (lead — the card as an instrument) · `home-family` (household debt) · `big-business` (the card networks).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1962: three discount chains in one year" -->
- Kmart opened its first discount store in Garden City, Michigan on March 1, 1962. Target opened its first store in Roseville, Minnesota on May 1, 1962. Sam Walton opened the first Wal-Mart in Rogers, Arkansas on July 2, 1962. Woolco, from the Woolworth company, also opened that year (Forbes retail history; Britannica; Target and Walmart corporate histories; Minnesota news accounts).
- All four sold department store goods at lower prices in plain buildings with wide aisles and self-service. Kmart came from the Kresge five-and-dime chain, Target from the Dayton department stores, and Woolco from Woolworth, so three of the four were older store companies remaking themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sam-walton" name="Sam Walton" movie="Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sam Walton
> **Who:** the small-town storekeeper who built the largest retailer in the United States by going where competitors would not. · **When and where:** Rogers, Arkansas, July 2, 1962, and outward from there.
> **Movie:** Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price (2005)
Walton ran variety stores in Arkansas before he opened the first Wal-Mart in Rogers in 1962. His plan was the opposite of his competitors': put big discount stores in small towns that chains had judged too small, buy in enormous quantity, and undercut everything nearby. It worked, and the company grew into the country's largest retailer and largest private employer. Both effects of that are documented and both belong in the telling. Rural families paid less for goods, sometimes far less. Main-street stores in the same towns lost their customers and closed, and studies of Walmart's effect on wages and small business have found real harm alongside the lower prices (Britannica; company histories; the 2005 documentary, which argues one side and should be named as such). `big-business` leads the company's power and its court cases; `work-workers` leads pay and conditions inside the stores.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sam-walton" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The franchise" -->
- Franchising means the owner of a name and a method sells other people the right to run a business under it, with rules about how. A. W. Allen began franchising A&W root beer stands in 1924 and 1925. Howard Johnson granted his first restaurant franchise to Reginald Sprague in 1935; there were 41 Howard Johnson's restaurants by the end of 1936 and 107 by 1939 (International Franchise Association; University of New Hampshire Rosenberg International Franchise Center).
- After 1950 the model spread from restaurants to motels, gas stations, ice cream, haircuts, and hardware. A traveler could tell what a place would be like before going in, which was the point.
- Shared with: `food-farming` (lead — McDonald's, Ray Kroc, and the food) · `work-workers` (who employs the workers in a franchise) · `transportation` (the highway that made the sign matter).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The barcode" -->
- At 8:01 in the morning on June 26, 1974, at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio, cashier Sharon Buchanan scanned a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum for Clyde Dawson, Marsh's head of research. It cost 67 cents and it was the first item ever sold using a Universal Product Code (GS1; History.com; Smithsonian Magazine).
- The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds one of the first ten Spectra-Physics Model A scanners; accounts differ over whether the museum holds the original gum pack or a stand-in, so do not claim the pack itself (Smithsonian NMAH object record).
- The code changed more than checkout speed. A store could now know exactly what had sold, hour by hour, and reorder automatically, which is the beginning of the tracking that online shopping now runs on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sharon-buchanan" name="Sharon Buchanan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sharon Buchanan
> **Who:** the supermarket cashier who made the first barcode sale in history. · **When and where:** Marsh supermarket, Troy, Ohio, 8:01 a.m., June 26, 1974.
Buchanan was working the register the morning the store switched on its new scanners. Clyde Dawson picked a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum out of a basket, and Buchanan passed it over the glass. The scanner read the striped code, the register showed 67 cents, and the sale was recorded. Every barcode scanned since, in every store in the world, followed that one. The people who ran the project chose the gum on purpose, to prove the code could be printed small enough for a package that size (GS1; History.com; Smithsonian Magazine).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sharon-buchanan" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling on television, and the end of the big catalog" -->
- S&H Green Stamps were given out by grocers and gas stations and pasted into books to trade for goods from a catalog. In the 1960s and 1970s about 80 percent of American households collected them, and the company printed about 35 million catalogs a year (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com; Antique Trader).
- The Home Shopping Network began in 1982 as a local cable channel in Pinellas County, Florida and went national on July 1, 1985. Its origin was a radio station that had been paid in electric can openers in 1977 and sold them on the air. QVC was founded in 1986 by Joe Segel and began broadcasting that November (HSN and QVC company histories; Britannica).
- Sears announced on January 25, 1993 that it was ending its general catalog, and the last one was mailed that spring. Sources give the catalog's age as 97, 101, or 105 years, because they date its start differently; say the range (Baltimore Sun, January 26, 1993; Britannica; company accounts).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on a computer begins" -->
- Jeff Bezos incorporated the company that became Amazon on July 5, 1994, working from a rented house in Bellevue, Washington. Amazon.com opened for orders on July 16, 1995. The first book it sold was Douglas Hofstadter's *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies*, and it took about $12,000 in book orders in its first week (Amazon corporate history; HistoryLink; History.com).
- Pierre Omidyar started the auction site that became eBay over Labor Day weekend 1995. His own account says the first item sold was a broken laser pointer, for $14.83; some retellings say a broken printer, so state the object as disputed (eBay corporate history; Academy of Achievement).
- Almost nobody shopped this way yet. What these two years set up is told in the next section.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000 a growing share of buying has happened on screens instead of in buildings. About one dollar in six of American retail spending now goes online, orders arrive in a day or two, and a phone serves as the catalog, the store, and the wallet at once. Advertising changed with it: instead of one message to everyone, sellers buy the right to show a different message to each person, chosen from what that person has done online. This section is current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big online shopping actually is" -->
- The Census Bureau measures it every quarter. E-commerce was 16.4 percent of all U.S. retail sales in 2025, and 16.6 percent in the fourth quarter of that year (U.S. Census Bureau, Quarterly Retail E-Commerce Sales).
- Said plainly: more than five dollars in six is still spent in physical stores. The change is real, and it is not the total replacement it is often described as.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeff-bezos" name="Jeff Bezos" movie="Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos (Frontline, 2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jeff Bezos
> **Who:** the founder of Amazon, which turned a mail-order bookshop into a store selling nearly everything. · **When and where:** Bellevue and Seattle, Washington, from July 1994.
> **Movie:** Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos (Frontline, 2020)
Bezos left a New York finance job in 1994, drove west, and started an online bookshop from a rented house in Bellevue, Washington. He chose books because there were more titles than any shop could stock, so a list on a screen could offer what a building could not. The site opened on July 16, 1995. Amazon added music, then everything else, then other people's goods, then its own delivery network. On February 2, 2005 it launched Prime: $79 a year for unlimited two-day shipping. Prime changed what shoppers expected of every other seller, because once shipping felt free and fast, waiting a week felt broken. Membership passed 200 million worldwide by 2021 and the U.S. price reached $139 a year (Amazon corporate history; SEC filings; History.com; PBS Frontline). `big-business` leads Amazon's market power and the antitrust cases; `work-workers` leads warehouse and delivery conditions. The Frontline documentary is an investigation and argues a case; name it as such.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeff-bezos" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Malls empty out" -->
- Counts of American malls disagree because "mall" is defined differently. Green Street Advisors put the number of enclosed malls at roughly 1,000 in recent years; other counts give about 700 enclosed malls by 2023, and broader counts including open-air centers reach about 1,150 for 2024. Give the range and say why it varies (Green Street Advisors, cited in retail trade press; industry statistics compilations — journalism and company research, labeled).
- Store closures are counted more consistently. The research firm Coresight recorded 7,325 U.S. store closures in 2024 and forecast about 15,000 for 2025 (Coresight Research, reported in retail press — journalism, labeled).
- Said plainly, without personifying the buildings: shoppers went online or to newer centers, anchor department stores closed, the smaller shops that had depended on the crowds those anchors brought lost their customers, and owners could not fill the space. Some empty malls have been rebuilt as apartments, clinics, schools, and warehouses.
- Shared with: `city-building` (what replaces them) · `work-workers` (retail jobs lost) · `economy` (retail spending overall).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising that knows who you are" -->
- Explained simply and neutrally: when a person visits a website, small files and identifiers record what they looked at. Companies called data brokers buy and combine those records with offline records such as purchases, warranty cards, loyalty cards, and property records, then sell the ability to show an advertisement to a described group of people.
- The Federal Trade Commission's report of May 2014, *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability*, found that data brokers held billions of data elements covering nearly every American consumer, that they collected most of it without people knowing, and that consumers had almost no way to see or correct it. The FTC asked Congress to require that people be able to see and control it (Federal Trade Commission, May 2014).
- Both sides, stated without loading them: targeted advertising pays for services people use without paying money, and it can show a person something useful. It also means a record of what a person reads and buys is bought and sold by companies they have never heard of. Congress has passed no general federal law on it; several states have passed their own.
- Shared with: `news-communication` (advertising paying for the feed) · `government-politics` (privacy law) · `technology` (how the tracking works) · `big-business` (the ad market's concentration).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Secondhand goes mainstream" -->
- Buying used clothes moved from thrift shops and yard sales to apps and resale sites. The resale company ThredUp reported that the U.S. secondhand apparel market grew 14 percent in 2024, that online resale grew 23 percent, and that 58 percent of shoppers bought something secondhand in 2024, up from 52 percent in 2023.
- Label the source: ThredUp sells resale, so its report is a company estimate rather than a government count, and the projections in it are forecasts (ThredUp, 13th Annual Resale Report, 2025; reported in Forbes and trade press — journalism, labeled).
- Shared with: `styles` (clothing tastes) · `land-environment` (waste and reuse) · `economy` (household spending).
