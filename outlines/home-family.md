## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Native households were built around kin, and each nation's house fitted its climate and the way the family got its food. In many nations several related families shared one roof. Children learned work beside adults, and elders lived in the household.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four houses, four ways of living" -->
- The longhouse (Haudenosaunee and neighbors): bark over a post frame; 20 or more related families in the big ones — all related through the mothers. The eldest respected woman of the clan headed it; women owned the house and its goods; a husband moved into his wife's longhouse (NYS Museum; Haudenosaunee Confederacy).
- The pueblo: stone-and-adobe apartment blocks. Taos Pueblo's five-story North and South Houses were built about 1000–1450 and have been lived in ever since — over 1,000 years (UNESCO).
- The earth lodge (Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara on the upper Missouri): a timber dome packed with earth; women built, owned, and maintained it; 10 to 20 people inside; a lodge lasted about ten years (NPS Knife River).
- The wigwam (Eastern Woodlands: sapling frame, bark or mats) and the tipi (Plains: hide cover, made to pack up and follow the hunt).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A thin era for a new kind of American household: the newcomers of this century were mostly soldiers, priests, and sailors, not families. Native households continued as before, now hit by new diseases.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine's mixed households" -->
St. Augustine, founded 1565, is the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in the continental United States. Fewer than half its Spanish soldiers had wives, and many married Timucua and other Native women — mixed households from the start (NPS). The town's church records begin January 24, 1594: the oldest surviving parish records in the country, and the first place American household life gets written down name by name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Teresa and Francisco Camacho
> **Who:** a soldier, his Native wife, her sister, and her sister's son — one documented St. Augustine household. · **When and where:** 1580, St. Augustine, Florida.
Francisco came as a soldier in the 1560s; Teresa was a Native woman; the 1580 household held the two of them plus Teresa's sister Catalina and Catalina's son Juan (NPS Fort Matanzas).

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonial house is one room, everyone is in it, and nearly everyone in it works. The hearth at one end gives the heat, the light, and the cooking fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One room, then two" -->
The basic New England house was a single room (the "hall") plus a chimney bay; the step up was "hall and parlor" — hall for cooking and work, parlor for the best bed. One-room houses kept being built into the 1700s (Plymouth Colony Archive Project; First Period studies). Sleeping, eating, working, birth, and death all happened in the same space.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Big families, short childhoods" -->
New England families averaged about 8 births, with about 6 children living to grow up; in the Chesapeake about 1 baby in 4 died in infancy and many more before age 20 — most children there lost a parent before they were grown (EH.net; the regional split is real, keep both numbers). Servants lived inside the household as working members; enslaved people were increasingly held inside households too (the system is `slavery-freedom`'s). At about 14 — some younger — a child could leave home for good: apprenticed to a master until 21, and about two-thirds of indentures required the master to school the child in reading (EH.net; Gilder Lehrman). Whatever schooling most younger children got happened at a kitchen table (`education` leads the dame school).
Shared with: slavery-freedom (the system) · immigration (servants' arrival) · education (early schooling).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-ring" name="Mary Ring" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Ring
> **Who:** a Plymouth widow whose will and household inventory survive — one ordinary home, written down object by object. · **When and where:** died July 1631, Plymouth Colony; inventory recorded 1633.
Came to Plymouth a widow with three children; signed her own will; her recorded inventory lists her books (a Bible, a psalmbook, named religious titles) and money owed to her by William Bradford and Edward Winslow. All three children married in the colony (Pilgrim Hall Museum; Plymouth Colony Archive Project). The inventory is the window into what a one-room household actually contained.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For the better-off, the house starts having rooms with jobs. For most families, it is still one or two rooms, and the hearth is still the center of heat, light, cooking, and work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Georgian house" -->
Symmetry and matched windows; formal parlors and dining rooms toward the front, work spaces behind; a central hall as the spine (Met Museum). Separate rooms meant privacy — invented here for the few who could afford it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The hearth as the job center" -->
Open-hearth cooking, and a household that made much of what it used: cloth, candles, soap, butter and cheese, preserved food — chiefly women's and children's work (documented in Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's Good Wives, and day by day in Martha Ballard's diary next era).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-kemble-knight" name="Sarah Kemble Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Kemble Knight
> **Who:** Boston businesswoman who rode alone from Boston toward New Haven and New York and kept a journal of the households she slept in. · **When and where:** October 1704, on the road through Massachusetts, Connecticut, and New York; journal published 1825.
Her diary records the houses, inns, food, and families along the road — one of the few surviving pieces of American women's writing from the period, and a rare eyewitness tour of ordinary households (EBSCO; Connecticut History).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sarah-kemble-knight" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Frontier families lived in log cabins and settled families in farmhouses. Both households made most of what they used. When the Revolutionary War took men away, women ran the farms and the accounts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The log cabin: fact and myth" -->
The notched-corner log cabin came with the Swedish and Finnish settlers of New Sweden on the Delaware from 1638 — the Nothnagle Log House (about 1638–43) still stands in New Jersey. The English at Plymouth and Jamestown never built log cabins; the cabin became the standard first house of the frontier in this era and after (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Swedish Finn Historical Society).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Household production at its peak" -->
Spinning, weaving, candle- and soap-making, preserving — the house as a small factory run by women and children (Ulrich, Good Wives). Store-bought would start replacing homemade in the next century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="abigail-adams-home-family" name="Abigail Adams" movie="John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abigail Adams
> **Who:** ran the family farm, finances, and children's education through the whole Revolution while John was at Congress and in Europe. · **When and where:** 1774–1784, Braintree (Quincy), Massachusetts.
> **Movie:** John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries) — about John and the family; she is a lead character, not the subject.
Over 1,100 letters between them document the household: crops, tenants, wartime shortages, teaching the children, and the 1776 smallpox inoculation she arranged for the whole household (NPS Adams). Her "remember the ladies" letter is `rights-movements`' story; the household is ours.
Shared with: rights-movements (remember the ladies) · government-politics (John's career).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="abigail-adams-home-family" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="martha-ballard" name="Martha Ballard" movie="A Midwife's Tale (1997)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Martha Ballard
> **Who:** Maine midwife whose daily diary — 27 years, about 10,000 entries — is the best record of an ordinary American household's work. · **When and where:** Hallowell, Maine, diary kept January 1785–1812.
> **Movie:** A Midwife's Tale (1997, PBS American Experience) — a documentary about her.
816 deliveries, each named and numbered — and around them the household economy: garden, spinning, weaving, quilting, brewing, daughters' work, boarders, weather (DoHistory.org; Ulrich's Pulitzer-winning A Midwife's Tale).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In towns and cities, middle-class families began to treat home as a place apart from paid work, and childhood as a time to protect. Most farm and working families could not live this way. Enslavers could sell any member of an enslaved family on any day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The parlor and the cult of domesticity" -->
The middle-class ideal: the mother at home making a refuge from the working world, the parlor as the show room. Catharine Beecher's Treatise (1841) was its handbook (story below). Most farm and working households could not live this way — the ideal and the reality both go in.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The stove replaces the fire" -->
Cast-iron cookstoves spread from the 1830s. By 1850 most middle-class city homes cooked on a stove. Poorer and country homes changed later. For the woman cooking, the stove meant less bending and lifting, less smoke and no ash in the food. It also meant hard cleaning and a fire that was hard to control. In 1846 Henry David Thoreau complained that the stove hid the fire (Alice Ross, Encyclopedia of Food and Culture). The stove as an invention is `technology`'s. The changed kitchen is ours.
Shared with: technology (the stove as invention).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A child's day, and where school fits into it" -->
Chores came first: district schools ran a summer term (younger children, women teachers) and a winter term (older boys, spared from field work; men often hired to keep order), and attendance bent to planting and harvest (one-room-school histories). The teacher often lived with the students' families in turn — "boarding around" — so school reached into the house directly (`education` leads the school itself). Discipline at home ran on obedience and the rod, "spare the rod" the standard justification; advice manuals were beginning to urge calm, firm correction instead (the real shift came in the 1920s–30s).
Shared with: education (the school) · work-workers (children's labor).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Home under slavery" -->
Enslavers could sell any member of an enslaved family at any time, and the law gave enslaved people's marriages no standing. Between about 1790 and 1860 slave traders moved more than 1 million enslaved people from the Upper South to the Deep South, in chained lines called coffles and by ship to New Orleans. The Equal Justice Initiative counts that the trade separated nearly half of the people it moved from a husband, a wife or a parent. The system and the trade are `slavery-freedom`'s. This span tells what it meant for the household.
Shared with: slavery-freedom (lead — the system) · migration (the forced journeys).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="catharine-beecher-home-family" name="Catharine Beecher" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Catharine Beecher
> **Who:** wrote the book that told Americans how a home should run, and argued housekeeping was a profession to be studied. · **When and where:** born 1800, East Hampton, NY; Treatise on Domestic Economy published 1841; died 1878.
Founded the Hartford Female Seminary (1823); her Treatise (1841) — the first American book covering all of domestic life — went through some 15 editions; with her sister Harriet Beecher Stowe she later wrote The American Woman's Home (1869) (Britannica). Her teaching campaign is `education`'s story (their slug `catharine-beecher-education`).
Shared with: education (teaching as women's work).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="catharine-beecher-home-family" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="buffalo-bird-woman-home-family" name="Buffalo Bird Woman (Waheenee)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Buffalo Bird Woman (Waheenee)
> **Who:** a Hidatsa woman who grew up in her mothers' earth lodge and later told her life to the anthropologist Gilbert Wilson. · **When and where:** about 1839 to 1932. Born by the Knife River, raised at Like-a-Fishhook village on the Missouri, North Dakota. Interviewed every summer from 1906 to 1918.
- Born in an earth lodge about three years after the smallpox of 1837. The American Fur Company steamboat St. Peters had landed sick passengers at Fort Clark, near the villages, in June 1837. About 2,000 Mandans were alive that spring and 138 by October. Her great-grandmother told her that more than half of the Hidatsa died.
- Her father, Small Ankle, had four wives, all sisters: her mother Weahtee, Red Blossom, Stalk-of-Corn and Strikes-Many Woman. "I was taught to call all these my mothers." A grandmother and a great-grandmother lived with them.
- About 1845 the Mandan and Hidatsa chiefs moved their people together to Like-a-Fishhook Point. Smallpox broke out again in a winter camp. Her brother, her mother Weahtee and her aunt Stalk-of-Corn died.
- The women of a household built and owned the lodge. Her mothers' lodge measured more than 40 feet across, with the fire in the middle, her father's bed in front of it and the family's beds along the back wall.
- In the late 1880s, under the federal allotment rules of the Dawes Act, families took up separate allotments (family plots of reservation land) and the village emptied. By 1921 she lived "in a house with chimneys", and her son's wife cooked on a stove. She still rose at dawn to hoe the corn and sing to it.
- Source: her own telling, Waheenee: An Indian Girl's Story (1921). Her son Edward Goodbird drew the pictures for Wilson's books.
Shared with: native-nations (the smallpox, the reservation and allotment) · health (the epidemic).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The gap between homes widens: a tenement flat with the privy in the yard, a Victorian house with a bathroom inside, and on the plains a house cut from the ground itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement flat" -->
97 Orchard Street, New York, was built in 1863. About 7,000 people from more than 20 countries lived there over the years (Tenement Museum). Families had small rooms, took in boarders to make the rent, carried water and shared privies in the yard. The 1879 law required air shafts. Governor Theodore Roosevelt appointed a commission in 1900, and its secretary Lawrence Veiller helped write the 1901 law. It required a window in every room and a toilet in every new apartment. Old buildings had to add one toilet for every two families (Village Preservation). The building type and the city are `city-building`'s (their family is the Moores, 1869). The rooms and the toilet are ours.
Shared with: city-building (lead — the building and the city) · health (disease in crowded housing).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Victorian house and the help" -->
Rooms with jobs multiply for those who can pay; running water and indoor plumbing arrive unevenly, city first. Middle-class homes ran on paid "help": the 1870 census — the first to count women's work — found 867,354 women in domestic service, about 47 percent of all women in paid work, the biggest women's job in the country (Census comparative statistics).
Shared with: work-workers (domestic service as a job).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sod houses and dugouts" -->
On the treeless plains, families cut houses from sod or dug them into creek banks. The Ingalls family's dugout on Plum Creek near Walnut Grove, Minnesota (1874–76) is documented and the site preserved — a family of five in one earth room (walnutgrove.org). `migration` features Laura Ingalls Wilder for the family's moves; the houses are ours.
Shared with: migration (the moves — their story Laura Ingalls Wilder) · food-farming (the farms; their family the Oblingers).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The law reaches for the children" -->
Massachusetts passed the first compulsory school attendance law in 1852 — ages 8 to 14, twelve weeks a year (all states would have one by 1918). A household that needed a child's wages or hands now had the law on the other side; Native families had children taken to distant boarding schools — the house with the children gone (`native-nations` leads; `education` covers the schools).
Shared with: education (the laws and schools) · native-nations (lead — boarding schools).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nathalie-gumpertz" name="Nathalie Gumpertz" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nathalie Gumpertz
> **Who:** tenement mother of four who became the household's earner when her husband vanished. · **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, New York; Julius disappeared 1874; court declared him dead 1883.
During the depression that followed the Panic of 1873, Julius Gumpertz left for work one morning in 1874 and never came back. Nathalie raised four children on dressmaking done in the front room — about three dresses and 8 to 9 dollars a week. In 1883 a court declared Julius dead so she could claim a 600-dollar inheritance, and she moved the family uptown. Museum researchers later traced Julius to Cincinnati (Tenement Museum).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nathalie-gumpertz" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rachel-calof" name="Rachel Calof" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rachel Calof
> **Who:** came from Russia at 18 for an arranged marriage and wrote down what homestead housekeeping actually was. · **When and where:** near Devils Lake, North Dakota, 1894–1904; memoir written 1936, published 1995.
Her memoir records the 12-by-14-foot dirt-floored shanty; winters when Abraham's parents and brothers — and the chickens — shared the one room to save fuel; and her verdict that of all the privations, the lack of privacy was the hardest to bear (Rachel Calof's Story, Indiana University Press).

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Electric power and running water reached city homes first and farms last. The Depression cost families their houses, and federal officials rebuilt the home loan. After the war, builders put up the first mass-produced suburbs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Water, wires and washday" -->
Washday by hand took more than one day. Women soaked the clothes for a day or two, scrubbed them on a washboard, boiled them in soap and soda, rinsed them, hung them to dry and pressed them with irons heated over coals (NPS Fort Scott). In 1890, 24 households in 100 had running water and 8 had electricity. By 1950 the figures were 83 and 94 (Census, via Cardia). In 1940 nearly half of American homes still lacked hot and cold running water, a bathtub or shower, or a flush toilet. In about ten states the share was near 70 percent or more (Census Bureau). Homes with a mechanical refrigerator rose from 14 in 100 in 1930 to 44 in 1940 and 80 in 1950. Farms waited longest for power: fewer than 11 farms in 100 had it in 1935, and about 9 in 10 by 1950 (EH.net). The wires are `energy`'s and the machines are `technology`'s. Washday and the kitchen are ours.
Historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan argued in More Work for Mother (1983) that the machines mostly replaced the work of servants, children and men, while standards of cleanliness rose, so the housewife's own hours did not fall. That is her argument. The census count of domestic servants fell 32 percent between 1940 and 1950 (Cardia).
Shared with: energy (lead — the power) · technology (the appliances) · work-workers (housework as labor).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bungalow and the mail-order house" -->
The bungalow was the era's small family house. Sears sold whole houses by catalog, 1908–1940 — parts, plans, and nails; roughly 70,000–75,000 sold, with estimates running to 100,000 (no official count; state the range) (HUD User; Sears Modern Homes literature).
Shared with: marketplace (mail-order selling).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The mortgage remade" -->
Before the 1930s a home loan ran 5 to 10 years. The borrower paid only interest, then owed the whole amount at the end, and usually put about half down. When Depression-era borrowers could not pay, lenders foreclosed. The federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC, 1933) stretched loans to 15 years of equal payments. The Federal Housing Administration (FHA, 1934) insured 20- to 25-year loans with small down payments. After the war the 30-year loan became standard (Richmond Fed). A family could now buy a house by monthly payment. Between 1935 and 1940 HOLC staff, working with local lenders, appraisers and real estate agents, graded city neighborhoods from A (green) to D (red, "Hazardous"). FHA officials used similar maps and usually refused to back loans in Black neighborhoods, which they rated D. The maps are `city-building`'s and the campaign against them is `rights-movements`'.
Shared with: money (credit) · city-building (the maps) · rights-movements (redlining injustice).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Depression households" -->
Marriages postponed (10.1 per 1,000 people in 1929, 7.9 by 1932); the birthrate below replacement for the first time; families doubled up — young couples back with parents, grandparents moved in; by 1940, 1.5 million married women lived apart from their husbands (Encyclopedia.com). In Chicago's Bronzeville, landlords split flats into one-room "kitchenettes" — 80,000-plus of them, a family to a room, bath down the hall; Richard Wright's 12 Million Black Voices (1941) recorded the life (Chicago Public Library).
Shared with: economy (lead — the Depression) · migration (the Great Migration).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The teenager, and the war in the kitchen" -->
The word "teen-ager" reached print by 1941 (Popular Science) after older two-word uses, and caught on in the late 1940s — a stage of life created partly by children staying in high school instead of going to work, and named partly by advertisers (Time; word-history sources disagree on firsts; state the range). In the war, the ration book ran the kitchen (`food-farming` leads rationing) and households moved for war work.
Shared with: food-farming (lead — rationing) · education (high school).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### The Baldizzi family
> **Who:** a Sicilian immigrant family whose Depression-era tenement kitchen is preserved because the daughter came back and remembered it aloud. · **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, New York, 1928–1935.
Adolfo, a trained woodworker who could only find odd jobs; Rosaria, lining coats in a garment factory; Josephine and Johnny at the kitchen table with the radio and the cards, under a cabinet their father built. In 1935 the landlord, rather than pay for required upgrades, evicted the families and sealed the residential floors — the Baldizzis had to move. After the Tenement Museum opened (1988), Josephine recorded hours of oral history; the recreated apartment rests on her memory (Tenement Museum).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="levittown-family" name="Theodore and Patricia Bladykas" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Theodore and Patricia Bladykas
> **Who:** the first family to move into Levittown, with their 14-month-old twin daughters, Patricia Ann and Betty Ann. · **When and where:** October 1, 1947, 67 Bellmore Road, Levittown, Long Island, New York.
- A Newsday photographer took their picture on moving day. About 300 families moved in that first day.
- Rent was 60 dollars a month, with the right to buy the house for 7,500 dollars after a year. The 1949 ranch model sold for 7,990 dollars. Levitt and Sons built 17,447 houses by 1951. Many of the fathers were returning veterans.
- Clause 25 of the lease barred anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race" from living in the house. In 1948 the Supreme Court justices ruled that courts could not enforce such rules. William Levitt dropped the written clause and kept refusing to sell to Black families. In 1954 he told the Saturday Evening Post: "We can solve a housing problem, or we can solve a racial problem. But we cannot combine the two."
- The boundary rule puts 1947 in this era. The campaign against the exclusion is `rights-movements`'.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Home ownership rose from 43.6 percent of households in 1940 to 61.9 percent in 1960, and the suburban house became the common picture of family life. Lenders, builders and white neighbors kept Black families out of many of those suburbs, through loan maps, whites-only sales rules and, in Levittown, Pennsylvania, a mob. Inside the house, the television arrived first. Later, more mothers took paid jobs and more marriages ended in divorce.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Suburbia as the norm" -->
Homeownership rose from 43.6 percent of households (1940) to 61.9 percent (1960) (Census); 76 million babies were born 1946–1964 (Census); more Levittowns followed (Pennsylvania, 1952) and the ranch house spread. Redlining and covenant practice kept shutting families out long after the covenants became unenforceable — the fight to end it (through the Fair Housing Act, 1968) is `rights-movements`'; the family shut out, and the family who bought anyway, are ours.
Shared with: city-building (sprawl) · rights-movements (lead — the fight) · economy (the boom).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The TV in the living room" -->
Households with a television set: about 9 percent in 1950, 64.5 percent in 1955, 87 percent in 1960 (Sage; TVB). The furniture turned to face it and the evening rearranged around it.
Shared with: news-communication (broadcasting) · technology (the set).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The household changes shape" -->
Divorce more than doubled between 1960 and 1980 (9.2 to 22.6 per 1,000 married women), peaking in 1981, then easing (NBER/BLS). Married mothers in the labor force: about 17 percent in 1948, 61 percent in 1985, about 70 percent in 1995 (BLS). Day care grew with two-income households; counts of "latchkey" children home alone after school disagreed — a 1987 Census analysis said about 2.1 million children 5–13; advocacy estimates of the era said 6 to 7 million. State the disagreement.
Shared with: work-workers (women's paid work) · education (after-school time).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School comes home: homeschooling begins" -->
Two separate roots: John Holt's newsletter Growing Without Schooling (August 1977) on the secular school-reform side, and Raymond Moore (Home Grown Kids, 1981) on the religious side. Homeschooling was illegal or effectively illegal in many states into the 1980s; families taught at the kitchen table anyway, one parent giving up paid work, families forming groups (`education` leads the schooling and the legal fight; the household day is ours).

### Daisy and William Myers
> **Who:** the first Black family in Levittown, Pennsylvania — a veteran, a teacher, and three small children keeping house while a mob stood outside. · **When and where:** August 1957, Levittown, PA.
> **Movie:** Crisis in Levittown (1957) — a documentary filmed as it happened.
William, a Second World War Army veteran, and Daisy, a teacher, bought 43 Deepgreen Lane and moved in on August 13, 1957. Crowds of 200 to 1,000 local people gathered outside for more than a week. People in the crowds threw rocks through the windows and burned crosses, and callers threatened the family by phone. Milk and bread deliveries stopped and the insurance company canceled their fire insurance. Members of the Levittown Betterment Committee organized the campaign. Governor George Leader sent the state police, and a judge ordered the Betterment group to stop. Two people were convicted of burning a cross on the lawn of Lewis Wechsler, a white friend who had helped the Myerses buy the house. Neighbors and Quaker families brought food. The Myers family stayed until 1961. Daisy Myers wrote it down in Sticks 'n Stones (2005). The housing-discrimination fight is `rights-movements`'.
Shared with: rights-movements (the campaign) · crime-justice (the mob and the police).

### Clyde Ross
> **Who:** a Second World War veteran and soup-factory worker who could not get a mortgage and had to buy his Chicago house on contract. · **When and where:** 1961, North Lawndale, on Chicago's West Side.
- Born in 1923 near Clarksdale, Mississippi. He served on Guam in the war, came to Chicago in 1947 and worked as a taster at Campbell's Soup.
- He tried to get a mortgage in another neighborhood. A loan officer told him no financing was available. FHA officials used maps that rated Black neighborhoods "D", colored them red and usually refused to back loans there.
- In 1961 he bought a North Lawndale house for 27,500 dollars from a speculator who had paid 12,000 dollars for it. Under the contract the seller kept the deed until the last payment. Missing one payment meant losing the 1,000-dollar down payment, every monthly payment and the house. (Another account gives 1958 and 27,000 dollars.)
- One estimate from the period says 85 percent of Black home buyers in Chicago bought on contract.
- In 1968 he joined the Contract Buyers League and became its vice president. About 500 families stopped their payments together. By July 1971, 155 contracts had been renegotiated, with an average saving of 14,000 dollars per family. About 70 families were evicted.
- In 2014, at 91, he still lived in the house and owned it.
- Source: journalism, Ta-Nehisi Coates, "The Case for Reparations", The Atlantic, 2014, with The Chicago Reporter.
Shared with: rights-movements (the campaign against housing discrimination) · money (credit) · city-building (the maps).

### David and Micki Colfax and their sons
> **Who:** two former teachers who built a house by hand on a California ranch and taught their four sons at home. · **When and where:** from 1973, near Boonville, Mendocino County, California.
- Their eldest son, Grant, started public school in St. Louis. His parents took him out after six weeks because they found the lessons "very rote and uncreative".
- In 1973 they moved to land near Boonville with no house, no electricity and no running water (40 acres in one account, 50 in another). The parents and the young sons built the house, barns and sheds. They raised goats, sheep, chickens and pigs and sold the meat to San Francisco restaurants.
- Lessons fit around the ranch. Reed Colfax said some days they did a lot of homework and some days none, depending on the weather and the ranch work. They studied four or five days a week, summers included.
- Grant, Drew and Reed went to Harvard (classes of 1987, 1990 and 1992). The parents wrote Homeschooling for Excellence about their method.
- Source: The Harvard Crimson, March 16, 1989, and the Anderson Valley Advertiser, 2023 (journalism).
Shared with: education (lead on homeschooling as schooling and the legal fight).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Houses grew bigger while households grew smaller, and the 2008 foreclosure crisis and the 2020 pandemic both reached inside the home. Figures are current to 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="More house, fewer people" -->
The median new single-family house grew from 983 square feet in 1950 to a peak of 2,467 in 2015 (Census). The average household shrank from 3.67 people in 1940 to 2.50 in 2025. In 2025, 29 percent of households were one person living alone, up from 20 percent in 1975 (Census Bureau). In the second quarter of 2026, 65.0 percent of households owned their home (Census Bureau).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008 at the kitchen table" -->
About 3.8 million homes were foreclosed in 2007 to 2010. Estimates for the whole crisis run from about 7.7 million to 10 million homes lost, and the methods differ (Federal Reserve History, Chicago Fed). Countrywide Financial, run by its founder Angelo Mozilo, was the largest subprime lender, with at least 97.2 billion dollars in high-cost loans in 2005 to 2007 (Center for Public Integrity). Filings peaked at nearly 2.9 million homes in 2010. In 2025 there were 367,460 (ATTOM). Families moved in with relatives. The share of Americans in multigenerational households rose from 7 percent in 1971 to 18 percent, 59.7 million people, in 2021 (Pew).
Shared with: economy (lead — the crash) · money (mortgages and credit).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homelessness, counted" -->
On one night in January 2024, local volunteers counted 771,480 people homeless, the highest number recorded, up 18 percent in a year. Homelessness among families with children rose 39 percent that year (HUD). In January 2025 the count was 745,652, a drop of 3 to 3.4 percent depending on the source. People in families with children fell 11 percent, to 230,366. People counted as chronically homeless reached 155,750, the highest on record (HUD, National Low Income Housing Coalition).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2020: office and school at once" -->
The share of workers working mainly from home tripled, from 5.7 percent in 2019 to 17.9 percent in 2021 (Census ACS). In 2025 about 22 percent worked from home at least part of the time (Census data, via Minneapolis Fed). Homeschooling doubled in 2020, from 5.4 percent of households with school-age children in spring to 11.1 percent in fall. Among Black households it rose from 3.3 to 16.1 percent (Census Household Pulse). In 35 percent of lower-income households with school-age children there was no home broadband, against 6 percent of higher-income ones (Pew). In the 2022-23 school year parents reported that 5.2 percent of children aged 5 to 17 learned at home, including full-time online school, against 3.7 percent in 2018-19 (National Center for Education Statistics). The two surveys ask different questions, so they show the rise was partly lasting but cannot give an exact trend. `education` leads homeschooling as schooling.
Shared with: education (lead — remote school and homeschooling) · work-workers (remote work) · health (the pandemic).

### Pierre Solon and Katty Familia
> **Who:** immigrant parents, from Haiti and the Dominican Republic, who lost their Boston house to foreclosure. · **When and where:** about 2006 to 2010, Hyde Park, Boston, Massachusetts.
- They bought a green wooden house for 584,000 dollars and borrowed all of it. The loans came from Fremont Investment and Loan, a subprime lender (a lender to borrowers with weaker credit, at higher cost). Their lawyer said the two monthly mortgage payments came to almost 5,000 dollars a month more than their income.
- Each parent worked two jobs. Then each lost one. Katty Familia said: "Sometimes we don't even have food."
- They fell more than 150,000 dollars behind. Wells Fargo, which collected the payments, offered lower monthly payments on a debt of more than 600,000 dollars, and the couple said no. Wells Fargo's staff foreclosed, and the family moved out with their children in late 2009.
- In November 2010 Wells Fargo sold the house at auction to an investor for 115,000 dollars. Two weeks later he sold it for 270,000 dollars.
- The Massachusetts Attorney General sued Fremont for deceptive lending. Fremont went bankrupt.
- In 2010 the couple ran a day care center in Boston. Katty Familia: "I worked 20 years in this country for this."
- Source: journalism, NPR Morning Edition, Anthony Brooks, December 20, 2010.
Shared with: economy (lead on the crash) · money (mortgages and credit).

### The Munoz family
> **Who:** a Las Vegas family whose four school-age children did school online at the dining room table while both parents went out to work. · **When and where:** August 2020, Las Vegas, Nevada.
- The family had five children. Four were in school, all on laptops at the dining room table. Both parents worked in the food business.
- While the parents were at work, their 10th-grade son helped one younger child with reading and another with writing, and emailed teachers when he got stuck.
- On the second day the father, Johnathan Munoz, said the first day had been hard "because we couldn't log in". He recorded the family's first week as an audio diary for NPR.
- Source: journalism, NPR Weekend Edition Sunday, August 30, 2020.
Shared with: education (lead on remote school) · work-workers (parents who could not work from home).
