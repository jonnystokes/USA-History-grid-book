## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, every nation in this land had its own songs, and most music was made for a purpose: a ceremony, a healing, a dance, a game or a burial. Nobody wrote the music down, and no singer's or maker's name survives from these centuries. What survives is the instruments. Archaeologists have dug up flutes and panpipes that are more than a thousand years old. The nations kept their songs by teaching them, one generation to the next, and many of those songs are still sung.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Flutes and panpipes that survive" -->
- In 1931 the archaeologist Earl H. Morris dug in 15 cliff caves in the Prayer Rock district of northeastern Arizona, which is now part of the Navajo Nation. In the largest cave he found four wooden flutes and named the place Broken Flute Cave (Arizona State Museum).
- The flutes date from 620 to 670 CE. The Arizona State Museum, which holds them in Tucson, calls them the oldest known wooden flutes found in North America. The museum lists them as Ancestral Pueblo flutes, made by the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples (Arizona State Museum).
- Each flute is a tube of box elder wood about 69 to 74 centimeters long (a little over two feet), with six finger holes, played by blowing across one end. The finger holes are almost the same on each flute, which shows the maker planned the notes. Red, blue and black feathers were tied to one end (Arizona State Museum, *Arizona Highways*).
- Two flutes lay broken in trash above a grave. The other two lay under a dirt floor, whole and tied together (*Arizona Highways*).
- Farther east, people of the Hopewell culture built earthworks in the woodlands of eastern North America from 1 to 400 CE (National Park Service, Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, Ohio). Archaeologists have found 105 panpipes at 55 Hopewell sites, from Wisconsin and Ontario to the Gulf Coast. A panpipe here is three or four tubes of cane, reed or bone, bound in a jacket of hammered metal. Of the 102 jackets whose metal is known, 81 are copper and the rest are silver, copper covered with silver, or iron. Almost all were found in graves or burial mounds, not in houses (Turff and Carr, "Hopewellian Panpipes from Eastern North America," 2005).
- Nobody knows what tunes were played on these instruments. No written music exists from before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums, rattles, and songs that belong to someone" -->
- The Onondaga Nation, one of the Haudenosaunee nations of what is now New York, plays its songs on a water drum and a horn rattle. The drum is made of cedar with deer hide stretched over the top. The horn rattle is a dried, hollowed animal horn with seeds inside. A turtle-shell rattle is used only in a few ceremonies (Onondaga Nation website).
- The Onondaga keep two kinds of songs. Social songs can be sung anywhere. Ceremonial songs are sung only in the longhouse and are not for public viewing (Onondaga Nation website).
- In the Tlingit nations of what is now southeast Alaska, a clan owns its songs, stories, crests and ceremonial clothing. The Tlingit word for these things is *at.óow*, which means "something owned or purchased." The songs and stories that tell of a clan's losses in a place are, in the Tlingit view, the clan's deed to that place (National Park Service, Glacier Bay, "At.óowu: Tlingit Homeland").
- These rules come from the nations' own present-day accounts. No written record from before 1500 describes them, because nobody here wrote before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="r-carlos-nakai-music" name="R. Carlos Nakai" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### R. Carlos Nakai
> **Who:** A flute player of Navajo (Diné) and Ute descent who plays a traditional cedar flute. · **When and where:** Born April 16, 1946, in Flagstaff, Arizona. Still performing in 2026 (his own 2026 concert calendar).
- No flute player's name survives from before 1500. Nakai plays the same kind of instrument today and has recorded more than forty albums with Canyon Records in Phoenix, starting with *Changes* in 1983 (Nakai's own biography, rcarlosnakai.com).
- He first studied the trumpet. A car accident ruined his embouchure, the way a trumpet player shapes the lips against the mouthpiece, and he could no longer play it. He was given a cedar flute as a gift and challenged to master it (Nakai biography).
- His albums *Canyon Trilogy* and *Earth Spirit* were the first recordings of the traditional Native flute to sell 500,000 copies each. In 2014 *Canyon Trilogy* passed one million copies. He has been nominated for ten Grammy awards between 1994 and 2013 (Nakai biography).
- His father, Raymond Nakai, was chairman of the Navajo Nation from 1963 to 1970 (Wikipedia).
- Nakai calls himself "an iconoclastic traditionalist." He means that he treats his heritage as something that changes and grows, not something frozen (Nakai biography).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s almost all music in this land was still Native music. European music arrived only where European ships landed, and it came as church singing: Spanish priests singing Latin hymns, and French and English Protestants singing psalms, which are songs from the Bible. The Europeans sang these hymns and psalms when they claimed land and when they held services. Some Native people who watched and listened left their reactions in the written record. No European music was printed or taught in a school here in this century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns sung while claiming land, Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
- In June 1564 French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline on the St. Johns River, near today's Jacksonville, Florida, on Timucua land. Their leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on 30 June he ordered the men to sing aloud on the beach to give thanks to God for their safe arrival (Laudonnière, *L'histoire notable de la Floride*, quoted by the National Huguenot Society). The historian Francis Parkman wrote in 1865 that on the morning they began the fort, the men sang a psalm before they started work (Parkman, *Pioneers of France in the New World*).
- On 8 September 1565 the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at the Timucua town of Seloy and founded St. Augustine. His chaplain, Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, wrote that Menéndez came ashore "to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery," and that he himself walked to meet him with a cross, "singing the hymn Te Deum laudamus," a Latin hymn of praise. "A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done" (Mendoza Grajales, his account of the founding).
- Menéndez's soldiers took Fort Caroline on 20 September 1565 and killed most of the men there. When Menéndez came back to St. Augustine, Mendoza and his companions walked ahead of him "in a procession, singing the Te Deum laudamus" (Mendoza Grajales). In the weeks after, Menéndez had about 245 more French prisoners killed at Matanzas Inlet (NPS Fort Matanzas counts). `war` tells the killings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalms at Drake's landing, California, 1579" -->
- In June 1579 the English captain Francis Drake stopped on the coast north of San Francisco Bay to repair his ship. The land belonged to the Coast Miwok people. Drake's men held prayers, sang psalms and read from the Bible while Miwok people watched (*The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake*, London, 1628, drawn mainly from the notes of Drake's chaplain, Francis Fletcher, per Heizer).
- The account says the Miwok "tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes, that whensoeuer they resorted to vs, their first request was Gnaah, by which they intreated that we would sing." The anthropologist Robert Heizer found that *gnaah* is a Coast Miwok word (Heizer, *Francis Drake and the California Indians, 1579*, 1947).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
- In 1598 Juan de Oñate led about 500 colonists north across the Rio Grande to found a Spanish colony in New Mexico, among the Pueblo peoples. At a ceremony on 30 April 1598, near today's El Paso, a Te Deum was sung and Franciscan priests said Mass (Wikipedia, "La Toma," from Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá's 1610 *Historia de la Nueva México*) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The music historian John Koegel writes that Pueblo people began to hear Spanish military music and to sing Catholic chant and part-songs "during the Oñate expedition and its aftermath" (Koegel, "Music and Christianization on the Northern Frontier of New Spain"). The teaching that is documented by name belongs to the 1600s.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s the colonists' music that was written down was church music. In New England, Puritan congregations sang psalms from a book printed in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640, and for most of the century that book printed only the words. In New Mexico, Spanish friars built organs and trained Pueblo singers to sing Latin church music. The same friars and Spanish governors banned Pueblo religious ceremonies and punished the Pueblo leaders who kept them. Africans were brought to the English colonies from 1619, and they brought their own music, but almost nothing about it was written down in this century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bay Psalm Book, 1640, and lining out" -->
- *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre*, now called the *Bay Psalm Book*, was printed by Stephen Daye at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640. It is the first book printed in British North America. Richard Mather and other New England ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into rhyming verse so that congregations could sing them. Eleven copies of the first printing survive (Library of Congress, in `religion` and `technology` banks).
- The 1640 book had no printed music. It named tunes that people already knew from English psalm books. The ninth edition, printed in 1698, was the first to include music, 13 tunes taken from an English music book by John Playford (Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music," Wikipedia).
- Most people in a congregation had no book, and some could not read. In 1644 an assembly of English and Scottish ministers at Westminster wrote a guide for worship. It said that where many could not read, the minister or another person he chose should "read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof" (*Directory for the Public Worship of God*, 1644).
- This practice is called lining out. A leader read or sang one line, and the congregation sang it back. Colonists from England and Scotland brought it to America. Old Regular Baptist churches in the Appalachian Mountains still sing this way today (Wikipedia, "Lining out").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" name="Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones
> **Who:** A Spanish Franciscan friar who built the church at San Felipe Pueblo, put an organ in it, and trained Pueblo singers. · **When and where:** San Felipe Pueblo, a Keres-speaking town in New Mexico. Died in 1609.
- Quiñones learned the Keres language, which the people of San Felipe speak, and baptized many people there. He was the custos, the head of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico (Koegel, quoting Agustín de Vetancurt's *Menologio Franciscano*).
- He built the church and the friars' house at San Felipe Pueblo, with a pharmacy and a place to care for the sick. Vetancurt wrote that he "installed an organ to enrich the Divine Service and encouraged music-making by the skilled singers" he trained (Koegel).
- The music historian John Koegel calls him "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music" living in what is now the US Southwest (Koegel).
- Neither Vetancurt nor Koegel records where he was born or what the San Felipe singers thought of the music they were taught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church music and banned ceremonies in New Mexico" -->
- Fray Bernardo de Marta came to New Mexico about 1605 and taught Pueblo people in many towns to sing and to play European instruments. He died in 1635. By 1634 Fray Roque de Figueredo was teaching part-singing to Zuni boys (Koegel).
- From 1656 to 1665, the Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered friars to go into the kivas, the Pueblo ceremonial rooms, to burn the masks and to take or destroy sacred objects (Dennis Zotigh, National Museum of the American Indian, 2026, in `religion` bank).
- In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested and charged with sorcery. Three were hanged, one killed himself, and the rest were whipped in public (Zotigh, in `religion` and `holidays` banks). Pueblo people drove the Spanish out of New Mexico in the revolt of 1680 and killed about 400 settlers, 21 of them Franciscans (Koegel). `native-nations` and `religion` tell the revolt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
- In late August 1619 the English privateer ship *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes," in the colonist John Rolfe's words, at Point Comfort, Virginia. They had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship and came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa (`slavery-freedom` bank, from the National Park Service). `slavery-freedom` tells the arrival.
- Enslaved Africans brought their own songs and ways of making instruments. Records from the 1600s mainland colonies say almost nothing about that music. The first written description of a banjo-like instrument in the Americas comes from Jamaica, where the English doctor Hans Sloane wrote about a "strum strump" in 1687 (OUP blog, "10 fun facts about the banjo," 2014). The first mention on the mainland is from 1736 and belongs to the next era.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, New England ministers set out to teach their congregations to sing from written notes, and they started the first singing schools and printed the first books of music lessons in the colonies. In Boston people could buy a ticket to a concert from 1731. In the plantation colonies, enslaved Black musicians played the fiddle for white dances, and the only written records of many of them are the newspaper notices their enslavers printed when they ran away. After enslaved men rose up at Stono in 1739 with drums beating, the men who ran South Carolina made it a crime for enslaved people to use drums and horns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Regular singing and the first singing schools, 1720s" -->
- By about 1700, most New England congregations sang by lining out and by ear. The historian Henry Wilder Foote wrote that by the end of the 1600s "singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies." In 1720 the Reverend Thomas Symmes of Bradford, Massachusetts, published *The Reasonableness of Regular Singing, or Singing by Note*. He wrote that singing had declined because "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn" (Henry Wilder Foote, "Musical Life in Boston in the Eighteenth Century," American Antiquarian Society, 1939).
- The Reverend John Tufts of Newbury, Massachusetts, published *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes*, the first book of music lessons printed in the English colonies. Foote dates the first edition to about 1712, and other sources date it to about 1721. No copy of the first edition is known, so the exact date is disputed. Tufts travelled to give talks and to set up singing schools (Foote).
- In 1721 the Reverend Thomas Walter, who was 25, published *The Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained*, with 24 psalm tunes. James Franklin printed it in Boston. It was the first music in the colonies printed with bar lines, and it stayed in use in New England singing schools for more than forty years (Foote. Matt B. Jones, American Antiquarian Society, 1932).
- Young people took up the new singing first. On 20 March 1721 the Boston judge Samuel Sewall wrote in his diary that Cotton Mather preached at night "in the school house to the young musicians." "House was full, and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston" (Sewall's diary, quoted by Foote).
- Older church members fought the change. Symmes reported that "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." In 1728 the Reverend Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut answered the objections. One was that singing by note "leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly." Another was that the new way must be wrong "because the young people fall in with it" (Foote).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concerts for a ticket" -->
- The *Boston News-Letter* of December 1731 advertised a "Concert of Music" on several instruments for 30 December 1731 at Mr. Pelham's great room, with tickets at five shillings each. It is the earliest advertised concert in Boston that the historian Oscar Sonneck found (Foote, citing Sonneck, *Early Concert Life in America*).
- After a concert at Faneuil Hall in 1744, the town's selectmen received £205 for the poor of Boston (Foote).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and the enslaved musicians who played them" -->
- The banjo came from West Africa. Enslaved Africans rebuilt it in the Americas. Scholars have found more than 60 West African plucked instruments that may be its ancestors (OUP blog, "10 fun facts about the banjo," 2014).
- The first known mention of a banjo in the mainland colonies is a letter in John Peter Zenger's *New-York Weekly Journal* in 1736 (OUP blog). The letter describes an enslaved servant tuning his "banger." It was printed without a name and may have been made up (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Enslavers in Virginia printed notices in the *Virginia Gazette* when enslaved people ran away. The notices describe each person's body, clothes and skills so that strangers could catch them. Playing the fiddle was one of the skills they listed. On 5 December 1745 Robert Whitfield of Isle of Wight County offered four pistoles, a Spanish gold coin, for "a likely young Negroe Man, named Tom," who "plays very well on the Violin." In April 1746 John Thornton of Fredericksburg advertised for a man named Tom, about 46, who "plays on the Violin, and is a Sawyer" (*Virginia Gazette*, in the University of Virginia's Geography of Slavery database).
- These notices are the only records that survive of many enslaved musicians. They survive because the men ran.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Harry, a fiddler who ran to his wife, 1746
> **Who:** An enslaved man who played the fiddle. His enslaver advertised for his capture. · **When and where:** Near Jamestown, Virginia, March 1746.
- In March 1746 Harry ran away from the man who held him, near Jamestown, Virginia. The notice gives him no last name (*Virginia Gazette*, 20 to 27 March 1746).
- The notice describes Harry as about 35 years old, about 5 feet 7 inches tall, with small eyes and "a very large Beard," and says he "plays upon the Fiddle." He had once belonged to Colonel Grymes of Richmond County.
- The advertiser, William Newgent (a later reprint spells his first name James), guessed that Harry had gone to Richmond County, "where he has a Wife." He offered one pistole, a gold coin, for Harry's return.
- Newgent added: "As he ran away without any Cause, I desire he may be punish'd by Whipping, as the Law directs."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harry-fiddler-music" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums at Stono, and the 1740 law against drums and horns" -->
- On 9 September 1739 about 20 enslaved men, whom a report of the time called "Angola Negroes," led by a man called Jemmy, seized guns near the Stono River, south of Charleston, South Carolina, and marched south toward Spanish Florida. The report says that more men joined them, "calling out Liberty," and "marched on with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating." When there were sixty to a hundred of them, they stopped in a field and "set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them" ("An Account of the Negroe Insurrection in South Carolina," 1739, a report attributed to James Oglethorpe, the governor of Georgia).
- The same report says that the rebels killed white men, women and children as they marched. The militia, made up of armed planters, attacked them in the field. The planters shot many of the captured men on the spot, including men who ran back to their plantations. The report counts about 40 Black people and 20 white people killed. Other counts give about 25 white colonists and 35 to 50 rebels (`slavery-freedom` bank). `slavery-freedom` tells the uprising.
- On 10 May 1740 the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. Section 36 said it was "absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province" to stop enslaved people from "using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments, which may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes" (Negro Act of 1740, section 36, US History Scene). The section is confirmed on the page, and the exact wording is from search results (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The law fined any master or overseer who allowed enslaved people to beat drums, blow horns or gather in public ten pounds for each offence. It allowed any master, overseer or other person to seize an enslaved person found off the plantation without a written pass and without a white person, especially on Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays. A person found carrying a weapon could be disarmed and whipped (Negro Act, section 36, via US History Scene).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, people in the colonies began to publish music they had written themselves. The best known was William Billings, a Boston tanner, who printed his first book of church songs in 1770. During the Revolution, soldiers marched and camped to the orders of fifes and drums, and both armies sang new words to old tunes. Enslaved and free Black musicians played fiddles, horns, drums and banjos in the port towns and on plantations. Newport Gardner, taken from Africa as a boy, became a music teacher in Newport, Rhode Island, and bought his freedom in 1791. On the Pacific coast in the same year, a Spanish expedition wrote down Tlingit songs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="William Billings and the New England singing masters" -->
- In 1770 William Billings, a 24-year-old Boston tanner, published *The New-England Psalm-Singer*. The Library of Congress calls it "the first publication consisting of sacred music composed entirely by a native-born American" (Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music"). Paul Revere, the silversmith, engraved the picture at the front (David W. Stowe, The Conversation, 2026).
- Billings opened a singing school in Boston with John Barry in 1769 and taught one in Stoughton, Massachusetts, in 1774 (Wikipedia, "William Billings").
- The historian Henry Foote wrote in 1939 that about forty American books of church music were published before 1800, most of them in Boston (Foote, American Antiquarian Society).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-billings" name="William Billings" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Billings
> **Who:** A tanner and singing teacher who wrote about 340 songs for choirs. His 1770 book was the first book of church music written entirely by an American-born composer. · **When and where:** Boston, born October 7, 1746, died September 26, 1800.
- Billings's father died when he was 14, and Billings went to work learning to tan leather. He taught himself music, mostly from books (Wikipedia, "William Billings").
- The music historian Nathaniel Gould wrote in 1853 that Billings had "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered" (quoted by Stowe, The Conversation, 2026).
- His first book, *The New-England Psalm-Singer* (1770), included his song "Chester." Its first words are "Let tyrants shake their iron rod, And Slav'ry clank her galling chains, We fear them not, we trust in God, New England's God forever reigns." In 1778 he printed a longer version with more verses against the British in his second book, *The Singing Master's Assistant* (Scott Giltner, "Chester," Sons of the American Revolution, 2024).
- Soldiers "learned the words by heart" and carried the songs wherever they were sent (Stowe).
- He published six tune books. American copyright law was weak, and other printers copied his tunes without paying him. The men of the Massachusetts legislature once passed a bill to protect his work, but the governor refused to sign it (Stowe. Wikipedia).
- His wife, Lucy, died on March 26, 1795, and left him with six children under 18. Friends paid to print his last book, *The Continental Harmony*, in 1794. In the 1790s he worked as a street cleaner (Wikipedia. Stowe).
- He died poor in Boston in 1800 and was buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common (Stowe).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-billings" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs and signals of the Revolution" -->
- "Yankee Doodle" began as a British song that made fun of the colonists. The words are said to have been written by a British army surgeon, Richard Shuckburgh, around 1755 to 1758, during the French and Indian War. The date is disputed (Wikipedia, "Yankee Doodle").
- When British soldiers arrived to occupy Boston in 1768, their musicians played it in the streets to mock the colonists. Early in the war, British and Loyalist troops played it to make fun of the Continental soldiers. The Americans then made up their own verses, and by 1781 it was a favorite American song (Scott Giltner, "Yankee Doodle," Sons of the American Revolution, 2024).
- In July 1768 the *Boston Gazette* printed "The Liberty Song," written by John Dickinson of Pennsylvania to the tune of a British navy song, "Heart of Oak." Dickinson sent it to his friend James Otis and wrote that "indifferent songs are very powerful on certain occasions" (Massachusetts Historical Society).
- In the Continental Army, drums gave orders. "The Reveille" woke the soldiers in the morning. "The General" told them to take down their tents and get ready to march. "The Assembly" told them to line up at the flags. "To Arms" told them to get their weapons and prepare to fight. "The Tattoo," beaten at 9 or 10 at night, told tavern keepers to stop serving the soldiers (American Battlefield Trust, "Music in the 18th Century Army").
- The American Battlefield Trust writes that boys under 14 were recommended as drummers, and that some fifers were 9 or 10 years old.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black musicians, enslaved and free" -->
- Virginia enslavers kept printing notices that named enslaved musicians. On 4 May 1769 William Gregory advertised for Peter, about 44, who had taken "a fiddle, which he is much delighted in." On 8 September 1775 David Walker of Dinwiddie County advertised for Jemmy, who "is very fond of singing hymns and preaching" and was living near Williamsburg under the name James Williams (*Virginia Gazette*, in the University of Virginia's Geography of Slavery database).
- In August 1780 William Allason offered forty pounds for four men who had run away together from near Fauquier Court House, Virginia. One was Mark, about 25, born on the island of St. Jago (Santiago, in the Cape Verde Islands), who spoke Portuguese. The notice says he "blows the French horn, can play on the fiddle, whistles many tunes well," and loved marches and Catholic church music. Allason guessed that the four men planned to get aboard a ship (*Virginia Gazette*, 9 August 1780, Geography of Slavery database).
- In Charleston, South Carolina, a newspaper notice of 12 December 1766 describes "a croud of negroes assembled at the lower end of Elliott-street, with a banjer playing," on the evening of 2 December. A Charleston town rule of 1750 banned beating drums in town without an officer's permission (Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library, "A 'Banjer' on the Bay of Charleston in 1766," 2021).
- A watercolor painting called *The Old Plantation*, made between about 1785 and 1795, shows enslaved Black men and women dancing. One man plays an early gourd banjo, another plays a drum, and two women hold rattles. It is the earliest known painting of a banjo. In 2010 the librarian Susan Shames identified the painter as John Rose, a South Carolina planter who held people in slavery (Wikipedia, "The Old Plantation". Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library). The Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia, owns it.
- Thomas Jefferson, who held people in slavery at Monticello (`slavery-freedom` bank, from Monticello.org), wrote about Black musicians in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1787 edition). He wrote that "In music they are more generally gifted than the whites," and that "Whether they will be equal to the composition of a more extensive run of melody, or of complicated harmony, is yet to be proved." In a note he wrote, "The instrument proper to them is the Banjar, which they brought hither from Africa" (Jefferson, *Notes*, Query XIV). In the same passage he wrote that Black people had "no poetry" and dismissed the poems of Phillis Wheatley.
- In New England, Black communities held their own election days, when they chose a "king" or "governor," with parades that had "tambourines, banjos, fiddles and drums" (New England Historical Society). `holidays` tells the election days.

### Newport Gardner
> **Who:** Born Occramer Marycoo in West Africa, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, where he became a music teacher and composer. · **When and where:** Born about 1746. Newport, from about 1760. Died in Liberia in 1826.
- Occramer Marycoo was about 14 when slave traders brought him to Newport. Caleb Gardner, a merchant and ship captain, bought him and renamed him Newport Gardner (Sophie Lowe, Early Music America, 2023. John Millar, *Online Review of Rhode Island History*, 2016). Lowe writes that Caleb Gardner "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings" through the slave trade.
- He learned English and music in Newport. Many writers name the composer Andrew Law as his teacher, but Law is first known in Rhode Island in 1783, when Gardner was in his late thirties. Lowe concludes that he "probably had a variety of musical teachers" (Lowe).
- In late December 1791 Gardner and friends won a lottery. He used his share to buy freedom for himself and his family (Millar. Congregational Library, on Edward Andrews's book *Newport Gardner's Anthem*, Cornell University Press).
- He opened a school in Newport where he taught music (Millar). He was a deacon of Newport's First Congregational Church and later helped found a Black church, which Millar dates to 1824.
- A song called "Crooked Shanks," printed in 1803, is often credited to him. Lowe reports newer research finding the same tune in print in London in 1768, so the credit is uncertain. The words of his anthem survive: "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race. Hear the words of promise" (Lowe).
- He led about thirty to three dozen Black New Englanders to Liberia, sailing in late December 1825 or on 4 January 1826 (the sources differ). He died there of disease within months, in 1826 (Millar. Lowe. Congregational Library).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="newport-gardner" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tlingit songs written down, 1791" -->
- In 1791 two Spanish ships of an expedition led by Alejandro Malaspina and José de Bustamante anchored in Yakutat Bay, in what is now Alaska, on Tlingit land. Tlingit people sang "to and with" the Spanish sailors several times (Sam Anderson, ExplorersWeb, 2022).
- Tadeo Haenke, a botanist on the ships who had trained in music, wrote the songs down in musical notes on four sheets of paper. The sheets stayed in the Museo de América in Madrid for more than 200 years. The Tlingit ethnomusicologist Maria Shaa Tláa Williams studied them for the Sealaska Heritage Institute and found that the singers used harmony. The Tlingit scholar Judy Daxootsu Ramos said, "we're still practicing that today" (Anderson).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1800 to 1850, enslaved Black people in the South made songs at work, at worship and on their few days off, and some of them later wrote down what those songs meant. In the same years, white performers blacked their faces with burnt cork and sang and danced as Black characters who were lazy, foolish and happy to be enslaved. This blackface minstrel show became one of the most popular kinds of entertainment in the country. Stephen Foster, a white songwriter from Pittsburgh, wrote many of its best-known songs. A free Black bandleader in Philadelphia, Francis Johnson, published more than 200 pieces of music. In the South, singers learned church music from books printed in shape notes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the singers said the songs meant" -->
- Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland, until he escaped in 1838. In 1845 he wrote that enslaved people sang as they walked to collect their monthly food. They made up the words as they went. He wrote that people in the North who heard this singing as a sign that enslaved people were happy made a mistake: "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy." He added, "I have often sung to drown my sorrow, but seldom to express my happiness" (Douglass, *Narrative*, 1845).
- Douglass also wrote that enslavers wanted to hear singing. Overseers shouted "Make a noise" at workers who went quiet, because the singing told them where each worker was and that the work was going on (Douglass, *My Bondage and My Freedom*, 1855).
- Some songs had a second meaning. In 1836 Douglass and five men he names, Henry Harris, John Harris, Sandy Jenkins, Charles Roberts and Henry Bailey, planned to escape. They kept singing a hymn with the words "I am bound for the land of Canaan." Douglass wrote that "the north was our Canaan." Someone told the plan to the enslaver, and it failed (Douglass, 1855).
- At Christmas, enslaved people on the Maryland farms played fiddles and beat out rhythms on their own bodies with their hands, called "juba" beating. One song Douglass wrote down lists the work they did and what they got for it: "We raise de wheat, Dey gib us de corn. We bake de bread, Dey gib us de cruss" (Douglass, 1855).
- Harriet Tubman escaped from Maryland in 1849 and went back to lead others north. She told the writer Sarah Bradford how she used hymns as signals. People hiding in the woods came out only when she sang a certain hymn twice. If she sang "Moses go down in Egypt," they stayed hidden, "for dere's danger in de way" (Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman*, 1869).
- A different claim is that the song "Follow the Drinking Gourd" was a map that led people from Alabama to the Ohio River. That claim comes from one article, printed in 1928 by H. B. Parks, a Texas insect scientist who collected songs. The best-known words, including "the old man is awaiting for to carry you to freedom," were written by the folk singer Lee Hays in 1947. The researcher Joel Bresler found the song was never collected in Alabama and found no older use of "drinking gourd" to mean the Big Dipper. He and the scholar James Kelley doubt that it was ever a map (Bresler, "Follow the Drinking Gourd: A Cultural History").
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (Douglass's and Tubman's escapes, worship).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Congo Square, New Orleans" -->
- In 1817 the mayor of New Orleans limited the gatherings of enslaved people to one open space behind the old city, later called Congo Square, on Sunday afternoons. They sold goods, sang, danced and played music there (Wikipedia, "Congo Square". New Orleans Historical).
- In 1819 the architect Benjamin Latrobe wrote in his journal that he saw 500 to 600 enslaved people dancing there. He called it "savage" (Wikipedia, "Congo Square").
- City officials stopped the Sunday music and dancing in 1835. It started again, and officials stopped it again in 1851. No record names those officials (New Orleans Historical, "Congo Square: Mythology and Music").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blackface minstrelsy, from about 1830" -->
- Blackface minstrelsy was a stage show in which white men darkened their faces with burnt cork and acted as Black people. The characters they played were lazy, stupid, greedy and happy to be enslaved. White audiences paid to laugh at them (Jim Crow Museum, Ferris State University).
- Thomas Dartmouth Rice, a white actor born in New York in 1808, began singing and dancing a song called "Jump Jim Crow" in blackface. The Jim Crow Museum dates his first performance to 1828. Other sources say he had made it his main act by 1832. Rice said he copied a disabled Black man who cared for horses and sang as he worked, and that he bought the man's clothes to wear on stage. Historians doubt parts of this story, and no record names the man (Wikipedia, "Thomas D. Rice". Jim Crow Museum).
- Rice played to sold-out theaters from about 1832 to 1844 and took the act to London in 1836. By 1838, white Americans were using "Jim Crow" as an insult for Black people. By the end of the 1800s, "Jim Crow" was the name for the laws and customs that kept Black people separate from white people and took away their rights (Jim Crow Museum).
- On January 31, 1843, four white men in New York, Dan Emmett, Billy Whitlock, Dick Pelham and Frank Brower, performed as the Virginia Minstrels. Earlier blackface acts had one singer or dancer. The Virginia Minstrels performed as a group, with fiddles, banjos, bones and tambourines (Wikipedia, "Virginia Minstrels". Jim Crow Museum).
- From 1845 Edwin P. Christy's Christy Minstrels set the form most later shows copied: the players sat in a half circle, with a joker at each end and a leader in the middle (Jim Crow Museum).
- The money went to the white performers and to the music publishers. Rice made a fortune, although he died poor in 1860. No record names a Black musician who was paid for the songs and dances the minstrels said they copied (Wikipedia, "Thomas D. Rice").
- Shared with: `storytelling-evolution` (the show, the makeup and the caricature on stage).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The banjo goes on stage" -->
- Enslaved Africans and their children made and played the first banjos in America (see 1700 to 1750). Joel Walker Sweeney, a white man born in 1810 in Appomattox County, Virginia, learned to play from enslaved people there. In April 1839 he played the banjo on a stage in New York, the earliest known time anyone did. From then on, white minstrels played the banjo in blackface shows (Wikipedia, "Joel Sweeney").
- Many books say Sweeney added the banjo's fifth string. There is no proof of it, and older banjos and pictures already show the short fifth string (Wikipedia, "Joel Sweeney").

### Stephen Foster
> **Who:** A white songwriter who wrote songs for blackface minstrel shows and for singing at home, and one of the first Americans to earn a living only by writing songs. · **When and where:** Born July 4, 1826, in Lawrenceville, Pennsylvania, now part of Pittsburgh. Worked in Pittsburgh, Cincinnati and New York. Died January 13, 1864, in New York.
- Foster's family held a Black girl named Kitty as a bound servant with more than three years left to serve. In 1834 his father wrote that a neighbor had given her to the family as "a present," and that it saved them the cost of hiring a girl (University of Pittsburgh, Center for American Music).
- At nine, Foster sang blackface songs such as "Jim Crow" in a boys' theater in a carriage house and was paid a small sum each week. He learned the style mostly from songs that white performers wrote to mock Black people (University of Pittsburgh).
- His song "Oh! Susanna" was first advertised in Pittsburgh in September 1847 and printed as a song of a blackface troupe. At the time no American song had sold more than 5,000 copies of sheet music, and "Oh! Susanna" sold more than 100,000. Its first words used a racist insult for Black people and joked about an explosion that killed 500 of them (Wikipedia, "Oh! Susanna". University of Pittsburgh).
- From 1850 the minstrel leader Edwin P. Christy paid Foster $10 a song to sing each new song first. For "Old Folks at Home" (1851) Christy paid $15, and the sheet music named Christy as the writer. When Foster asked in 1852 to have his own name put back, Christy refused (Center for American Music, "Old Folks at Home").
- In 1852 Foster wrote to Christy that he had changed the words of his songs to suit "refined people," instead of the "trashy and really offensive words" of other minstrel songs. The Pittsburgh historians write that he most likely did this to sell more sheet music. His only political songs before the Civil War supported the Democrats who opposed ending slavery, including "The Abolition Show" (1856) (University of Pittsburgh).
- Frederick Douglass said in 1855 that songs such as "Old Kentucky Home" and "Uncle Ned" could make listeners feel for enslaved people. The Hutchinson Family sang "Old Folks at Home" at their antislavery concerts (University of Pittsburgh).
- About 1857 Foster sold his rights to future payments on his songs for quick cash. He moved to New York in 1860 and kept writing, including more minstrel songs. In January 1864 he fell in his hotel room and cut his neck and face. He died at Bellevue Hospital three days later, at 37, with 37 cents in paper money and three pennies in his wallet (University of Pittsburgh. Wikipedia, "Stephen Foster").
- The words people sing now were changed by others. In 1986 two Kentucky lawmakers, Representative Carl Hines and Senator Georgia Davis Powers, won votes to sing "people" in place of a racist word in "My Old Kentucky Home," the state song (Wikipedia, "My Old Kentucky Home").
<!-- hb-story:end slug="stephen-foster" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church songs: Richard Allen's hymn book, camp meetings and shape notes" -->
- In 1801 Richard Allen, a Philadelphia minister who had been born enslaved, put together a hymn book of 54 hymns for his Black Methodist congregation. It printed words and no music (Hymnology Archive). In 1816 Allen formally organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church (`slavery-freedom` bank).
- `religion` tells the outdoor revival meetings called camp meetings, such as the one at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in the summer of 1801.
- In 1801 William Little and William Smith printed a music book in Philadelphia called *The Easy Instructor*. It used shape notes, a way of printing music in which each note has a shape, such as a triangle or a square, that tells the singer which sound to sing. Singing schools used shape notes to teach people who could not read ordinary written music (Wikipedia, "Shape note").
- In 1844 two Georgia men, Benjamin Franklin White and Elisha J. King, published *The Sacred Harp*, a shape-note book of more than 250 songs. Some of its oldest songs are by William Billings (see 1750 to 1800). Sacred Harp singers sit in a square facing each other and sing in four parts with no instruments. Singers still use the book, and a new edition was printed in 2025 (Wikipedia, "Sacred Harp").

### Francis Johnson
> **Who:** A free Black bandleader, bugle player and composer in Philadelphia who published more than 200 pieces of music. · **When and where:** Born June 16, 1792, in Philadelphia. Died there April 6, 1844.
- Johnson grew up in a free Black neighborhood in Philadelphia. By 15 he could play the flute, violin, bugle and piano, and he played the fiddle for dances at a coffee house whose customers were mostly white. Some books say he was born on the island of Martinique, but a Philadelphia church record gives his birth as June 16, 1792 (Richard Grant, *Smithsonian*, 2023).
- A Philadelphia publisher, George Willig, printed his dance music. *Smithsonian* dates the first piece to 1810 and calls it the first music published by a Black American. Other sources date his first known collection to 1818 (Grant. Wikipedia, "Francis Johnson").
- He played the keyed bugle, a brass horn with keys. In 1818 a book about Philadelphia called him the "leader of the band at all balls." He wrote more than 200 pieces, including dances, marches and songs (Grant).
- In November 1837 Johnson took his four-man band to England. Grant writes that no Black American musician had taken a band to Europe before. Some historians say he played for Queen Victoria, and others say this is not proven (Grant. Wikipedia).
- In 1842 and 1843, Johnson and nine Black musicians toured eight states. In St. Louis in December 1842, officers served them with warrants for being "Free Negros within the State of Missouri without a license." In Allegheny City, Pennsylvania, a white mob waited outside the hall and attacked the musicians in the street with stones, bricks and rotten eggs. Several were badly hurt. The band played the next night anyway. No record names anyone in the mob (Grant).
- Johnson died of a burst blood vessel near his heart on April 6, 1844. His band played a funeral march he had written, and one of the largest crowds Philadelphia had seen came to his funeral (Grant).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="francis-johnson" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hutchinson Family sing against slavery" -->
- John, Asa, Jesse and Judson Hutchinson, brothers from a farm family in Milford, New Hampshire, gave their first concert there on November 6, 1840. Their sister Abby joined them at about 12. They sang in four-part harmony and earned up to $130 a concert in 1842 (Wikipedia, "Hutchinson Family Singers").
- From 1843 they sang songs against slavery. Jesse wrote "Get Off the Track!" in 1844, to the tune of the minstrel song "Old Dan Tucker." In 1845 they toured Britain with Frederick Douglass (Wikipedia. Office of Historic Alexandria, 2018).
- In the spring of 1847, Philadelphia's mayor, John Swift, said their concerts would cause a riot. He ruled that no antislavery speech could be given at the hall and that no Black person could sit in the audience. The Hutchinsons refused to sing for a white audience only, and they did not sing in Philadelphia again (PhillyHistory Blog, 2012, citing the historian Scott Gac).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, soldiers on both sides of the Civil War marched and camped to band music, and freed Black singers from Fisk University took the spirituals of slavery on tour to raise money for their school. At the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, the army officer in charge gave Native boys brass band instruments so that they would stop drumming and singing their own songs. In 1883 federal officials made the Sun Dance and other ceremonial dances an offense punished by taking away a family's food. From 1890, singers recorded songs on wax cylinders for machines that played them back. Black pianists in Missouri began to publish ragtime, and in the South, Black farm workers were making the music later called the blues.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jenny Lind's tour, 1850 to 1852" -->
- In January 1850 the showman P. T. Barnum agreed to pay the Swedish singer Jenny Lind $1,000 a concert to tour the United States. She arrived in New York in September 1850, and Barnum sold some tickets by auction. She sang 93 concerts for Barnum and earned about $350,000. Barnum made at least $500,000. Lind gave her share to charities, most of it to free schools in Sweden (Wikipedia, "Jenny Lind").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War songs and army bands, 1859 to 1865" -->
- "Dixie" was first sung by Bryant's Minstrels, a white blackface troupe, in New York on April 4, 1859. It is credited to Dan Emmett. People in Mount Vernon, Ohio, have said since the 1910s or 1920s that Emmett, who later lived nearby, took the song from the Snowdens, a Black family of musicians in Knox County. Some historians think that may be true, and others reject it. In December 1860 a band played "Dixie" in Charleston after each vote to leave the Union, and in February 1861 a band played it when Jefferson Davis became president of the Confederacy (Wikipedia, "Dixie").
- Union soldiers from Massachusetts made up "John Brown's Body" in 1861 to the tune of an old camp-meeting hymn. Julia Ward Howe heard soldiers singing it near Washington and wrote new words in November 1861. *The Atlantic Monthly* paid her $4 and printed her "Battle Hymn of the Republic" on its front page in February 1862 (Wikipedia, "Battle Hymn of the Republic").
- In August 1861 the War Department allowed each Union regiment a band of 24 musicians. In July 1862 Congress voted to send the regimental bands home and keep one band for each brigade, a group of about four or five regiments. Bandsmen also carried the wounded. A Massachusetts bandsman wrote that the surgeon taught the band to use "stretchers, bandages and tourniquets" and to "rescue the wounded, if possible, and carry them to the field hospital" (Library of Congress, "The Civil War Bands").
- At Gettysburg, in July 1863, the bands of two North Carolina regiments played for their soldiers during the fighting. A British visitor heard a Confederate band play polkas and waltzes while cannon shells burst nearby (Library of Congress).
- In January 1862 the Hutchinson Family sang a song against slavery with the words "Let Slavery die!" to Union soldiers in Virginia. Some soldiers booed, and General George McClellan, who opposed using the war to end slavery, banned the Hutchinsons from the army camps (Office of Historic Alexandria, 2018).
- Shared with: `war` (the fighting) and `holidays` (Julia Ward Howe).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spirituals written down" -->
- In 1862 Thomas Wentworth Higginson, a white abolitionist, commanded the 1st South Carolina Volunteers, a Union regiment of men who had been enslaved. A drummer boy in the regiment told him that enslaved people in Georgetown, South Carolina, had been whipped for singing "We'll soon be free" when Abraham Lincoln was elected in 1860. The boy said the enslavers thought "de Lord" in the song meant the Yankees. In a later version of the story, Higginson wrote that the singers had been put in jail. Neither account names the drummer boy or the people who punished the singers (Higginson, *Army Life in a Black Regiment*, 1869).
- In 1867 three white Northerners, William Francis Allen, Lucy McKim Garrison and Charles Pickard Ware, published *Slave Songs of the United States*, 136 songs. Most came from Gullah Geechee people on St. Helena Island, South Carolina, who had been freed during the war. It was the first book of spirituals ever printed (Wikipedia, "Slave Songs of the United States").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Fisk Jubilee Singers, from 1871" -->
- Fisk University opened in Nashville after the Civil War to teach freed Black people. By 1871 it was out of money, and its classes met in old Union army hospital buildings. George L. White, the school's white treasurer and music teacher, took nine students on a singing tour on October 6, 1871, to raise money: Isaac Dickerson, Maggie Porter, Minnie Tate, Jennie Jackson, Benjamin Holmes, Thomas Rutling, Eliza Walker, Greene Evans and Ella Sheppard (*Tennessee Encyclopedia*. Tennessee Historical Commission marker).
- At first they sang spirituals only as extra songs at the end. Audiences asked for more. White named them the Jubilee Singers, after the "year of jubilee" in the Bible, when slaves were set free (*Tennessee Encyclopedia*).
- The tour was hard. After one early concert in Ohio they gave their $30 of profit to people burned out by the Chicago fire. Maggie Porter later said, "There were many times, when we didn't have place to sleep or anything to eat." Ella Sheppard wrote of "the ill treatment at hotels, on railroads, poorly attended concerts, and ridicule" (Wikipedia, "Fisk Jubilee Singers").
- In March 1872 they sang for President Ulysses S. Grant at the White House. In 1873 they sang for Queen Victoria in England (Wikipedia. *Tennessee Encyclopedia*).
- Accounts give different totals for different years: $20,000 by spring 1872, about $40,000 from the first American tours, nearly $50,000 by May 1874, and about $150,000 over seven years of touring. The money paid for Jubilee Hall, Fisk's first permanent building, opened on January 1, 1876. It still stands (*Tennessee Encyclopedia*. Wikipedia. Tennessee Historical Commission marker).
- Many white audiences in the North had never heard spirituals before the Jubilee Singers sang them (Wikipedia, "Fisk Jubilee Singers").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ella-sheppard" name="Ella Sheppard" movie="Jubilee Singers: Sacrifice and Glory (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ella Sheppard
> **Who:** Born into slavery, she became the pianist, arranger and assistant director of the Fisk Jubilee Singers. · **When and where:** Born February 4, 1851, at the Hermitage, Andrew Jackson's plantation near Nashville, Tennessee. Toured the United States and Europe. Died in Nashville in 1914 or 1915 (the records differ). · **Movie:** *Jubilee Singers: Sacrifice and Glory* (2000), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about the group
- Ella's father, Simon Sheppard, paid $1,800 to buy his own freedom and then bought Ella's. The woman who held Ella's mother, Sarah, refused to sell her and said, "She is mine and she shall die mine. Let Simon get another wife." When Ella was three, her mother was sold to a plantation in Mississippi. Ella did not see her again until she was 15 (Wikipedia, "Ella Sheppard").
- After a riot in Nashville in 1856, white people in power there made stricter rules for free Black people. Simon could not work, fell into debt, and was afraid his family would be seized and sold. He took Ella to Cincinnati. There a German woman taught her piano, and a white teacher gave her singing lessons in secret, at night, through the back door (Wikipedia. Andrew Ward, BlackPast).
- Her father died of cholera in 1866. Ella taught music in Gallatin, Tennessee, saved about $6 in five months, and entered Fisk in 1868. The money lasted three weeks. She stayed by giving music lessons and washing dishes (Wikipedia. Ward).
- She played piano, organ and guitar, arranged most of the spirituals for the group to sing in four parts, and led the singers from her place among them on stage. She collected more than a hundred songs (Ward. Wikipedia).
- In the summer of 1875 she wrote down the music of eight spirituals for a printed book of the group's songs. The book did not name her (Wikipedia).
- She wrote that the old songs "were sacred to our parents, who used them in their religious worship," and that at first the students did not want to sing them for strangers (Sheppard, 1911, quoted in Wikipedia, "Fisk Jubilee Singers").
- Her mother told her, "My back was never struck, but my heart is like a checkerboard with its stripes of sorrow." In 1890 Sheppard brought her mother to live with her at Fisk, where she trained new Jubilee Singers (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ella-sheppard" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native songs, drums and the first recordings" -->
- The Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, run by the army officer Richard Henry Pratt, opened in 1879. The school's own paper wrote in 1896 that in the first year the only music from the boys' rooms was the drum, and that the school's aim was "the complete transformation of the Indians," even "in song and language." Staff made a rule early on that students could not speak their own languages or practice "Indian customs" (*The Red Man*, February 1896).
- Pratt told a Boston donor, Mrs. Walter Baker, that he wanted to stop the drumming and singing: "If you will give me a set of brass band instruments I will give them to the 'tom tom' boys ... and this will stop the 'tom tom.'" The school band began in 1881. The paper wrote that no one ever ordered the students to stop singing their own songs, but that as the band grew, the singers of those songs fell silent (*The Red Man*, 1896).
- Dennison Wheelock, an Oneida student who entered Carlisle in 1885, later led the school band (Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center. *The Red Man*).
- On March 30, 1883, Hiram Price, the federal Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued rules making "the 'sun-dance,' the 'scalp-dance,' the 'war-dance,'" and similar gatherings "Indian offenses." Rations were the food the government had promised by treaty. The punishment for a first offense was losing those rations for up to ten days. For a later offense it was up to 30 days without food rations or up to 30 days in the agency jail. The rules could be enforced until 1978 (`religion` bank, from the 1883 document). `religion` tells the ban on ceremonies.
- `native-nations` and `religion` tell the Ghost Dance and the killings at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890, where soldiers of the 7th Cavalry killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children (`native-nations` bank).
- From March 15 to 18, 1890, in Calais, Maine, the scientist Jesse Walter Fewkes recorded Passamaquoddy songs and stories on 36 wax cylinders. The singers were Peter Selmore, Newell Josephs and Peter Lacoute. Most historians count these as the first recordings of Native American music. Since the 1980s Passamaquoddy speakers have used them to bring back old songs. Wayne Newell said in 2009 that people were working on them at home "so we can sort of resurrect older songs" (Library of Congress, National Recording Registry essay by Kelly Revak).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-philip-sousa" name="John Philip Sousa" movie="Stars and Stripes Forever (1952)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Philip Sousa
> **Who:** Leader of the United States Marine Band and writer of more than 100 marches. · **When and where:** Born November 6, 1854, in Washington, D.C. Led the Marine Band from 1880 to 1892, then his own band. Died March 6, 1932, in Reading, Pennsylvania. · **Movie:** *Stars and Stripes Forever* (1952), a Hollywood film loosely based on his memoir
- Sousa's father played trombone in the Marine Band. When Sousa was 13, his father signed him into the Marine Corps as an apprentice musician, to keep him from joining a circus band. He served seven years (Wikipedia, "John Philip Sousa". U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs history office).
- On October 1, 1880, he became the 17th leader of the Marine Band. He led it for 12 years under five presidents, from Rutherford B. Hayes to Benjamin Harrison, and in 1892 he formed his own band (VA. Wikipedia).
- He began "The Stars and Stripes Forever" on a ship crossing the Atlantic on Christmas Day 1896, after he learned that his band's manager, David Blakely, had died. He finished it in April 1897. In 1987 Congress made it the national march of the United States (VA).
- He wrote more than 100 marches and 15 operettas and helped develop the sousaphone, a large brass horn made for marching bands (VA. Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-philip-sousa" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Recording begins" -->
- Thomas Edison's workers built his first phonograph, a machine that recorded sound on tinfoil and played it back, in December 1877. Machines good enough to sell came on the market in 1888. `technology` tells the machine (Rutgers Edison Papers, in `technology` bank. Library of Congress).
- In 1890 two phonograph companies in New York and New Jersey paid George W. Johnson, a Black street whistler born in Virginia in 1846, 20 cents for each two-minute song he recorded on a wax cylinder. The cylinders played in coin-operated machines. Each cylinder had to be recorded one at a time, so Johnson sang the same songs over and over. Between 1890 and 1895 he made an estimated 25,000 to 50,000 cylinders (Wikipedia, "George W. Johnson", citing the historian Tim Brooks).
- His two best sellers were "The Laughing Song," which he wrote, and "The Whistling Coon," a song by a white writer that mocked Black people. In 1891 "The Laughing Song" was the best-selling record in the country for ten weeks. Johnson is counted as the first Black recording star. By 1905, companies could press thousands of copies from one recording, and they no longer needed him to sing each one. He worked as a doorman for a friend's office, died in 1914, and was buried in a grave without a marker (Wikipedia).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ragtime and coon songs" -->
- Ragtime is music with a "ragged" rhythm: the tune lands between the beats while a steady beat keeps time underneath. On the piano, the left hand keeps the steady beat and the right hand plays the tune. It began among African Americans in the late 1800s. Historians disagree about the first printed ragtime piece, dating it to 1895 or 1897 (Wikipedia, "Ragtime" and "Scott Joplin").
- In 1895 and 1896 Ernest Hogan, a Black performer from Bowling Green, Kentucky, who had started in minstrel shows, wrote "All Coons Look Alike to Me." It sold well, and many other writers copied it in "coon songs," songs that used racist words and pictures to mock Black people (Wikipedia, "Ernest Hogan").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="scott-joplin" name="Scott Joplin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Scott Joplin
> **Who:** A Black composer and pianist, the best-known writer of ragtime. · **When and where:** Born in northeastern Texas between mid-1867 and early 1868 (the date is not known for certain). Lived in Texarkana, Sedalia and St. Louis, Missouri, and New York. Died April 1, 1917, in New York.
- Joplin's father, Giles, had been enslaved in North Carolina. His mother, Florence, was born free in Kentucky. She cleaned houses, and Scott learned piano on her employer's piano. A German-born teacher, Julius Weiss, gave him lessons (State Historical Society of Missouri. Wikipedia).
- As a young man he traveled as a musician. Churches and brothels, houses where men paid women for sex, were among the few places that gave a Black pianist steady work. In 1891 he sang with the Texarkana Minstrels, a group raising money for a monument to Jefferson Davis (Wikipedia, "Scott Joplin").
- He moved to Sedalia, Missouri, in 1894, played cornet in a town band, and studied music at George R. Smith College (State Historical Society of Missouri).
- On August 10, 1899, he signed a contract with John Stark, a Sedalia music seller, to publish the "Maple Leaf Rag." Joplin got one cent for each copy sold. His first biographer said it sold 75,000 copies in six months. A later biographer, Edward A. Berlin, found that the first 400 copies took a year to sell, which paid Joplin about $1. Later sales gave him a small, steady income, about $600 a year by 1909 (Wikipedia, "Maple Leaf Rag").
- He moved to St. Louis in 1901 and to New York in 1907. He wrote an opera, *Treemonisha*, about a young Black woman who leads her people out of superstition through education. No one would pay to stage it. In 1911 he printed it himself, and in 1915 he played it for a small audience in a Harlem hall with only a piano (State Historical Society of Missouri. Wikipedia).
- By 1916 Joplin had syphilis in its late stage. Syphilis is a disease passed during sex, and without treatment it can spread to the brain, causing confusion, changes in personality and dementia, the loss of memory and the ability to think. In February 1917 he was admitted to Manhattan State Hospital, a hospital for mental illness, and he died there on April 1, 1917. No record describes how he was treated there. He was buried in a grave with no marker until 1974 (Wikipedia. CDC, "About Syphilis").
- *Treemonisha* was first fully staged in January 1972 in Atlanta. In 1976 Joplin was given a Pulitzer Prize, 59 years after his death (State Historical Society of Missouri. Wikipedia).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The blues begins, and nobody wrote it down" -->
- The blues grew out of the work songs and field hollers that enslaved people sang, and out of the songs of Black farm workers, dock workers and river boat workers in the South after slavery ended. No one knows who first played it or when. Historians place its beginning between 1870 and 1900, the years when freed Black families in the South moved from slavery into sharecropping, farming another man's land for a share of the crop (Wikipedia, "Origins of the blues").
- No one wrote down a description of the blues before 1900. The first written accounts come from just after 1900 (see 1900 to 1950). `slavery-freedom` and `work-workers` tell sharecropping and convict leasing.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1900 to 1950, Black musicians in the South made the blues and jazz. Singers and fiddlers in the Southern mountains, white and Black, played the music later called country. Record companies began to record all three. The singers were usually paid a set fee for each side of a record, and the companies kept the rest of the money. In 1921 Americans spent $106 million on records. Radio brought music into homes for free from the 1920s. Owners and managers of clubs, hotels and halls kept Black people out. The owners of the Cotton Club in New York hired Black musicians and let in only white customers. Billie Holiday recorded "Strange Fruit," a song about Black people murdered by white mobs. Marian Anderson sang to about 75,000 people outdoors in Washington after white officials refused her two indoor halls.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first written accounts of the blues, 1901 to 1903" -->
- In May and June of 1901 and 1902 Charles Peabody, a Harvard archaeologist, hired nine to fifteen Black men from Clarksdale, Mississippi, to dig into a Native mound in Coahoma County. The men sang while they dug. Peabody wrote in 1903 that bosses sometimes hired a strong singer just to keep the workers going. Their songs were about "hard luck," love and pay day. One went: "They had me arrested for murder / And I never harmed a man." Peabody did not call the songs blues (*Journal of American Folk-Lore*, 1903).
- In about 1903, the Black bandleader W. C. Handy was waiting for a train at Tutwiler, Mississippi. A man beside him played a guitar by sliding a knife along the strings and sang "Goin' where the Southern cross' the Dog." The man's name was never written down. Handy later turned the song into "Yellow Dog Blues" (Mississippi Blues Trail marker).
- Handy sold "The Memphis Blues" and all rights to it in 1912 for $100. He published "Saint Louis Blues" in 1914. With the Black banker Harry Pace he ran a company, Pace and Handy, that published music. He called himself the "Father of the Blues." The pianist Jelly Roll Morton said Handy had not started the music (Wikipedia, "W. C. Handy").
- Gertrude "Ma" Rainey said that in 1902 a girl in Missouri sang her a sad song about a man who had left a woman, and she began singing it in her tent shows. Paramount Records' advertisements called her the "Mother of the Blues." Between 1923 and 1928 she made more than 100 records (Wikipedia, "Ma Rainey").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Records for Black buyers, and who was paid, 1920 to 1938" -->
- On August 10, 1920, Mamie Smith, a Black singer, recorded "Crazy Blues" for the Okeh record company in New York. It was written by the Black songwriter Perry Bradford. In two months buyers paid a dollar each for 75,000 copies. It was the first big-selling blues record (Wikipedia, "Crazy Blues").
- Record company managers then went looking for Black singers to sell to Black buyers. By 1922 Okeh's managers were calling these records "race records." In 1921 the Black businessman Harry Pace started Black Swan Records, which paid its singers fairly and printed their real names. Paramount took over Black Swan in 1924 (Wikipedia, "Race record").
- Most singers got a set fee for each side of a record and no royalty. A royalty is a small payment for every copy sold, paid for as long as the record sells. Bessie Smith first got $125 a side from Columbia and no royalty (*Contemporary Musicians*).
- Robert Johnson, a Black guitarist from Mississippi, recorded 29 songs in 1936 and 1937 in a hotel room in San Antonio and in Dallas. His best seller, "Terraplane Blues," sold about 10,000 copies. He died near Greenwood, Mississippi, on August 16, 1938, at 27. No record shows what killed him. In 1998 a Mississippi court ruled that his son, Claud Johnson, should get the royalties, which by then were more than $1 million (Wikipedia, "Robert Johnson").
- In June 1949 the music magazine *Billboard* renamed its "race records" sales chart "rhythm and blues." The writer Jerry Wexler suggested the new name (Wikipedia, "Race record").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bessie-smith" name="Bessie Smith" movie="Bessie (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bessie Smith
> **Who:** A Black blues singer, the best-selling blues singer of the 1920s. · **When and where:** Born in Chattanooga, Tennessee, on April 15, 1894 (the 1900 census gives July 1892). Recorded in New York. Died near Clarksdale, Mississippi, on September 26, 1937. · **Movie:** *Bessie* (2015), an HBO drama film, with Queen Latifah as Smith
- Both her parents died when she was a child. She and her brother Andrew sang and danced for coins on the streets of Chattanooga. In 1912 she joined a traveling show as a dancer. Ma Rainey sang in the same show (Wikipedia, "Bessie Smith").
- Frank Walker of Columbia Records signed her in 1923. Her first record, "Down Hearted Blues," sold 780,000 copies in six months. Columbia paid her $125 for each usable side and no royalty. Later contracts paid $150 and then $200 a side, still with no royalty. Record companies paid white singers more and gave them royalties (*Contemporary Musicians*. American Blues Scene, 2015).
- She recorded between 150 and 200 songs (the Library of Congress essay gives both numbers). On tour she earned up to $2,000 a week, and she traveled in her own railroad car (Library of Congress, Cary O'Dell. *Contemporary Musicians*).
- On September 26, 1937, her car hit the back of a slow truck on Route 61 near Clarksdale, Mississippi. Her partner, Richard Morgan, was driving. Her right arm was almost torn off at the elbow. A white doctor from Memphis, Hugh Smith, stopped to help. She was taken to the G. T. Thomas Afro-American Hospital in Clarksdale. Doctors there amputated her arm, which means they cut it off, and she died that morning (Wikipedia).
- The record producer John Hammond reported in 1937 that a white hospital had refused to treat her. Dr. Smith said the ambulance would never have taken her to a white hospital. Her biographer, Chris Albertson, found no evidence for Hammond's story (Wikipedia. *Contemporary Musicians*).
- More than 10,000 people came to her funeral in Philadelphia. Her grave had no stone until 1970, when the singer Janis Joplin helped pay for one. In a lawsuit, the son of her husband tried to get her royalties from Columbia. The judges ruled against him because he had waited too long to sue (Library of Congress. American Blues Scene).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bessie-smith" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jazz on record, 1917 to 1931" -->
- Jazz began among Black musicians in New Orleans in the early 1900s. The first jazz record was made in New York on February 26, 1917, by the Original Dixieland Jass Band, five white musicians from New Orleans. Victor's managers put it on sale in May 1917. By some counts it sold more than a million copies. The band's leader, Nick LaRocca, later claimed that he had invented jazz (John Edward Hasse, *Smithsonian*, 2017. Wikipedia).
- A story says that Victor asked the Black New Orleans cornet player Freddie Keppard to record first, in 1915, and that he said no. Historians call it a legend (Hasse).
- The Cotton Club in Harlem, New York, was run by Owney Madden, a gangster who sold illegal liquor. Madden's managers hired Black musicians and dancers and let in only white customers, except a few Black stars. Its dancers had to be light-skinned, at least 5 feet 6 inches tall and under 21. Duke Ellington's band played there from December 4, 1927, to June 30, 1931. At Ellington's request, the owners let in a few more Black customers (Wikipedia, "Cotton Club").
- `migration` tells the Great Migration of Black families from the South to Chicago, New York and other cities, which carried jazz and blues north.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="louis-armstrong" name="Louis Armstrong" movie="Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Louis Armstrong
> **Who:** A Black trumpet player and singer from New Orleans who changed how jazz was played and sung. · **When and where:** Born in New Orleans on August 4, 1901, by his baptism record. He always said July 4, 1900. Lived in Chicago and New York. Died in New York on July 6, 1971. · **Movie:** *Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues* (2022), a documentary about him
- On New Year's Eve 1912, when he was 11, he fired his stepfather's gun into the air, loaded with a blank. Police arrested him. A judge sent him to the Colored Waif's Home, a reform home for Black boys. There a teacher, Peter Davis, taught him the cornet in the home's band (Wikipedia, "Louis Armstrong").
- In 1922 the bandleader Joe "King" Oliver invited him to Chicago to play second cornet in his band. From November 1925 Armstrong made records for Okeh with his own groups, the Hot Five and the Hot Seven (Wikipedia).
- On September 17, 1957, in Grand Forks, North Dakota, a reporter asked him about the fight over nine Black students entering Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. Armstrong said: "The way they are treating my people in the South, the government can go to hell." He called President Dwight Eisenhower "two faced" and said he had "no guts" (Ricky Riccardi, Louis Armstrong House Museum).
- Staff at a radio station in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, threw out his records. Officials at the University of Arkansas cancelled his invitation to play a dance. The Black congressman Adam Clayton Powell said Armstrong "didn't know what he was talking about." After Eisenhower sent soldiers to protect the students, Armstrong sent him a telegram that said, "If you decide to walk into the schools with the little colored kids, take me along Daddy. God bless you" (Riccardi).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="louis-armstrong" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music on record: Bristol, 1927" -->
- From July 25 to August 5, 1927, Ralph Peer of the Victor record company recorded 76 songs by 19 singers and groups in an empty hat factory in Bristol, Tennessee. Each performer got $50 in cash for each side, plus 2½ cents for each record sold. The Carter Family recorded on August 1 and 2, and Jimmie Rodgers on August 4 (Wikipedia, "Bristol sessions").
- Peer took no salary from Victor. He kept the copyright of the songs he recorded, including very old songs that no one had written down. A copyright is the legal right to be paid when a song is printed, recorded or played. He started Southern Music Publishing in 1928 to collect that money (Wikipedia, "Ralph Peer").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="maybelle-carter" name="Maybelle Carter" movie="The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maybelle Carter
> **Who:** Guitarist and singer in the Carter Family, who changed how the guitar is played in country music. · **When and where:** Born May 10, 1909, in Nickelsville, Virginia. Recorded at Bristol, Tennessee, in 1927 at age 18. Died October 23, 1978, in Hendersonville, Tennessee. · **Movie:** *The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken* (2005), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about the family
- She sang with Sara Carter and A. P. Carter. At Bristol in August 1927 each of them was paid Victor's rate of $50 a side and 2½ cents a record (Wikipedia, "Bristol sessions").
- She picked out the tune with her thumb on the low strings and brushed the chords with her fingers on the high strings. Her way made the guitar a lead instrument. People call it the "Carter scratch." She first recorded "Wildwood Flower" in 1928 (Wikipedia, "Maybelle Carter").
- From December 1928 A. P. Carter went looking for old songs in the mountains of Virginia and Tennessee with Lesley Riddle, a Black guitarist from North Carolina. Riddle had lost his right leg at the knee in an accident at a cement plant in 1927. Carter wrote down the words while Riddle remembered the tunes and taught them to Maybelle and Sara (Wikipedia, "Lesley Riddle").
- A. P. Carter copyrighted many of the songs they found, and Ralph Peer's company published them. No record shows that Riddle was paid or named as a writer. Maybelle said she learned guitar runs from him, including the ones in "Cannonball Blues" (Wikipedia. Search record).
- Later she led a group with her daughters, Mother Maybelle and the Carter Sisters. From 1968 she toured with her son-in-law, Johnny Cash (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maybelle-carter" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Grand Ole Opry, from 1925" -->
- George D. Hay started a Saturday night country music show called the *WSM Barn Dance* on November 28, 1925, on a Nashville radio station owned by the National Life and Accident Insurance Company. The first player was a 77-year-old fiddler, Uncle Jimmy Thompson (Wikipedia, "Grand Ole Opry").
- On December 10, 1927, after a program of opera music, Hay said "from now on, we will present 'The Grand Ole Opry.'" He said it after the Black harmonica player DeFord Bailey played his "Pan American Blues." From 1939 to 1956 one hour of the show went out across the country on the NBC radio network (Wikipedia. Country Music Hall of Fame).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="deford-bailey" name="DeFord Bailey" movie="DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost (2002)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### DeFord Bailey
> **Who:** A Black harmonica player and the first star of the Grand Ole Opry. · **When and where:** Born December 14, 1899, in Smith County, Tennessee. Played on Nashville radio from 1926 to 1941. Died July 2, 1982. · **Movie:** *DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost* (2002), a Nashville Public Television documentary about him
- Polio struck him at age three. Polio is a disease caused by a virus that can damage the nerves that move the muscles. It stunted his growth and left his back bent. His family played what he called "Black hillbilly music" (Country Music Hall of Fame).
- He played on the *WSM Barn Dance* from June 1926. His playing was the reason George D. Hay gave the show the name Grand Ole Opry in December 1927 (Country Music Hall of Fame. Wikipedia).
- Sources disagree about his pay. One says he got a fixed $5 a show, less than the white players he toured with. The Country Music Hall of Fame says he was one of the best-paid stars on the show (Wikipedia, "DeFord Bailey". Country Music Hall of Fame).
- On tour in the South with white stars such as Roy Acuff, Bill Monroe and Uncle Dave Macon, hotels and restaurants would not serve him because he was Black. Other players brought him food or got him into hotels by the fire escape (Wikipedia).
- In 1941 most radio stations stopped playing songs licensed by ASCAP, a group that collected money for songwriters, after it raised its fees. Station managers at WSM told Bailey not to play his ASCAP songs, which were his most popular ones. They fired him at the end of 1941 (Country Music Hall of Fame. Wikipedia, "Broadcast Music, Inc.").
- In 1945 Hay wrote a book about the show. He called Bailey "lazy" and wrote that he "refused to learn new tunes" (George D. Hay, *A Story of the Grand Ole Opry*, 1945). Bailey later said that Hay had treated him kindly and wrote those words to please his bosses (Saving Country Music, 2025).
- After 1941 Bailey ran a shoeshine stand in Nashville and rented out rooms. He almost never played in public again until 1974. In 2005 he was named to the Country Music Hall of Fame (Country Music Hall of Fame).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="deford-bailey" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio, the Depression and two fights over pay, 1921 to 1944" -->
- Americans spent $106 million on records in 1921. They did not spend that much again until 1945 (*Phonograph Boom*, University of Michigan Press). In the early 1930s record sales fell to about one tenth of what they had been in the 1920s. People who had lost jobs in the Great Depression stopped buying records, and radio music was free (IASA).
- In 1939 radio station owners started their own song-licensing group, BMI, after ASCAP, the older group, announced higher fees. In 1941 almost every station and all three national networks stopped playing ASCAP songs. ASCAP had turned away most writers of blues, gospel, country and Latin music. BMI signed them, and radio played more of their songs (Wikipedia, "Broadcast Music, Inc.").
- On August 1, 1942, James Petrillo, head of the musicians' union, told its members to stop making records. He wanted musicians paid when radio stations and jukeboxes played records instead of hiring live bands. Decca's owners agreed in September 1943 to pay a fee on every record into a union fund. The heads of Victor and Columbia agreed on November 11, 1944. Singers were allowed to keep recording with voices only, so for two years singers made records without bands (Wikipedia, "1942-1944 musicians' strike").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gospel, shape notes and field recordings" -->
- Thomas A. Dorsey had played piano for Ma Rainey as "Georgia Tom." In August 1932 his wife Nettie and their newborn son died, and he wrote "Take My Hand, Precious Lord." That year he started the first Black-owned company to publish gospel music, and in 1933 a national convention of gospel choirs. He led the music at Pilgrim Baptist Church in Chicago for fifty years. He said, "I'm not ashamed of my blues. It's all the same talent" (Wikipedia, "Thomas A. Dorsey").
- Mahalia Jackson came to Chicago from New Orleans in 1927, and Dorsey trained her. She would sing only gospel, even when managers at Decca offered to keep recording her if she sang blues. Her "Move On Up a Little Higher" (1947) sold about two million copies (Wikipedia, "Mahalia Jackson").
- In 1934 Judge Jackson, a Black singing teacher in Ozark, Alabama, printed *The Colored Sacred Harp*, a book of 77 shape-note hymns by Black singers. Eighteen were his own. He and Bishop J. D. Walker paid to print 1,000 copies, and Jackson sold them door to door (Wikipedia, "Judge Jackson").
- From 1933 John Lomax and his son Alan recorded Black singers in Southern prisons for the Library of Congress. John Lomax said he wanted music "not yet influenced by jazz and the radio." At Angola prison in Louisiana they recorded Huddie Ledbetter, called Lead Belly, who was serving time for stabbing a man in a fight. He was let out in 1934. A prison official wrote that his singing had nothing to do with his release (Wikipedia, "John Lomax" and "Lead Belly").
- John Lomax then managed Lead Belly and took part of his pay. They split over money in 1935. Lomax's book of 1936 printed Lead Belly's song "Goodnight, Irene" with both men named as writers, though Lomax had recorded it, not written it. In 1950, a year after Lead Belly died, a group called the Weavers sold 2 million copies of it (Wikipedia, "Goodnight, Irene". American Blues Scene, 2014).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native songs, Hawaiian guitar and a new songwriter" -->
- On March 28, 1900, the Carlisle Indian School band played the "Aboriginal Suite" at Carnegie Hall in New York. Its composer and conductor, Dennison Wheelock, was an Oneida man and Carlisle's first Native bandmaster. He later became a lawyer and argued cases for Native nations before the Supreme Court (Wikipedia, "Dennison Wheelock").
- Scientists recorded thousands of Native songs on wax cylinders. By the 1980s the Library of Congress held nearly 8,000 of these cylinders. Many held sacred songs meant only for ceremonies. One collector, Francis La Flesche, took singers from Oklahoma to Washington so that neighbors who objected could not stop him recording ceremonial songs. No record shows the singers were paid (*Cultural Survival Quarterly*). From 1907 Frances Densmore recorded songs of the Ojibwe, Sioux, Mandan, Hidatsa, Tohono O'odham, Seminole and other nations for the Smithsonian (Wikipedia, "Frances Densmore").
- In 1889 Joseph Kekuku, a Hawaiian student at the Kamehameha School for Boys, found he could change a guitar's pitch by sliding a steel bar along the strings. From 1904 he played this steel guitar in theaters across the mainland United States. Congress had annexed Hawaii, making it American land, in 1898 (Wikipedia, "Joseph Kekuku". `america-world` bank).
- Irving Berlin, born Israel Beilin, came to New York from the Russian Empire in 1893, at age five. He could not read music and could play the piano in only one key. His "Alexander's Ragtime Band" was a hit in 1911. He gave all the money from "God Bless America" (1938) to the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts. He owned his own songs through his own company (Wikipedia, "Irving Berlin"). `immigration` tells his arrival.
- In 1906 John Philip Sousa wrote that records and player pianos were becoming a "substitute for human skill, intelligence and soul." He called records "canned music" (Kat Eschner, *Smithsonian*, 2017). In 1909 Congress passed a copyright law that let anyone record a song without asking, as long as they paid the song's owner a set fee (Wikipedia, "Copyright Act of 1909").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="woody-guthrie" name="Woody Guthrie" movie="Bound for Glory (1976)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Woody Guthrie
> **Who:** A folk singer and songwriter who wrote about poor farm families, workers and migrants in the 1930s and 1940s. · **When and where:** Born July 14, 1912, in Okemah, Oklahoma. Lived in Texas, California and New York. Died October 3, 1967, in New York. · **Movie:** *Bound for Glory* (1976), a Hollywood film based loosely on his own book about his life
- His mother, Nora, had Huntington's disease and was put in a hospital. Huntington's is a disease passed down in families. It destroys parts of the brain, causing movements a person cannot control, changes in mood, and loss of the ability to think (Wikipedia. MedlinePlus Genetics).
- In the 1930s he joined the farm families who left the drought-stricken Great Plains for California. He sang on a Los Angeles radio station, KFVD, and in 1940 made the album *Dust Bowl Ballads* for Victor (Wikipedia). `migration` tells the Dust Bowl move itself.
- On February 23, 1940, in a New York hotel, he wrote "This Land Is Your Land." He wrote it as an answer to Irving Berlin's "God Bless America," which he heard over and over on the radio. His first title was "God Blessed America for Me" (Wikipedia, "This Land Is Your Land").
- His handwritten copy had two verses that were often left out later. One is about a sign that says "No Trespassing." In the other, hungry people stand in line at a relief office, a place that gave out food or money to the poor, near a church (Wikipedia).
- In May 1941 the Bonneville Power Administration, a federal agency that sold power from dams on the Columbia River, hired him for one month for $266. His boss, Stephen Kahn, wanted three pages of songs a day for a film about the dams. Guthrie wrote 26 songs, among them "Roll On, Columbia." Kahn later said the government never got "a better investment on its money" (Robert C. Carriker, *Columbia Magazine*, 2001).
- On his guitar he wrote "This machine kills fascists." He served in the Merchant Marine in the Second World War (Wikipedia).
- Doctors found that he had Huntington's disease in 1952. He spent his last years in hospitals in New Jersey and New York and died at Creedmoor hospital in New York in 1967, at 55 (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woody-guthrie" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Strange Fruit, 1939" -->
- "Strange Fruit" is a song about the lynching of Black Americans. Lynching means a mob killing a person without a trial, often by hanging. The song describes Black people's bodies hanging from trees in the South, their eyes bulging and mouths twisted, and the smell of burning flesh (Wikipedia, "Strange Fruit").
- Abel Meeropol, a white English teacher in the Bronx, wrote it under the name Lewis Allan. He wrote it after he saw a photograph of the lynching of Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith in Marion, Indiana (Wikipedia).
- On August 7, 1930, a white mob of between 5,000 and 10,000 people broke into the jail in Marion. The mob wanted three young Black men in the jail who had been accused of killing a white man and sexually assaulting a white woman. Men in the mob beat Shipp and Smith, both 19, and hanged them. People in the crowd took pieces of their bodies home. A third prisoner, James Cameron, 16, was beaten but lived when someone in the crowd said he was innocent. The NAACP named 27 men it suspected. All-white juries found the leaders not guilty (Equal Justice Initiative).
- Billie Holiday first sang it at Café Society in New York in 1939. She sang it last, the waiters stopped serving, and the room went dark except for a light on her face. Managers at Columbia would not record it. John Hammond, her producer there, said Southern record stores and radio stations would object. Milt Gabler of the small Commodore label recorded it on April 20, 1939. It became her best-selling record (Wikipedia).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Billie Holiday
> **Who:** A Black jazz singer who recorded "Strange Fruit," a song about lynching, in 1939. · **When and where:** Born Eleanora Fagan on April 7, 1915, in Philadelphia. Grew up in Baltimore. Sang in New York. Died July 17, 1959, in New York. · **Movie:** *Billie* (2019), a documentary about her
- At nine she was sent to the House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school for girls in Baltimore, for missing school. As a young girl she ran errands in a brothel, a house where men paid women for sex, and scrubbed floors (Wikipedia, "Billie Holiday").
- In 1938 she sang with Artie Shaw's white band. At the Lincoln Hotel in New York the managers made her use the service elevator because white guests complained. She left the band (Wikipedia).
- Harry Anslinger ran the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, the government's drug police, from 1930 to 1962. On May 16, 1947, federal agents arrested Holiday for having narcotics in her New York apartment. She pleaded guilty, and a federal judge sent her to the Alderson Federal Prison Camp in West Virginia. She said: "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt" (Wikipedia. `drugs-alcohol` bank).
- Because of the conviction, New York City officials took away her cabaret card, the license a performer needed to work in any club that sold alcohol. After that she could sing in New York only in concert halls and theaters. In January 1949 the federal agent George White arrested her again, in a San Francisco hotel (Wikipedia).
- The writer Johann Hari wrote in 2015 that Anslinger's agents went after her from 1939 because she sang "Strange Fruit." The jazz writer Lewis Porter points out that no earlier source says this (Wikipedia).
- In 1959 she was in Metropolitan Hospital in New York with liver and heart disease. On May 31, federal narcotics agents arrested her in her hospital bed and handcuffed her to it. Police guarded her room until a judge ordered the handcuffs taken off. She died there on July 17, 1959, at 44, with 70 cents in the bank. Her liver was scarred, a disease called cirrhosis, and her heart failed (Wikipedia. John H. Halpern and David Blistein, *TIME*, 2019).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="billie-holiday" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marian Anderson at the Lincoln Memorial, 1939" -->
- In January 1939 Howard University asked to rent Constitution Hall in Washington for an Easter concert by Marian Anderson, a Black singer. The hall belonged to the Daughters of the American Revolution (DAR), a women's club. Its leaders refused. Since 1932 the club had let only white performers use the hall (FDR Library. National Archives).
- Frank Ballou, the head of Washington's public schools, also refused her the hall of Central High School, a white school. The school board backed him (Jessie Kratz, National Archives, 2022).
- First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt quit the DAR on February 26, 1939. She wrote that its leaders had "an opportunity to lead in an enlightened way" and had "failed" (FDR Library).
- Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, set up a free concert on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson sang there to more than 75,000 people, and millions more heard her on the radio. She began with "My Country, 'Tis of Thee" (Wikipedia. FDR Library). `rights-movements` tells the concert as a rights event, and `landmarks` tells the memorial.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marian-anderson" name="Marian Anderson" movie="Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert (1939)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Marian Anderson
> **Who:** A Black contralto, a singer with a low woman's voice, who sang at the Lincoln Memorial in 1939 and was the first Black singer at the Metropolitan Opera. · **When and where:** Born February 27, 1897, in Philadelphia. Died April 8, 1993, in Portland, Oregon. · **Movie:** *Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert* (1939), a short film of the concert
- As a young woman she applied to a music school in Philadelphia. The school's officials turned her away because they took only white students (Wikipedia, "Marian Anderson").
- She became famous singing in Europe. In Salzburg, Austria, in 1935 the conductor Arturo Toscanini told her she had "a voice heard once in a hundred years" (Wikipedia).
- In 1939 the DAR's president, Sarah Corbin Robert, and its leaders kept her out of Constitution Hall. She sang outdoors at the Lincoln Memorial instead, on April 9, 1939 (Wikipedia. FDR Library).
- On January 7, 1955, at 57, she became the first Black singer to perform at the Metropolitan Opera in New York. She sang Ulrica, a fortune teller, in Verdi's opera *Un ballo in maschera* (Wikipedia).
- She sang at the March on Washington in August 1963. That year she was given the Presidential Medal of Freedom (Wikipedia).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Black musicians made rock and roll in the early 1950s out of rhythm and blues and gospel. White singers then sold more copies of some of their songs. Big Mama Thornton said she got one check for $500 for "Hound Dog." Record company owners, publishers and managers kept most of the money from many of these songs. In Detroit, Berry Gordy built Motown into the largest Black-owned business on *Black Enterprise* magazine's list. Civil rights marchers sang old church songs with new words, in jails and at mass meetings. In the Bronx in 1973, a teenage DJ and his sister held a party that people now name as the start of hip-hop. In 1985 senators held a hearing on song lyrics, and the heads of record companies agreed to put warning labels on some albums.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rock and roll, and who got paid for it" -->
- Sister Rosetta Tharpe, a Black gospel singer from Arkansas, played electric guitar in church music from the late 1930s. Her "Strange Things Happening Every Day" (1944) was the first gospel record to reach the rhythm and blues sales chart, and some writers call it the first rock and roll record. Wikipedia names her as an influence on Elvis Presley, Little Richard, Chuck Berry and Johnny Cash (Wikipedia, "Sister Rosetta Tharpe").
- Fats Domino's "The Fat Man" (1949) sold a million copies by 1951. Chuck Berry's "Maybellene" (1955) sold more than a million. When Berry first saw the record, the label named the disc jockey Alan Freed and another man as co-writers, so they got part of his writer's pay. Berry later won back full credit (Wikipedia, "Fats Domino" and "Chuck Berry").
- White singers made cover versions, which means new recordings of songs other singers had already recorded. In 1955 Georgia Gibbs, a white singer, copied LaVern Baker's "Tweedle Dee" note for note. Baker's record reached No. 14 on the national pop chart, and Gibbs's reached No. 1. Baker tried to sue Gibbs for $250,000 and failed. She asked Congress to make copying another singer's arrangement illegal, and Representative Charles Diggs led an inquiry into "song theft" (Wikipedia, "LaVern Baker").
- Pat Boone's 1955 cover of Fats Domino's "Ain't That a Shame" was Boone's first No. 1 record. Domino and his co-writer Dave Bartholomew still earned writers' royalties from it. Boone's cover of Little Richard's "Tutti Frutti" ranked higher on the national pop chart than Little Richard's own record (Wikipedia).
- Little Richard said Specialty Records cut his royalty rate without telling him. In 1984 he sued the company and its owner, Art Rupe, for $112 million in unpaid royalties. They settled out of court in 1986 (Wikipedia, "Little Richard").
- Payola is paying a disc jockey or radio station in secret to play a record. In 1959 and 1960 a House committee led by Representative Oren Harris held hearings on it. Alan Freed refused to sign a statement that he had taken no bribes, and the station WABC fired him in November 1959. He pleaded guilty to commercial bribery in 1962 and paid a $300 fine. In 1960 Congress made payola a crime punished by up to a year in jail. The older song publishers in ASCAP pushed for the hearings partly because they opposed rock and roll (Wikipedia, "Payola" and "Alan Freed").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hound Dog: Big Mama Thornton and Elvis Presley" -->
- Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller, two white songwriters, wrote "Hound Dog." Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton, a Black blues singer from Alabama, recorded it on August 13, 1952. Don Robey's Peacock Records released it in 1953, and it was No. 1 on the rhythm and blues chart for seven weeks. Robey guessed it sold 500,000 to 750,000 copies (Wikipedia, "Hound Dog (song)").
- Thornton said: "That song sold over two million records. I got one check for $500 and never saw another." Robey put his own name on the copyright form as a writer (Wikipedia).
- Presley recorded "Hound Dog" for RCA on July 2, 1956. It was No. 1 on the pop chart for 11 weeks and sold about 10 million copies. He copied the version of a white Las Vegas band, Freddie Bell and the Bellboys, more than Thornton's. Leiber and Stoller got large royalties only after Presley's record (Wikipedia).
- Thornton died poor in a Los Angeles boarding house in 1984, of heart and liver disease. She was buried in a grave shared with two other people (Wikipedia, "Big Mama Thornton").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="elvis-presley" name="Elvis Presley" movie="Elvis (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Elvis Presley
> **Who:** A white singer from Mississippi and Tennessee, the best-selling rock and roll singer of the 1950s. · **When and where:** Born January 8, 1935, in Tupelo, Mississippi. Moved to Memphis, Tennessee, in 1948. Died August 16, 1977, at his Memphis home, Graceland, at 42. · **Movie:** *Elvis* (2022), a Hollywood drama film about him and his manager
- He first heard music at his family's Assembly of God church. In Memphis he listened to Black blues and rhythm and blues singers on the radio (Wikipedia, "Elvis Presley").
- Sam Phillips owned Sun Records in Memphis. He recorded Black blues musicians such as Howlin' Wolf and B. B. King. His office manager, Marion Keisker, said he often told her: "If I could find a white man who had the Negro sound and the Negro feel, I could make a billion dollars" (Encyclopedia.com, "Sam Phillips").
- On the night of July 5, 1954, at Sun, Presley sang "That's All Right," a blues song by the Black singer Arthur Crudup. Phillips recorded it (Wikipedia).
- Crudup had recorded the song for RCA in 1946. He said later: "I realised I was making everybody rich, and here I was poor." A publisher backed out of a $60,000 settlement in 1968 at the last moment. In 1971 a songwriters' group got him more than $10,000 in back pay. He died in 1974 (Wikipedia, "Arthur Crudup").
- In November 1955 Phillips and Presley's manager, Colonel Tom Parker, sold Presley's contract to RCA for $40,000 (Wikipedia).
- Parker took 25 percent of Presley's pay. In 1967 Presley agreed to raise Parker's share to 50 percent on some of his income. In 1973 Parker sold RCA the rights to all of Presley's old records for $5.4 million, so Presley's family got no royalties on them after he died. In 1981 a lawyer appointed by a court to check Parker's work called the 50 percent share "extortionate" (Wikipedia, "Colonel Tom Parker").
- Presley served in the Army from 1958 to 1960. Estimates of his record sales are about 500 million worldwide (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elvis-presley" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Motown, soul and gospel" -->
- On January 12, 1959, Berry Gordy Jr., a Black songwriter in Detroit, started a record company, later called Motown, with an $800 loan from his family's savings fund. From 1960 to 1969, 79 Motown songs reached the top ten of the national pop chart (Wikipedia, "Motown").
- In 1973 the magazine *Black Enterprise* began ranking the largest Black-owned businesses. Motown was first, with $40 million in sales, and it stayed first until 1983 (*Black Enterprise*). Gordy sold Motown in 1988 for $61 million (Wikipedia).
- A group of Detroit studio musicians later called the Funk Brothers played on most Motown hits from 1959 to 1972. No Motown record listed their names until Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On* in 1971. Gordy fined players he caught recording for other companies (Wikipedia, "The Funk Brothers").
- Ray Charles, blind since he was about seven, mixed gospel and blues in "I Got a Woman" (1954), and some church people objected. In 1961 he refused to play a dance in Augusta, Georgia, when he learned the audience would be split by race. The promoter sued him, and a Georgia court fined him $757. His 1959 contract with ABC-Paramount gave him ownership of his own master recordings, the original tapes that every copy is made from (Wikipedia, "Ray Charles").
- Sam Cooke, a gospel singer from Mississippi, started his own record company and his own song-publishing company. From 1963 his own company owned his master recordings. Wikipedia counts him among the first Black artists to control his own business. A Los Angeles motel manager, Bertha Franklin, shot and killed him on December 11, 1964. A coroner's jury called it justifiable homicide, a killing the law excuses, and his family has disputed that account (Wikipedia, "Sam Cooke").
- James Brown paid for the recording of his album *Live at the Apollo* himself in 1963. On April 5, 1968, the day after Martin Luther King Jr. was killed, he gave a free televised concert in Boston. His "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud" came out in August 1968 (Wikipedia, "James Brown").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="aretha-franklin" name="Aretha Franklin" movie="Amazing Grace (2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aretha Franklin
> **Who:** A Black singer and pianist who recorded "Respect" in 1967. · **When and where:** Born March 25, 1942, in Memphis, Tennessee. Grew up in Detroit, Michigan. Died there August 16, 2018. · **Movie:** *Amazing Grace* (2018), a documentary film of her 1972 gospel concert in a Los Angeles church
- Her father, the Reverend C. L. Franklin, led New Bethel Baptist Church in Detroit, and she sang there as a child. At 14 she recorded a gospel album, *Songs of Faith* (1956) (Wikipedia, "Aretha Franklin").
- She had her first child at 12 and her second at 15 (Wikipedia).
- She recorded for Columbia from 1960 to 1966 with few hits. The producer Jerry Wexler brought her to Atlantic Records. In January 1967 she recorded for one day at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, before a fight in the studio ended the session (Wikipedia).
- Otis Redding wrote and recorded "Respect" in 1965. In his song a man wants respect when he comes home. Franklin recorded it on February 14, 1967, in New York. She and her sister Carolyn made it a woman's demand, spelled out "R-E-S-P-E-C-T" and added new lines. It was No. 1 on the pop chart for two weeks and on the rhythm and blues chart for eight. Many women and civil rights marchers took it as their own song. Redding said it was the song "that a girl took away from me" (Wikipedia, "Respect (song)").
- In 1970 the Black activist Angela Davis was jailed. Franklin said, "Angela Davis must go free" (Wikipedia).
- In January 1972 she recorded the gospel album *Amazing Grace* at New Temple Missionary Baptist Church in Los Angeles. It sold more than two million copies. The film made at the same time was not shown until 2018 because the sound and the pictures could not be matched (Wikipedia. AFI).
- In 1987 she became the first woman named to the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aretha-franklin" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs of the civil rights movement" -->
- "We Shall Overcome" started as a church hymn by Charles Albert Tindley, printed in 1901. In 1945 and 1946 Black tobacco workers went on strike at a cigar factory in Charleston, South Carolina, and one of them, Lucille Simmons, led the strikers in singing "We Will Overcome." Zilphia Horton of the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee learned it from her. Pete Seeger changed "will" to "shall," and Guy Carawan taught it to young civil rights workers from 1959 (Wikipedia, "We Shall Overcome").
- On March 7, 1965, Alabama state troopers and other officers attacked about 600 marchers in Selma, Alabama, with nightsticks and tear gas (`rights-movements` bank). In a speech on March 15, President Lyndon Johnson told Congress, "And we shall overcome." In January 2018, after a court case, the owners of the company that held the song's copyright gave it up, and the song became free for anyone to use (Wikipedia). `rights-movements` tells the marches.
- Members of SNCC, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, formed the Freedom Singers in 1962 after the protests in Albany, Georgia. They drove 50,000 miles in nine months to sing about the movement, and their first tour raised nearly $50,000 for SNCC (SNCC Digital Gateway).
- On August 28, 1963, at the March on Washington, Mahalia Jackson, Marian Anderson, Joan Baez, Bob Dylan, Odetta, and Peter, Paul and Mary sang. During Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech, Jackson called out, "Tell them about the dream, Martin!" (Wikipedia, "March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" name="Bernice Johnson Reagon" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bernice Johnson Reagon
> **Who:** A Black student in Albany, Georgia, who led singing at civil rights meetings and sang with the SNCC Freedom Singers. · **When and where:** Born October 4, 1942, in Albany, Georgia. Died July 16, 2024.
- She joined the NAACP Youth Council in Albany before SNCC workers arrived there in the fall of 1961. Officials at Albany State College suspended her for her part in the protests (SNCC Digital Gateway).
- At mass meetings she led the singing. She took old spirituals and changed the words to fit the protests. She said: "So instead I put in freedom and by the second line everyone was singing" (SNCC Digital Gateway).
- In 1962 she joined the Freedom Singers, with Cordell Reagon, Rutha Mae Harris and Chuck Neblett. She married Cordell Reagon. She said of the group's songs, "There weren't soloists, there were song leaders," and called the group "a singing newspaper" (SNCC Digital Gateway).
- In 1973 she started Sweet Honey in the Rock, a group of Black women who sing without instruments. She later worked for the Smithsonian Institution recording and studying Black American songs and stories (SNCC Digital Gateway).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bob-dylan" name="Bob Dylan" movie="No Direction Home: Bob Dylan (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bob Dylan
> **Who:** A songwriter of the folk music revival of the early 1960s and after, and winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature. · **When and where:** Born Robert Allen Zimmerman on May 24, 1941, in Duluth, Minnesota. Grew up in Hibbing, Minnesota. Moved to New York in 1961. · **Movie:** *No Direction Home: Bob Dylan* (2005), a documentary by Martin Scorsese
- He moved to New York in January 1961, partly to meet Woody Guthrie, who was then in a mental hospital in New Jersey with Huntington's disease (Wikipedia, "Bob Dylan").
- His 1963 album *The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan* made his name as a writer of protest songs. At the March on Washington that August he sang "Only a Pawn in Their Game" (Wikipedia).
- At the Newport Folk Festival in 1965 he played an electric guitar with a band, and leading people in folk music reacted with anger (Wikipedia).
- In 2016 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition" (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bob-dylan" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music from Nashville and Bakersfield" -->
- Hank Williams learned guitar as a boy in Alabama from a Black street musician, Rufus "Tee-Tot" Payne, who taught him for money or meals. He had 12 No. 1 country records. He was born with a defect of the spine that caused him pain all his life, and he drank and took painkillers. Grand Ole Opry managers dropped him in August 1952 for drunkenness and missed shows. His heart failed in the back seat of a car on January 1, 1953, while he was being driven to a show. He was 29 (Wikipedia, "Hank Williams").
- Patsy Cline recorded "Crazy," by Willie Nelson, in 1961. She died in a plane crash near Camden, Tennessee, on March 5, 1963, at 30. In 1973 she became the first woman in the Country Music Hall of Fame (Wikipedia, "Patsy Cline").
- In the late 1950s Buck Owens, Merle Haggard and other musicians in Bakersfield, California, played a loud, plain country music with electric guitars. It was a break from the Nashville records of those years, which added orchestras. Many of their listeners were children of Dust Bowl families (Wikipedia, "Bakersfield sound").
- Johnny Cash recorded two concerts for prisoners at Folsom State Prison in California on January 13, 1968. The album ends with "Greystone Chapel," a song by a prisoner, Glen Sherley. It sold 500,000 copies by October 1968. Cash himself never served a prison sentence (Wikipedia, "At Folsom Prison").
- Loretta Lynn's "The Pill" (1975) is about a wife tired of being pregnant who starts taking birth-control pills. Managers at some country radio stations refused to play it. It still reached No. 5 on the country chart (Wikipedia, "The Pill (song)").
- In 1967 Charley Pride, the son of Black sharecroppers from Sledge, Mississippi, became the first Black performer on the Grand Ole Opry since DeFord Bailey left in 1941. RCA's staff sent his first records to radio stations without his photograph. He had 30 No. 1 country records and was named Entertainer of the Year by the Country Music Association in 1971 (Wikipedia, "Charley Pride").
- Dolly Parton's life and songs are told in 2000 to Today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hip-hop from the Bronx, 1973 onward" -->
- On August 11, 1973, Clive Campbell, a teenage DJ called Kool Herc, played records at a party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx, New York. His sister Cindy Campbell planned the party to earn money for back-to-school clothes. In 2007 city officials named the building the birthplace of hip-hop (Wikipedia, "DJ Kool Herc").
- In the same years, fires burned much of the South Bronx. Most were arson. Landlords paid local people to set fires in their buildings so they could collect insurance money. After a study by the RAND Corporation, city officials closed 12 fire companies in the South Bronx between 1974 and 1976, when the city was nearly broke. Between 1970 and 1980, seven census tracts, small areas the census counts separately, lost more than 97 percent of their buildings to fire and abandonment. About 250,000 people lost their homes (Wikipedia, "1970s South Bronx building fires"). `city-building` tells the fires.
- "Rapper's Delight" by the Sugarhill Gang, released on September 16, 1979, by Sylvia Robinson's Sugar Hill Records, reached No. 36 on the national pop chart. Many writers call it the first rap record sold widely. It used the music of Chic's "Good Times," and Chic's writers, Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards, got credit only after they threatened to sue. One of its rappers used rhymes written by Grandmaster Caz and did not credit him (Wikipedia, "Rapper's Delight").
- "The Message" (1982), by Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five, described poverty in the inner city. In 2002 it became the first hip-hop record added to the National Recording Registry, the Library of Congress's list of recordings chosen to be preserved (Wikipedia, "The Message").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="dj-kool-herc" name="DJ Kool Herc" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DJ Kool Herc
> **Who:** Born Clive Campbell in Jamaica, the DJ at the 1973 Bronx party that people name as the start of hip-hop. · **When and where:** Born April 16, 1955, in Kingston, Jamaica. Moved to the Bronx, New York, in November 1967, at 13.
- At parties he played funk records on two turntables. He used two copies of the same record, so when the "break," the part with only drums, ended on one, he started it again on the other. That made a short drum break last as long as he wanted. He called this the "Merry-Go-Round" (Wikipedia, "DJ Kool Herc").
- He called the dancers who danced to the breaks "b-boys" and "b-girls" (Wikipedia).
- His sister Cindy Campbell planned the August 11, 1973, party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue, to raise money for back-to-school clothes (Wikipedia).
- He was stabbed at a club called the Executive Playhouse while trying to stop a fight, and he pulled back from playing. By 1980 he had stopped working as a DJ. The records do not show what he earned from hip-hop (Wikipedia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dj-kool-herc" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Music television, compact discs, Woodstock and the lyrics hearing" -->
- The organizers of the Woodstock music festival, on Max Yasgur's dairy farm in Bethel, New York, from August 15 to 18, 1969, expected about 50,000 people. Estimates of the crowd run from 400,000 to more than 460,000, and no source names who counted. The fences were not finished, so the festival became free. Three people died: two from drug overdoses, and a 17-year-old who was run over by a tractor while he slept (Wikipedia, "Woodstock").
- The MTV channel began on August 1, 1981. Its managers said it played rock, and they played few Black artists. In 1983 Walter Yetnikoff, president of CBS Records, threatened to pull all CBS videos unless MTV played Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean." MTV began playing it often in March 1983 (Wikipedia, "MTV").
- Compact disc players went on sale in Japan on October 1, 1982, and in the United States in March 1983 (Wikipedia, "Sony CDP-101").
- In 1985 Tipper Gore, Susan Baker and two other women in Washington started the Parents Music Resource Center to warn parents about sex, violence and drugs in songs. On September 19, 1985, the Senate Commerce Committee held a hearing. The musicians Frank Zappa, Dee Snider and John Denver spoke against labels. On November 1, 1985, the heads of the record companies agreed to put warning labels on some albums, and in 1990 they began using a standard "Parental Advisory" sticker. Managers at Walmart and some other stores would not sell albums with the sticker (Wikipedia, "Parents Music Resource Center").
- In 1978 Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act. Its members wrote that Native "ceremonies have been intruded upon, interfered with, and in a few instances banned" (Public Law 95-341, from the `religion` bank). The rules against Native dances of 1883 could be used until then.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="selena-quintanilla" name="Selena Quintanilla" movie="Selena (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Selena Quintanilla
> **Who:** A Mexican American singer of Tejano music, the Spanish-language music of Texas. · **When and where:** Born April 16, 1971, in Lake Jackson, Texas. Killed March 31, 1995, in Corpus Christi, Texas, at 23. · **Movie:** *Selena* (1997), a Hollywood drama film about her, with Jennifer Lopez
- She sang with her brother and sister in their father Abraham Quintanilla Jr.'s band, Selena y Los Dinos. She learned the Spanish words of her songs by their sound, with her father's help (Wikipedia, "Selena").
- She won the Tejano Music Award for Female Vocalist of the Year in 1987 and in each of the next nine years. In 1994 she won a Grammy Award for her album *Live!* (Wikipedia).
- Yolanda Saldívar ran her fan club and managed her clothing stores. Saldívar had secretly taken money from the business, more than $30,000 or more than $60,000 by different counts. Selena meant to fire her (Wikipedia, "Selena" and "Murder of Selena").
- On March 31, 1995, at a Days Inn motel in Corpus Christi, Saldívar shot Selena with a revolver as Selena tried to leave the room. Selena ran to the lobby and named Saldívar. She died at the hospital that afternoon from loss of blood. Saldívar gave herself up to police more than nine hours later (Wikipedia, "Murder of Selena").
- Saldívar was convicted of murder and sentenced to life in prison. On March 27, 2025, members of the Texas parole board refused to let her out and set her next review for March 2030 (Texas Public Radio).
- Between 30,000 and 40,000 people came to see her coffin. Her album *Dreaming of You* came out after her death, and she became the first Latin artist whose album entered the national album chart at No. 1 (Wikipedia).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Sales of recorded music in the United States peaked in 1999, when most people bought CDs. A free program called Napster then let people copy songs from each other's computers. Sales fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015, and after counting rising prices, they ended about two-thirds lower. By 2025 most people paid a monthly fee to stream songs, and streaming brought in 82 cents of every dollar the record companies earned. A streaming service pays a fraction of a cent each time a song is played, and it pays the company that owns the recording, not the singer directly. Most working musicians earn more from playing live than from recordings. In 2017 Americans bought and streamed more hip-hop and R&B than any other kind of music. Dolly Parton, a songwriter from the Tennessee mountains, died on August 25, 2026, at 80. By September 2026, record companies were in court against companies whose computer programs make songs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Napster, the iPod and streaming, 1999 to 2025" -->
- Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker started Napster in June 1999. It let people copy music files from each other's computers for free. In December 1999 record companies sued. A federal judge ordered Napster to block copyrighted songs, and Napster could not do it. It shut down in July 2001, when it had about 26.4 million users (Wikipedia, "Napster").
- Apple showed the iPod on October 23, 2001, and its boss, Steve Jobs, said it held "1,000 songs in your pocket." The iTunes Store opened on April 28, 2003, and sold most songs for 99 cents each. By 2014 it had sold 35 billion songs (Wikipedia, "iPod" and "iTunes Store").
- Spotify, a Swedish company, began streaming in the United States in July 2011. Streaming means playing a song over the internet without buying a copy. By March 2026 Spotify had 300 million paying subscribers around the world (Wikipedia, "Spotify").
- The Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), the record companies' trade group, counts what they earn. Sales peaked in 1999 at $20.9 billion, counted in 2016 dollars. They fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015, and in 2016 they were $7.57 billion (American Enterprise Institute, 2017, using RIAA figures). In 2016, for the first time, Americans paid more for streaming than for CDs and downloads. In 2025 the companies took in a record $11.5 billion at wholesale, the price the companies are paid, and $9.5 billion of it came from streaming (RIAA, March 16, 2026).
- Vinyl records came back. Americans spent $1.04 billion on them in 2025 (Music Business Worldwide, citing the RIAA).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a stream pays" -->
- Spotify pays about 70 percent of the money it takes in to the owners of the music. About 58.5 percent goes to whoever owns the recording, usually a record company. About 12 percent goes to whoever owns the song itself, the publisher and the songwriter. The singer gets a share from the record company, under the singer's contract (Wikipedia, "Spotify").
- Spotify pays by each recording's share of all plays, not a set price per play. In 2025 a music distributor, iMusician, put the average at $0.003 to $0.005 per play, and about $0.004 for a listener in the United States (iMusician, February 14, 2025).
- From April 1, 2024, Spotify stopped paying anything for a recording played fewer than 1,000 times in a year. It said tens of millions of such recordings had each earned about 3 cents a month (Spotify for Artists, November 21, 2023).
- Spotify says it paid the music business more than $11 billion in 2025. By its count, more than 1,500 artists earned over $1 million from it that year, and the artist ranked 100,000th earned about $7,300 (Spotify, "Loud & Clear," March 11, 2026).
- In March 2021 a musicians' group, the United Musicians and Allied Workers, protested in 31 cities and asked for one cent per play (Wikipedia, "Spotify").
- In 2018 the Music Industry Research Association surveyed 1,227 American musicians. Most earned money from playing live, and most could not live on music alone. Seventy-two percent of the women said they had faced discrimination because they were women (Nashville Scene, July 5, 2018).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owns a recording: masters and songs" -->
- Every recorded song has two copyrights. One covers the song, its words and tune. The other covers the master, the first recording that every copy is made from. Whoever owns the master is paid for every copy and every stream of that recording (Wikipedia, "Taylor Swift masters dispute").
- Record companies owned most masters. Ray Charles and Sam Cooke were among the few singers of the 1950s and 1960s who owned their own (see 1950 to 2000).
- In December 2020 Bob Dylan sold the rights to all of his songs to Universal Music Publishing Group. The New York Times estimated the price at more than $300 million, and other reports said about $400 million (Wikipedia, "Bob Dylan").
- Dolly Parton owned the rights to her songs all her life. Sony Music owns her recordings for RCA, and probably for Columbia, about 23 albums (Billboard, August 28, 2026). Her story is below.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Music Modernization Act, 2018" -->
- Members of the House passed the Music Modernization Act on April 25, 2018. Senators passed it unanimously on September 19, and President Donald Trump signed it on October 11, 2018 (Wikipedia, "Music Modernization Act").
- It set up a nonprofit group, the Mechanical Licensing Collective, to keep a list of who owns each song and pay songwriters for streams. In 2021 the group began paying out money that had been held because no one knew whose songs had been played (Wikipedia).
- Recordings made before February 15, 1972, had no federal copyright, so internet radio services did not have to pay for playing them. The act changed that, so the singers and musicians on those older records are paid when digital radio plays them (Wikipedia).

- Auto-Tune is a computer program that moves a singer's off-key notes onto the right pitch. The engineer Andy Hildebrand invented it, and his company released it on September 19, 1997. Cher used it in 1998 to make her voice sound like a machine. The rapper and singer T-Pain used it that way from his 2005 album *Rappa Ternt Sanga* on, and Kanye West used it on his 2008 album *808s & Heartbreak* (Wikipedia, "Auto-Tune").
- On June 1, 2008, a worker at Universal Studios Hollywood used a blowtorch to heat roof shingles and left before they cooled. The fire burned a video vault. In 2019 a New York Times Magazine writer, Jody Rosen, reported that the same fire also destroyed 118,000 to 175,000 of Universal Music Group's master tapes, possibly including records by Chuck Berry, Muddy Waters and John Coltrane. Universal's managers said the report contained "numerous inaccuracies." They confirmed that the masters of one Soundgarden album burned (Wikipedia, "2008 Universal Studios fire").
- Companies such as Suno and Udio sell computer programs that make whole songs from a typed request. In June 2024 the largest record companies sued both, saying the programs had been trained on their recordings without permission. Universal settled with Udio in October 2025, and Warner settled with Suno in November 2025. Udio and Suno agreed to build new programs trained only on licensed music. In September 2026 Universal and Sony were still suing Suno, and Suno admitted in court papers that it had downloaded audio from YouTube (Wikipedia, "Suno (platform)" and "Udio").
- On September 1, 2026, the songwriter Jason Isbell and three other musicians sued Suno for themselves and other artists (Wikipedia, "Suno (platform)").
- In March 2025 a federal judge, Eumi Lee, refused music publishers' request to stop the company Anthropic from using song lyrics to train its computer program while their case went on (Hollywood Reporter, March 2025).
- By the end of September 2026, no American court had given a final ruling on whether a company may train a song-making program on recordings without permission.

### Dolly Parton
> **Who:** A singer and songwriter from the Great Smoky Mountains of Tennessee who wrote more than 3,000 songs, owned the rights to them, and mailed free books to children. · **When and where:** Born January 19, 1946, in a one-room cabin in Pittman Center, Sevier County, Tennessee. Moved to Nashville in 1964. Died August 25, 2026, in Nashville, at 80. · **Movie:** *Dolly Parton: Here I Am* (2019), a documentary about her
- She was the fourth of 12 children. Her father, Robert Lee Parton, grew tobacco and could not read or write. He paid the doctor who delivered her with a sack of cornmeal. Her mother, Avie Lee Parton, sang old ballads to the children. She sang in her grandfather's church from age six (Wikipedia, "Dolly Parton").
- By ten she sang on a Knoxville radio and television show. At 13 she made her first record and sang at the Grand Ole Opry. The day after she finished high school in 1964, she moved to Nashville (Wikipedia).
- In 1967 Porter Wagoner hired her for his weekly television show. Their duets sold well for six years. From 1969 Wagoner owned nearly half of her song-publishing company (Wikipedia).
- She wrote her first No. 1 song, "Joshua," alone, and it reached the top of the country chart in February 1971 (NPR. Wikipedia). "Coat of Many Colors" (1971) is about her family's poverty.
- In 1973 she wrote "Jolene" and "I Will Always Love You" within a few days. She wrote "I Will Always Love You" as a goodbye to Wagoner when she left his show in 1974 (Wikipedia, "I Will Always Love You").
- In 1974 Elvis Presley wanted to record "I Will Always Love You." His manager, Colonel Tom Parker, told her that the songwriter had to give Presley's side half the publishing rights. She said no. In 1992 Whitney Houston's recording of the song sold more than 24 million copies around the world. Parton said, "I made enough money to buy Graceland" (Wikipedia, "I Will Always Love You").
- She wrote "9 to 5" for the 1980 film about three women office workers, in which she acted. It reached No. 1 on the country and pop charts in February 1981 (Wikipedia).
- She sold more than 100 million records, had at least 25 No. 1 country songs and won 11 Grammy Awards. She turned down the Presidential Medal of Freedom three times (Wikipedia).
- In 1986 she became a partner in a theme park in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, and renamed it Dollywood. It has about 4,000 to 4,500 workers, the most of any employer in Sevier County. From 2022 it paid college costs for workers who chose to go (Wikipedia. CNN, August 27, 2026).
- In 1995 she started the Imagination Library in Sevier County because her father could not read. It mails one free book a month to each child who signs up, from birth to age five. Her foundation and local partners pay for the books. By 2026 it had mailed more than 300 million books in five countries (imaginationlibrary.com. EdWeek, August 31, 2026).
- In the spring of 2020 she gave $1 million to Vanderbilt University Medical Center for coronavirus research, in honor of her friend Dr. Naji Abumrad. A research paper on Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine listed her fund among its supporters. The US government put about $2.5 billion into that vaccine (Fortune, August 26, 2026. USA Today, November 26, 2020).
- After wildfires burned homes near Gatlinburg, Tennessee, in November 2016, her My People Fund paid $1,000 a month for six months to more than 900 families affected by the fires, and $5,000 in the last month (Wikipedia).
- She owned the rights to her songs all her life. Sony Music owns her recordings for RCA, and probably for Columbia. From 1995 she owned most of her new recordings on her own label (Billboard, August 28, 2026).
