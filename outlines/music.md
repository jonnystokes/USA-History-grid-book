## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Music here was not something a person sat and watched. It belonged to ceremonies, to healing, to games, to work, to war, and to putting children to sleep, and in many nations particular songs belonged to particular families or societies and could not be sung by anyone else. Nobody wrote the music down, and no composers' names survive from before 1500. The traditions continued, and singers and drum groups working in them today are named and documented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Voices, drums, flutes, and rattles" -->
Instruments to name with places and dates [VERIFY each]: hand drums and large frame drums; water drums; gourd and turtle-shell rattles; notched-stick rasps; cane and wooden flutes, including flutes recovered from dry caves in the Southwest that are more than a thousand years old. Say what is known about the archaeology and what is not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs that belong to someone" -->
In many nations a song was property: it was received, given, or inherited, and singing another person's song without the right was a real offence [VERIFY with a specific documented tradition rather than a general claim]. This matters later in the book, when songs from these traditions were recorded and sold by people who had no right to them [VERIFY the twentieth-century cases and park the strong ones in `native-nations`].

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Almost no Europeans lived in this land in the 1500s, so there was almost no European music being made here. What there was came with Spanish soldiers and priests: church singing at the missions in Florida and, at the very end of the century, New Mexico. Native music continued everywhere else.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mission singing" -->
Spanish missionaries taught plainchant and simple polyphony to Native singers and used the singing as part of conversion [VERIFY the earliest documented mission choirs, where, and in what year]. Where missionaries banned Native ceremonies, they were banning the songs with them; find the documented orders and quote them rather than describing them [VERIFY].

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the English colonies of the 1600s, most music was singing in church, and most of it was psalms. Many New England meetinghouses had no instruments at all. There were no concerts to buy tickets for and no music printed here except psalm books.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalm singing and the Bay Psalm Book, 1640" -->
The *Bay Psalm Book*, printed at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640, was the first book printed in the English colonies; it held the words, not the tunes [VERIFY, including which later edition first carried printed music]. Congregations sang by lining out: one person called a line and everyone sang it back, which is how people who could not read music sang together [VERIFY the term and the practice]. That call-and-response habit stayed in American church singing for centuries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
The first Africans sold into slavery in Virginia arrived in 1619 [VERIFY the number and the ship; `slavery-freedom` leads]. They came from societies with their own instruments, tunings, and ways of singing together, and those did not stop at the dock. What can actually be documented about music in the 1600s colonies, as opposed to inferred from later evidence, is thin — say which is which [VERIFY].

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two kinds of music grew at once. In New England, ministers who thought congregational singing had gone bad started singing schools to teach it, and those schools became the first music lessons most colonists ever had. In the plantation colonies, enslaved people were making music that white owners used at their dances and feared at every other time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Singing schools" -->
Singing schools spread through New England from the 1720s: an itinerant teacher, a few weeks in a town, shape-note or letter methods, a fee per pupil [VERIFY the earliest documented school and the fee]. They were also one of the few places young men and women could gather in the evening, which is part of why they took hold [VERIFY that this is documented and not folklore].
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and dance tunes" -->
Fiddle and dance music at taverns and in houses. The banjo descends from West African gourd-and-skin instruments, brought and rebuilt here by enslaved people; the earliest colonial descriptions of it are documented and should be quoted [VERIFY the sources and dates]. Enslaved musicians were made to play at white weddings and balls, and colonial newspapers advertised runaways by naming them as fiddlers, which is how some of their names survive at all [VERIFY with actual advertisements].
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1740 South Carolina law against drums" -->
After the Stono Rebellion of 1739, South Carolina passed the Negro Act of 1740, which among other things made it illegal for enslaved people to beat drums, blow horns, or use other loud instruments, on the stated ground that they could be used to signal [VERIFY the wording of the section and quote it]. Say plainly what that law did: it made a kind of music a crime, and the reason given was that the music could carry a message. `slavery-freedom` leads on Stono itself.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first American composers published their own music, and it was mostly written for people who could not read music to sing in a room together. Armies moved to fifes and drums. In the port towns, free and enslaved Black musicians played for a living, and a few of them ran their own schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two things happened in these fifty years that shaped everything after. Enslaved people made a body of song — the spirituals, the work songs, the field hollers — that later became the root of most American popular music. And white performers built the country's most popular entertainment out of pretending to be Black people and making them look stupid.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The music made under slavery" -->
Spirituals, work songs, field hollers, and ring shouts. What is documented: the songs themselves, collected and printed from 1867 onward in *Slave Songs of the United States*, and the descriptions written by observers before then [VERIFY]. What is disputed: the claim that particular spirituals carried coded escape directions. Historians disagree about it, and the book states the disagreement rather than choosing [VERIFY who argues what]. Owners banned drums but not singing, and singing was used to set the pace of work and to say things that could not be said plainly [VERIFY specific documented cases]. `slavery-freedom` leads on worship and on survival; this chapter leads on the music as music.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blackface minstrelsy, from the 1830s" -->
Tell this plainly, at a young reader's level, without softening. White men blacked their faces with burnt cork, put on ragged clothes, and performed as Black characters who were lazy, greedy, stupid, and happy to be enslaved. Audiences paid to laugh at that. Thomas D. Rice's Jump Jim Crow act dates from about 1830, and the Virginia Minstrels put the full show together in 1843 [VERIFY both]. Within a decade it was the most popular stage entertainment in the country, playing in the North far more than the South [VERIFY]. Two facts to carry: the segregation laws later called Jim Crow took that name from a minstrel character [VERIFY the path of the name], and much of the music the shows used was taken from Black musicians who were paid nothing [VERIFY documented cases]. Stephen Foster wrote songs for minstrel shows, including songs still sung today, and the original words of several are racist caricature; say so and say what he changed later [VERIFY].
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns, shape notes, and camp meetings" -->
Camp-meeting songs of the revivals; shape-note singing books, ending with *The Sacred Harp* in 1844 [VERIFY]. Shape notes let people who could not read standard notation sing four-part music from a book; the tradition is still practiced [VERIFY where]. `religion` leads on the revivals themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concert halls, brass bands, and singing families" -->
Town brass bands. Touring European performers, and the money Americans paid to hear them [VERIFY figures for the 1850 Jenny Lind tour, which is in the next era]. The Hutchinson Family Singers toured with antislavery songs and were attacked for it [VERIFY specific incidents].

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Music became something that could be sold as an object. Printed sheet music went into millions of parlors with pianos in them, and by the 1890s a machine could hold a performance and play it back. In the same years, the songs made under slavery went on tour, and the blues and ragtime took shape in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Fisk Jubilee Singers, from 1871" -->
Fisk University in Nashville was nearly out of money, and in 1871 a group of its students, most of them born enslaved, went on tour singing spirituals to raise it [VERIFY the number of singers and the amount raised]. They sang in northern cities and then in Britain, and were refused hotels and meals along the way [VERIFY documented incidents]. Their tour is how most white audiences first heard this music sung straight rather than in a minstrel show.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War songs and brass bands" -->
Songs both armies sang, and the ones each side wrote about the other [VERIFY]. The Battle Hymn of the Republic, words written by Julia Ward Howe in 1861 and published in 1862, set to a tune already in use [VERIFY the dates and the tune's origin]. Regimental bands; John Philip Sousa led the Marine Band from 1880 and then his own band [VERIFY dates and the marches].
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ragtime" -->
Ragtime published as sheet music from the 1890s. Scott Joplin's Maple Leaf Rag was published in 1899 and sold in numbers no piano piece had sold before [VERIFY the sales figure and the royalty arrangement]. <!-- /hb-zoom -->

Edison's tin-foil phonograph, 1877; wax cylinders sold commercially from the late 1880s; coin-operated machines in arcades in the 1890s [VERIFY dates; `technology` leads on the machines]. George W. ## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This is the half-century when American music became the music the rest of the world listened to. Jazz, blues, country, and gospel were all recorded for the first time, radio carried them into houses for free, and the people who made the music and the people who owned the records were mostly not the same people.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Teenagers with money became the customer the record business worked for. Rock and roll, soul, country, and hip-hop were each built by particular people in particular places, and each one was sold widest by someone other than the people who built it. By the end of the century American music was the largest single share of what the world listened to.
