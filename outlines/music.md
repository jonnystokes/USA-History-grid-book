## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, every nation in this land had its own songs, and most music was made for a purpose: a ceremony, a healing, a dance, a game or a burial. Nobody wrote the music down, and no singer's or maker's name survives from these centuries. What survives is the instruments. Archaeologists have dug up flutes and panpipes that are more than a thousand years old. The nations kept their songs by teaching them, one generation to the next, and many of those songs are still sung.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Flutes and panpipes that survive" -->
- In 1931 the archaeologist Earl H. Morris dug in 15 cliff caves in the Prayer Rock district of northeastern Arizona, which is now part of the Navajo Nation. In the largest cave he found four wooden flutes and named the place Broken Flute Cave (Arizona State Museum).
- The flutes date from 620 to 670 CE. The Arizona State Museum, which holds them in Tucson, calls them the oldest known wooden flutes found in North America. The museum lists them as Ancestral Pueblo flutes, made by the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples (Arizona State Museum).
- Each flute is a tube of box elder wood about 69 to 74 centimeters long (a little over two feet), with six finger holes, played by blowing across one end. The finger holes are almost the same on each flute, which shows the maker planned the notes. Red, blue and black feathers were tied to one end (Arizona State Museum, *Arizona Highways*).
- Two flutes lay broken in trash above a grave. The other two lay under a dirt floor, whole and tied together (*Arizona Highways*).
- Farther east, people of the Hopewell culture built earthworks in the woodlands of eastern North America from 1 to 400 CE (National Park Service, Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, Ohio). Archaeologists have found 105 panpipes at 55 Hopewell sites, from Wisconsin and Ontario to the Gulf Coast. A panpipe here is three or four tubes of cane, reed or bone, bound in a jacket of hammered metal. Of the 102 jackets whose metal is known, 81 are copper and the rest are silver, copper covered with silver, or iron. Almost all were found in graves or burial mounds, not in houses (Turff and Carr, "Hopewellian Panpipes from Eastern North America," 2005).
- Nobody knows what tunes were played on these instruments. No written music exists from before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums, rattles, and songs that belong to someone" -->
- The Onondaga Nation, one of the Haudenosaunee nations of what is now New York, plays its songs on a water drum and a horn rattle. The drum is made of cedar with deer hide stretched over the top. The horn rattle is a dried, hollowed animal horn with seeds inside. A turtle-shell rattle is used only in a few ceremonies (Onondaga Nation website).
- The Onondaga keep two kinds of songs. Social songs can be sung anywhere. Ceremonial songs are sung only in the longhouse and are not for public viewing (Onondaga Nation website).
- In the Tlingit nations of what is now southeast Alaska, a clan owns its songs, stories, crests and ceremonial clothing. The Tlingit word for these things is *at.óow*, which means "something owned or purchased." The songs and stories that tell of a clan's losses in a place are, in the Tlingit view, the clan's deed to that place (National Park Service, Glacier Bay, "At.óowu: Tlingit Homeland").
- These rules come from the nations' own present-day accounts. No written record from before 1500 describes them, because nobody here wrote before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="r-carlos-nakai-music" name="R. Carlos Nakai" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### R. Carlos Nakai
> **Who:** A flute player of Navajo (Diné) and Ute descent who plays a traditional cedar flute. · **When and where:** Born April 16, 1946, in Flagstaff, Arizona. Still performing in 2026 (his own 2026 concert calendar).
- No flute player's name survives from before 1500. Nakai plays the same kind of instrument today and has recorded more than forty albums with Canyon Records in Phoenix, starting with *Changes* in 1983 (Nakai's own biography, rcarlosnakai.com).
- He first studied the trumpet. A car accident ruined his embouchure, the way a trumpet player shapes the lips against the mouthpiece, and he could no longer play it. He was given a cedar flute as a gift and challenged to master it (Nakai biography).
- His albums *Canyon Trilogy* and *Earth Spirit* were the first recordings of the traditional Native flute to sell 500,000 copies each. In 2014 *Canyon Trilogy* passed one million copies. He has been nominated for ten Grammy awards between 1994 and 2013 (Nakai biography).
- His father, Raymond Nakai, was chairman of the Navajo Nation from 1963 to 1970 (Wikipedia).
- Nakai calls himself "an iconoclastic traditionalist." He means that he treats his heritage as something that changes and grows, not something frozen (Nakai biography).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s almost all music in this land was still Native music. European music arrived only where European ships landed, and it came as church singing: Spanish priests singing Latin hymns, and French and English Protestants singing psalms, which are songs from the Bible. The Europeans sang these hymns and psalms when they claimed land and when they held services. Some Native people who watched and listened left their reactions in the written record. No European music was printed or taught in a school here in this century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns sung while claiming land, Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
- In June 1564 French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline on the St. Johns River, near today's Jacksonville, Florida, on Timucua land. Their leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on 30 June he ordered the men to sing aloud on the beach to give thanks to God for their safe arrival (Laudonnière, *L'histoire notable de la Floride*, quoted by the National Huguenot Society). The historian Francis Parkman wrote in 1865 that on the morning they began the fort, the men sang a psalm before they started work (Parkman, *Pioneers of France in the New World*).
- On 8 September 1565 the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at the Timucua town of Seloy and founded St. Augustine. His chaplain, Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, wrote that Menéndez came ashore "to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery," and that he himself walked to meet him with a cross, "singing the hymn Te Deum laudamus," a Latin hymn of praise. "A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done" (Mendoza Grajales, his account of the founding).
- Menéndez's soldiers took Fort Caroline on 20 September 1565 and killed most of the men there. When Menéndez came back to St. Augustine, Mendoza and his companions walked ahead of him "in a procession, singing the Te Deum laudamus" (Mendoza Grajales). In the weeks after, Menéndez had about 245 more French prisoners killed at Matanzas Inlet (NPS Fort Matanzas counts). `war` tells the killings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalms at Drake's landing, California, 1579" -->
- In June 1579 the English captain Francis Drake stopped on the coast north of San Francisco Bay to repair his ship. The land belonged to the Coast Miwok people. Drake's men held prayers, sang psalms and read from the Bible while Miwok people watched (*The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake*, London, 1628, drawn mainly from the notes of Drake's chaplain, Francis Fletcher, per Heizer).
- The account says the Miwok "tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes, that whensoeuer they resorted to vs, their first request was Gnaah, by which they intreated that we would sing." The anthropologist Robert Heizer found that *gnaah* is a Coast Miwok word (Heizer, *Francis Drake and the California Indians, 1579*, 1947).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
- In 1598 Juan de Oñate led about 500 colonists north across the Rio Grande to found a Spanish colony in New Mexico, among the Pueblo peoples. At a ceremony on 30 April 1598, near today's El Paso, a Te Deum was sung and Franciscan priests said Mass (Wikipedia, "La Toma," from Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá's 1610 *Historia de la Nueva México*) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The music historian John Koegel writes that Pueblo people began to hear Spanish military music and to sing Catholic chant and part-songs "during the Oñate expedition and its aftermath" (Koegel, "Music and Christianization on the Northern Frontier of New Spain"). The teaching that is documented by name belongs to the 1600s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s the colonists' music that was written down was church music. In New England, Puritan congregations sang psalms from a book printed in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640, and for most of the century that book printed only the words. In New Mexico, Spanish friars built organs and trained Pueblo singers to sing Latin church music. The same friars and Spanish governors banned Pueblo religious ceremonies and punished the Pueblo leaders who kept them. Africans were brought to the English colonies from 1619, and they brought their own music, but almost nothing about it was written down in this century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bay Psalm Book, 1640, and lining out" -->
- *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre*, now called the *Bay Psalm Book*, was printed by Stephen Daye at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640. It is the first book printed in British North America. Richard Mather and other New England ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into rhyming verse so that congregations could sing them. Eleven copies of the first printing survive (Library of Congress, in `religion` and `technology` banks).
- The 1640 book had no printed music. It named tunes that people already knew from English psalm books. The ninth edition, printed in 1698, was the first to include music, 13 tunes taken from an English music book by John Playford (Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music," Wikipedia).
- Most people in a congregation had no book, and some could not read. In 1644 an assembly of English and Scottish ministers at Westminster wrote a guide for worship. It said that where many could not read, the minister or another person he chose should "read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof" (*Directory for the Public Worship of God*, 1644).
- This practice is called lining out. A leader read or sang one line, and the congregation sang it back. Colonists from England and Scotland brought it to America. Old Regular Baptist churches in the Appalachian Mountains still sing this way today (Wikipedia, "Lining out").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church music and banned ceremonies in New Mexico" -->
- Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones, a Franciscan friar who died in 1609, learned the Keres language and built the church at San Felipe Pueblo. A Franciscan history by Agustín de Vetancurt says he "installed an organ" and "encouraged music-making by the skilled singers" he trained. The music historian John Koegel calls him "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music" living in what is now the US Southwest (Koegel, quoting Vetancurt's *Menologio Franciscano*).
- Fray Bernardo de Marta came to New Mexico about 1605 and taught Pueblo people in many towns to sing and to play European instruments. He died in 1635. By 1634 Fray Roque de Figueredo was teaching part-singing to Zuni boys (Koegel).
- From 1656 to 1665, the Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered friars to go into the kivas, the Pueblo ceremonial rooms, to burn the masks and to take or destroy sacred objects (Dennis Zotigh, National Museum of the American Indian, 2026, in `religion` bank).
- In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested and charged with sorcery. Three were hanged, one killed himself, and the rest were whipped in public (Zotigh, in `religion` and `holidays` banks). Pueblo people drove the Spanish out of New Mexico in the revolt of 1680 and killed about 400 settlers, 21 of them Franciscans (Koegel). `native-nations` and `religion` tell the revolt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
- In late August 1619 the English privateer ship *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes," in the colonist John Rolfe's words, at Point Comfort, Virginia. They had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship and came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa (`slavery-freedom` bank, from the National Park Service). `slavery-freedom` tells the arrival.
- Enslaved Africans brought their own songs and ways of making instruments. Records from the 1600s mainland colonies say almost nothing about that music. The first written description of a banjo-like instrument in the Americas comes from Jamaica, where the English doctor Hans Sloane wrote about a "strum strump" in 1687 (OUP blog, "10 fun facts about the banjo," 2014). The first mention on the mainland is from 1736 and belongs to the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, New England ministers set out to teach their congregations to sing from written notes, and they started the first singing schools and printed the first books of music lessons in the colonies. In Boston people could buy a ticket to a concert from 1731. In the plantation colonies, enslaved Black musicians played the fiddle for white dances, and the only written records of many of them are the newspaper notices their enslavers printed when they ran away. After enslaved men rose up at Stono in 1739 with drums beating, the men who ran South Carolina made it a crime for enslaved people to use drums and horns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Regular singing and the first singing schools, 1720s" -->
- By about 1700, most New England congregations sang by lining out and by ear. The historian Henry Wilder Foote wrote that by the end of the 1600s "singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies." In 1720 the Reverend Thomas Symmes of Bradford, Massachusetts, published *The Reasonableness of Regular Singing, or Singing by Note*. He wrote that singing had declined because "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn" (Henry Wilder Foote, "Musical Life in Boston in the Eighteenth Century," American Antiquarian Society, 1939).
- The Reverend John Tufts of Newbury, Massachusetts, published *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes*, the first book of music lessons printed in the English colonies. Foote dates the first edition to about 1712, and other sources date it to about 1721. No copy of the first edition is known, so the exact date is disputed. Tufts travelled to give talks and to set up singing schools (Foote).
- In 1721 the Reverend Thomas Walter, who was 25, published *The Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained*, with 24 psalm tunes. James Franklin printed it in Boston. It was the first music in the colonies printed with bar lines, and it stayed in use in New England singing schools for more than forty years (Foote. Matt B. Jones, American Antiquarian Society, 1932).
- Young people took up the new singing first. On 20 March 1721 the Boston judge Samuel Sewall wrote in his diary that Cotton Mather preached at night "in the school house to the young musicians." "House was full, and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston" (Sewall's diary, quoted by Foote).
- Older church members fought the change. Symmes reported that "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." In 1728 the Reverend Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut answered the objections. One was that singing by note "leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly." Another was that the new way must be wrong "because the young people fall in with it" (Foote).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concerts for a ticket" -->
- The *Boston News-Letter* of December 1731 advertised a "Concert of Music" on several instruments for 30 December 1731 at Mr. Pelham's great room, with tickets at five shillings each. It is the earliest advertised concert in Boston that the historian Oscar Sonneck found (Foote, citing Sonneck, *Early Concert Life in America*).
- After a concert at Faneuil Hall in 1744, the town's selectmen received £205 for the poor of Boston (Foote).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and the enslaved musicians who played them" -->
- The banjo came from West Africa. Enslaved Africans rebuilt it in the Americas. Scholars have found more than 60 West African plucked instruments that may be its ancestors (OUP blog, "10 fun facts about the banjo," 2014).
- The first known mention of a banjo in the mainland colonies is a letter in John Peter Zenger's *New-York Weekly Journal* in 1736 (OUP blog). The letter describes an enslaved servant tuning his "banger." It was printed without a name and may have been made up (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Enslavers in Virginia printed notices in the *Virginia Gazette* when enslaved people ran away. The notices describe each person's body, clothes and skills so that strangers could catch them. Playing the fiddle was one of the skills they listed. On 5 December 1745 Robert Whitfield of Isle of Wight County offered four pistoles, a Spanish gold coin, for "a likely young Negroe Man, named Tom," who "plays very well on the Violin." In April 1746 John Thornton of Fredericksburg advertised for a man named Tom, about 46, who "plays on the Violin, and is a Sawyer" (*Virginia Gazette*, in the University of Virginia's Geography of Slavery database).
- These notices are the only records that survive of many enslaved musicians. They survive because the men ran.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Harry, a fiddler who ran to his wife, 1746
> **Who:** An enslaved man who played the fiddle. His enslaver advertised for his capture. · **When and where:** Near Jamestown, Virginia, March 1746.
- In March 1746 Harry ran away from the man who held him, near Jamestown, Virginia. The notice gives him no last name (*Virginia Gazette*, 20 to 27 March 1746).
- The notice describes Harry as about 35 years old, about 5 feet 7 inches tall, with small eyes and "a very large Beard," and says he "plays upon the Fiddle." He had once belonged to Colonel Grymes of Richmond County.
- The advertiser, William Newgent (a later reprint spells his first name James), guessed that Harry had gone to Richmond County, "where he has a Wife." He offered one pistole, a gold coin, for Harry's return.
- Newgent added: "As he ran away without any Cause, I desire he may be punish'd by Whipping, as the Law directs."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harry-fiddler-music" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums at Stono, and the 1740 law against drums and horns" -->
- On 9 September 1739 about 20 enslaved men, whom a report of the time called "Angola Negroes," led by a man called Jemmy, seized guns near the Stono River, south of Charleston, South Carolina, and marched south toward Spanish Florida. The report says that more men joined them, "calling out Liberty," and "marched on with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating." When there were sixty to a hundred of them, they stopped in a field and "set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them" ("An Account of the Negroe Insurrection in South Carolina," 1739, a report attributed to James Oglethorpe, the governor of Georgia).
- The same report says that the rebels killed white men, women and children as they marched. The militia, made up of armed planters, attacked them in the field. The planters shot many of the captured men on the spot, including men who ran back to their plantations. The report counts about 40 Black people and 20 white people killed. Other counts give about 25 white colonists and 35 to 50 rebels (`slavery-freedom` bank). `slavery-freedom` tells the uprising.
- On 10 May 1740 the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. Section 36 said it was "absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province" to stop enslaved people from "using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments, which may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes" (Negro Act of 1740, section 36, US History Scene). The section is confirmed on the page, and the exact wording is from search results (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The law fined any master or overseer who allowed enslaved people to beat drums, blow horns or gather in public ten pounds for each offence. It allowed any master, overseer or other person to seize an enslaved person found off the plantation without a written pass and without a white person, especially on Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays. A person found carrying a weapon could be disarmed and whipped (Negro Act, section 36, via US History Scene).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, people in the colonies began to publish music they had written themselves. The best known was William Billings, a Boston tanner, who printed his first book of church songs in 1770. During the Revolution, soldiers marched and camped to the orders of fifes and drums, and both armies sang new words to old tunes. Enslaved and free Black musicians played fiddles, horns, drums and banjos in the port towns and on plantations. Newport Gardner, taken from Africa as a boy, became a music teacher in Newport, Rhode Island, and bought his freedom in 1791. On the Pacific coast in the same year, a Spanish expedition wrote down Tlingit songs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="William Billings and the New England singing masters" -->
- In 1770 William Billings, a 24-year-old Boston tanner, published *The New-England Psalm-Singer*. The Library of Congress calls it "the first publication consisting of sacred music composed entirely by a native-born American" (Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music"). Paul Revere, the silversmith, engraved the picture at the front (David W. Stowe, The Conversation, 2026).
- Billings opened a singing school in Boston with John Barry in 1769 and taught one in Stoughton, Massachusetts, in 1774 (Wikipedia, "William Billings").
- The historian Henry Foote wrote in 1939 that about forty American books of church music were published before 1800, most of them in Boston (Foote, American Antiquarian Society).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-billings" name="William Billings" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Billings
> **Who:** A tanner and singing teacher who wrote about 340 songs for choirs. His 1770 book was the first book of church music written entirely by an American-born composer. · **When and where:** Boston, born October 7, 1746, died September 26, 1800.
- Billings's father died when he was 14, and Billings went to work learning to tan leather. He taught himself music, mostly from books (Wikipedia, "William Billings").
- The music historian Nathaniel Gould wrote in 1853 that Billings had "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered" (quoted by Stowe, The Conversation, 2026).
- His first book, *The New-England Psalm-Singer* (1770), included his song "Chester." Its first words are "Let tyrants shake their iron rod, And Slav'ry clank her galling chains, We fear them not, we trust in God, New England's God forever reigns." In 1778 he printed a longer version with more verses against the British in his second book, *The Singing Master's Assistant* (Scott Giltner, "Chester," Sons of the American Revolution, 2024).
- Soldiers "learned the words by heart" and carried the songs wherever they were sent (Stowe).
- He published six tune books. American copyright law was weak, and other printers copied his tunes without paying him. The men of the Massachusetts legislature once passed a bill to protect his work, but the governor refused to sign it (Stowe. Wikipedia).
- His wife, Lucy, died on March 26, 1795, and left him with six children under 18. Friends paid to print his last book, *The Continental Harmony*, in 1794. In the 1790s he worked as a street cleaner (Wikipedia. Stowe).
- He died poor in Boston in 1800 and was buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common (Stowe).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-billings" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs and signals of the Revolution" -->
- "Yankee Doodle" began as a British song that made fun of the colonists. The words are said to have been written by a British army surgeon, Richard Shuckburgh, around 1755 to 1758, during the French and Indian War. The date is disputed (Wikipedia, "Yankee Doodle").
- When British soldiers arrived to occupy Boston in 1768, their musicians played it in the streets to mock the colonists. Early in the war, British and Loyalist troops played it to make fun of the Continental soldiers. The Americans then made up their own verses, and by 1781 it was a favorite American song (Scott Giltner, "Yankee Doodle," Sons of the American Revolution, 2024).
- In July 1768 the *Boston Gazette* printed "The Liberty Song," written by John Dickinson of Pennsylvania to the tune of a British navy song, "Heart of Oak." Dickinson sent it to his friend James Otis and wrote that "indifferent songs are very powerful on certain occasions" (Massachusetts Historical Society).
- In the Continental Army, drums gave orders. "The Reveille" woke the soldiers in the morning. "The General" told them to take down their tents and get ready to march. "The Assembly" told them to line up at the flags. "To Arms" told them to get their weapons and prepare to fight. "The Tattoo," beaten at 9 or 10 at night, told tavern keepers to stop serving the soldiers (American Battlefield Trust, "Music in the 18th Century Army").
- The American Battlefield Trust writes that boys under 14 were recommended as drummers, and that some fifers were 9 or 10 years old.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black musicians, enslaved and free" -->
- Virginia enslavers kept printing notices that named enslaved musicians. On 4 May 1769 William Gregory advertised for Peter, about 44, who had taken "a fiddle, which he is much delighted in." On 8 September 1775 David Walker of Dinwiddie County advertised for Jemmy, who "is very fond of singing hymns and preaching" and was living near Williamsburg under the name James Williams (*Virginia Gazette*, in the University of Virginia's Geography of Slavery database).
- In August 1780 William Allason offered forty pounds for four men who had run away together from near Fauquier Court House, Virginia. One was Mark, about 25, born on the island of St. Jago (Santiago, in the Cape Verde Islands), who spoke Portuguese. The notice says he "blows the French horn, can play on the fiddle, whistles many tunes well," and loved marches and Catholic church music. Allason guessed that the four men planned to get aboard a ship (*Virginia Gazette*, 9 August 1780, Geography of Slavery database).
- In Charleston, South Carolina, a newspaper notice of 12 December 1766 describes "a croud of negroes assembled at the lower end of Elliott-street, with a banjer playing," on the evening of 2 December. A Charleston town rule of 1750 banned beating drums in town without an officer's permission (Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library, "A 'Banjer' on the Bay of Charleston in 1766," 2021).
- A watercolor painting called *The Old Plantation*, made between about 1785 and 1795, shows enslaved Black men and women dancing. One man plays an early gourd banjo, another plays a drum, and two women hold rattles. It is the earliest known painting of a banjo. In 2010 the librarian Susan Shames identified the painter as John Rose, a South Carolina planter who held people in slavery (Wikipedia, "The Old Plantation". Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library). The Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia, owns it.
- Thomas Jefferson, who held people in slavery at Monticello (`slavery-freedom` bank, from Monticello.org), wrote about Black musicians in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1787 edition). He wrote that "In music they are more generally gifted than the whites," and that "Whether they will be equal to the composition of a more extensive run of melody, or of complicated harmony, is yet to be proved." In the same passage he wrote that Black people had "no poetry" and dismissed the poems of Phillis Wheatley.
- In New England, Black communities held their own election days, when they chose a "king" or "governor," with parades that had "tambourines, banjos, fiddles and drums" (New England Historical Society). `holidays` tells the election days.

### Newport Gardner
> **Who:** Born Occramer Marycoo in West Africa, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, where he became a music teacher and composer. · **When and where:** Born about 1746. Newport, from about 1760. Died in Liberia in 1826.
- Occramer Marycoo was about 14 when slave traders brought him to Newport. Caleb Gardner, a merchant and ship captain, bought him and renamed him Newport Gardner (Sophie Lowe, Early Music America, 2023. John Millar, *Online Review of Rhode Island History*, 2016). Lowe writes that Caleb Gardner "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings" through the slave trade.
- He learned English and music in Newport. Many writers name the composer Andrew Law as his teacher, but Law is first known in Rhode Island in 1783, when Gardner was in his late thirties. Lowe concludes that he "probably had a variety of musical teachers" (Lowe).
- In late December 1791 Gardner and friends won a lottery. He used his share to buy freedom for himself and his family (Millar. Congregational Library, on Edward Andrews's book *Newport Gardner's Anthem*, Cornell University Press).
- He opened a school in Newport where he taught music (Millar). He was a deacon of Newport's First Congregational Church and later helped found a Black church, which Millar dates to 1824.
- A song called "Crooked Shanks," printed in 1803, is often credited to him. Lowe reports newer research finding the same tune in print in London in 1768, so the credit is uncertain. The words of his anthem survive: "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race. Hear the words of promise" (Lowe).
- He led about thirty to three dozen Black New Englanders to Liberia, sailing in late December 1825 or on 4 January 1826 (the sources differ). He died there of disease within months, in 1826 (Millar. Lowe. Congregational Library).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tlingit songs written down, 1791" -->
- In 1791 two Spanish ships of an expedition led by Alejandro Malaspina and José de Bustamante anchored in Yakutat Bay, in what is now Alaska, on Tlingit land. Tlingit people sang "to and with" the Spanish sailors several times (Sam Anderson, ExplorersWeb, 2022).
- Tadeo Haenke, a botanist on the ships who had trained in music, wrote the songs down in musical notes on four sheets of paper. The sheets stayed in the Museo de América in Madrid for more than 200 years. The Tlingit ethnomusicologist Maria Shaa Tláa Williams studied them for the Sealaska Heritage Institute and found that the singers used harmony. The Tlingit scholar Judy Daxootsu Ramos said, "we're still practicing that today" (Anderson).

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Two things happened in these fifty years that shaped everything after. Enslaved people made a body of song — the spirituals, the work songs, the field hollers — that later became the root of most American popular music.
