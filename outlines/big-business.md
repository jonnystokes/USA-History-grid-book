## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were no companies in this land. There were no corporations, no shareholders, no charters, and no business that went on after the people who ran it died. Native nations traded goods across thousands of miles, and that trade is told in `economy` and `marketplace`. Trading is not the same thing as owning a company, and this chapter has nothing to report until the idea of a company crossed the ocean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="big-business" label="The 1500s" state="empty" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
What reached this coast in the 1500s were expeditions paid for by kings and queens, not businesses. There is still no American business history to tell.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Europeans were inventing meanwhile" -->
One honest aside, told as a fact about Europe and nothing more. In this century English merchants worked out the chartered joint-stock company: a group of investors given a written monopoly by their government to trade in a named part of the world.
- The Muscovy Company, chartered 1555, was the first English joint-stock company whose money stayed invested instead of being paid back after each voyage (Britannica).
- The Levant Company followed in 1592 (charter approved 11 September 1592, from the merger of the Turkey and Venice companies).
- Neither traded here. The English East India Company was chartered on 31 December 1600, which by this book's boundary rule places it in the next section.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="big-business" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first lasting English settlements in this land were founded by companies, not by governments. A group of investors bought shares, a king signed a charter giving them a monopoly over a stretch of coast, and the colony was expected to pay the shareholders back. Most of these companies lost money, and two of the biggest lost their charters as well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Virginia Company, a colony run as a business" -->
- Chartered by James I in 1606; Jamestown founded 13 May 1607. The second charter (23 May 1609) moved control from the king's council to the shareholders; the third (12 March 1612) added Bermuda and the right to run public lotteries (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Shares sold at £12 10s. for a seven-year term. The 1609 sale may have raised as much as £10,000. The 1616 dividend was paid in land, not money, because the company had no money (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Lotteries became the main income. By May 1620 lotteries had supplied £7,000 of the company's £9,831 in cash. King James I stopped the lotteries in 1621, soon after members of the House of Commons attacked them in February 1621 (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- The company's business plan cost lives, and this chapter says so. Between 1619 and 1622 the company shipped 3,570 more settlers to Virginia. After the Powhatan attack of 22 March 1622 killed about 347 colonists, 1,240 people were left alive out of 4,270. An investor named Samuel Wrote counted 2,683 people missing, and historians have found that most of them died of disease (Encyclopedia Virginia). The attack and the war are told in `native-nations` and `war`.
- The Privy Council opened an investigation into the company's finances in May 1623. The Crown revoked the charter on 24 May 1624 and took Virginia over as a royal colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-sandys" name="Sir Edwin Sandys" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sir Edwin Sandys
> **Who:** the treasurer who ran the Virginia Company in its last five years. · **When and where:** London, 1619 to 1624.
Elected treasurer on 28 April 1619. He wanted a permanent colony rather than a quick profit, so he pushed the headright system, which gave 50 acres to anyone who paid a person's passage, and he sent settlers faster than Virginia could feed or shelter them. Of the 4,270 people counted in the colony over those years, 1,240 were still alive in 1622. The Crown took the charter away in 1624. Encyclopedia Virginia sums up modern scholarship, including the historian Edmund S. Morgan's 1975 book, as showing that any responsible monarch would have had to stop the shipments.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-sandys" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The other chartered companies" -->
- **Dutch West India Company**, chartered by the States General on 3 June 1621, with a shipping and trading monopoly covering all of America and Africa south of the Tropic of Cancer. It built Fort Orange in 1624 on Mohican land and settled Manhattan, Lenape land, in 1625 to 1626, making New Netherland (Avalon Project, charter of 1621, and New York State Museum). Men the company enslaved dug the New Amsterdam wall's trench in 1653, among them Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo and Anthony Portuguese (see the bank).
- **Massachusetts Bay Company**, chartered 1629. In the Cambridge Agreement of 26 August 1629 the Puritan shareholders agreed to emigrate only if the charter went with them, which turned a trading company's paperwork into a colony's government. The charter was revoked on 23 October 1684.
- **Hudson's Bay Company**, chartered by Charles II on 2 May 1670. It is British and its territory, Rupert's Land, is in what is now Canada. It reached this country later: after merging with the North West Company in 1821 it ran Fort Vancouver, on the Columbia River in what is now Washington state, from the 1820s (National Park Service; Oregon Encyclopedia). Said plainly so no one mistakes it for an American company.
- **Royal African Company**, chartered by Charles II in 1672 and headed by his brother James, Duke of York, with a monopoly on English trade with West Africa, including the trade in enslaved people. From 1672 to 1731 ships the company paid for carried 186,748 enslaved Africans on 652 voyages to English colonies in the Americas, Virginia among them. 16,077 of them died on the way (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database via the bank). Many were branded with the letters DoY or RAC on the chest: a red-hot iron stamp was pressed into the skin, burning the letters in and leaving a permanent scar. The historian William Pettigrew writes that the company shipped more enslaved Africans to the Americas than any other single institution. The Middle Passage itself is told in `slavery-freedom`.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This half-century is thin for this chapter, and the reason is worth explaining. In the colonies a "company" almost never meant a corporation. It meant a partnership: two or three men, usually related or married into each other's families, who put money together, shared the profits, and were each liable for all the debts. The economic historian Robert E. Wright writes that before the Revolution American business corporations were few, small, and of little consequence, because British rules made a charter expensive and gave the holder little in return. The largest business in the colonies was a family merchant house with a handful of ships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a merchant house was" -->
- A merchant house bought and sold on its own account, owned or chartered ships, extended credit to smaller traders, and kept correspondents in London and the West Indies. Its records were letterbooks and account books, and many survive.
- Its business ran on Atlantic circuits: fish, timber, and grain out; sugar, molasses, and manufactured goods in; and enslaved people carried as cargo by some of the same firms. That part is not left out of this chapter.
- British monopolies shaped what colonists could sell. The Royal African Company's monopoly on the English slave trade ended in 1698; the Molasses Act of 1733 and the Iron Act of 1750 are told from the economy's side in `economy`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hancock" name="Thomas Hancock" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hancock
> **Who:** the Boston merchant who built one of the largest businesses in the colonies. · **When and where:** Boston, about 1724 to 1764.
Apprenticed at fourteen to a Boston bookseller, on his own by 1724, and married in 1730 to Lydia Henchman, the daughter of his business partner. He exported codfish, whale oil, logwood, and potash, supplied the Newfoundland fishing fleet with rum and molasses, ran a group of fishing vessels, and made paper. From 1746 to 1758 he and his partner Charles Apthorp held contracts to supply British forces in King George's War and the French and Indian War with guns, lumber, food, medicine, and ships. Apthorp was a slave trader, and that is part of the record. In the autumn of 1755 Governor Charles Lawrence of Nova Scotia and Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts chartered about 24 ships from Apthorp and Hancock, paid by the month and by the head, to carry the Acadians, French-speaking farm families whom British officials were expelling from Nova Scotia. The firm refitted the ships in Boston to hold two people per ton of the ship's size. No passenger lists survive, so no one knows how many Acadians died at sea (Acadian Genealogy, citing the firm's own accounts in the Nova Scotia public documents). Hancock left an estate of about £70,000 when he died in 1764, one of the largest in the colonies, and made his nephew John his partner in 1763. His letterbook and receipt book survive at Harvard's Baker Library and the Massachusetts Historical Society.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists went to war partly over a company's privileges, and then, once independent, began handing out privileges of their own. State legislatures wrote charters one at a time, for banks, bridges, canals, turnpikes, and water works. Robert E. Wright counted 319 business corporations chartered in the United States before 1801, almost all of them after the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tea Act and the East India Company" -->
- Parliament passed the Tea Act on 10 May 1773. The East India Company was in financial trouble and holding about 17 million pounds of unsold tea. The Act let it ship tea straight to America through hand-picked agents, undercutting every colonial merchant, legal or smuggling.
- Boston's seven consignees were chosen from merchants loyal to the Crown. The grievance was not only the tax. A company with a government-granted monopoly was being handed the American market.
- On the night of 16 December 1773 colonists destroyed 340 chests of tea, more than 92,000 pounds, aboard the *Dartmouth*, *Eleanor*, and *Beaver*. The East India Company's February 1774 invoice claimed £9,659 6s. 4d. in damage. Many accounts give 342 chests; the company's own paperwork says 340 (Boston Tea Party Ships and Museum; UK National Archives).
- Shared with: `government-politics` (the protest and the Coercive Acts) · `war` (the road to war) · `marketplace` (the boycott at the counter). This chapter's angle is the company's privileges.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Americans start chartering companies" -->
- The Bank of North America was chartered by the Continental Congress on 31 December 1781 and opened on 7 January 1782; Pennsylvania chartered it again in 1782. It was the first commercial bank in the country (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Richmond Fed).
- Most early charters went to transportation and finance, not manufacturing. Between 1786 and 1798, 59 private toll-bridge companies were chartered in the northeast alone.
- Most charters did not limit shareholders' liability, and the historians Oscar and Mary Handlin counted only 16 early charters that said plainly that shareholders could not be made to pay the company's debts (reported by Wright, who argues that liability was limited in practice anyway).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-morris" name="Robert Morris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Morris
> **Who:** the merchant who financed the Revolution and founded the country's first chartered bank, and who ended in debtors' prison. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1781 to 1801.
As Superintendent of Finance he wrote the charter of the Bank of North America and used the bank to steady the currency of a government that could not tax. After the war he bought land on borrowed money: millions of acres in Pennsylvania, New York, Kentucky, Virginia, Georgia and the Carolinas, and thousands of building lots in the new District of Columbia. In 1797 his agents bought the Seneca Nation's claim to most of western New York at the Treaty of Big Tree for $100,000. He entered the Prune Street debtors' apartment in Philadelphia in February 1798 (15 or 16 February by different accounts) and stayed about three and a half years. Accounts give his release as 26 August 1801 or October 1801, under the new federal Bankruptcy Act of 1800. At his release, bankruptcy commissioners found he owed $2,948,711.11. Some historians argue his imprisonment helped get that Act passed. George Washington visited him there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-morris" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="big-business" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Getting a charter went from a favour a legislature granted to a form a clerk filed. Corporations spread from bridges and banks into cotton mills and factories, the Supreme Court ruled that a charter was a contract a state could not tear up, and one of the largest companies in the country, a bank, was destroyed by a president who did not think a private company should hold that much power.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From special charter to general law" -->
- New York's Act Relative to Incorporations for Manufacturing Purposes, 22 March 1811, was the first American law that let people incorporate by following a procedure instead of lobbying for a private act. It covered manufacturing only, capped capital at $100,000, and limited the term of the charter.
- Connecticut's 1837 law is generally counted as the first that let people incorporate for any lawful business.
- The scale of what followed: Richard Sylla and Robert E. Wright count 22,419 business corporations chartered by special acts of state legislatures from 1790 to 1860, with at least $4.5 billion in authorized capital, and several thousand more under general laws.
- Some Southern states barred people who were not white from forming corporations under their general laws (the economic historian Eric Hilt).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 1819" -->
- New Hampshire tried in 1816 to turn privately chartered Dartmouth College into a state university by rewriting its charter. Chief Justice John Marshall's Court held that the charter was a contract, protected by the Contract Clause, and that a legislature could not change it at will.
- What it meant for business: a charter granted in one year could not be taken back in the next. States responded by writing clauses into new charters reserving the right to amend or repeal them. Shared with: `government-politics` (**lead** — the case as law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston Associates and the mills" -->
- Francis Cabot Lowell and a group of Boston merchants formed the Boston Manufacturing Company in 1813 and raised $400,000 by selling shares, then built at Waltham the first American mill that took raw cotton in one end and finished cloth out the other.
- Within six years the Waltham mill was working 5,376 spindles and 175 power looms and using 450,000 pounds of cotton a year. The same investors built at East Chelmsford, which was incorporated as the town of Lowell in 1826; the new town had 2,500 people in 1826 and 6,477 in 1830. The mills stood at Pawtucket Falls, a fishing place of the Pennacook people, and they spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — the mill girls and the working day) · `technology` (the power loom) · `city-building` (the mill town).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-biddle" name="Nicholas Biddle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Biddle
> **Who:** the president of the Second Bank of the United States, one of the largest companies in the country, during the fight that destroyed it. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1822 to 1836.
The Second Bank was chartered on 10 April 1816 with $35 million in capital, a fifth of it the federal government's. That was three and a half times the $10 million capital of the First Bank. Biddle became president in 1822 or 1823 (sources differ) and by the end of the decade ran 29 branches doing about $70 million of business a year (another count gives 25 branches). In 1832 he asked Congress to recharter the Bank four years early. Andrew Jackson vetoed the bill, won re-election, and pulled the government's deposits out in 1833. Biddle answered by tightening credit, which he said was necessary and his opponents said was proof of his power. The federal charter died in 1836. Shared with: `money` (**lead**: what a national bank is) · `government-politics` (the veto and the election).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This is the chapter's central era. Railroads became the first companies larger than the governments that chartered them. Oil, steel, sugar, and tobacco were reorganized so that a handful of men set prices for a whole industry. Congress wrote the first federal rules for business in 1887 and 1890, and courts spent the next fifteen years deciding what those rules meant, mostly in the companies' favour. When workers struck, companies answered with private guards, court injunctions, and troops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The railroads, the first giant corporations" -->
- In 1880 the Pennsylvania Railroad employed 30,000 people and held $400 million in capital, which made it the largest corporation in the country. The whole federal government's ordinary receipts for fiscal 1880 were $333,526,610.98 (ExplorePAHistory; Treasury Annual Report 1880 via FRASER). By the early 1890s its workforce passed 110,000.
- Land grants: railroad companies received about 131 million acres from the federal government, and states granted about 49 to 51 million more. Sources vary within that range, and the totals depend on which grants are counted and which lapsed.
- Shared with: `transportation` (**lead** — the lines and how they ran) · `migration` (settlers who bought the land) · `native-nations` (whose land it was) · `work-workers` (building it).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Men Who Built America (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller
> **Who:** the bookkeeper who built Standard Oil and invented the trust. · **When and where:** Cleveland and New York, 1870 to 1911.
> **Movie:** The Men Who Built America (2012), an eight-part History Channel series that uses dramatized reenactments with actors alongside historians. Treat the acted scenes as illustration, not evidence.
He incorporated the Standard Oil Company of Ohio on 10 January 1870 with $1,000,000 in capital and two Cleveland refineries holding about a tenth of the country's refining capacity. In 1872 he joined the South Improvement Company, a secret arrangement in which the railroads raised their rates and then paid Standard Oil a rebate of $1.06 a barrel on its own oil plus a drawback of $1.06 on every barrel its competitors shipped. Rival refiners would pay the higher rate and pay Standard as well. The scheme was cancelled after a public revolt, but the threat did its work: in six weeks to three months in early 1872, Rockefeller bought out 21 or 22 of the 26 refiners in Cleveland (counts differ by source). By 1880 he refined 90 to 95 percent of American oil.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a trust actually was" -->
- Company law was state law, and a company chartered in Ohio could not simply own companies in other states. Standard Oil's lawyer Samuel C. T. Dodd designed a way around it.
- On 2 January 1882, 41 shareholders signed the Standard Oil Trust Agreement, handing the shares of some 40 companies to nine trustees who then ran all of them as one business. The trust was valued at about $70 million. The trustees included John and William Rockefeller, Henry Flagler, Charles Pratt, and John Archbold.
- Other industries copied the device, which is why "trust" came to mean any very large combination and why the laws against them are called antitrust laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="franklin-tarbell" name="Franklin Tarbell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Franklin Tarbell
> **Who:** an independent oil man in the Pennsylvania fields, and the father of the reporter who later investigated Standard Oil. · **When and where:** Oil Creek and Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859 to 1905.
Franklin Tarbell was on his way home to Iowa in 1859 when he heard about the first oil well at Titusville. He built a wooden tank that held 500 barrels or more, got his first order, and built oil tanks for ten years. In 1867 he began producing oil himself, and in 1870 he moved his family into a new house in Titusville. In 1872 he and scores of other producers signed a pledge not to sell oil to Standard Oil, the only company that had gained from the South Improvement scheme. The fight with Standard went on for twenty years. About 1892 one of his business partners, ruined in it, shot himself and left Franklin owing money on the man's notes. To pay them he mortgaged the family home in the panic of 1893. His daughter Ida, who was fifteen in 1872, recorded all of this in her 1939 memoir, which does not name the partner. She spent most of five years investigating Standard Oil. He died in Titusville on 1 March 1905.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="franklin-tarbell" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" name="Andrew Carnegie" movie="The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Carnegie
> **Who:** the steelmaker who owned every step of making steel, and whose company broke its own workers' union. · **When and where:** Pittsburgh, 1870s to 1901.
> **Movie:** The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)
His method was to own the whole chain rather than buy from anyone. In the 1890s Carnegie Steel took a controlling share of Henry Clay Frick's coke business, leased ore in Minnesota's Mesabi Range, on land the Ojibwe had ceded in treaties of 1854 and 1855, bought lake steamers to carry the ore, and bought and extended a railroad to carry ore from Lake Erie to its mills. Owning each step let him cut prices below what competitors who had to buy their inputs could match. In 1892 his general manager Frick shut the Homestead works to break the union. The story of that summer is below and in `work-workers`. Carnegie's sale of the company in 1901 falls in the next section.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892, from the company's side" -->
- Henry Clay Frick, general manager and second-largest shareholder of Carnegie Steel, offered the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers a contract with large pay cuts, built a fence topped with barbed wire around the Homestead works, and locked the workers out on 30 June 1892.
- He hired the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. About 300 to 316 armed Pinkerton men were brought up the Monongahela River on two barges before dawn on 6 July 1892, at five dollars a day each. A gun battle followed.
- Death counts differ: Britannica gives seven workers and three Pinkertons; an American Heritage account gives about nine strikers and seven Pinkertons, with roughly 40 strikers and 20 Pinkertons shot, and 35 deaths from the whole episode. The range is stated, not resolved.
- Carnegie Steel then asked Pennsylvania's Governor Robert Pattison for help. About 8,500 National Guard soldiers took over the plant on 12 July. The union collapsed in November. Congress investigated the use of private armies; Frick testified.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — the strikers, the town, and what the defeat cost working people) · `crime-justice` (the Pinkerton agency itself).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-w-holway" name="John W. Holway" movie="The River Ran Red (1993)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John W. Holway
> **Who:** a twenty-three-year-old medical student hired as a Pinkerton guard for the Homestead job. · **When and where:** Chicago to Homestead, Pennsylvania, July 1892.
> **Movie:** The River Ran Red (1993), a documentary about the Homestead lockout told from the workers' side. Holway is not its subject. It is named here because it covers the same days.
He was one of the college students the Pinkertons signed up for summer work at five dollars a day. He was put on a train at the Lake Shore depot in Chicago and not allowed off at the stops, and he said afterward that he had a feeling there would be gunplay. He does not appear to have fired a shot all day. When the Pinkertons surrendered and were marched through the town, three strikers knocked him down and accused him of killing men. He was hit in the head by a stone and ran about half a mile before his legs gave out. Then 20 or 30 men kicked him and beat him with stones until two or three strikers with rifles pushed the crowd back. He described it under oath to the congressional committee. Henry Clay Frick had contracted with William and Robert Pinkerton for the guards, and the agency's Chicago office hired Holway without telling him where he was going.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-w-holway" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal rules, and how little they did at first" -->
- **Interstate Commerce Act, 4 February 1887.** It created the Interstate Commerce Commission and made the railroads the first industry under federal regulation. The ICC was the first federal regulatory commission.
- **Sherman Antitrust Act, 2 July 1890.** Two short sections outlawing combinations in restraint of trade and monopolizing.
- **United States v. E. C. Knight Co., 1895.** The Court held the American Sugar Refining Company had not violated the Act even though it controlled about 98 percent of American sugar refining, on the reasoning that manufacturing was not commerce.
- The honest part: in its first decade the Sherman Act was used against labour unions more often than against large companies, and courts treated unions as illegal combinations. The Pullman boycott of 1894 was broken with it. Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — Pullman and Debs) · `government-politics` (the statutes as law).

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1895 and 1904 bankers and owners merged more than 1,800 manufacturing firms into 157 large corporations. The largest, U.S. Steel, was the first company in the world with more than a billion dollars in stock. The reporter Ida Tarbell spent about four years reading court records and government reports on Standard Oil. Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft had their lawyers sue the biggest combinations, and in 1911 the Supreme Court justices ordered Standard Oil and American Tobacco broken into pieces. Members of Congress voted for two new antitrust laws in 1914. After the 1929 crash they voted for a law that made companies selling stock report their finances to the public. In the Second World War federal officials gave about two-thirds of the value of their prime contracts to 100 companies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great merger movement and U.S. Steel" -->
- Between 1895 and 1904 more than 1,800 manufacturing firms were merged into 157 consolidated corporations (Naomi Lamoreaux, *The Great Merger Movement in American Business, 1895–1904*). Lamoreaux argues these combinations were not inevitable and that many quickly lost ground to new rivals.
- United States Steel was incorporated in New Jersey in February 1901 (25 February by the newspaper notice Harvard's Baker Library shows, 26 February by EBSCO). Its declared capital was about $1.4 billion ($1.403 billion by EBSCO, $1.466 billion by the historian Kenneth Warren), the first billion-dollar company in the world. Charles M. Schwab was its first president and Elbert Gary its chairman.
- The federal Bureau of Corporations later valued what the company actually owned at about $682 million, about half the stock issued. Shares issued for more than the property behind them are called watered stock.
- In 1901 and 1902 it made about two-thirds of all American steel (65.7 percent in 1901 by Warren's count, 67 percent in 1902 by others) and in 1902 employed 168,000 people. By 1911 its share had fallen to between 40 and 50 percent, depending on the source.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan
> **Who:** the banker who put companies together and made U.S. Steel. · **When and where:** New York, 1901 to 1904.
Morgan was a banker. He raised the money, bought the companies and set the terms. In 1901 he agreed to Andrew Carnegie's price of $480 million for Carnegie Steel and joined it with Federal Steel, National Steel and seven other firms to make U.S. Steel. In 1901 he and James J. Hill also formed the Northern Securities Company. It was a holding company, a company that owns controlling shares in other companies, over the Great Northern, the Northern Pacific and the Burlington railroads. Theodore Roosevelt's lawyers sued it in 1902. His rescue of the banking system in the panic of 1907 is told in `money`.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" name="Ida Tarbell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ida Tarbell
> **Who:** the reporter who took Standard Oil apart in print. · **When and where:** New York and the Pennsylvania oil fields, 1900 to 1904.
She grew up in Titusville, where her father Franklin was an independent oil man squeezed by the 1872 rebate scheme. She began in 1900 and spent about four years on the work. She read court records, sworn testimony and government reports, and she interviewed Standard Oil's Henry H. Rogers. *The History of the Standard Oil Company* ran in *McClure's* in 19 installments, from November 1902 to October 1904, and became a two-volume book in 1904. One installment said Rockefeller had paid a Cleveland widow, Mrs. Backus, next to nothing for her late husband's lubricating plant in 1878. Rockefeller's biographer Ron Chernow says Rockefeller paid $79,000, including $10,000 above the appraisers' value, for an out-of-date plant, and that she had asked up to $200,000. Historians still disagree about that installment. Rockefeller called Tarbell "that poisonous woman." No documentary about Tarbell alone was found. She appears in *The Rockefellers* (American Experience, 2000). Shared with: `news-communication` (**lead**: muckraking and how the magazine worked).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt
> **Who:** the president called the trust-buster, and what his record actually was. · **When and where:** Washington, 1901 to 1909.
> **Movie:** TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)
In 1902 he ordered the Justice Department to sue the Northern Securities Company under the Sherman Act. The Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 for the government on 14 March 1904 and ordered the company dissolved, and that one case made his reputation. His administration brought 44 antitrust suits in seven and a half years. William Howard Taft's brought more in four years: 75 by one count and 99 by another. Roosevelt divided trusts into what he called good and bad ones. When Taft's lawyers sued U.S. Steel in 1911, Roosevelt said Taft could not tell the difference. Shared with: `government-politics` (the presidency).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standard Oil is broken up, 1911" -->
- On 15 May 1911 the Supreme Court held that Standard Oil of New Jersey had restrained trade and monopolized commerce in petroleum, and ordered it dissolved within six months. Chief Justice Edward D. White wrote that the Sherman Act banned only unreasonable limits on trade, a test called the rule of reason. Standard Oil was split into 34 companies (Library of Congress).
- Rockefeller was worth about $300 million in May 1911 and more than $900 million by the end of 1913. Roosevelt, according to Rockefeller's biographer Ron Chernow, said Wall Street's prayer was now "give us another dissolution." Newspapers called Rockefeller the world's first billionaire in 1916.
- On 29 May 1911 the justices also ruled against James B. Duke's American Tobacco Company. In November 1911 a federal court in New York divided it among a new American Tobacco, Liggett and Myers, P. Lorillard, R. J. Reynolds and others.
- Successors of Standard Oil later became Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco, and others.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New laws, and who owned the country" -->
- **Federal Trade Commission Act**, signed by Woodrow Wilson on 26 September 1914, and **Clayton Antitrust Act**, signed 15 October 1914. The FTC Act set up a commission to police unfair methods of competition alongside the Justice Department. It opened on 16 March 1915. The Clayton Act named practices the Sherman Act had left vague: price discrimination, mergers that cut competition, and the same men sitting on the boards of competing companies. It also stated that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity," which protected unions from the Sherman Act.
- **Berle and Means, 1932.** In *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means showed that by 1930 the 200 largest corporations held 49.2 percent of American corporate wealth, and that inside those companies the managers, not the shareholders who legally owned them, made the decisions.
- **Securities Exchange Act, 6 June 1934**, creating the Securities and Exchange Commission and requiring public companies to report their finances honestly. Franklin Roosevelt made Joseph P. Kennedy, a Wall Street speculator who had used the practices the new law banned, the first chairman at the start of July 1934 (1 July by one account, June by another). Kennedy had given, lent and raised money for Roosevelt's 1932 campaign. Newspaper writers compared the choice to putting the fox in the henhouse. Several accounts say Roosevelt answered in private that it took a thief to catch a thief. The wording differs from account to account.
- **The La Follette committee, 1936 to 1940.** Senator Robert M. La Follette Jr. of Wisconsin led Senate hearings on how employers fought unions. Witnesses told how companies had planted spies in unions, stockpiled submachine guns, rifles and tear gas, and hired their own police forces. Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead**: the Memorial Day Massacre of 1937).
- **The territories.** Five firms known as the Big Five (C. Brewer, Theo H. Davies, H. Hackfeld, later Amfac, Castle & Cooke, and Alexander & Baldwin) controlled about 94 percent of Hawaii's sugar production by 1920 and ran much of its shipping, banking, and politics. Some sons of Big Five partners were in the Committee of Safety that overthrew the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893. The plantations stood on Native Hawaiian land. Shared with: `america-world` (**lead**: Hawaii's annexation and the territories).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War contracts, 1940 to 1944" -->
- Federal officials placed $175.066 billion in prime defence contracts with American corporations between 1940 and 1944 (a 2011 *Business History* study). The top 100 contractors received about two-thirds of the value and the top 10 about 30 percent. General Motors alone received nearly 8 percent (the economist Robert Higgs).
- By 30 June 1944, 26 firms had the use of half the value of all government-financed industrial plant leased to private contractors.
- Shared with: `war` (**lead**: the fighting and mobilization) · `economy` (what war production did to the national income) · `work-workers` (the workforce).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
General Motors employed 618,365 Americans at its peak in 1979, and the Bell telephone system employed about a million in 1983. Company heads also joined unlike businesses into conglomerates: ITT owned hotels, car rentals, insurance and a bakery at the same time. After 1971 business leaders organized in Washington, and the number of firms with registered lobbyists there grew from 175 in 1971 to 2,445 in 1982. Between 1978 and 1980 members of Congress voted to remove federal rules from airlines, banks, trucking and railroads. In 1954 cigarette makers paid for newspaper advertisements that disputed the studies linking smoking to lung cancer, and in 1994 seven tobacco company heads told Congress under oath that nicotine is not addictive. Justice Department lawyers and AT&T agreed to split up the Bell System, which took effect in 1984, and the department won a monopoly ruling against Microsoft in 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="General Motors, and a sentence nobody quotes correctly" -->
- GM sold about half the new cars in the country in 1954: 54 percent by the Associated Press's count, 50.7 percent by the historian Sally Clarke's. Its United States employment peaked at 618,365 in 1979.
- In 1955 it became the first company to earn more than a billion dollars in profit in one year. It had made $912,887,537 in the first three quarters alone (TIME, November 1955).
- A secondary figure, not yet confirmed in a source: by 1953 about one American worker in 200 worked for GM, and its revenues equalled about 3 percent of the country's output.
- At his 1953 hearing to become Secretary of Defense, GM's president Charles E. Wilson owned 40,000 GM shares, and GM was the country's largest defence contractor. Senator Robert C. Hendrickson asked whether he could make a decision against GM's interest. Wilson said he could, and added that "for years, I thought what was good for our country was good for General Motors, and vice versa." It is usually quoted as "what's good for General Motors is good for the country."
- In 1965 Ralph Nader's book *Unsafe at Any Speed* criticized the GM Corvair. GM managers hired private detectives to look into Nader's private life. In 1966 GM president James M. Roche apologized to a Senate committee, and Nader later settled his privacy suit against GM for $425,000. Congress passed the first federal rules for car design in 1966.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eisenhower's warning, 1961" -->
- On 17 January 1961, in his farewell speech, President Dwight D. Eisenhower warned against the "unwarranted influence" of the "military-industrial complex." He meant the armed forces and the companies that made their weapons, the same companies that had received most of the war contracts of 1940 to 1944. Shared with: `war` (**lead**: the armed forces).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The conglomerate" -->
- Harold Geneen ran International Telephone and Telegraph (ITT) from 1959 to 1977 and bought between 250 and 350 companies, depending on the count. They included Sheraton hotels, Avis car rental, Hartford insurance, and Continental Baking, the maker of Wonder Bread.
- ITT's sales went from about $700 million to about $17 billion, and it became the eleventh largest corporation in the United States. Geneen believed that good managers could make a success of any business.
- In 1966 federal antitrust officials stopped ITT from buying the ABC television network for $700 million. In the 1970s ITT admitted that it had given the CIA $350,000 for "political" purposes in Chile, where the government of President Salvador Allende had seized ITT's telephone company.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Business organizes in Washington" -->
- On 23 August 1971 the lawyer Lewis F. Powell Jr. sent a confidential memo titled "Attack On American Free Enterprise System" to Eugene B. Sydnor Jr. of the U.S. Chamber of Commerce. It urged business to organize to defend itself in politics, the courts, the press and the universities. Less than two months later President Nixon nominated Powell to the Supreme Court. The columnist Jack Anderson made the memo public in September 1972.
- The Business Roundtable was formed on 13 October 1972 when three groups of company heads merged. Its founders included John D. Harper of Alcoa and Roger Blough, the retired head of U.S. Steel, and they set out to resist the growing power of unions.
- The counts: corporations with public affairs offices in Washington went from 100 in 1968 to more than 500 in 1978. Firms with registered lobbyists went from 175 in 1971 to 2,445 in 1982 (political scientists' counts). The Federal Election Commission counted 89 company political action committees at the end of 1974, 294 in May 1976, and 1,206 at the end of 1980. A political action committee collects money and gives it to candidates.
- Members of Congress then voted rules away, and President Jimmy Carter signed each act: airlines on 24 October 1978, banks on 31 March 1980, trucking on 1 July 1980 and railroads on 14 October 1980. Shared with: `government-politics` (**lead**: campaign finance law and lobbying rules).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Products that hurt people, and what the companies said" -->
- On 4 January 1954 the major tobacco companies ran "A Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers" in more than 400 newspapers. The public relations firm Hill and Knowlton designed it to dispute the new studies linking smoking to lung cancer.
- In a memo of 17 July 1963, Addison Yeaman, the chief lawyer of Brown and Williamson, wrote that to protect its earnings the industry must either disprove that smoking causes cancer or find the carcinogens, the cancer-causing substances in the smoke, and remove them.
- On 14 April 1994 Representative Ron Wyden asked seven tobacco company heads at a House hearing whether nicotine is addictive. William Campbell of Philip Morris answered, "I believe nicotine is not addictive, yes." The others gave the same answer in different words.
- On 23 November 1998 the four largest cigarette makers agreed with the attorneys general of 46 states to pay at least $206 billion over 25 years toward the states' costs of treating smokers, and to limit their advertising.
- Johns-Manville, the largest asbestos maker, filed for bankruptcy in August 1982 while facing thousands of lawsuits from people whose lungs asbestos had damaged. Asbestos is a mineral fibre used to fireproof buildings. Its dust scars the lungs and causes a cancer called mesothelioma. By the end of 1988 a trust set up in the bankruptcy had settled more than 12,600 claims for almost $500 million.
- On 3 December 1984 more than 40 tons of a poison gas called methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India, owned by a company controlled by the American firm Union Carbide. It killed at least 2,000 people within hours and at least 3,800 in all by one medical review. Union Carbide paid $470 million in 1989. Shared with: `health` (**lead**: the illnesses) · `america-world` (Bhopal).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bell System is split, 1984" -->
- In the early 1980s the Bell System held about $150 billion in assets and employed about a million people.
- Justice Department lawyers sued AT&T in 1974. In January 1982 the company and the department agreed that AT&T would give up its local telephone companies. Judge Harold Greene approved the agreement with changes, and the case was dismissed on 24 August 1982. On 1 January 1984 the local companies became seven regional companies, called the Baby Bells. AT&T kept long distance, Bell Labs and Western Electric, and $34 billion of its $149.5 billion in assets.
- Shared with: `technology` (**lead**: the telephone network) · `news-communication` (what it did to phone service).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bill-gates" name="Bill Gates" movie="Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bill Gates
> **Who:** the head of Microsoft when the United States sued it for monopolizing personal computer operating systems. · **When and where:** Redmond, Washington, and Washington, D.C., 1998 to 2001.
> **Movie:** Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019). A three-part Netflix series directed by Davis Guggenheim, mostly about his later charitable work. The antitrust case is not its subject.
The Justice Department, twenty states and the District of Columbia sued Microsoft on 18 May 1998. They argued that Gates's company had used its control of Windows to shut out rival web browsers. On 5 November 1999 Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found that Microsoft held a monopoly, and on 7 June 2000 he ordered it split into two companies. On 28 June 2001 seven appeals court judges upheld the finding that Microsoft had illegally kept its monopoly. They threw out the breakup order because Judge Jackson had talked about the case to reporters while it was still going on. Government lawyers then dropped the demand for a breakup, and in November 2001 they settled with Microsoft on rules for how it could do business. Microsoft lost on the law and stayed in one piece. Shared with: `technology` (**lead**: the software).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="steve-young-vlasic" name="Steve Young" movie="Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Steve Young
> **Who:** a Vlasic pickle executive who described what it was like to supply the largest retailer in the world. · **When and where:** the late 1990s to January 2001. His account was published in December 2003.
> **Movie:** Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004). A PBS investigation into Walmart's power over suppliers. Young is not in it. It covers the same subject.
Young was vice president of grocery marketing for pickles at Vlasic. In the late 1990s a Walmart buyer wanted Vlasic's gallon jar of pickles at $2.97, and it went into about 3,000 stores. Each store sold about 80 jars a week, roughly 240,000 gallons a week, and Vlasic and Walmart made a penny or two a jar. Shoppers who used to buy Vlasic's spears and chips in supermarkets bought the cheap gallon instead. Walmart was 30 percent of Vlasic's business, and Vlasic's pickle profits fell by 25 percent or more. Young says Vlasic asked to raise the price and the Walmart buyers said that if it did, they would stop buying Vlasic's other products. His colleague Pat Hunn remembers being told that another company would sell the gallon if Vlasic would not. Walmart later let Vlasic shrink the jar to just over half a gallon at $2.79. Vlasic filed for bankruptcy in January 2001, and everyone involved agrees the pickles were not the main cause. The account is journalism: Charles Fishman, "The Wal-Mart You Don't Know," *Fast Company*, December 2003. Shared with: `marketplace` (**lead**: the stores and the shopper) · `work-workers` (wages and conditions).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In September 2026 the three most valuable American companies were Nvidia, Apple and Alphabet, each worth more than $4 trillion. Walmart employed about 1.6 million people in the United States, about two and a half times General Motors' peak in 1979. Enron's chief executives were convicted of fraud in 2006, and Lehman Brothers failed in 2008 with $619 billion in debts. Members of Congress voted $700 billion in October 2008 to rescue banks, and federal officials later lent part of it to car makers. From 2020 on, lawyers at the Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission sued Google, Meta, Amazon and Apple. By September 2026 judges had ruled twice that Google broke the law, one judge had ruled for Meta, and the Amazon and Apple cases had not come to trial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big, in numbers" -->
- Nvidia became the first company worth five trillion dollars on 29 October 2025. On 7 September 2026 the three largest companies by market value were Nvidia at about $5.6 trillion, Apple at about $4.7 trillion and Alphabet at about $4.1 trillion (The Motley Fool). Market values change every trading day, so these figures carry their date.
- Walmart reported revenue of $713.2 billion for the year that ended on 31 January 2026. It employed about 2.1 million people, about 1.6 million of them in the United States, and about 92 percent of its U.S. workers were paid by the hour. Amazon employed about 1,576,000 people at the end of 2025, plus contractors and temporary workers (company filings with the SEC).
- The comparison across the grid: General Motors' U.S. workforce peaked at 618,365 in 1979. Walmart's U.S. workforce in 2026 was about two and a half times as large.
- Shared with: `technology` (**lead**: the products) · `marketplace` (**lead**: Amazon and Walmart as places to shop) · `economy` (what these firms are worth to the whole economy).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fraud at the top: Enron and the tobacco case" -->
- Enron, a Houston energy company, filed for bankruptcy on 2 December 2001. About 20,000 employees lost their jobs, and many lost their savings. On 25 May 2006 a federal jury in Houston convicted its former chief executives, Kenneth L. Lay and Jeffrey K. Skilling, of conspiracy and fraud.
- On 17 August 2006 Judge Gladys Kessler ruled that the major tobacco companies had broken the federal racketeering law. That law punishes people who run a business through a pattern of fraud. She wrote that the companies had sold "their lethal products with zeal, with deception," and ordered them to publish statements about the harm of smoking and the addictiveness of nicotine.
- In 2007 Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to misleading the public about the risk of addiction to its pain pill OxyContin and paid $600 million. Its president, its top lawyer and its former chief medical officer pleaded guilty as individuals. In November 2020 the company pleaded guilty to three federal felonies. In June 2025 every state joined a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sackler family, its owners. No member of the family was charged with a crime. Shared with: `drugs-alcohol` (**lead**: the overdoses) · `health`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 2008 rescue" -->
- Lehman Brothers, run by chief executive Richard S. Fuld Jr., filed the largest bankruptcy in United States history on 15 September 2008, with about $619 billion in debts against $639 billion in assets. A court-appointed examiner later reported that Lehman had used transactions called Repo 105 to make its books look stronger. No Lehman executive was charged with a crime.
- Members of Congress authorized $700 billion under the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act on 3 October 2008. Federal officials paid out $443.5 billion, and most was repaid. The Government Accountability Office puts the final cost at $31.1 billion. The bank programs made money, while the housing programs, the rescue of the insurer AIG and the car-maker loans cost money.
- Shared with: `economy` (**lead**: the recession) · `money` (the banking system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Antitrust returns" -->
- **Google, search.** On 5 August 2024 Judge Amit Mehta ruled that Google had illegally kept a monopoly in general search and search text advertising, mainly through deals that paid companies such as Apple to make Google the default search engine. In September 2025 he refused to make Google sell its Chrome browser. In December 2025 he ordered Google to share parts of its search index and user data with competitors, under a five-member technical committee. Both sides appealed, and the appeal was still being argued on paper in September 2026.
- **Google, advertising technology.** In April 2025 Judge Leonie Brinkema ruled that Google had illegally monopolized the tools that websites use to sell advertising space. In September 2026 she refused to make Google sell its ad exchange and ordered new rules for how the tools work, watched by a court-appointed monitor.
- **Meta.** Lawyers at the FTC sued in 2020 over Meta's purchases of Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. After a trial that began in April 2025, Judge James Boasberg ruled for Meta on 18 November 2025. He found that Meta has no monopoly today, because TikTok and YouTube compete with it. The FTC appealed on 20 January 2026.
- **Amazon.** The FTC and 17 state attorneys general sued on 26 September 2023. The trial, before Judge John Chun without a jury, was set for 29 March 2027. In a separate case, Amazon agreed in September 2025 to pay $2.5 billion over the way it signed customers up for Prime and made it hard to cancel.
- **Apple.** The Justice Department and 16 state attorneys general sued Apple on 21 March 2024, arguing it had illegally kept a monopoly in smartphones. Judge Julien Neals refused to dismiss the case in June 2025.
- The government won the Google cases, lost the Meta case at trial, and the Amazon and Apple cases had not been tried by September 2026.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lina-khan" name="Lina Khan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lina Khan
> **Who:** the law student whose article changed the antitrust argument, and who later ran the agency that brought the cases. · **When and where:** New Haven and Washington, 2017 to 2025.
As a student she published "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" in the *Yale Law Journal* in 2017. She argued that American antitrust law had come to ask only whether prices went up. That test, she wrote, missed a company like Amazon that sells cheaply while also owning the marketplace, the delivery network, the cloud servers, and products that compete on its own shelves. On 15 June 2021 the Senate confirmed her to the Federal Trade Commission, 69 to 28, and she was sworn in as its chair the same day. At 32 she was the youngest chair in the agency's history. Under her, FTC lawyers brought the Amazon case and carried on the Meta case, which the FTC lost at trial in November 2025. She left the agency in January 2025. In 2023 the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, a research group partly funded by technology companies, published a report arguing that her 2017 analysis was wrong about how two-sided online markets work.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lina-khan" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corporate money in politics" -->
- On 21 January 2010, in *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, the Supreme Court justices ruled that the government may not limit independent political spending by corporations and unions. The ruling belongs to `government-politics`. The spending belongs here.
- Super PACs are committees that may raise and spend unlimited money as long as they do not work directly with a candidate. They spent about $6.4 billion on federal elections from 2010 to 2022 and at least $2.7 billion in 2024. Spending by groups that do not have to name their donors passed $1 billion in 2024 (Brennan Center for Justice).
- Organizations spent $5.08 billion on federal lobbying in 2025, the first year above $5 billion, and 15,768 organizations reported lobbying (OpenSecrets). The U.S. Chamber of Commerce spent the most, $72.1 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Working for a company without being employed by it" -->
- Some companies now pay people for work without hiring them as employees. Ride and delivery companies call their drivers independent contractors, who get no minimum wage or overtime under federal law.
- How many Americans do this work depends on the question asked. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 1.6 million people, 1.0 percent of workers, who found short jobs through an app or website in May 2017. The Pew Research Center found in 2021 that 16 percent of American adults had ever earned money through an online gig platform, and 9 percent in the past year.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead**: what the work is like and the fight over employee status) · `transportation` (ride-hailing).
