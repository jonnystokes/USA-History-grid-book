## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
There were no companies in this land. There were no corporations, no shareholders, no charters, and no business that went on after the people who ran it died. Native nations traded goods across thousands of miles, and that trade is told in `economy` and `marketplace`. Trading is not the same thing as owning a company, and this chapter has nothing to report until the idea of a company crossed the ocean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="big-business" label="The 1500s" state="empty" progress="researched" -->
## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
What reached this coast in the 1500s were expeditions paid for by kings and queens, not businesses. There is still no American business history to tell.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Europeans were inventing meanwhile" -->
One honest aside, told as a fact about Europe and nothing more. In this century English merchants worked out the chartered joint-stock company: a group of investors given a written monopoly by their government to trade in a named part of the world.
- The Muscovy Company, chartered 1555, was the first English joint-stock company whose money stayed invested instead of being paid back after each voyage (Britannica).
- The Levant Company followed in 1592 (charter approved 11 September 1592, from the merger of the Turkey and Venice companies).
- Neither traded here. The English East India Company was chartered on 31 December 1600, which by this book's boundary rule places it in the next section.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="big-business" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first lasting English settlements in this land were founded by companies, not by governments. A group of investors bought shares, a king signed a charter giving them a monopoly over a stretch of coast, and the colony was expected to pay the shareholders back. Most of these companies lost money, and two of the biggest lost their charters as well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Virginia Company, a colony run as a business" -->
- Chartered by James I in 1606; Jamestown founded 13 May 1607. The second charter (23 May 1609) moved control from the king's council to the shareholders; the third (12 March 1612) added Bermuda and the right to run public lotteries (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Shares sold at £12 10s. for a seven-year term. The 1609 sale may have raised as much as £10,000. The 1616 dividend was paid in land, not money, because the company had no money (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Lotteries became the main income. By May 1620 lotteries had supplied £7,000 of the company's £9,831 in cash. Parliament banned them after February 1621 (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- The company's business plan cost lives, and this chapter says so. Between 1619 and 1622 the company shipped 3,570 more settlers to Virginia. After the Powhatan attack of 22 March 1622 killed about 347 colonists, 1,240 people were left alive out of 4,270. One investor's count blamed disease for 2,683 of the other deaths (Encyclopedia Virginia). The attack and the war are told in `native-nations` and `war`.
- The Privy Council opened an investigation into the company's finances in May 1623. The Crown revoked the charter on 24 May 1624 and took Virginia over as a royal colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-sandys" name="Sir Edwin Sandys" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sir Edwin Sandys
> **Who:** the treasurer who ran the Virginia Company in its last five years. · **When and where:** London, 1619 to 1624.
Elected treasurer on 28 April 1619. He wanted a permanent colony rather than a quick profit, so he pushed the headright system, which gave 50 acres to anyone who paid a person's passage, and he sent settlers faster than Virginia could feed or shelter them. Of the 4,270 people counted in the colony over those years, 1,240 were still alive in 1622. The Crown took the charter away in 1624. The historian Edmund S. Morgan wrote in 1975 that any responsible monarch would have had to stop the shipments (Encyclopedia Virginia).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-sandys" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The other chartered companies" -->
- **Dutch West India Company**, chartered by the States General on 3 June 1621, with a shipping and trading monopoly covering all of America and Africa south of the Tropic of Cancer. It built Fort Orange in 1624 and settled Manhattan in 1625, making New Netherland (Library of Congress, charter of 1621; Britannica).
- **Massachusetts Bay Company**, chartered 1629. In the Cambridge Agreement of 26 August 1629 the Puritan shareholders agreed to emigrate only if the charter went with them, which turned a trading company's paperwork into a colony's government. The charter was revoked on 23 October 1684.
- **Hudson's Bay Company**, chartered by Charles II on 2 May 1670. It is British and its territory, Rupert's Land, is in what is now Canada. It reached this country later: after merging with the North West Company in 1821 it ran Fort Vancouver, on the Columbia River in what is now Washington state, from the 1820s (National Park Service; Oregon Encyclopedia). Said plainly so no one mistakes it for an American company.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This half-century is thin for this chapter, and the reason is worth explaining. In the colonies a "company" almost never meant a corporation. It meant a partnership: two or three men, usually related or married into each other's families, who put money together, shared the profits, and were each liable for all the debts. The economic historian Robert E. Wright writes that before the Revolution American business corporations were few, small, and of little consequence, because British rules made a charter expensive and gave the holder little in return. The largest business in the colonies was a family merchant house with a handful of ships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a merchant house was" -->
- A merchant house bought and sold on its own account, owned or chartered ships, extended credit to smaller traders, and kept correspondents in London and the West Indies. Its records were letterbooks and account books, and many survive.
- Its business ran on Atlantic circuits: fish, timber, and grain out; sugar, molasses, and manufactured goods in; and enslaved people carried as cargo by some of the same firms. That part is not left out of this chapter.
- British monopolies shaped what colonists could sell. The Royal African Company's monopoly on the English slave trade ended in 1698; the Molasses Act of 1733 and the Iron Act of 1750 are told from the economy's side in `economy`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hancock" name="Thomas Hancock" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hancock
> **Who:** the Boston merchant who built one of the largest businesses in the colonies. · **When and where:** Boston, about 1724 to 1764.
Apprenticed at fourteen to a Boston bookseller, on his own by 1724, and married in 1730 to Lydia Henchman, the daughter of his business partner. He exported codfish, whale oil, logwood, and potash, supplied the Newfoundland fishing fleet with rum and molasses, ran a group of fishing vessels, and made paper. From 1746 to 1758 he and his partner Charles Apthorp held contracts to supply British forces in King George's War and the French and Indian War with guns, lumber, food, medicine, and ships. Apthorp was a slave trader, and that is part of the record. Hancock left an estate of about £70,000 when he died in 1764, one of the largest in the colonies, and made his nephew John his partner in 1763. His letterbook and receipt book survive at Harvard's Baker Library and the Massachusetts Historical Society.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-hancock" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="big-business" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists went to war partly over a company's privileges, and then, once independent, began handing out privileges of their own. State legislatures wrote charters one at a time, for banks, bridges, canals, turnpikes, and water works. Robert E. Wright counted 319 business corporations chartered in the United States before 1801, almost all of them after the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tea Act and the East India Company" -->
- Parliament passed the Tea Act on 10 May 1773. The East India Company was in financial trouble and holding about 17 million pounds of unsold tea. The Act let it ship tea straight to America through hand-picked agents, undercutting every colonial merchant, legal or smuggling.
- Boston's seven consignees were chosen from merchants loyal to the Crown. The grievance was not only the tax. A company with a government-granted monopoly was being handed the American market.
- On the night of 16 December 1773 colonists destroyed 340 chests of tea, more than 92,000 pounds, aboard the *Dartmouth*, *Eleanor*, and *Beaver*. The East India Company's February 1774 invoice claimed £9,659 6s. 4d. in damage. Many accounts give 342 chests; the company's own paperwork says 340 (Boston Tea Party Ships and Museum; UK National Archives).
- Shared with: `government-politics` (the protest and the Coercive Acts) · `war` (the road to war) · `marketplace` (the boycott at the counter). This chapter's angle is the company's privileges.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Americans start chartering companies" -->
- The Bank of North America was chartered by the Continental Congress on 31 December 1781 and opened on 7 January 1782; Pennsylvania chartered it again in 1782. It was the first commercial bank in the country (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Richmond Fed).
- Most early charters went to transportation and finance, not manufacturing. Between 1786 and 1798, 59 private toll-bridge companies were chartered in the northeast alone.
- Most charters did not limit shareholders' liability, and only 16 of the pre-1801 charters said plainly that shareholders could not be made to pay the company's debts (Wright).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-morris" name="Robert Morris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Morris
> **Who:** the merchant who financed the Revolution and founded the country's first chartered bank, and who ended in debtors' prison. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1781 to 1801.
As Superintendent of Finance he wrote the charter of the Bank of North America and used the bank to steady the currency of a government that could not tax. After the war he bought land on borrowed money, millions of acres across seven states and more than seven thousand building lots in the new District of Columbia. When the credit turned in 1797 he owed nearly three million dollars. He entered the Prune Street debtors' apartment in Philadelphia on 15 February 1798 and stayed about three and a half years; accounts give his release as 26 August 1801 or October 1801, under the new federal Bankruptcy Act of 1800. Some historians argue his imprisonment helped get that Act passed. George Washington visited him there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-morris" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1800-1850" order="06" chapter="big-business" label="1800 to 1850" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Getting a charter went from a favour a legislature granted to a form a clerk filed. Corporations spread from bridges and banks into cotton mills and factories, the Supreme Court ruled that a charter was a contract a state could not tear up, and the largest company in the country, a bank, was destroyed by a president who did not think a private company should hold that much power.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From special charter to general law" -->
- New York's Act Relative to Incorporations for Manufacturing Purposes, 22 March 1811, was the first American law that let people incorporate by following a procedure instead of lobbying for a private act. It covered manufacturing only, capped capital at $100,000, and limited the term of the charter.
- Connecticut's 1837 law is generally counted as the first that let people incorporate for any lawful business.
- The scale of what followed: Robert E. Wright counts about 20,000 specially chartered corporations, with roughly $25 billion in authorized capital, created in the United States by 1860.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 1819" -->
- New Hampshire tried in 1816 to turn privately chartered Dartmouth College into a state university by rewriting its charter. Chief Justice John Marshall's Court held that the charter was a contract, protected by the Contract Clause, and that a legislature could not change it at will.
- What it meant for business: a charter granted in one year could not be taken back in the next. States responded by writing clauses into new charters reserving the right to amend or repeal them. Shared with: `government-politics` (**lead** — the case as law).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston Associates and the mills" -->
- Francis Cabot Lowell and a group of Boston merchants formed the Boston Manufacturing Company in 1813 and raised $400,000 by selling shares, then built at Waltham the first American mill that took raw cotton in one end and finished cloth out the other.
- Within six years the Waltham mill was working 5,376 spindles and 175 power looms and using 450,000 pounds of cotton a year. The same investors built at East Chelmsford, which was incorporated as the town of Lowell in 1826; its population went from about 200 in 1820 to about 2,000 by 1826.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — the mill girls and the working day) · `technology` (the power loom) · `city-building` (the mill town).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-biddle" name="Nicholas Biddle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Biddle
> **Who:** the president of the Second Bank of the United States, the largest company in the country, during the fight that destroyed it. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1822 to 1836.
The Second Bank was chartered on 10 April 1816 with $35 million in capital, a fifth of it the federal government's. For comparison, the whole federal government took in about $25 million a year at the time, and about $75 million in money of all kinds was circulating in the country. Biddle became president in 1822 and by the end of the decade ran 29 branches doing about $70 million of business a year. In 1832 he asked Congress to recharter the Bank four years early; Andrew Jackson vetoed the bill, won re-election, and pulled the government's deposits out in 1833. Biddle answered by tightening credit, which he said was necessary and his opponents said was proof of his power. The federal charter died in 1836. Shared with: `money` (**lead** — what a national bank is) · `government-politics` (the veto and the election).

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
This is the chapter's central era. Railroads became the first companies larger than the governments that chartered them. Oil, steel, sugar, and tobacco were reorganized so that a handful of men set prices for a whole industry. Congress wrote the first federal rules for business in 1887 and 1890, and courts spent the next fifteen years deciding what those rules meant, mostly in the companies' favour. When workers struck, companies answered with private guards, court injunctions, and troops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The railroads, the first giant corporations" -->
- In 1880 the Pennsylvania Railroad employed 30,000 people and held $400 million in capital, which made it the largest corporation in the country. The whole federal government's ordinary receipts for fiscal 1880 were $333,526,610.98 (ExplorePAHistory; Treasury Annual Report 1880 via FRASER). By the 1890s its workforce passed 110,000 and its assets were double those of any other American business.
- Land grants: railroad companies received about 131 million acres from the federal government, and states granted about 49 to 51 million more. Sources vary within that range, and the totals depend on which grants are counted and which lapsed.
- Shared with: `transportation` (**lead** — the lines and how they ran) · `migration` (settlers who bought the land) · `native-nations` (whose land it was) · `work-workers` (building it).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Men Who Built America (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller
> **Who:** the bookkeeper who built Standard Oil and invented the trust. · **When and where:** Cleveland and New York, 1870 to 1911.
> **Movie:** The Men Who Built America (2012) — an eight-part History Channel series that uses dramatized reenactments with actors alongside historians. Treat the acted scenes as illustration, not evidence.
He incorporated the Standard Oil Company of Ohio on 10 January 1870 with $1,000,000 in capital and two Cleveland refineries holding about a tenth of the country's refining capacity. In 1872 he joined the South Improvement Company, a secret arrangement in which the railroads raised their rates and then paid Standard Oil a rebate of $1.06 a barrel on its own oil plus a drawback of $1.06 on every barrel its competitors shipped. Rival refiners would pay the higher rate and pay Standard as well. The scheme was cancelled after a public revolt, but the threat did its work: in about six weeks in early 1872, Rockefeller bought out 21 or 22 of the other 26 refiners in Cleveland (counts differ by source). By 1880 he refined 90 to 95 percent of American oil.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a trust actually was" -->
- Company law was state law, and a company chartered in Ohio could not simply own companies in other states. Standard Oil's lawyer Samuel C. T. Dodd designed a way around it.
- On 2 January 1882, 41 shareholders signed the Standard Oil Trust Agreement, handing the shares of some 40 companies to nine trustees who then ran all of them as one business. The trust was valued at about $70 million. The trustees included John and William Rockefeller, Henry Flagler, Charles Pratt, and John Archbold.
- Other industries copied the device, which is why "trust" came to mean any very large combination and why the laws against them are called antitrust laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="franklin-tarbell" name="Franklin Tarbell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="candidate" -->
### Franklin Tarbell
> **Who:** an independent oil man in the Pennsylvania fields, and the father of the reporter who later investigated Standard Oil. · **When and where:** Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859 to the 1870s.
He began by building wooden oil tanks that held 500 barrels each, prospered, and moved his family into Titusville in 1870. He was one of the independent producers who refused to join the South Improvement Company in 1872 and signed the pledge not to sell. Accounts of his daughter's life state that his business partner shot himself after the scheme and that Franklin took on the man's debts. That detail comes from biographies of Ida Tarbell rather than from a primary record, and the outline flags it as such. His daughter Ida grew up in that argument and spent four years investigating the company. [VERIFY the partner's name and a primary source before this goes into prose.]

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" name="Andrew Carnegie" movie="The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Carnegie
> **Who:** the steelmaker who owned every step of making steel, and whose company broke its own workers' union. · **When and where:** Pittsburgh, 1870s to 1901.
> **Movie:** The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)
His method was to own the whole chain rather than buy from anyone. In the 1890s Carnegie Steel took a controlling share of Henry Clay Frick's coke business, leased ore in Minnesota's Mesabi Range until it held about three-quarters of that range's output, bought lake steamers to carry the ore, and built the Union Railroad to move steel between its own works. Owning each step let him cut prices below what competitors who had to buy their inputs could match. In 1892 his general manager Frick shut the Homestead works to break the union; the story of that summer is below and in `work-workers`. Carnegie's sale of the company in 1901 falls in the next section.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892, from the company's side" -->
- Henry Clay Frick, general manager and second-largest shareholder of Carnegie Steel, offered the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers a contract with large pay cuts, built a fence topped with barbed wire around the Homestead works, and locked the workers out on 30 June 1892.
- He hired the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. About 300 to 316 armed Pinkerton men were brought up the Monongahela River on two barges before dawn on 6 July 1892, at five dollars a day each. A gun battle followed.
- Death counts differ: Britannica gives seven workers and three Pinkertons; an American Heritage account gives about nine strikers and seven Pinkertons, with roughly 40 strikers and 20 Pinkertons shot, and 35 deaths from the whole episode. The range is stated, not resolved.
- Carnegie Steel then asked Pennsylvania's Governor Robert Pattison for help. About 8,500 National Guard soldiers took over the plant on 12 July. The union collapsed in November. Congress investigated the use of private armies; Frick testified.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — the strikers, the town, and what the defeat cost working people) · `crime-justice` (the Pinkerton agency itself).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-w-holway" name="John W. Holway" movie="The River Ran Red (1993)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John W. Holway
> **Who:** a twenty-three-year-old medical student hired as a Pinkerton guard for the Homestead job. · **When and where:** Chicago to Homestead, Pennsylvania, July 1892.
> **Movie:** The River Ran Red (1993) — a documentary about the Homestead lockout told from the workers' side. Holway is not its subject; it is named here because it covers the same days.
He was one of the college students the Pinkertons signed up for summer work at five dollars a day. He was put on a train at the Lake Shore depot in Chicago and not allowed off at the stops, and he said afterward that he had a feeling there would be gunplay. He does not appear to have fired a shot all day. When the Pinkertons surrendered and were marched through the town, three strikers knocked him down and accused him of killing men; he ran about half a mile with a crowd behind him before his legs gave out, and he was hit in the head by a stone. He described it under oath afterward. The company hired him, the company sent him, and he had no idea what he was walking into.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-w-holway" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal rules, and how little they did at first" -->
- **Interstate Commerce Act, 4 February 1887.** It created the Interstate Commerce Commission and made the railroads the first industry under federal regulation. The ICC was the model for every federal regulatory commission that followed.
- **Sherman Antitrust Act, 2 July 1890.** Two short sections outlawing combinations in restraint of trade and monopolizing.
- **United States v. E. C. Knight Co., 1895.** The Court held the American Sugar Refining Company had not violated the Act even though it controlled about 98 percent of American sugar refining, on the reasoning that manufacturing was not commerce.
- The honest part: in its first decade the Sherman Act was used against labour unions more often than against large companies, and courts treated unions as illegal combinations. The Pullman boycott of 1894 was broken with it. Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — Pullman and Debs) · `government-politics` (the statutes as law).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="big-business" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The wave of mergers that ran from 1895 to 1904 produced the first company worth a billion dollars. Then came the reply: a reporter who spent four years going through Standard Oil's own records, a president who took the biggest combinations to court, a Supreme Court that broke Standard Oil into pieces, two new laws in 1914, and after 1929 a set of rules about how companies must report themselves to the public. The Second World War then handed the largest companies the largest contracts the government had ever written.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great merger movement and U.S. Steel" -->
- Between 1895 and 1904 more than 1,800 manufacturing firms were merged into 157 consolidated corporations (Naomi Lamoreaux, *The Great Merger Movement in American Business, 1895–1904*). Lamoreaux argues these combinations were not inevitable and that many quickly lost ground to new rivals.
- United States Steel was incorporated on 25 February 1901 with authorized capital of $1.403 billion, the first billion-dollar company in the world. Charles M. Schwab was its first president and Elbert Gary its chairman.
- In 1902, its first full year, it made 67 percent of all American steel and employed 168,000 people. Its share had slipped to about 50 percent by 1911.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan
> **Who:** the banker who put companies together and made U.S. Steel. · **When and where:** New York, 1901 to 1904.
He did not run factories. He arranged the money, bought the pieces, and set the terms. In 1901 he bought Carnegie Steel from Andrew Carnegie for $480 million and folded it together with Federal Steel and other firms into U.S. Steel. In 1901 he also joined James J. Hill and E. H. Harriman in the Northern Securities Company, a holding company over the Great Northern, Northern Pacific, and Burlington railroads, which is the company Theodore Roosevelt then sued. His rescue of the banking system in the panic of 1907 is told in `money`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" name="Ida Tarbell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ida Tarbell
> **Who:** the reporter who took Standard Oil apart in print. · **When and where:** New York and the Pennsylvania oil fields, 1900 to 1904.
She grew up in Titusville, where her father Franklin was an independent oil man squeezed by the 1872 rebate scheme. She spent about four years on the work, reading court records, government investigations, and company documents rather than relying on interviews. *The History of the Standard Oil Company* ran in *McClure's* from November 1902; sources give the series as 18 or 19 installments and its end as April or October 1904, and this book states the disagreement instead of picking one. It became a two-volume book in 1904. One installment retold the affidavit of Mrs. Fred M. Backus, a Cleveland widow who said Rockefeller had underpaid her for her late husband's lubricating works in 1878. Later historians, including Ron Chernow, argue Standard's $79,000 was fair for an out-of-date plant and that her asking price of up to $200,000 was not. The dispute is part of the story and is told as a dispute. No documentary about Tarbell alone was found; she appears in *The Rockefellers* (American Experience, 2000). Shared with: `news-communication` (**lead** — muckraking and how the magazine worked).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt
> **Who:** the president called the trust-buster, and what his record actually was. · **When and where:** Washington, 1901 to 1909.
> **Movie:** TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)
In 1902 he ordered the Justice Department to sue the Northern Securities Company under the Sherman Act. The Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 for the government on 14 March 1904 and ordered the company dissolved, and that one case made his reputation. The full record is smaller than the nickname suggests: he brought 44 antitrust suits in seven and a half years, while William Howard Taft brought 90 in four. Roosevelt also drew a line between what he called good and bad trusts and did not think size was by itself an offence, which Taft did not accept. Shared with: `government-politics` (the presidency).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standard Oil is broken up, 1911" -->
- On 15 May 1911 the Supreme Court held that Standard Oil of New Jersey had restrained trade and monopolized commerce in petroleum, and ordered it dissolved within six months. Sources give the number of successor companies as 33 or 34; the Library of Congress says about 34.
- Rockefeller received roughly 25 percent of the shares in each successor. The pieces were worth more separately than together, and his fortune is generally reported to have risen from about $300 million to about $900 million in the months after the ruling. Newspapers called him the world's first billionaire in 1916. Demand for gasoline was rising fast, which is part of the reason.
- Successors of Standard Oil later became Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco, and others.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New laws, and who owned the country" -->
- **Clayton Antitrust Act** and the **Federal Trade Commission Act**, both 1914. The FTC Act created an independent commission with power to police unfair methods of competition alongside the Justice Department; the Clayton Act named specific practices the Sherman Act had not clearly reached.
- **Berle and Means, 1932.** In *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means showed that by 1930 the 200 largest corporations held 49.2 percent of American corporate wealth, and that inside those companies the managers, not the shareholders who legally owned them, made the decisions.
- **Securities Exchange Act, 6 June 1934**, creating the Securities and Exchange Commission and requiring public companies to report their finances honestly. Franklin Roosevelt made Joseph P. Kennedy, a Wall Street speculator who had used the practices the new law banned, the first chairman on 1 July 1934. The press called it putting the fox in the henhouse. Roosevelt is widely reported to have answered in private that you set a thief to catch a thief; the wording varies between accounts and no single documented version exists.
- **The territories.** Five firms known as the Big Five — C. Brewer, Theo H. Davies, H. Hackfeld (later Amfac), Castle & Cooke, and Alexander & Baldwin — controlled about 94 percent of Hawaii's sugar production by 1920 and ran much of its shipping, banking, and politics. Shared with: `america-world` (**lead** — Hawaii's annexation and the territories).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War contracts, 1940 to 1944" -->
- The federal government placed $175.066 billion in prime defence contracts with American corporations between 1940 and 1944. Two-thirds of that went to 100 companies, and 20 percent to five.
- By 30 June 1944, 26 firms had the use of half the value of all government-financed industrial plant leased to private contractors.
- Shared with: `war` (**lead** — the fighting and mobilization) · `economy` (what war production did to the national income) · `work-workers` (the workforce).

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For most of these fifty years the largest American company was a car maker or a telephone company, and both were eventually cut down: one by foreign competition, one by a court. In between, businessmen built conglomerates that owned hotels, car rentals, insurance, and bread all at once; organized themselves in Washington on a scale they never had before; and persuaded Congress to remove rules from airlines, trucking, railroads, and banks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="General Motors, and a sentence nobody quotes correctly" -->
- GM held 54 percent of the American car market in 1954. In 1955 it became the first company in history to earn more than a billion dollars in profit in a single year; it had made $912,887,537 in the first three quarters alone. Its United States employment peaked at 618,365 in 1979.
- Britannica records that by 1953 about one American worker in 200 worked for GM and the company's revenues equalled about 3 percent of the country's entire output.
- At his 1953 confirmation hearing to be Secretary of Defense, GM's president Charles E. Wilson was asked whether he could make a decision against the company's interest. He said he could, and added that for years he had thought what was good for the country was good for General Motors, and the other way round. It is almost always quoted as "what's good for General Motors is good for the country," which reverses his meaning. He spent years correcting it and gave up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The conglomerate" -->
- Harold Geneen ran International Telephone and Telegraph from 1959 to 1977 and bought more than 350 companies, among them Sheraton hotels, Avis car rental, Hartford Insurance, and Continental Baking, the maker of Wonder Bread.
- ITT's sales went from about $700 million to about $17 billion, and it became the eleventh largest corporation in the United States. The idea was that a company did not have to be in any particular business to be worth owning.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Business organizes in Washington" -->
- In August 1971 the lawyer Lewis F. Powell Jr., two months before he was nominated to the Supreme Court, sent a 34-page confidential memorandum titled "Attack on American Free Enterprise System" to Eugene B. Sydnor Jr. of the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, arguing that business should organize to defend itself in politics, the courts, the media, and the universities.
- The Business Roundtable was formed in 1972 from three business groups, with membership limited to chief executives of large companies.
- The counts historians use: corporations with public affairs offices in Washington went from 100 in 1968 to more than 500 in 1978; firms with registered lobbyists went from 175 in 1971 to nearly 2,500 in 1982; corporate political action committees went from fewer than 300 in 1976 to more than 1,200 by mid-1980. These are figures from political scientists and journalists rather than a government count; labelled as such.
- Deregulation followed: airlines in 1978, trucking and railroads in 1980, banking in stages. Shared with: `government-politics` (**lead** — campaign finance law and lobbying rules).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bell System is split, 1984" -->
- In 1983, its last full year whole, the Bell System held about $150 billion in assets, more than General Motors, IBM, General Electric, U.S. Steel, Eastman Kodak, and Xerox put together, and employed just under a million people, the largest private workforce in the country.
- Under a consent decree agreed in 1982 with the Justice Department and approved by Judge Harold Greene, AT&T gave up its local telephone companies on 1 January 1984. They became seven regional holding companies, commonly called the Baby Bells. AT&T kept long distance, Bell Labs, and Western Electric, and just under $40 billion of the assets.
- Shared with: `technology` (**lead** — the telephone network) · `news-communication` (what it did to phone service).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="bill-gates" name="Bill Gates" movie="Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bill Gates
> **Who:** the head of Microsoft when the United States sued it for monopolizing personal computer operating systems. · **When and where:** Redmond, Washington, and Washington, D.C., 1998 to 2001.
> **Movie:** Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019) — a three-part Netflix series directed by Davis Guggenheim, mostly about his later charitable work; the antitrust case is not its subject.
The Justice Department and twenty states sued Microsoft on 18 May 1998, arguing it had used its control of Windows to shut out rival web browsers. On 5 November 1999 Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found that the company held monopoly power, and on 7 June 2000 he ordered it split into two companies. On 28 June 2001 the appeals court agreed unanimously that Microsoft had broken antitrust law, threw out the breakup order, and ruled that Judge Jackson had committed serious judicial misconduct by talking about the case to reporters. The government dropped the demand for a breakup, and the case ended in a settlement of conduct rules. The plain summary: the company lost on the law and kept itself intact. Shared with: `technology` (**lead** — the software).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bill-gates" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="steve-young-vlasic" name="Steve Young" movie="Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Steve Young
> **Who:** a Vlasic pickle executive who described what it was like to supply the largest retailer in the world. · **When and where:** the late 1990s to January 2001; account published December 2003.
> **Movie:** Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004) — a PBS investigation into Walmart's power over suppliers. Young is not in it; it covers the same subject.
Young was vice president of grocery marketing for pickles at Vlasic. A Walmart buyer asked for Vlasic's gallon jar at $2.97, and it went into about 3,000 stores. Each store sold about 80 jars a week, roughly 240,000 gallons a week, with Vlasic and Walmart making a penny or two a jar. Shoppers stopped buying the higher-margin spears and chips elsewhere, and Vlasic's pickle profits fell by 25 percent or more while Walmart accounted for 30 percent of its business. When Vlasic asked for relief, Young says the answer was no, and his colleague Pat Hunn recalls being told competitors would take the deal. Walmart eventually let the jar shrink to just over half a gallon at $2.79. Vlasic filed for bankruptcy in January 2001; everyone involved agrees the pickles were not the main cause. This account is journalism, not a public record: Charles Fishman, "The Wal-Mart You Don't Know," *Fast Company*, December 2003. Shared with: `marketplace` (**lead** — the stores and the shopper) · `work-workers` (wages and conditions).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The largest American companies are now technology companies, and their market values are counted in trillions of dollars. The federal government rescued banks and car makers in 2008 and 2009 with money Congress voted in a weekend. After nearly a century in which few big antitrust cases were brought, the Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission sued Google, Amazon, Apple, and Meta, and the results so far are mixed. This section is current through 2026 and several of these cases are unfinished.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big, in numbers" -->
- Nvidia became the first company to reach a market value of five trillion dollars, in October 2025. In mid-2026 the largest American companies by market value were Nvidia at about $5.09 trillion, Alphabet about $4.55 trillion, Apple about $4.49 trillion, Microsoft about $3.69 trillion, and Amazon about $2.99 trillion. Market values change daily; these are a snapshot, and the book says so.
- Walmart reported revenue of about $713 billion for its 2026 financial year and about 2.1 million workers worldwide, roughly 1.6 million of them in the United States. Amazon reported revenue of about $717 billion for 2025 and about 1,576,000 employees at the end of that year (company annual reports and SEC filings).
- The comparison across the grid: General Motors, the largest employer of the 1950s, employed a little over 600,000 people. Walmart today employs more than three times that number.
- Shared with: `technology` (**lead** — the products) · `marketplace` (**lead** — Amazon and Walmart as places to shop) · `economy` (what these firms are worth to the whole economy).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 2008 rescue" -->
- Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in United States history on 15 September 2008, with about $619 billion in debts against $639 billion in book assets.
- Congress authorized $700 billion under the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act on 3 October 2008. About $443 to $444 billion was actually paid out; most was repaid. The Government Accountability Office puts the lifetime cost at roughly $31 billion, nearly all of it in the housing programs, with the bank programs returning a profit.
- Shared with: `economy` (**lead** — the recession) · `money` (the banking system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Antitrust returns" -->
- **Google, search.** On 5 August 2024 Judge Amit Mehta ruled that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in general search and search text advertising, mainly through exclusive deals to be the default search engine. In September 2025 he ordered remedies that stopped short of what the Justice Department asked for: he refused to make Google sell the Chrome browser, limited exclusive contracts, and required some data sharing with competitors.
- **Google, advertising technology.** On 17 April 2025 Judge Leonie Brinkema ruled that Google had illegally monopolized markets for publisher ad servers and ad exchanges and had unlawfully tied its products together. A remedies hearing ran into October 2025.
- **Meta.** The FTC sued in 2020 over the purchases of Instagram and WhatsApp. After a six-week trial, Judge James Boasberg ruled for Meta on 18 November 2025, finding the FTC had not proved Meta holds monopoly power once TikTok and YouTube are counted as competitors. The FTC said in January 2026 it would appeal.
- **Amazon.** The FTC and 17 state attorneys general sued on 26 September 2023. The trial is scheduled for 9 February 2027 and had not been held when this book went to press.
- Told plainly and neutrally. Some of these cases the government won, some it lost, and some are not finished.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lina-khan" name="Lina Khan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lina Khan
> **Who:** the law student whose article changed the antitrust argument, and who later ran the agency that brought the cases. · **When and where:** New Haven and Washington, 2017 to 2025.
As a student she published "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" in the *Yale Law Journal* in 2017. Her argument was that American antitrust law had come to ask only whether prices went up, and that this missed a company that sells cheaply while also owning the marketplace, the delivery network, the cloud servers, and the products competing on its own shelves. Confirmed as chair of the Federal Trade Commission on 15 June 2021 at age 32, the youngest person to hold the job, she brought the Amazon and Meta cases. The Meta case was lost in November 2025 and the Amazon case has not been tried. Her critics, including the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, published a 2023 report arguing her original analysis was wrong on the facts. Both sides are named because the argument is live.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lina-khan" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corporate money in politics" -->
- The Supreme Court decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* on 21 January 2010, holding that the government may not limit independent political spending by corporations and unions. The ruling itself belongs to `government-politics`; the spending is this chapter's.
- Super PAC spending went from $62.6 million in 2010 to more than $4.1 billion in 2024. Total outside spending in the 2024 federal elections came close to $4.5 billion. More than $9 billion has been spent by outside groups since 2010, and about three dollars in every ten came from donors whose names are not disclosed (OpenSecrets).
- Federal lobbying set records too: about $4.4 billion in 2024 and $5.08 billion in 2025, reported by 15,768 organizations (OpenSecrets).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Working for a company without being employed by it" -->
- Companies now buy labour without hiring workers. Uber reports roughly one million active drivers in the United States as of late 2025.
- How many Americans do this kind of work is genuinely disputed and depends entirely on the definition. The Bureau of Labor Statistics finds about 1 percent of the workforce, roughly 1.7 million people, uses an online platform to arrange work in a given month. Private surveys that count all freelance and side work reach 42 million or even 70 million. The book gives the range and explains why it is a range.
- Shared with: `work-workers` (**lead** — what the work is like and the fight over employee status) · `transportation` (ride-hailing).
