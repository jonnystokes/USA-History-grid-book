## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Careful, tested knowledge about the world existed here long before laboratories — held by nations, taught from person to person, and checked against the sky, the plants, and the seasons for thousands of years. No one called it science; it worked the same way: watch, record, test, pass on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="reading the sky" -->
- Cahokia's Woodhenge (Illinois, about A.D. 900–1100): circles of upright posts marking where the sun rises through the year, including the solstices — sky-watching precise enough to build a calendar from (Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site). The posts as a built thing → `technology`; the sky-knowledge is ours.
- The Sun Dagger on Fajada Butte, Chaco Canyon (New Mexico): three stone slabs channel sunlight into "daggers" that cross two spiral carvings at the solstices and equinoxes; recorded by Anna Sofaer in 1977. Honest status: by 1989 two slabs had shifted — erosion sped up by human visits — and the daggers no longer land as they once did (NPS; Solstice Project). Claims about moon-cycle markings are argued by researchers, not settled.
Shared with: native-nations (the peoples themselves) · technology (Woodhenge as built calendar) · landmarks (Chaco's great houses).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="knowledge of plants and fire" -->
Plant knowledge — which plants feed, which heal, when to harvest — and the use of controlled fire to keep land open and productive were systematic, taught knowledge (the burning's story on the land → `land-environment`; healing practice → `health`). Scientists today call this Traditional Ecological Knowledge and work with it as knowledge, not folklore (Ecological Society of America TEK section).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robin-wall-kimmerer" name="Robin Wall Kimmerer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robin Wall Kimmerer
> **Who:** Citizen Potawatomi Nation botanist; professor at SUNY's College of Environmental Science and Forestry; founded its Center for Native Peoples and the Environment; MacArthur Fellow, 2022. · **When and where:** New York State, present day.
A present-day scientist who works with both kinds of knowledge — laboratory botany and Potawatomi plant teachings — and shows they test and strengthen each other. Her book *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013) became a national bestseller. The living answer to "was that science?" (ESF; MacArthur Foundation).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A thin era, honestly told: European science barely exists yet — this is still the age of alchemy — and no newcomer is doing sustained study here. What crosses the ocean is collecting and describing, carried back to Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the first field report" -->
Thomas Hariot, an English mathematician on the 1585–86 Roanoke expedition, spent a year recording plants, animals, foods, and the Algonquian people; his *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* (1588) was the first book in English about North America written by someone who had been here, printed in 1590 with John White's drawings (Encyclopedia Virginia; UNC).
Shared with: exploration (the voyages, lead) · native-nations (the people being described).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A thin era: a college, an almanac press, a borrowed telescope, and a handful of colonists writing down what they saw. Observation was real; the observers still read the sky as sermon.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="college, almanac, telescope" -->
- Harvard founded 1636, the first college in the English colonies (harvard.edu).
- The colonies' first almanac — *An Almanack for New England for the Year 1639*, calculated by mariner William Pierce — was among the first two things printed in British America; almanacs carried astronomy into ordinary houses for two centuries (Britannica).
- John Winthrop Jr. of Connecticut, elected to the Royal Society in London (1662–63) among its first colonial members, gave Harvard its first scientific instrument about 1672: a 3.5-foot telescope (Linda Hall Library).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the comet argument" -->
When the Great Comet of 1680 blazed over Boston, Thomas Brattle tracked it by telescope and reasoned its two appearances were one body swinging around the sun — measurements that reached Isaac Newton and were cited in his *Principia* (American Antiquarian Society). Increase Mather preached the same comet as God's warning, yet his *Kometographia* (1683) used measurement to show comets were distant bodies, not vapors; his Boston Philosophical Society (1683) lasted about three years (Marco Institute).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-campanius-holm" name="Rev. John Campanius Holm" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rev. John Campanius Holm
> **Who:** Chaplain to the small Swedish colony on the Delaware; kept the first known systematic weather record in what is now the United States. · **When and where:** Near present-day Wilmington, Delaware, 1644–1645.
Twice a day, with no instruments, he wrote down the weather. The National Weather Service still gives volunteer observers an award named for him (NWS/NOAA).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-campanius-holm" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists began doing original work — and organized themselves to share it. By mid-century a Philadelphia printer's electrical experiments were being read in London.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the American Philosophical Society, 1743" -->
The colonies' first lasting scientific society. Botanist John Bartram floated the idea in 1739; Franklin organized it in 1743 with his pamphlet *A Proposal for Promoting Useful Knowledge among the British Plantations in America* — an American answer to London's Royal Society, still operating today (amphilsoc.org). [Kept in this era — the 1743 date controls.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="electricity in Philadelphia" -->
From about 1747 Franklin ran electrical experiments and mailed the results to London: positive and negative charge (his words, still used), charge that is never created or destroyed, and the idea that lightning is electricity. The letters were published as *Experiments and Observations on Electricity* (1751); the Royal Society gave him its Copley Medal in 1753 — its top honor, first time to anyone outside Britain (Library of Congress; NMAH).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-science" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin
> **Who:** Printer who became the first American scientist with a world reputation — the theory of electric charge, and the proof that lightning is electricity. · **When and where:** Philadelphia, experiments from about 1747. · **Movie:** Benjamin Franklin (2022)
The scientist Franklin: careful experiments, plain reports, results shared free. His stove and lightning rod → `technology`; his newspaper → `news-communication`. The film is Ken Burns's PBS documentary.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-science" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-bartram" name="John Bartram" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Bartram
> **Who:** Quaker farmer-botanist; planted America's oldest surviving botanic garden and proposed the society that became the APS. · **When and where:** Kingsessing, near Philadelphia; garden from 1728; expeditions 1730–1765.
Self-taught; collected from New York to Florida; named the King's Botanist for North America in 1765; he and his sons introduced over 200 American plants to science. Linnaeus reportedly called him the greatest natural botanist in the world — the line is an attribution, not a record (Bartram's Garden).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-bartram" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
American science earned its first international respect — a lightning experiment copied across Europe, a planet timed across the sun, and a free Black farmer's almanac used against slavery's defenders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the kite, told honestly" -->
Franklin designed the lightning test; France ran it first — at Marly-la-Ville, May 10, 1752, a tall iron rod drew sparks from a storm cloud, following Franklin's published plan (Founders Archives; APS News). Franklin's own kite flight in Philadelphia is traditionally dated June 1752; he never recorded the day, told it in print only that October, and historians still argue over details. The knowledge held either way: lightning is electricity. The rod on the roof → `technology`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="timing Venus, 1769" -->
The APS organized teams to time Venus crossing the sun on June 3, 1769 — the measurement nations wanted for the distance to the sun. The Norriton team (Rittenhouse, Smith, Lukens, Sellers) filled the first volume of the APS *Transactions*, reprinted by the Royal Society: colonial science treated as equal work for the first time (APS museum; Royal Society).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson's bones and Buffon's insult" -->
Europe's leading naturalist, Buffon, taught that animals and people grew smaller and weaker in America. Jefferson answered with evidence — *Notes on the State of Virginia*, moose antlers shipped to Paris, and in 1797 the fossil claws he named Megalonyx, presented to the APS: the start of American paleontology (Monticello; American Scientist). Jefferson the founder → `government-politics`; his home → `landmarks`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="david-rittenhouse" name="David Rittenhouse" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### David Rittenhouse
> **Who:** Self-taught instrument maker and astronomer; led the 1769 transit observations at Norriton. · **When and where:** Pennsylvania, 1732–1796.
Built the era's finest American instruments and mechanical models of the solar system; succeeded Franklin as APS president in 1791. His Mint job → `money`.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-rittenhouse" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-banneker-science" name="Benjamin Banneker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Banneker
> **Who:** Free Black tobacco farmer, self-taught astronomer and mathematician; calculated complete almanacs published 1792–1797. · **When and where:** Baltimore County, Maryland, 1731–1806.
Taught himself astronomy from borrowed books and instruments; correctly predicted the April 14, 1789 solar eclipse against better-known calculators. On August 19, 1791 he mailed Jefferson his manuscript almanac with a letter confronting him on slavery; Jefferson sent the almanac to Paris. Anti-slavery societies printed his almanacs as walking proof against claims of Black inferiority (Britannica; Founders Archives). His survey of the federal district → `city-building` (its story).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-banneker-science" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Science became a job in America: professors, a journal, government charts — and, from a stranger's gift, a national institution for knowledge.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a professor, a journal, a fallen stone" -->
Benjamin Silliman took Yale's new chemistry chair in 1802; when a meteorite fell on Weston, Connecticut on December 14, 1807, he collected and analyzed it — the first scientifically documented meteorite fall in the New World; in 1818 he founded the American Journal of Science, the country's first lasting scientific journal (Britannica; Connecticut History).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="electricity and honest credit" -->
Joseph Henry, teaching at Albany, built the strongest electromagnets of his day and in 1832 published the discovery of self-induction. On induction itself, Faraday in London published first (1831) and keeps the credit; Henry had seen it and published late. The unit of inductance is named the henry (Smithsonian Archives; Britannica). His magnets made the telegraph possible — the wire → `technology`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="charting sea and sky by crowd" -->
- Navy lieutenant Matthew Fontaine Maury mined thousands of old ships' logs and issued the first Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic (1847), then traded charts for captains' standardized logs — crowdsourced ocean science that cut weeks off voyages (Navy history; LOC). He later joined the Confederacy in 1861 — say so.
- From 1849 Smithsonian Secretary Joseph Henry ran about 150 volunteer weather observers reporting by mail and free telegraph — the first national weather maps, and the seed of the National Weather Service (Smithsonian Archives).
Shared with: transportation (what the charts did for shipping) · news-communication (the telegraph carrying weather).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-smithson" name="James Smithson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### James Smithson
> **Who:** English chemist whose will gave the United States — a country he never saw — the money to found the Smithsonian Institution. · **When and where:** Died Genoa, Italy, June 27, 1829; the institution founded in Washington, August 10, 1846.
His will left everything, if his nephew died without heirs, "to the United States of America, to found at Washington... an establishment for the increase & diffusion of knowledge." The nephew died in 1835; more than 100,000 gold sovereigns — over $500,000 — reached the Philadelphia mint in 1838; Congress argued eight years before Polk signed the act. He never explained why. Nobody knows (Smithsonian Institution Archives).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-smithson" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-henry" name="Joseph Henry" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Henry
> **Who:** Albany schoolteacher turned leading American physicist; electromagnetism, self-induction (1832), and first Secretary of the Smithsonian (December 1846). · **When and where:** Albany, Princeton, and Washington, 1797–1878.
Turned Smithson's gift into a working engine of research — including the volunteer weather network that became the Weather Service (Smithsonian Archives; Princeton).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-henry" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="maria-mitchell" name="Maria Mitchell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maria Mitchell
> **Who:** Nantucket librarian and astronomer; first American to discover a telescopic comet with a standing royal prize — and the first American woman astronomer by profession. · **When and where:** Nantucket, Massachusetts; the comet found October 1, 1847.
From the roof of the Pacific National Bank, sweeping the sky with a small telescope, she found a comet no chart showed. The King of Denmark's gold medal came to her — first woman and first American to win it; in 1848 she was the first woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. Her Vassar professorship (1865) and the all-women eclipse expedition she led to Denver (1878) belong to the next era — note them forward (Maria Mitchell Association; Smithsonian).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maria-mitchell" -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
American universities became places where research happened, and American measurements started setting world standards. The era also shows science misused — its most famous professor put its authority behind racism.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Darwin argument in America" -->
Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) split Harvard: botanist Asa Gray — who arranged Darwin's American publication — defended the evidence; Louis Agassiz, the country's most celebrated scientist, insisted every species was separately created and attacked the book at the American Academy of Arts and Sciences from January 1860. Gray argued the case in print and in debate; working biologists went with the evidence within a generation (AAAS archives; Harvard Magazine).
Shared with: religion (the faith response, and Scopes later) · education (evolution in schools).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what Agassiz also did" -->
Agassiz promoted the doctrine that races were separately created, and in 1850 had enslaved South Carolina people — among them Renty and his daughter Delia — stripped and photographed as "evidence." The daguerreotypes, rediscovered at Harvard in 1976, are the earliest known photographs of enslaved Americans. Science's authority can be misused; this era proves it (Harvard Magazine; Peabody Museum).
Shared with: slavery-freedom (lead — the people photographed and their descendants' fight).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="colleges for everyone, stations in every state" -->
The Morrill Act (1862) built the land-grant colleges → `education` leads. Our slice: they carried science teaching into every state, and the Hatch Act (1887) added an agricultural experiment station to each — research as a public service, not a private hobby (Congress.gov CRS).
Shared with: education (the colleges, lead) · food-farming (what the stations studied).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="women measure the sky" -->
At Harvard Observatory, women hired as "computers" did the measuring and cataloguing. Maria Mitchell, by then a Vassar professor, led an all-women team about 2,000 miles to Denver for the total solar eclipse of July 29, 1878 — a women's scientific expedition with the national press watching (Smithsonian NMAH).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-willard-gibbs" name="Josiah Willard Gibbs" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Willard Gibbs
> **Who:** Yale physicist whose 1876–78 paper founded chemical thermodynamics — the rules of energy in chemistry. · **When and where:** New Haven, Connecticut, 1839–1903.
Held America's first engineering doctorate (Yale, 1863); taught nine years unpaid until a Johns Hopkins offer made Yale pay him. He published his masterwork in an obscure Connecticut journal, and Europe understood it before America did — Maxwell built a plaster model of one of Gibbs's surfaces with his own hands and mailed it to New Haven. Copley Medal, 1901 (Britannica; Yale).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-willard-gibbs" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-michelson" name="Albert Michelson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Michelson
> **Who:** Immigrant from Prussia, raised in Western mining towns; measured the speed of light with world-record precision. · **When and where:** Annapolis, Maryland (1879) and Cleveland, Ohio (1887).
His 1879 measurement came within about 0.05 percent of today's value. In 1887, with Edward Morley, his light-splitting instrument found no trace of the "ether" light was supposed to travel through — a famous nothing that pointed toward Einstein's relativity. In 1907 he became the first American to win a science Nobel — that prize sits in the next era (USNA; NobelPrize.org).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-michelson" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="williamina-fleming" name="Williamina Fleming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Williamina Fleming
> **Who:** Scottish immigrant hired as the observatory director's maid; became one of the world's leading star classifiers. · **When and where:** Harvard College Observatory, from about 1879 to 1911.
Classified more than 10,000 stars for the 1890 Draper Catalogue, spotted the Horsehead Nebula on an 1888 photographic plate, and in 1899 became Curator of Astronomical Photographs. Her note in Harvard's 1900 time capsule records $1,500 a year against men's $2,500 for comparable work: "Does he ever think that I have a home to keep and a family to take care of as well as the men?" (Harvard Magazine; Linda Hall Library).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="williamina-fleming" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Physics moved to the center of American science, refugees from fascist Europe filled American laboratories, and by the era's end the federal government had become science's biggest funder. The universe got bigger; the atom came apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="counting and weighing the invisible" -->
Michelson's 1907 Nobel was the first American science Nobel; Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiments (1909–1913) caught the exact electric charge of a single electron (Nobel 1923); Thomas Hunt Morgan's Columbia "fly room," starting from one white-eyed fruit fly in 1910, proved genes sit in line on chromosomes (Nobel 1933 — the first for genetics) (NobelPrize.org; APS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="measuring the universe" -->
Henrietta Leavitt — a Harvard computer paid 30 cents an hour, deaf since an illness in her twenties — published the law in 1912 that made blinking Cepheid stars into distance markers. Edwin Hubble used it at Mount Wilson's 100-inch telescope: in 1923–25 he proved Andromeda is a separate galaxy, and in 1929 that the farther a galaxy, the faster it runs away — the expanding universe. Honest credit: Belgium's Georges Lemaître published expansion in 1927 in a little-read journal; the law is now the Hubble–Lemaître law. Leavitt died in 1921; a Nobel nomination was started in 1925 by someone who did not know she had died (Harvard Plate Stacks; NASA; APS).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the atom comes apart" -->
Fission was discovered in Berlin (December 1938, Hahn and Strassmann); the explanation came from Lise Meitner — a Jewish refugee excluded from the 1944 Nobel for it — and Otto Frisch (January 1939). In America the response built the wartime laboratory system: the Chicago Metallurgical Lab, Oak Ridge, Los Alamos — and on December 2, 1942, under the stands of a disused Chicago football field, Enrico Fermi's team of 49 ran the first controlled nuclear chain reaction, about half a watt for 28 minutes. Fermi had sailed straight from his 1938 Nobel ceremony to New York because Italy's racial laws targeted his Jewish wife. Berkeley's Radiation Laboratory (independent 1936) had already set the model of the big-machine, big-team lab; Seaborg's actinide concept redrew the periodic table (APS; Atomic Heritage; LBL).
Shared with: war (lead — the bomb and the decision) · elements (plutonium and the discovery run) · city-building (the secret cities).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the transistor's physics, 1947" -->
On December 16, 1947 at Bell Labs, Bardeen and Brattain made a solid crystal amplify a signal — quantum mechanics doing work in a piece of germanium; announced June 30, 1948; Nobel 1956 with Shockley. The physics of surfaces and semiconductors is ours; the device that replaced the vacuum tube → `technology` (Computer History Museum; IEEE Milestone).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-chapman-bird-count" name="Frank Chapman and the first Christmas Bird Count" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank Chapman and the first Christmas Bird Count
> **Who:** Museum ornithologist who replaced the Christmas "side hunt" — shooting every bird in reach — with a count; 27 volunteers ran 25 counts the first day. · **When and where:** Across North America, December 25, 1900.
The first count tallied about 18,500 birds of 90 species, from Toronto to Pacific Grove, California. It has run every winter since — ordinary people building one of the longest scientific datasets on Earth (Audubon).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-chapman-bird-count" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-goddard" name="Robert Goddard" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Goddard
> **Who:** Physics professor who worked out and flew the first liquid-fuel rockets — and was mocked for it by the New York Times. · **When and where:** Worcester and Auburn, Massachusetts; first flight March 16, 1926.
His 1920 Smithsonian paper argued rockets could reach space; a Times editorial (January 13, 1920) jeered that he lacked "the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools." His first liquid-fuel rocket flew 41 feet in 2.5 seconds off a farm in Auburn. On July 17, 1969 — Apollo 11 en route to the Moon — the Times printed a correction: "The Times regrets the error." The knowledge is ours; the rockets that flew to the Moon → `technology` and `exploration` (NASA history).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-goddard" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-hubble" name="Edwin Hubble" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Hubble
> **Who:** Astronomer who proved other galaxies exist and that the universe is expanding. · **When and where:** Mount Wilson Observatory, California; 1923–1929.
One marked photographic plate — "VAR!" — recorded the star that put Andromeda far outside the Milky Way; six years later his velocity–distance law changed cosmology. Built on Leavitt's yardstick and Humason's spectra; shares law-credit now with Lemaître (NASA; Carnegie Observatories).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-hubble" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-einstein-science" name="Albert Einstein" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Einstein
> **Who:** The century's most famous physicist, driven out of Germany; an American from 1933 on. · **When and where:** Princeton, New Jersey, 1933–1955.
Relativity was done in Europe; his American decades are the refugee's story — Institute for Advanced Study from October 1933, citizen October 1, 1940. On August 2, 1939 he signed Szilard's letter warning Roosevelt about German atomic bombs (the bomb → `war`). At Lincoln University in 1946 he called racism "a disease of white people" and said, "I do not intend to be quiet about it" (IAS; Founders-era archives; Harvard Gazette).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-einstein-science" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-mcclintock" name="Barbara McClintock" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Barbara McClintock
> **Who:** Geneticist who discovered that genes can move — decades before the field could believe it. · **When and where:** Cold Spring Harbor, New York; the key experiments from 1944.
Working alone with corn, she found "controlling elements" that jump between chromosomes and switch other genes on and off; published 1950, presented 1951. Colleagues respected her and mostly set the idea aside until molecular biology confirmed it in the 1960s–70s — tell it as delay, not mockery. Nobel in 1983, unshared — noted in the next era (NLM Profiles in Science; CSHL).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-mcclintock" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-segre-science" name="Emilio Segrè" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Segrè
> **Who:** Italian physicist stranded at Berkeley in 1938 when Mussolini's racial laws erased his job at home. · **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from summer 1938.
Visiting the Radiation Lab when the antisemitic laws passed, he stayed — Lawrence hired him cheap as a research assistant — and became one of America's leading physicists: Nobel 1959 for the antiproton, with Owen Chamberlain. The refugee scientists' story in one life (immigration and language thread). His element discoveries → `elements` (its Segrè story) (OAC Segrè papers; NobelPrize.org).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="science" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Federal money, big teams, and the Cold War set what got studied. The era's discoveries redrew the deepest maps: the molecule of heredity, the ocean floor, the inside of the proton, the afterglow of the Big Bang, and the first volcano beyond Earth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="DNA, 1953 — with honest credit" -->
American James Watson and Briton Francis Crick, in Cambridge, England, published DNA's double helix (Nature, April 25, 1953). They depended on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray work: Wilkins showed Watson her "Photo 51" in early 1953 without her knowledge, and her data reached them in an internal report. Franklin died in 1958 at 37; the 1962 Nobel went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins — no posthumous prizes. Watson's later racist statements cost him his honorary titles in 2019; say so in a line (Nature; CSHL).
Shared with: health (what the molecule changed in medicine).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the sky hisses, the proton has parts" -->
- 1964–65: Penzias and Wilson at Bell Labs in New Jersey could not silence a faint microwave hiss — they even shoveled pigeon droppings out of the antenna — until Princeton physicists identified it: the cooled afterglow of the Big Bang. Nobel 1978 (APS historic site).
- Gell-Mann named the quark in 1964 (Nobel 1969); the 1968 MIT–SLAC scattering experiments found point-like pieces inside the proton (Nobel 1990) (NobelPrize.org).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the ground moves" -->
Marie Tharp's seafloor maps (with Bruce Heezen, 1957 Atlantic; 1977 world map) revealed the rift valley splitting the mid-ocean ridge; Harry Hess's 1962 seafloor-spreading paper and a decade of evidence made plate tectonics the accepted frame by about 1970 — the continents move (LOC; Lamont-Doherty).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="what the space probes taught" -->
The journeys → `exploration`; the learning is ours. Six Apollo crews brought back 382 kilograms of Moon rock — still being studied at NASA Johnson. Viking's 1976 Mars life tests came back mixed and stand unresolved: one experiment looked positive, another found no organics; most scientists credit soil chemistry (NASA; the Viking debate is on the record). Voyager 1 and 2 (launched 1977) remade the outer solar system — Io's volcanoes, Europa's cracked ice, Titan's haze, Uranus, Neptune. Carl Sagan's *Cosmos* (PBS, 1980) carried the results to hundreds of millions (journalism figure — label).
Shared with: exploration (lead — the journeys and craft) · technology (the machines).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="measurement that would not stop climbing" -->
Charles David Keeling (Scripps) began continuous carbon-dioxide measurement on Mauna Loa in March 1958 — about 315 parts per million then, past 430 by 2026. The Keeling Curve is the base measurement under climate science (Scripps; NOAA; ACS landmark).
Shared with: land-environment (lead — the warming and the land) · energy (the fuels).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the lab network, and genetics pays its debts" -->
The national laboratories (Berkeley, Livermore, Argonne, Los Alamos, Oak Ridge) ran Cold War science at industrial scale — the U.S.–Soviet element race included (`elements` tells those discoveries). McClintock's 1983 Nobel — unshared, a first for a woman in that prize — arrived three decades after her experiments. The Human Genome Project launched October 1, 1990: 15 planned years, about $3 billion (genome.gov).

### Richard Feynman
> **Who:** Physicist who rebuilt the theory of light and matter, drew the diagrams every physicist now uses, and explained hard things plainly. · **When and where:** Los Alamos in the war; Caltech from the 1950s; died 1988. · **Movie:** The Fantastic Mr Feynman (2013)
Nobel 1965 (with Schwinger and Tomonaga) for quantum electrodynamics. On live television, February 11, 1986, on the Challenger commission, he dropped O-ring rubber into ice water and showed the failure in one minute. The film is a BBC documentary with interviews of Feynman, family, and colleagues (NobelPrize.org; commission record).

### Marie Tharp
> **Who:** Cartographer-geologist who mapped the ocean floor and found the rift valley that helped prove the continents move. · **When and where:** Lamont Geological Observatory, Columbia University, from 1948.
Barred from the research ships as a woman (she first sailed in 1968), she built the maps ashore from soundings. When she pointed out the rift in 1952–53, her partner Bruce Heezen called it "girl talk" — then the data kept agreeing with her. The Library of Congress later named her among the 20th century's great cartographers (LOC; Lamont-Doherty).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="linda-morabito" name="Linda Morabito" movie="The Farthest (2017)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Linda Morabito
> **Who:** JPL navigation engineer who found the first active volcano beyond Earth. · **When and where:** Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Pasadena, California, March 9, 1979.
Processing a routine Voyager 1 navigation image of Jupiter's moon Io, she noticed a faint 270-kilometer plume off its edge — an erupting volcano, the first ever seen off Earth. A working engineer on the low-priority frames, making one of the mission's biggest discoveries. The film is the Voyager documentary; she appears in it (her arXiv historical review; NASA/JPL).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="darleane-hoffman-science" name="Darleane Hoffman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Darleane Hoffman
> **Who:** Nuclear chemist who ran chemistry on single atoms of the heaviest elements. · **When and where:** Los Alamos and Berkeley, 1950s–2000s.
Found plutonium-244 occurring in nature (1971) — a "man-made" element that nature made first — and tested where the periodic table's patterns start to bend, one atom at a time. Her element work → `elements` (its Hoffman story) (ACS; Physics Today).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Science grew bigger and more international — thousands of people per experiment, machines the size of towns — while public argument about science grew louder. Current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the genome, finished twice" -->
The Human Genome Project declared the sequence essentially complete in April 2003 — about 92 percent, timed to the double helix's 50th birthday; the last gaps were truly closed in March 2022. Reading a human genome fell from billions of dollars to hundreds (genome.gov). The medicine → `health`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Higgs, and the machine America cancelled" -->
On July 4, 2012, CERN in Geneva announced the Higgs boson; about 1,700 U.S. scientists worked on the LHC experiments through Fermilab and Brookhaven. The plain truth alongside: Congress cancelled the Superconducting Super Collider in Texas in October 1993 — $2 billion spent, 14 miles of tunnel dug, then abandoned — so the discovery machine was Europe's (CERN; DOE; Physics Today).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="new eyes: JWST" -->
The James Webb Space Telescope launched December 25, 2021; first images July 2022. It has pushed the record for earliest known galaxies into the universe's first few hundred million years and read carbon dioxide in an exoplanet's air (WASP-39b) (NASA; STScI).
Shared with: exploration (the mission as journey) · technology (the machine).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="AI joins the lab" -->
AlphaFold (2020) solved practical protein-structure prediction; free databases now hold over 200 million predicted structures. The 2024 Nobel in Chemistry split between American David Baker (designing new proteins) and DeepMind's Hassabis and Jumper in Britain — say the mix plainly. Wider AI use in labs is still unfolding; recent claims are journalism — label them (NobelPrize.org 2024; EMBL).
Shared with: technology (the AI systems themselves, lead).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the element hunt goes international" -->
Superheavy-element claims are now judged by international working parties; Dubna–Livermore–Oak Ridge collaborations and Berkeley's 2024 result point at an attempted element 120 — a possible new row of the periodic table. The "island of stability" idea is the physics motive (IUPAC; Berkeley Lab). `elements` leads the discoveries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rainer-weiss" name="Rainer Weiss" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rainer Weiss
> **Who:** MIT physicist, refugee from Nazi Berlin, who conceived the detector that first heard gravitational waves. · **When and where:** MIT and the LIGO sites in Washington State and Louisiana; detection September 14, 2015.
His family fled Berlin via Prague, reaching New York in January 1939. In 1972 he worked out the laser interferometer idea; four decades later the NSF-funded LIGO detectors caught spacetime ringing from two black holes that collided 1.3 billion light-years away — announced February 11, 2016, a century after Einstein predicted the waves. Nobel 2017 with Barish and Thorne (NobelPrize.org; LIGO/Caltech).

### Jennifer Doudna
> **Who:** Berkeley biochemist; with Emmanuelle Charpentier turned a bacterial defense system into CRISPR, the programmable DNA editor. · **When and where:** UC Berkeley; the key paper June 28, 2012. · **Movie:** Human Nature (2019)
The 2020 Nobel in Chemistry to Doudna and Charpentier was the first science Nobel shared by two women alone. The editing tool is the knowledge; the therapies → `health`. The film is a CRISPR documentary in which she is a central voice (NobelPrize.org; Science).

### Monica Dunford
> **Who:** American physicist, then a postdoc on the ATLAS experiment — one of six scientists a film crew followed from the LHC's start-up to the Higgs announcement. · **When and where:** CERN, Geneva, 2008–2012. · **Movie:** Particle Fever (2013)
A documented picture of what working science is now: one young scientist among 3,000 on a single experiment, hauling cables, debugging detectors, and standing in the room on July 4, 2012 (Particle Fever; Symmetry magazine).
