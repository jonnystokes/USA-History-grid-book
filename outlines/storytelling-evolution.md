## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People in this land told stories out loud and acted them out. There was no building called a theatre, no ticket, and no job called actor. Performing was part of ceremony, healing, teaching, and law, and in many nations it still is. Stories were held in memory and in performance rather than in writing, so what survives is the traditions themselves, carried by the people who still perform them, together with the objects used to perform: masks, rattles, costume, paint, and the places built to hold a crowd.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Playing someone who is not you" -->
Masked performance is documented across the continent: the False Face Society among the Haudenosaunee, whose masked members performed healing rites; katsina dances in the Hopi and Zuni pueblos, in which masked dancers take on katsinam; masks carved in Yup'ik villages in what is now Alaska for a single ceremony and then set aside; crest costume and hinged transformation masks among the Tlingit and Haida of the Northwest Coast. The evidentiary problem has to be stated plainly rather than hidden: nearly all the written descriptions were made by outsiders after 1500, and the nations themselves date the practices earlier. [VERIFY each tradition and how far back the physical evidence runs.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rules about who tells what, and when" -->
Telling was governed. In many northern nations certain stories may be told only in winter, when snow is on the ground. Stories can belong to a family, a clan, or a society, and someone who does not hold a story does not tell it. This is the opposite of the arrangement that came later, in which a play is written down and anyone who buys the script may perform it. [VERIFY which nations, and find published statements from the nations themselves rather than from ethnographers only.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Places built to hold an audience" -->
Ceremony needed room, and the ground shows it. The great plaza at Cahokia in what is now Illinois was leveled and maintained for large gatherings; the great kivas at Chaco Canyon in New Mexico were built to seat groups; pueblo plazas in the Southwest are still used for dances today. [VERIFY the size of the Cahokia plaza and the seating capacity claims for the great kivas.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the record does not have" -->
No individual performer from before 1500 in this land is named in the surviving record. The traditions themselves continued through every era below, and named performers working in them are documented from the 1800s onward.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Almost no Europeans lived in this land in the 1500s, so almost no European plays were performed here. Native performance continued. Two European things arrived at the very end of the century: religious plays used to teach Christian doctrine to people who did not read Spanish, and the first recorded performance of a European play in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="April 30, 1598: the play at the river" -->
On April 30, 1598, Juan de Onate's colonizing expedition reached the Rio Grande near what is now El Paso. After a Mass, soldiers and friars performed a play written by one of Onate's captains, Marcos Farfan de los Godos, on bare ground, with a canvas curtain hung between two cottonwoods for a backdrop. Its subject was the settlement of New Mexico that the expedition was about to carry out. Eight months later, in January 1599, that same expedition attacked Acoma Pueblo: about 500 Acoma men and about 300 women and children were killed over three days, and Onate then sentenced every surviving man over 25 to have a foot cut off and to twenty years of servitude. Twenty-four men were mutilated; historians disagree over whether whole feet or toes were taken, and the disagreement should be stated rather than settled. `native-nations` leads on Acoma. [VERIFY all figures and the date range of the attack.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plays as mission work" -->
Spanish missionaries in New Mexico and Florida staged religious plays to teach doctrine, including *Los moros y los cristianos*, a mock battle between Moors and Christians, still performed in some New Mexico communities today. The same missions worked to stop Native ceremonies, which were performances too, so the century holds both a new kind of drama arriving and an old kind being suppressed. `religion` leads on the missions as religion. [VERIFY the 1598 date usually given for the first New Mexico performance, and the continuity of the modern performances.]

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies had no theatre buildings and nobody who made a living acting. In New England the largest regular public performance was the sermon, delivered to a meetinghouse full of people who were required to attend. Ministers there argued that plays were lies told for money by men pretending to be people they were not, and the English Parliament had closed London's theatres from 1642 to 1660, so the colonists who agreed with that argument had just watched it win at home. [VERIFY the New England attendance laws and the sermon length.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="August 27, 1665: a play, a complaint, and a court" -->
On August 27, 1665, three men performed a play called *Ye Bare and Ye Cubb* at Cowle's Tavern near Pungoteague in Accomack County, Virginia. A neighbour complained to the county court. The court ordered the three men to perform the play again in front of it, in the clothes they had acted in. Having watched it, the court found them not guilty and ordered the man who complained to pay the costs. No script survives. The entire record of the first play acted in English in what is now the United States is a court file about whether performing it was an offence. [VERIFY the complainant's name and the exact wording of the order.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The storytelling that was not on a stage" -->
Africans taken to Virginia by force from 1619 onward, and sold there and in the other colonies after, carried performance with them: storytelling that required the listeners to answer back, songs with characters in them, and dance. They continued it where they were allowed to and where they were not. `slavery-freedom` leads on the system; `music` leads on the songs. Native performance continued through the century and was attacked in two directions at once: the praying towns of New England and the missions of New Mexico both worked to end ceremonies. In 1680 the Pueblo Revolt drove the Spanish out of New Mexico and banned ceremonies were performed openly again. [VERIFY the 1680 restoration claim; `native-nations` leads on the revolt.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-darby-1665" name="William Darby" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="target" -->
### William Darby
> **Who:** One of three men who acted in *Ye Bare and Ye Cubb* in 1665, named in the court record as an author of it, and the one prosecuted for it. · **When and where:** Accomack County, Virginia, August 27, 1665; tried later that year.
He was accused, made to perform the play for the judges in costume, and acquitted. His two fellow actors were Cornelius Watkinson and Philip Howard. Nothing else about him is well established, which is itself worth saying: the first documented actor in English in this country is known from one court file. ## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A few buildings for plays went up in the southern colonies and a small number of touring performers worked in them. In Pennsylvania and New England, plays stayed illegal or close to it. The largest crowds any performer drew in these fifty years came to hear preachers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first playhouses" -->
Around 1716 a merchant named William Levingston built a playhouse in Williamsburg, Virginia, the first building put up for plays in the English colonies. Charleston, South Carolina had performances from the 1730s, and the Dock Street Theatre opened there in 1736. In New York and Philadelphia, plays were staged in warehouses and court rooms rather than in theatres. Pennsylvania's assembly passed acts against stage plays more than once, and the Crown disallowed them. [VERIFY the Williamsburg date, which sources give as 1716 to 1718, and the Pennsylvania acts.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Preaching as performance" -->
During the revivals of the 1730s and 1740s, George Whitefield preached outdoors to crowds larger than any theatre could hold. Benjamin Franklin, standing back from one Philadelphia sermon to work out how far the voice carried, calculated that Whitefield could be heard by more than thirty thousand people. Whitefield had acted in school plays and used gesture, weeping, and voices for other characters in the pulpit; ministers who disliked him said so in exactly those terms. `religion` leads on the Great Awakening as religion; this chapter's angle is the technique and the crowd. [VERIFY Franklin's figure and its context in his autobiography, and the acting-in-school claim.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster and Election Day" -->
In New York and New Jersey, enslaved and free Black people held Pinkster in the spring, with music, dance, and a chosen king. In several New England colonies, Black communities held what was called Negro Election Day, electing a governor or king and parading behind him. Both were performances, staged by people who were barred from every stage there was. `holidays` leads on them as festivals. [VERIFY earliest documented dates and where the accounts come from.]

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Professional acting companies began working in America in the 1750s, touring a circuit of a few towns and playing in buildings put up for the purpose. Then the Continental Congress asked Americans to give up plays, the war scattered the American companies, and British officers staged plays in the cities they occupied. After the war the bans came off and permanent theatres opened in Philadelphia, New York, Boston, and Charleston. The first plays written by Americans were performed in this period, and one of them invented a character type that stayed on American stages for a century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1752: the first professional company" -->
Lewis Hallam's London Company of Comedians landed in Virginia in 1752 and opened at Williamsburg on September 15 with *The Merchant of Venice*. The company was a family business of adults and children who played the parts their ages fitted, and it toured New York, Philadelphia, Charleston, and Jamaica. Its repertoire was English: Shakespeare and English comedies, performed in America by people who had learned them in London. [VERIFY the company's size, the opening date and play, and the Jamaica years.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first American plays" -->
*The Prince of Parthia*, by Thomas Godfrey of Philadelphia, was staged at the Southwark Theatre on April 24, 1767, the first play by an American author performed by a professional company. Mercy Otis Warren wrote plays attacking the royal governor of Massachusetts, including *The Adulateur* in 1772 and *The Group* in 1775; they were printed and read rather than acted, because there was nowhere to act them. Royall Tyler's *The Contrast* opened in New York on April 16, 1787, the first American comedy staged by professionals, and its servant Jonathan became the stage Yankee: plain, rural, and shrewder than the people laughing at him. [VERIFY all three dates and the claim about Jonathan as the first of the type.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Congress bans plays, and the army performs anyway" -->
In October 1774 the Continental Congress adopted the Continental Association, which called on Americans to give up shows, plays, and other expensive entertainments. In October 1778 Congress went further and provided that officials who attended plays could be dismissed. Both sides ignored it in practice. British officers put on plays in occupied Boston, New York, and Philadelphia, and in May 1778 Washington's officers performed Joseph Addison's *Cato* at Valley Forge. [VERIFY the wording of the 1774 article, the 1778 resolution, and the Valley Forge performance.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

> **Who:** A Massachusetts writer who used plays as political attack when there was no theatre to stage them in. · **When and where:** Plymouth and Barnstable, Massachusetts, 1772 onward.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Theatre went west with the country, into taverns, courthouses, and boats tied up at river towns. Shakespeare was popular entertainment rather than schoolwork, played for crowds that shouted at the actors and sometimes stopped the show. Two things began in these decades that shaped everything after. The first American-born stage stars appeared, and audiences took sides over them hard enough to be shot at. And white performers built an entire industry out of pretending to be Black people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shakespeare on the river and in the mining camps" -->
Companies played Shakespeare in towns of a few hundred people, cut to length, mixed with songs and acrobatics, and often parodied in the same evening by the same actors. In 1831 William Chapman's family launched a floating theatre that worked the Ohio and Mississippi, tying up at a town, playing, and moving on. Audiences in the cheap seats expected to talk back, and managers expected them to. [VERIFY the Chapman dates, and find published examples of the mixed programmes.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Minstrelsy: what it was, in plain words" -->
Around 1830 a white actor named Thomas Dartmouth Rice began performing a song and dance called Jump Jim Crow with his face blacked with burnt cork, playing a Black man as ragged, foolish, and delighted with his own life. In 1843 four white men calling themselves the Virginia Minstrels put on the first full evening of it in New York. For the next fifty years the minstrel show was the most popular entertainment in the United States, north and south. What was being sold was a picture of Black people as lazy, thieving, cowardly, comic, and contented under slavery, performed by white men in makeup for white audiences, at the same time as the people being caricatured were being bought and sold. The segregation laws passed after the Civil War took their name from Rice's act: Jim Crow. `music` leads on the songs and where they went afterwards; this chapter leads on the show, the makeup, and the caricature. [VERIFY the Rice date, which sources place between 1828 and 1832, the Virginia Minstrels date and venue, and the path from the act's name to the laws.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="May 10, 1849: the Astor Place riot" -->
The American actor Edwin Forrest and the English actor William Charles Macready had been publicly attacking each other for years, and their supporters had turned it into a fight about whether the theatre belonged to working men or to the people who could afford the Astor Place Opera House. On May 10, 1849, a crowd of about ten thousand gathered outside the opera house in New York and threw paving stones. The state militia fired into it. Published counts of the dead range from 22 to about 30, and more than 100 people were injured; the disagreement between sources should be stated rather than settled. It was the first time the militia fired on a civilian crowd in New York. [VERIFY the counts, the militia order, and the injured figure.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### William Alexander Brown
> **Who:** A retired ship's steward who founded the first Black theatre company in the United States, and wrote the first play by an African American to be staged. · **When and where:** New York City, 1816 to 1823.
He bought a house, ran a tea garden in the yard, then built a three-hundred-seat theatre on the upper floor and opened on September 21, 1821 with *Richard III*. The company played Shakespeare and English comedies, and in 1823 staged Brown's own *The Drama of King Shotaway*, about a Black uprising on St Vincent. Its leading actor was James Hewlett. White rowdies disrupted performances, and city officials closed the theatre in 1823 after complaints about the audience. Sources give his name as both William Alexander Brown and William Henry Brown; say so rather than choosing. [VERIFY the name, the opening date, the seat count, and who ordered the closure.]

### Ira Aldridge
> **Who:** A Black American actor who left the United States because no American stage would employ him, and became a leading Shakespearean actor in Britain and Europe. · **When and where:** born New York 1807; England from 1824; died on tour in Lodz, Poland.
He watched plays at Brown's African Grove Theatre as a teenager. In America the parts open to him were the parts a minstrel show offered. He sailed for England, played Othello, Shylock, Lear and Macbeth for forty years, toured Prussia, Russia and Poland, was decorated by several governments, and never worked on an American stage again. ## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Railroads let a company carry one show to hundreds of towns, and by the 1880s a small number of managers in New York decided which acts played where. Vaudeville sold a bill of short turns, continuously, all day. Wild West shows sold history as a performance, with some of the people who had lived it in the cast. And in the 1890s the first films were made in America. Almost all of them were recordings of stage acts, which is why this chapter does not treat film as a new subject.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tom shows" -->
George Aiken's stage version of *Uncle Tom's Cabin* opened in 1852, and touring companies played versions of it for more than seventy years, making it the most performed play of the century in the United States. `art` leads on the novel. On stage the characters were routinely handed to white actors in blackface and reshaped into minstrel types, with added dogs, songs, and comic business, so a story written against slavery was performed for decades in the makeup of the shows that defended it. [VERIFY the 1852 date, the run length, the performance-count claim, and examples of the blackface casting.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="April 14, 1865: an actor at Ford's Theatre" -->
John Wilkes Booth was a working actor from a family of actors, known to the staff at Ford's Theatre and able to walk through the building without being stopped. On the night of April 14, 1865, he waited in the corridor for a line in *Our American Cousin* that he knew would bring a laugh, went into the box, shot Abraham Lincoln in the head, jumped to the stage, and called out to the audience before going out the back. Lincoln died the next morning. The government took the building; no play was performed in Ford's Theatre again until 1968. `government-politics` and `war` lead on the assassination and its consequences; this chapter's angle is that the killer was an actor using a theatre he knew as a workplace. [VERIFY the line he waited for, the 1968 reopening, and the government's takeover.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vaudeville, and who was allowed on the bill" -->
In 1881 Tony Pastor opened a variety house in New York that barred drinking and off-colour material in order to bring in women and children. B. F. Keith and E. F. Albee built that into a chain of theatres running continuous performance from noon until night, booked from a single office, so an act could be sent across dozens of cities on one contract and dropped from all of them at once. Black performers were hired mainly to do minstrel material and were required to black up, including performers who were already dark-skinned. [VERIFY the Pastor date, the Keith and Albee circuit dates, and the blacking-up requirement with named sources.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Playing yourself for a white audience" -->
Buffalo Bill's Wild West opened in 1883 and toured for three decades. It hired Native performers, most of them Lakota, to ride out and act attacks and defeats several times a day. Sitting Bull travelled with it for one season in 1885 at fifty dollars a week; `native-nations` leads on him. For Lakota performers the work paid wages, allowed travel off reservations where movement was controlled by federal agents, and required them to act a version of their own history written by the man selling tickets to it. [VERIFY the wage, the season, the proportion of Native performers, and published statements by the performers themselves.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1893 to 1896: the first films are stage acts" -->
Thomas Edison's laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey built a film studio in 1893, a tarpaper shed on a turntable that the staff called the Black Maria, and filmed vaudeville turns in it: a strongman, a dancer, a man sneezing. Kinetoscope parlours, where one person at a time watched through an eyepiece, opened in 1894. Projection to a seated audience began in 1896. *The Kiss*, filmed that year, was a scene lifted from a Broadway musical and performed again for the camera by the same two actors. `technology` leads on the machines. [VERIFY the Black Maria date, the 1894 and 1896 dates, and the origin of *The Kiss*.]

### Edwin Booth
> **Who:** The leading American actor of his generation, and the brother of the man who killed Lincoln. · **When and where:** New York and on tour, 1850s to 1893.
He was known for *Hamlet*, which he played for a hundred consecutive nights in 1864. After his brother shot Lincoln he stopped acting, and returned in January 1866 to applause and to threats. He had earlier pulled a young man off a railway platform in Jersey City who turned out to be Lincoln's son Robert. He later built a theatre of his own and lost it. ### Charlotte Cushman
> **Who:** The leading American actress of the mid-century, who played male parts including Romeo and Hamlet. · **When and where:** Boston, New York, London, and Rome, 1830s to 1870s.

She lived openly with women for most of her adult life, in a circle of American women artists in Rome, and continued to tour while ill with cancer. ### Billy Kersands
> **Who:** A Black minstrel performer, comedian and dancer, and one of the highest-paid Black entertainers of the century. · **When and where:** touring the United States and Britain, 1860s to 1915.
He performed the caricature that had been built to mock him, in burnt cork, for white audiences and for Black ones, and packed both. The material is what it is and this chapter says so; the question the research has to answer honestly is what performers in his position said about doing it, in their own recorded words rather than in ours. ## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In fifty years the audience moved off the live stage. Films learned to tell stories, then to talk. Eight companies in Los Angeles came to make most of what Americans watched [VERIFY the count and name them: the Big Five plus the Little Three] and to own the theatres it played in, and they signed actors to contracts that assigned their parts, their names, their appearance, and what they could do outside work. Radio put acted stories into the house every night for free. Two of the most watched performances of the period were white performers playing Black characters, on screen and on the air.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nickelodeons, and the move to Los Angeles" -->
*The Great Train Robbery*, directed by Edwin S. Porter in 1903, ran about twelve minutes and told a story by cutting between places. Storefront theatres charging five cents spread from 1905. Film makers moved to Los Angeles in the 1910s. Early films carried no credits, because producers withheld the actors' names to stop them demanding more money; audiences invented names for them instead, and the first film star was known as the Biograph Girl before her employer admitted she was Florence Lawrence. [VERIFY the running time, the nickelodeon dates, and the Lawrence account.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1915: The Birth of a Nation" -->
D. W. Griffith's film ran about three hours and took more money than any American film before it. It showed the Ku Klux Klan riding to rescue white Southerners from Black men who were played by white actors in blackface, one of them pursuing a white girl until she jumps to her death. President Woodrow Wilson had it screened at the White House. The Klan, refounded in 1915, used the film to recruit for years afterwards. The NAACP campaigned against it and got it banned or cut in a number of cities. What the film told audiences about Reconstruction is contradicted by the historical record, and `slavery-freedom` and `rights-movements` lead on that record. Its place in this chapter is that it fixed the shape of the American feature film. [VERIFY the running time, the White House screening and the disputed quotation attributed to Wilson, the box office claim, and the cities that banned it.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sound, and the rules about who could touch whom" -->
*The Jazz Singer*, released in 1927, was the first feature with synchronised singing and speech, and its star Al Jolson performed in blackface. From 1934 the studios enforced the Production Code, which among other things forbade showing romance between a white character and a character of another race. That rule decided what parts existed for Asian, Black, and Latino actors for the next thirty years. [VERIFY the 1927 and 1934 dates and the exact wording of the code provision.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio drama" -->
From the 1920s families listened to plays performed live in studios, with sound effects made in the room. *Amos 'n' Andy*, on the air from 1928, was performed by two white men, Freeman Gosden and Charles Correll, playing Black characters in dialect, and was the most listened-to programme in the country. On October 30, 1938, the Mercury Theatre on the Air broadcast *The War of the Worlds* in the form of news bulletins; newspapers reported a national panic, and later research found the audience was small and the panic was largely a newspaper story. Say both parts. `news-communication` leads on radio as a medium. [VERIFY the Amos 'n' Andy audience figures and the research correcting the 1938 panic.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Federal Theatre Project, 1935 to 1939" -->
The federal government paid unemployed actors, stagehands, and writers to put on plays during the Depression, with most tickets free or a few cents. Its Negro Units staged plays with Black casts in cities across the country, including a *Macbeth* set in Haiti that opened at the Lafayette Theatre in Harlem in 1936, directed by Orson Welles, who was twenty. Congress ended the project in 1939 after hearings that accused it of communism. `economy` and `work-workers` lead on federal relief work. [VERIFY the number employed, the audience total, the number of Negro Units, the *Macbeth* opening date, and the terms of the 1939 shutdown.]

### Charlie Chaplin
> **Who:** A performer from the London music halls who became the most widely recognised person in the world by acting without speaking. · **When and where:** United States from 1913; films from 1914 to 1952.
He built one character, kept it across dozens of films, wrote and directed his own work, and went on making films without dialogue for years after sound arrived. In 1919 he and three others formed United Artists so that performers and directors could own and release their own films instead of working for studios. [VERIFY the United Artists founding and partners, the dates of the major films, and his contract terms.]

### Oscar Micheaux
> **Who:** A homesteader turned novelist turned film maker, who wrote, produced and directed about forty films for Black audiences outside the studio system. · **When and where:** South Dakota, Chicago and New York, 1919 to 1948.
He raised money by selling shares door to door, booked his films into the theatres that would show them, and made *Within Our Gates* in 1920, which includes a lynching. Censor boards in several cities cut or banned it. `big-business` leads on the studio system he was working around. [VERIFY the film count, the 1920 censorship, and the fundraising method.]

### Anna May Wong
> **Who:** The first Chinese American film star, working under rules that decided in advance what she could be cast as. · **When and where:** Los Angeles, born 1905; films from the 1920s to the 1960s.
The Production Code's ban on interracial romance kept her out of leading roles opposite white actors, so she was cast as villains and as women who die. When MGM filmed *The Good Earth* in 1937, the two Chinese leads went to white actors in makeup, and one of them won an Academy Award for it; Wong was offered a supporting part as the villain and turned it down. [VERIFY her birth date, the *Good Earth* casting and her response, and the roles she publicly objected to.]

### Hattie McDaniel
> **Who:** The first Black person to win an Academy Award. · **When and where:** Los Angeles; the award on February 29, 1940.
She won Best Supporting Actress for *Gone with the Wind*. The ceremony was held at the Cocoanut Grove in the Ambassador Hotel, which did not admit Black guests; the film's producer David O. Selznick asked for an exception, and she was seated at a small table away from the rest of the film's cast. Accounts differ on whether that table stood at the back of the room or in a separate room, and the difference should be stated rather than smoothed. She had not been allowed to attend the film's premiere in Atlanta in December 1939. Most of the parts offered to her over her career were maids, and Black critics including the NAACP said publicly that taking them did harm. [VERIFY the hotel policy and Selznick's intervention, the seating accounts, the Atlanta exclusion, the number of maid roles, and the sourcing of the remark about playing a maid that is usually attributed to her.]

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Television took the mass audience from film inside a decade. Congress investigated actors' politics, and studios agreed not to hire the ones who would not answer. Acting training changed, with a school that told actors to work from their own memories instead of from technique. Film changed twice more: a run of director-led films from 1967, then from 1975 a way of releasing films everywhere at once that made a single title the season's business. And by the end of the century actors were being hired to perform in video games.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The blacklist" -->
In 1947 the House Un-American Activities Committee subpoenaed Hollywood writers, directors and actors. Ten who refused to answer were convicted of contempt of Congress and went to prison in 1950. The studios then agreed not to employ anyone who would not clear themselves, and hundreds of people lost their work, some for more than a decade; some writers kept working under other people's names, and one won an Academy Award in 1957 under someone else's name [VERIFY: the pseudonym was Robert Rich, the real name of a relative of one of the producers]. Charlie Chaplin's permit to re-enter the country was revoked in 1952 while he was on a ship to England, and he did not come back until 1972. `government-politics` leads on the committee. [VERIFY the 1947 and 1950 dates, the Waldorf statement, the number blacklisted, the 1957 Academy Award, and the Chaplin revocation.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Method, and live television" -->
The Actors Studio, founded in New York in 1947 and led by Lee Strasberg from 1951, taught actors to build a performance out of their own remembered feeling and observed behaviour. Marlon Brando in *A Streetcar Named Desire*, on stage in 1947 and on film in 1951, is the performance usually named as the turning point. At the same time television was broadcasting plays performed live, with no way to fix a mistake once it happened, and actors trained on stage moved between the two. [VERIFY the founding dates, Strasberg's arrival, and the live anthology series.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The sitcom is a stage play that got filmed" -->
*I Love Lucy*, from 1951, was performed in front of a live audience and filmed by three cameras at once, so that it could be shown again later. The company that Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz owned kept the film, which is how reruns became a business. The form is a stage play with an audience laughing in the room, recorded, which is the same arrangement the first films used in 1894 with better equipment. [VERIFY the three-camera claim and its attribution, and the ownership terms.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was allowed to play whom" -->
White actors played Native, Asian and Latino characters in makeup into the 1960s, and studios cast them knowing that actors from those groups were available. Sidney Poitier won Best Actor in 1964, the first Black man to do so. In 1973 Marlon Brando refused the Best Actor award for *The Godfather* over how films portrayed Native Americans and over the standoff then going on at Wounded Knee, and sent a woman using the name Sacheen Littlefeather to say so from the stage; part of the audience booed her. In 2022 her sisters stated publicly that the family was not Native. Both facts belong in the account. [VERIFY the 1964 award, the 1973 statement and the reaction, and the 2022 reporting.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Home video, and the film everyone saw at once" -->
*Jaws* in 1975 opened in hundreds of theatres on the same day with national television advertising, and *Star Wars* in 1977 confirmed the method. The Production Code ended in 1968 and a ratings system replaced it. Sony sold the Betamax recorder from 1975 and JVC the VHS from 1976; Universal and Disney sued Sony to stop home taping, and in 1984 the Supreme Court ruled that recording a broadcast at home to watch later was lawful. Rental stores followed, and a performance became something a household could own a copy of. `marketplace` leads on the rental business. [VERIFY the release patterns, the recorder dates, and the 1984 decision and its margin.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Actors start working in games" -->
Arcade and console games of the 1970s and 1980s carried no acted performances; `sports-play` leads on games as play and `technology` on the machines. In the early 1990s games on CD-ROM began using filmed actors, shot against blank backgrounds and cut into the game, and by the late 1990s recorded voice acting was standard. The job existed before anyone called it acting. [VERIFY which titles came first, and the earliest union agreements covering the work.]

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The performance and the thing the audience sees have come apart. An actor can work in a suit covered in markers, on a bare stage, and be rendered years later as a body that is not theirs. Games hire actors the way films do. Subscriptions replaced tickets and tapes. Anyone with a phone can perform to a large audience with no company, no studio, and no stage. And software can now produce a face and a voice that nobody performed, which is what American performers struck over twice between 2023 and 2025. This chapter is current through 2026; say so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acting in a marker suit" -->
Andy Serkis performed Gollum on set with the other actors for *The Lord of the Rings* between 2001 and 2003, and the studio replaced his body with an animated one built from his movements. The same method carried *King Kong* in 2005 and the *Planet of the Apes* films from 2011. Performers now work in suits and head-mounted cameras that record the face as data, and the question of whose performance the audience is watching is not a philosophical one: it decides who is paid and who is credited. [VERIFY the production dates and the credit and award arguments that followed.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Games as acted stories" -->
*The Last of Us*, released in 2013, was performed by actors together on a capture stage, with the voice and the body recorded at the same time; Troy Baker and Ashley Johnson played the two leads. Ten years later the same story was performed again by different actors for television. Voice and capture work is now a regular acting job with its own contracts. `sports-play` leads on games as play; `technology` on the machines. [VERIFY the capture method, the cast, and the 2023 television adaptation.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Streaming, and the eighteen months with no theatre" -->
Netflix began streaming in 2007 and released its first original series in 2013. Audiences moved to subscriptions, and the number of scripted series made each year rose sharply before falling again. On March 12, 2020, Broadway closed because of COVID-19, and theatres did not fully reopen until September 2021: about eighteen months in which no one performed for a paying audience there. `health` leads on the pandemic. [VERIFY the Netflix dates, the series counts, and the closure and reopening dates.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Anyone can perform" -->
YouTube launched in 2005 and TikTok became available in the United States in 2018. A performer can now write, act, shoot, edit and publish alone, be paid by the platform according to rules the platform sets and changes, and reach an audience larger than a television network's without ever auditioning. `news-communication` leads on the platforms as media. [VERIFY the launch dates and the payment arrangements.]
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose face is it, and who agreed" -->
In 2016 *Rogue One* put a digital recreation of the actor Peter Cushing, who died in 1994, on screen with his estate's permission. In 2023 the Writers Guild struck from May 2 to September 27 and SAG-AFTRA from July 14 to November 9, the first time both struck at the same time since 1960. Among the central issues was scanning: background actors said they had been scanned for a day's pay without being told what the scans could later be used for. Those accounts are on the record but the people are not all named, so tell that part unnamed unless a named on-the-record account is found. The settlement requires a performer's specific written consent and separate payment before a digital replica is made or used, requires fresh consent for each new production, and holds after the performer's death unless agreed otherwise. Video game performers then struck from July 26, 2024, and the agreement ratified on July 9, 2025 requires consent, payment, and reports on how a replica is used. Tennessee and California passed state laws on digital replicas of living and dead performers in 2024. What is not settled: what happens to a performance the performer did not give. [VERIFY every date here, the strike lengths, the contract terms, and the state laws by name.]
