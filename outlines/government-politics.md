## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists start governing themselves in small ways very early — and slavery is written into law in the same century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charters, assemblies, and the first slave laws" -->
Governor George Yeardley called twenty-two elected burgesses from Virginia's eleven settlements to Jamestown for six days, 30 July to 4 August 1619. That was the first elected assembly in English America. Nobody wrote down who was allowed to vote for those burgesses; the colony did not limit that vote to adult men who owned land until 1670. The Crown took the Virginia Company's charter away in 1624 and the assembly kept meeting anyway, without permission, until it was quietly accepted again in 1627. In Massachusetts the men aboard the *Mayflower* signed an agreement in November 1620 to make and obey their own laws. Town meetings and colonial courts followed. Virginia's assembly also wrote slavery into law from 1662 onward (**`slavery-freedom` tells that**).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A town made to keep a school" -->
Massachusetts passed a law in 1642 telling parents and masters to teach their children to read English and to know the colony's capital laws. It built no school. If a household failed, the selectmen could fine it, or take the children away and put them with another master.
In 1647 the colony passed a second law. Its opening line says the point is to stop "that old deluder, Satan" keeping people from reading Scripture. A town of fifty households had to appoint someone to teach reading and writing. A town of a hundred families had to keep a grammar school. A town that let this slide for more than a year paid five pounds a year to the nearest school until it obeyed. The penalty lands on the town, not on the parent, which is what makes this a law about government (`education` leads on what the schools were like).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-pory" name="John Pory" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Pory
> **Who:** The colony secretary who ran the first elected assembly in English America and then wrote down what it did · **When and where:** Jamestown, Virginia, 30 July to 4 August 1619

John Pory was born about 1572 at Thompson in Norfolk, England. He took a degree at Cambridge in 1592 and a master's in 1595, and he had sat in the English Parliament, so he knew how a lawmaking meeting was supposed to run.

The Virginia Company of London made him secretary of the colony on 2 December 1618. He sailed from England in January 1619 with the new governor, George Yeardley, and reached Jamestown on 18 April 1619.

That summer Yeardley called an assembly. Eleven settlements each elected two burgesses, twenty-two men in all. They met in the church at Jamestown, the only building on the island big enough for the governor, his six councillors and the burgesses. Yeardley appointed Pory to be Speaker. Pory was not one of the elected men. He was an officer of the colony given the chair.

They sat for six days, from 30 July to 4 August 1619. In that time they set the rules for how the assembly itself would work, protected colonists' claims to their land, made the Church of England the colony's official church, and passed laws about tobacco, gambling, conduct and dealings with the Native nations around them. One burgess died during the session and the heat was bad enough that the governor and several others fell ill, which is part of why it ended after six days.

Pory then wrote it all up as "The Proceedings of the First General Assembly, July 30, 1619." His report is the reason anyone knows what happened. There is no other record of the meeting.

He left Virginia in the early summer of 1622 aboard the *Discovery*, after the company appointed Christopher Davidson secretary in his place. He died in 1636 and was buried at Sutton Saint Edmunds in Lincolnshire.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Self-rule becomes a habit long before it becomes a demand.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Assemblies, juries, and the Zenger trial" -->
Colonial assemblies held the purse. They voted the taxes and they voted the governor's salary, which gave an elected body real power over a governor the king had sent. Courts sat with juries drawn from the neighbourhood, and a jury could refuse to convict.
In 1735 a New York jury did exactly that, in the trial of the printer John Peter Zenger. The charge was seditious libel, which then meant printing anything that lowered people's opinion of the government. Under that law it made no difference whether the printed thing was true. The jury acquitted him anyway. **The trial changed no law.** New York did not allow truth as a defence in a libel case until the legislature passed a statute on 6 April 1805, seventy years later, and even then only where the thing was printed "with good motive and for justifiable ends." The newspaper is told in `news-communication`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-peter-zenger-government-politics" name="John Peter Zenger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Peter Zenger
> **Who:** A printer jailed for nine months for printing attacks on the governor, and acquitted by a jury that ignored the judge · **When and where:** New York City, November 1734 to August 1735

In 1733 the governor of New York, William Cosby, lost a court case called *Cosby v. Van Dam*. The chief judge, Lewis Morris, had written against him. Cosby removed Morris from the bench. Morris and his friends answered by starting a newspaper, the *New-York Weekly Journal*, and John Peter Zenger printed it. The first issue came out on 5 November 1733. Until then the only paper in the province was the *New York Gazette*, which printed the governor's side.

The *Journal* attacked Cosby week after week. A grand jury refused to indict Zenger in January 1734. Another refused in October. Cosby had copies of the paper burned in public by the hangman.

Zenger was arrested on 17 November 1734. Bail was set at £400, far more than he had, so he stayed in jail. His wife Anna kept the paper coming out while he was locked up.

His two lawyers, James Alexander and William Smith, argued at his arraignment that the court was not lawfully set up. On 16 April 1735 Chief Justice James De Lancey struck both their names off the roll of lawyers. Zenger went to trial with no lawyer of his own.

The trial was on 4 August 1735, on the second floor of City Hall. Andrew Hamilton came up from Philadelphia and took the case for nothing. He tried to prove that what Zenger printed was true. De Lancey stopped him: "The law is clear that you cannot justify a libel." Under the law of seditious libel, truth was no excuse.

So Hamilton spoke to the jury instead, and told them they could decide the law themselves: "I know that they have the right beyond all dispute to determine both the law and the fact." The jury found Zenger not guilty. He was let out of jail the next day.

This did not make truth a defence in libel cases. No judge was bound by it, and the law stayed as it was. What it showed was that a jury of ordinary men could refuse to convict, and that a royal governor could not stop them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-peter-zenger-government-politics" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-hamilton" name="Andrew Hamilton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Hamilton
> **Who:** The Philadelphia lawyer who defended Zenger for free and argued that a jury may judge the law as well as the facts · **When and where:** New York City, 4 August 1735; Philadelphia before and after

Andrew Hamilton was a lawyer in Philadelphia and the Speaker of the Pennsylvania Assembly. He is no relation to Alexander Hamilton, who was born decades later.

He travelled to New York and took Zenger's case without a fee, working with a local lawyer named John Chambers. When the judge refused to let him prove the printed words were true, he built the whole defence on the jury. He told them the case was "not of small or private concern," and that "it is the cause of liberty." He argued that a free country's laws give people "a right to liberty of both exposing and opposing arbitrary power... by speaking and writing truth."

The jury acquitted. The phrase "Philadelphia lawyer," meaning a lawyer clever enough to win a hopeless case, comes from what he did that day.

Back in Pennsylvania he ran the planning of the Pennsylvania State House and pushed to see it finished. That building is now called Independence Hall, and the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution were both argued out inside it.

His birth and death years are not settled in the sources this book could check, so they are not given here.

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The country writes itself down: a declaration, a failed first government, a constitution, and a list of rights.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Declaration, Constitution, and Bill of Rights" -->
The Declaration of Independence, 1776; the Articles of Confederation and their failure; the Constitutional Convention, 1787, and its compromises, including the ones over slavery; ratification and the Federalist–Antifederalist argument; the Naturalization Act of 1790, which limited becoming a citizen to a "free white person" who had lived here two years; the first parties forming while everyone said parties were a bad idea.
The Constitution counted an enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person when working out how many seats a state got in Congress. That is Article I, Section 2. It gave the states with the most enslaved people extra seats and extra electoral votes, and the people being counted had no vote at all. **`slavery-freedom` tells what that did to people; the clause as law is this chapter's.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the Bill of Rights actually got written" -->
The Constitution went into effect without a list of rights in it, and several states had only ratified after being promised one. James Madison introduced his list in the House of Representatives on 8 June 1789. The House passed 17 amendments. The Senate cut and merged them down to 12. Congress sent those 12 to the states on 25 September 1789, and Washington forwarded them on 2 October. Ten of the 12 were ratified on 15 December 1791, and those ten are what people mean by the Bill of Rights.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-madison" name="James Madison" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### James Madison
> **Who:** Argued against a bill of rights, then wrote one and pushed it through Congress · **When and where:** Virginia and New York, 1787 to 1791; born 16 March 1751, died 28 June 1836

Madison was one of the men who wrote the Constitution in Philadelphia in 1787. When people argued afterwards that it needed a list of protected rights added to it, he said no. He thought a written list would be treated as the only rights people had.

He changed his mind. Several states had ratified the Constitution only because they were promised a list of rights. On 8 June 1789 he stood up in the House of Representatives and introduced one. He told the House, "I think we should obtain the confidence of our fellow citizens, in proportion as we fortify the rights of the people against the encroachments of the government."

He wanted the new rules sewn into the body of the Constitution, in the places they belonged. The House disagreed, took Roger Sherman's advice, and voted to stick them on the end instead. That is why the amendments sit at the back of the document rather than inside it.

The House passed 17. The Senate cut and combined them into 12. Congress sent those 12 to the states on 25 September 1789. On 15 December 1791 ten of them had enough states behind them and became law.

Madison enslaved people the whole time he was doing this. About a hundred people were held at his plantation, Montpelier, at any one time, and close to three hundred over the years. He freed none of them in his will. He asked his wife Dolley not to sell them without their consent; she sold some anyway. When his farm income fell he sold sixteen enslaved people to his cousin William Taylor, who owned a sugar plantation and a cotton plantation in Louisiana.

At the Convention he had backed the rule that counted an enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person for working out seats in Congress. He defended it in print afterwards. He also said, often, that slavery went against everything the country said it stood for, and he never put forward a plan that would have ended it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-madison" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-washington-government-politics" name="George Washington" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Washington
> **Who:** The only president ever elected by every single electoral vote, who then set the rule that a president leaves · **When and where:** Federal Hall, New York, 30 April 1789; Philadelphia, 19 September 1796

The electoral votes for the first presidential election were counted on 6 April 1789, more than a month late, because snow had kept members of Congress from getting to New York. Washington had all 69 of them. He is the only person ever chosen unanimously by the Electoral College, and it happened to him twice, in 1789 and again in 1792.

He was sworn in on 30 April 1789 on the second-floor balcony of Federal Hall in New York, by Robert R. Livingston, the Chancellor of New York, with a crowd watching from the street below. Then he went inside and read the first inaugural address to both houses of Congress.

Nothing in the Constitution stopped a president from running again and again. Washington stopped anyway. In 1796 he had a farewell letter printed in a Philadelphia newspaper on 19 September. He never read it aloud. Alexander Hamilton did most of the drafting and Madison had written an earlier version in 1792, but Washington went over it himself.

The sentence that mattered was this: he was telling the country of "the resolution I have formed, to decline being considered among the number of those out of whom a choice is to be made." He would not be a candidate. He left office and went home, and the next president took over without a fight.

He also used the letter to warn against political parties, "the baneful effects of the spirit of party," and against permanent alliances with foreign countries. Parties formed anyway, within a few years, and he was already in one.

His two terms became a rule nobody had passed. It held for 150 years, until Franklin Roosevelt was elected a third time in 1940 and a fourth in 1944. Only then did Congress write it down. The 22nd Amendment became part of the Constitution on 27 February 1951.

Washington enslaved 123 people at Mount Vernon and managed 153 more who legally belonged to his wife's first husband's estate. Those 153 he could not free even if he wanted to. His will of 9 July 1799 freed his valet William Lee at once and ordered the other 122 freed when Martha died. Martha Washington freed them on 1 January 1801. The other 153 were taken back by the Custis estate and split among her grandchildren, which broke families apart. **`slavery-freedom` tells this side of Mount Vernon.**

Every year since 1896 a senator reads the farewell letter aloud in the Senate. It takes 7,641 words. The custom started on 22 February 1862, in the middle of the Civil War.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The courts claim the power to strike down laws, and politics becomes a mass business with rallies, newspapers, and jobs for supporters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Judicial review and Jacksonian democracy" -->
*Marbury v. Madison*, February 1803, and judicial review; the Louisiana Purchase's constitutional questions; the spoils system; the expansion of the vote to white men who owned no property; party conventions and campaign spectacle; the Indian Removal Act, passed 102 to 97 in the House and 28 to 19 in the Senate and signed on 28 May 1830 (**`native-nations` leads on what removal did**); *Worcester v. Georgia*, 1832, and the fact that the Court's ruling was not carried out.
Jackson vetoed the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States on 10 July 1832 and then campaigned on the veto and won. The Senate censured him on 28 March 1834 for taking the government's money out of the Bank. That is the only time the Senate has censured a president, and his own party expunged it from the record in January 1837 (**`money` tells what the Bank was**).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The school-tax fight" -->
Pennsylvania passed a Free Public School Act in 1834 setting up a state system of common schools paid for out of taxes. The next year, petitions signed by thousands of voters asked the legislature to repeal it, because of the tax. Repeal met no opposition at all in the state Senate.
On 11 April 1835 Thaddeus Stevens, then a member of the Pennsylvania House, came back to Harrisburg and argued against repeal. He said people objected to the school tax "not so much on account of its amount, as because it is for the benefit of others and not themselves," and that the same man "cheerfully pays the tax which is necessary to support and punish convicts, but loudly complains of that which goes to prevent his fellow being from becoming a criminal." The repeal collapsed. Stevens later called that speech the most useful thing he ever did (`education` leads on the schools themselves).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-marshall" name="John Marshall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Marshall
> **Who:** The Chief Justice who ruled that a court may throw out a law that breaks the Constitution · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1801 to 1835; *Marbury v. Madison* decided February 1803

John Adams nominated Marshall as Chief Justice of the United States on 20 January 1801, in the last weeks of his presidency. Marshall took his seat on 4 February 1801 and held the job for 34 years, until he died at Philadelphia on 6 July 1835. He heard more than a thousand cases and wrote more than five hundred opinions.

The case that mattered most was small and looked like nothing. William Marbury had been made a justice of the peace in the last days of the Adams presidency, but the paperwork was never delivered to him. The new administration would not hand it over. Marbury sued James Madison, the Secretary of State, to make him do it.

Marshall's Court decided in February 1803 that Marbury was entitled to the job, but that the Supreme Court had no power to order it. The part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 that gave the Court that power went further than the Constitution allowed. So the Court struck that part of the law down.

Marshall wrote the sentence the whole thing turns on: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." A law that goes against the Constitution is void, and when both apply to a case, judges follow the Constitution.

Nothing in the Constitution says the Supreme Court can do this. It says courts decide cases. Marshall reasoned the power out of that, and every American court has used it since. It is called judicial review, and it is why a court can cancel an act of Congress.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-marshall" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-gov" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson
> **Who:** President who used the veto as a weapon, handed out government jobs to his supporters, and signed the law that forced Native nations off their land · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1829 to 1837

Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act on 28 May 1830. It had passed the House by 102 votes to 97 and the Senate by 28 to 19, which means a great many people in Congress voted against it and it went through anyway. The act let the government trade land in the West for Native nations' homelands in the East. What happened to the people who were moved is told in `native-nations`.

In 1832 the Supreme Court decided *Worcester v. Georgia* and held that Georgia's laws had no force inside Cherokee territory. The ruling was not carried out. Schoolbooks often quote Jackson saying, "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it." He never said it. That sentence first turns up in a history book printed in 1864, about twenty years after he died. What he did write, in a letter to a friend in April 1832, was that the Court's decision "has fell still born."

On 10 July 1832 he vetoed the law that would have kept the Second Bank of the United States going, and then made the veto the centre of his re-election campaign and won. Before him, presidents mostly vetoed bills they thought were unconstitutional. He vetoed one he simply disagreed with, which made the veto a political tool.

The Senate censured him on 28 March 1834 for pulling the government's money out of the Bank. It is the only time the Senate has ever censured a president. When his own party got the majority, they expunged it from the record in January 1837.

He also filled government jobs with his own supporters, calling it rotation in office. He argued the work was "so plain and simple" that ordinary people could take turns doing it. A senator from New York, William L. Marcy, gave the practice its nickname when he said "to the victor belong the spoils of the enemy." The spoils system lasted about fifty years.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Constitution is amended to end slavery and promise equal protection — then courts and states hollow the promise out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Amendments, Reconstruction, and Jim Crow" -->
*Dred Scott*, 1857; secession as a constitutional crisis; the 13th Amendment, which the House passed 119 to 56 on 31 January 1865 and which was ratified on 6 December 1865; the 14th and 15th Amendments; Reconstruction governments; the end of Reconstruction in 1877; Jim Crow laws and *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 1896; the Page Act, 1875, and the Chinese Exclusion Act signed 6 May 1882; big-city political machines like Tammany Hall.
Congress passed the Pendleton Act on 16 January 1883 and President Chester A. Arthur signed it. From then on some federal jobs went to whoever passed an exam instead of to whoever had worked on the winning campaign. The push for it came after President James A. Garfield was shot by Charles Guiteau, a man who had wanted a government job and been refused. At the start the new rules covered about 10 percent of the government's 132,000 workers. The other 90 percent were still handed out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law" -->
Massachusetts passed the first compulsory attendance law in 1852. Children aged 8 to 14 had to go to school, the town's school committee had to look into every case of a child who did not, and a family could be fined up to $20. By 1900 thirty-two states had a law like it. Mississippi was the last, in 1918. The Morrill Act of 1862 gave federal land to the states to pay for colleges.
The Reconstruction constitutions in the South put public schools into state law for the first time. South Carolina's convention met at Charleston on 14 January 1868, with Black men voting for delegates for the first time in that state and three-fifths of the delegates Black. Article X of the constitution they wrote required a system of free public schools "free and open to all the children and youths of the State, without regard to race or color." It was not actually carried out for decades.
In *Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education*, decided 18 December 1899, the Supreme Court let a Georgia county close its only high school for Black students and keep funding the high schools for white students. The families who sued asked the court to stop the county spending school money that way, or to stop taxing them for schools their children could not enter. The Court said no, treating the closure as a money decision. The opinion was written by Justice John Marshall Harlan, the one justice who had dissented in *Plessy v. Ferguson* three years earlier (`education` leads on the schools).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="abraham-lincoln" name="Abraham Lincoln" movie="Lincoln (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abraham Lincoln
> **Who:** The president who suspended a basic legal right during the Civil War, was told by the Chief Justice that he had no power to do it, and did it anyway · **When and where:** Washington and Baltimore, April to July 1861
> **Movie:** Lincoln (2012) — about the fight to pass the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865, not about the events below.

Habeas corpus is the right to be taken before a judge who decides whether the government may go on holding you. Without it, the government can lock a person up and keep them there without ever explaining why to a court. The Constitution allows it to be suspended in a rebellion or an invasion, but it does not say who is allowed to do the suspending.

On 25 April 1861, two weeks into the Civil War, Lincoln wrote to General Winfield Scott approving "the most prompt, and efficient means" against Marylanders arming against the government, including suspending the writ. On 27 April he authorised Scott to suspend it along the routes his troops used between Philadelphia and Washington. Nobody in the federal government had suspended it since the War of 1812.

At two in the morning on 25 May 1861, soldiers came to the house of John Merryman, a planter in Baltimore County, and took him to Fort McHenry. His lawyers went to Chief Justice Roger Taney, who also sat as a judge in Maryland. Taney ordered the fort's commander to bring Merryman to court. The commander wrote back refusing. A deputy marshal carried a contempt order to the gate of the fort and a guard would not let him through.

On 28 May, in front of a packed room in Baltimore, Taney ruled that the president had no power at all to suspend habeas corpus, and that Merryman had to be released. Then he issued no order to make it happen, and said so.

Taney was 84. He was also the judge who had written *Dred Scott* four years earlier, holding that Black Americans, free or enslaved, could not be citizens. Very few people in 1861 saw him as a neutral figure.

Lincoln did not answer him directly. On 4 July 1861 he explained himself to Congress instead. He said the suspension had "purposely been exercised but very sparingly," and then put the question this way: "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" His argument was that the Constitution does not say who may suspend the writ, and that the framers cannot have meant the country should wait for Congress to be called together while a rebellion ran on.

Congress settled it in March 1863 by passing a law letting the president suspend the writ for the rest of the war. So the argument ended with a statute, not with a court decision.

On 31 January 1865 the House passed the Thirteenth Amendment, ending slavery, by 119 votes to 56. Lincoln signed the resolution the next day, which no president is required to do. `slavery-freedom` tells what that amendment did; `war` tells the fighting.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-rainey" name="Joseph Hayne Rainey" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Hayne Rainey
> **Who:** Born to enslaved parents, forced to build Confederate defences, and later the first Black man to sit in the U.S. House and the first to run its floor · **When and where:** Georgetown, South Carolina, and Washington, D.C.; born 21 June 1832, sworn in 12 December 1870, died 2 August 1887

Rainey's parents were enslaved. His father, Edward Rainey, was allowed to work as a barber, and he used what he earned to buy his family out of slavery in the early 1840s.

During the Civil War the Confederate army made Rainey work on the defences around Charleston, and put him to work as a steward on a ship running the blockade. In 1862 he and his wife got away to Bermuda, where he cut hair and she made dresses. They came back in 1866.

He won a full term to Congress on 19 October 1870 with 63 percent of the vote, and a special election on 8 November for the rest of that Congress with 86 percent. He was sworn in on 12 December 1870, the first African American ever to sit in the House of Representatives.

On 1 April 1871 he spoke for the Ku Klux Klan Act, the law aimed at the men who were murdering Black voters and their allies in the South. He argued the Constitution owed "protection to the humblest citizen, without regard to rank." He worked for the Civil Rights Act of 1875 as well, and pushed hardest on schools; he wanted Black and white children in the same classrooms and said it would "annihilate" prejudice.

On 29 April 1874 he took the Speaker's chair and ran the House, the first African American to do it.

He served more than eight years, on the committees for Freedmen's Affairs, Indian Affairs and Invalid Pensions. He lost in 1878, by 62 percent to 38, and left on 3 March 1879. No Black member of Congress served longer than he had until the 1950s.

He was the first of fourteen African American Representatives elected during Reconstruction. After Reconstruction ended, the number of Black members of Congress fell to nothing and stayed at or near nothing for the next seventy years. That did not happen by accident, and `rights-movements` tells how it was done.

Rainey worked for the Treasury Department in South Carolina, then ran a brokerage and banking business in Washington, then went home to Georgetown and died there in 1887.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Government grows: amendments, agencies, and a presidency that becomes the center of national life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Progressive amendments, the New Deal, and quotas" -->
Two amendments were added in 1913. The 16th let Congress tax incomes without dividing the money up among the states by population; it was ratified on 3 February 1913. Congress had tried an income tax in 1894 and the Supreme Court had struck it down by five votes to four. The 17th, ratified on 8 April 1913, took the choosing of senators away from state legislatures and gave it to voters. Augustus Bacon of Georgia was the first senator elected that way, on 15 July 1913.
Then Prohibition (18th, 1919) and repeal (21st, 1933); women's suffrage (19th, 1920, **`rights-movements` leads**); the Emergency Quota Act, 1921, and the Immigration Act of 1924; the Indian Citizenship Act, 1924; the New Deal and the fight with the Supreme Court; the Displaced Persons Act, 1948; radio and then television changing how campaigns were run.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law" -->
Oregon passed a law in 1922 ordering every child aged 8 to 16 into the public school in their district. A convent school and a military academy sued. On 1 June 1925 the Supreme Court struck the law down in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, holding that parents have the right to decide how their children are educated. Justice James McReynolds wrote the sentence the rest of American school law is built on: "The child is not the mere creature of the state." A state may make a child be educated. It may not make every child go to a government school. Every private school, church school and homeschool in this book stands on that ruling.
Tennessee's governor Austin Peay signed the Butler Act on 21 March 1925, making it illegal to teach in a state school "any theory that denies the story of Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible." Civic leaders in Dayton set up a test case, and John T. Scopes, a coach and science teacher, agreed to be the defendant. The trial opened on 10 July 1925 and he was found guilty on 21 July and fined $100. In January 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court threw the conviction out on a technicality: the state constitution said a jury, not a judge, had to set any fine over $50. The law itself was never ruled on, and it stayed on the books until 1967 (`religion` and `education` lead).
Also in this era: the school district settles into place as a small unit of government with its own elected board, and the property tax becomes the way American schools are paid for. That decision is why a school's money depends on what the houses around it are worth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="franklin-d-roosevelt" name="Franklin D. Roosevelt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Franklin D. Roosevelt
> **Who:** The president who lost in the Supreme Court and then tried to change the size of the Court · **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1933 to 1945; the court plan sent to Congress 5 February 1937

Roosevelt came in during the Depression and Congress passed his laws fast. The Supreme Court then began striking them down. On 27 May 1935, in a case about a poultry business called Schechter, the Court killed the heart of the National Industrial Recovery Act. It held that Congress had given the president powers Congress was not allowed to give away.

On 5 February 1937 Roosevelt sent Congress a plan to reorganise the federal courts. Buried in it was this: for every justice over the age of 70 who had not retired, the president could appoint one more. The Supreme Court could have gone from nine justices to fifteen, and Roosevelt would have picked the extra six.

Nothing in the Constitution fixes the number of justices. Congress sets it, and Congress had changed it before. What was new was a president asking for the change in order to win the cases he had been losing.

It failed. On 14 June 1937 the Senate Judiciary Committee, run by his own party, reported against it, calling the plan "needless, futile, and utterly dangerous" and urging the Senate to reject it so hard that nothing like it would ever be tried again. On 22 July 1937 the Senate rejected it, 70 votes to 20.

Meanwhile the Court had started upholding laws like the ones it used to strike down. On 29 March 1937 it upheld a minimum wage for women and girls in Washington State. People at the time said the threat had frightened the justices into changing their minds. Historians still argue about whether that is what happened. The dates line up; whether one caused the other is not settled.

Roosevelt was elected four times, in 1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944, breaking the rule Washington had started. He is why the two-term limit is now written into the Constitution as the 22nd Amendment.

He also signed Executive Order 9066 on 19 February 1942. It led to more than 120,000 people of Japanese descent being removed from their homes and held in camps. Two thirds of them were United States citizens. **`rights-movements` tells what happened to them.**

### George Washington Plunkitt
> **Who:** A New York machine politician who sat on a shoeshine stand outside the courthouse and explained to a reporter, without embarrassment, exactly how he made his money and kept his votes · **When and where:** the Fifteenth Assembly District, New York City; his talks printed in 1905; died 1924

Plunkitt was a district leader for Tammany Hall, the organisation that ran New York politics for most of a century. He was also a state senator and an assemblyman. He started poor and ended rich.

A newspaperman named William L. Riordon wrote down what Plunkitt said and published it in 1905 as *Plunkitt of Tammany Hall*. Plunkitt gave the talks from a shoeshine stand outside the New York County Court House, which is where he did much of his business. The book is not a recording. Riordon collected and shaped the talk, so read it as a reporter's version of a man who enjoyed being quoted.

Plunkitt divided graft into two kinds. Dishonest graft, he said, meant squeezing money out of gamblers and illegal businesses, and he claimed he never did it. Honest graft meant finding out where the city was going to build something, buying that land first, and selling it back at a profit. He did not think this needed defending. "I seen my opportunities and I took 'em," he said, and that sentence is on his gravestone.

The rest of what he described is the actual job. When a building burned at night, Plunkitt and his captains got there before the relief agencies and found the burned-out families a place to sleep and clothes to wear. He knew employers all over the city and got people hired. His men found families in trouble first, before any charity did. He was clear about why: those people remembered him on election day, and the charities could not deliver votes.

That was how a city machine worked. It was a welfare system paid for out of politics, and the payment was your vote. It also meant the city's contracts, jobs and pavements went to whoever was inside the organisation, and nothing at all went to whoever was outside it.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Supreme Court, Congress and the presidency each remake American life in these fifty years, and then a president resigns rather than be put on trial by the Senate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Five amendments in twenty years" -->
The 22nd Amendment, ratified 27 February 1951, says no person may be elected president more than twice. Washington had stopped at two terms by choice and Franklin Roosevelt had won four; now it is a rule.
The 23rd, ratified 29 March 1961, gave the District of Columbia presidential electors. It gave the District no senators and no voting member of the House, and it still has none.
The 24th, ratified 23 January 1964, banned the poll tax. Its words cover federal elections only: president, vice president, senator, representative. A state could still charge a fee to vote in its own elections until *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections*, decided 24 March 1966, held that a state breaks the Fourteenth Amendment when it makes "the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard." Virginia's tax had been up to $1.50 a year.
The 25th, ratified 10 February 1967, wrote down what happens when a president dies, resigns or is removed, and made a way to fill an empty vice presidency: the president nominates and both houses vote.
The 26th, ratified 1 July 1971, gave the vote to eighteen-year-olds. No amendment has ever been ratified faster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One person, one vote" -->
The phrase comes from Justice William O. Douglas in *Gray v. Sanders*, decided 18 March 1963, which struck down Georgia's county unit system: "The conception of political equality from the Declaration of Independence, to Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, to the Fifteenth, Seventeenth, and Nineteenth Amendments can mean only one thing — one person, one vote."
*Reynolds v. Sims*, decided 15 June 1964, applied the rule to state legislatures. Alabama had not redrawn its districts since 1901, using the 1900 census. By 1960 about a quarter of Alabamians could elect a majority of either house, and the largest gaps between districts ran about 41 to 1 in the state senate. There was no march for this ruling. It is a rule about arithmetic in district lines, and it forced almost every state legislature in the country to redraw itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Warren Court and the police station" -->
*Brown v. Board of Education*, 17 May 1954, held segregated schooling unconstitutional and said nothing about how to end it. The Warren Court's other best-known ruling is *Miranda v. Arizona*, decided 13 June 1966. Ernesto Miranda was arrested at his home in Phoenix on 13 March 1963 and questioned in a police room. The officers admitted at trial that nobody told him he could have a lawyer with him. He signed a confession after about two hours. The Court ruled that before questioning, police must tell a person in custody that he can stay silent, that what he says can be used against him, that he can have a lawyer, and that if he cannot pay for one the court will appoint one. If he asks for those rights, the questioning stops. **`crime-justice` tells what this changed inside police work.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the Civil Rights Act of 1964 actually passed" -->
Southern senators led by Richard Russell of Georgia held the Senate floor for sixty working days, including seven Saturdays. On 10 June 1964 the Senate voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the motion that cuts off debate. It was the first time the Senate had ever cut off debate on a civil rights bill. Senator Clair Engle of California had a brain tumour and could not speak; when his name was called he lifted a crippled arm and pointed to his eye to vote aye. The Senate passed the bill 73 to 27 on 19 June and President Johnson signed it on 2 July 1964.
The Act runs to ten titles. Title II covers restaurants, hotels, theatres and the like: "All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, and privileges ... of any place of public accommodation." Title VI bars discrimination in anything paid for with federal money, and that is the provision that later let Washington cut off money to segregated school districts. Title VII bars job discrimination and set up the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.
The Voting Rights Act, signed 6 August 1965, worked a different way. Section 4 named the places it covered by a formula: a state or county was covered if it had used a literacy test or a similar device on 1 November 1964 and fewer than half its voting-age residents were registered or had voted in the 1964 election. Section 5 then required those places to get permission from Washington before changing any voting rule at all. **`rights-movements` leads on the campaign that forced both laws, and owns what happened to the Voting Rights Act after 2013.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War powers and Watergate" -->
Congress passed the War Powers Resolution, President Nixon vetoed it on 24 October 1973, and on 7 November 1973 the House voted 284 to 135 and the Senate 75 to 18 to pass it over his veto. It says a president who sends armed forces into fighting without a declaration of war must report to Congress in writing within 48 hours, and must end the operation within sixty days unless Congress declares war, votes to authorise it, or extends the time.
In *United States v. Nixon*, decided 24 July 1974 by eight votes to none, the Supreme Court ordered the president to hand over tape recordings of his own conversations to a criminal prosecutor. The Court agreed that a president's talks are private by presumption, then held the privilege is not absolute: "The generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."
The House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment: obstruction of justice on 27 July 1974, abuse of power on 29 July, contempt of Congress on 30 July. The full House never voted on them.
Nixon signed a one-sentence letter of resignation on the morning of 9 August 1974. It is addressed to the Secretary of State, not to Congress and not to the country, because a law passed in 1792 says that is where a president's resignation is filed. It took effect when Secretary of State Henry Kissinger initialled it at 11:35 a.m. Gerald Ford took the oath at noon and said: "My fellow Americans, our long national nightmare is over. Our Constitution works; our great Republic is a government of laws and not of men. Here the people rule." He also said, plainly, "I am acutely aware that you have not elected me as your President by your ballots."
That last sentence was exact. Vice President Spiro Agnew had resigned in October 1973 and Ford was picked under the 25th Amendment, nominated by the president and confirmed by both houses. When Ford became president he chose Nelson Rockefeller the same way. For about two and a half years the United States had a president and a vice president whom no voter had chosen for either job. **`news-communication` tells the reporting; `crime-justice` tells the break-in and the prosecutions.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law becomes a whole body of law" -->
*Brown* had no deadline in it. The remedy came a year later, in *Brown v. Board of Education* II, decided 31 May 1955, which sent the cases back to district judges and told them to admit the children "with all deliberate speed." No date appears anywhere in the order. A school board that moved slowly was not obviously disobeying it, and that is why enforcement took twenty years of further orders.
Two rulings took organised prayer and Bible reading out of the public school. In *Engel v. Vitale*, 25 June 1962, the Court struck down a prayer the New York State Board of Regents had written for schools to say each morning. Saying it was voluntary for any child. It did not matter. Justice Hugo Black wrote that the ban on establishing religion "must at least mean that in this country it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group." In *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 17 June 1963, the Court struck down opening the school day with Bible readings in Pennsylvania and reciting the Lord's Prayer together in the Baltimore schools.
Federal money entered schools on a large scale with the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, signed 11 April 1965. Johnson signed it outside the one-room school near Stonewall, Texas that he had attended at the age of four, with his first teacher, Kate Deadrich Loney, sitting beside him. Its Title I sends money to school districts according to how many children in them come from low-income families, and attaches conditions to the money.
In *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, decided 21 March 1973 by five votes to four, the Court held that education is not a fundamental right under the Constitution. The record compared two districts in one city in 1967-68: Edgewood spent $356 a pupil, Alamo Heights $594. Texas only had to be rational, and keeping schools under local control counted as rational. After that the fight over unequal school money moved into state courts under state constitutions, where it still is.
In *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, 15 May 1972, three Amish fathers — Jonas Yoder, Wallace Miller and Adin Yutzy — were each fined $5 for not sending children to high school after the eighth grade. The Court ruled the state could not make them.
In *Ingraham v. Wright*, decided 19 April 1977 by five votes to four, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment "was designed to protect those convicted of crimes" and does not reach school discipline, and that a school need not give a child a hearing before hitting him. James Ingraham, a pupil at Drew Junior High in Dade County, Florida, had been held over a table in the principal's office in October 1970 and hit more than twenty times with a wooden paddle, for being slow to follow an instruction. The beating raised a hematoma, a pocket of blood under the skin, that needed a doctor and kept him out of school for days. School policy allowed one to five strokes. Because the Constitution left this alone, paddling in schools ended one state legislature at a time rather than all at once.
Title IX, 1972, and the education act for disabled children, 1975, are statutes, not rulings. Homeschooling became lawful across the country state by state through the 1980s and early 1990s. Michigan is usually named last, for *People v. DeJonge*, decided 25 May 1993. The same court on the same day ruled against a family with no religious objection, in *People v. Bennett*, so what fell that day was the rule as applied to families with a religious objection. Michigan's legislature wrote homeschooling into its school-attendance law in 1995, effective 1 July 1996. Minnesota passed the first charter school law in 1991; it allowed eight schools and called them outcome-based schools, and City Academy in St. Paul opened in 1992. **`education` tells what changed inside schools; `rights-movements` tells the campaigns; `religion` tells the prayer rulings as faith.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An impeachment at the end of the century" -->
The House impeached President Bill Clinton on 19 December 1998. The Senate voted on 12 February 1999 and acquitted him: 45 guilty to 55 not guilty on the first article, 50 to 50 on the second. Conviction takes two thirds of the Senate, which is 67 votes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Thurgood Marshall
> **Who:** The lawyer who won 29 of the 32 cases he argued in the Supreme Court, and later sat on it as its first Black justice · **When and where:** Baltimore, New York and Washington, D.C., 1908 to 1993

Thurgood Marshall was born in Baltimore on 2 July 1908. He wanted to go to the University of Maryland law school and it would not take him, because he was Black. He went to Howard University law school instead and graduated first in his class in 1933. One of his early cases forced that same Maryland law school to admit Black students.

He argued 32 cases in the Supreme Court and won 29. Among them: *Smith v. Allwright* in 1944, which ended the whites-only primary election in Texas; *Morgan v. Virginia* in 1946, on segregated interstate buses; *Sweatt v. Painter* in 1950, on the University of Texas law school; and *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954. From 1940 to 1961 he ran the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund.

Then he held three offices inside the federal government itself, and each one does a different job.

President Kennedy put him on the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit on 5 October 1961 by recess appointment, which is a president filling a seat while the Senate is away. A recess appointment runs out unless the Senate confirms it. Southern senators fought his, and the Senate did not confirm him until 11 September 1962, eleven months later.

He resigned that judgeship on 23 August 1965 to become Solicitor General of the United States. That officer argues the government's own cases in the Supreme Court. He won 14 of the 19 he argued.

President Johnson nominated him to the Supreme Court on 13 June 1967, to the seat Tom C. Clark had left. The Senate confirmed him on 30 August 1967 and he took the judicial oath on 2 October 1967, the first Black justice. He stepped back from active service on 1 October 1991 and died on 24 January 1993.

After his death Chief Justice William Rehnquist said this: "The great majority of Supreme Court Justices are almost always remembered for their contributions to constitutional law as a member of this Court. Justice Marshall, however, is unique because his contributions to constitutional law before becoming a member of the Court were so significant."

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lyndon-b-johnson" name="Lyndon B. Johnson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lyndon B. Johnson
> **Who:** A Senate leader who counted votes for a living, and then a president who used the same skill on the civil rights laws · **When and where:** Texas and Washington, D.C., 1948 to 1969

Lyndon Johnson reached the Senate in 1948 by 87 votes. That was the final margin in the Texas Democratic primary, and people called him Landslide Lyndon for the rest of his life.

He became Democratic whip in 1951, minority leader in 1953 and majority leader from 1955 to 1961. He was 45 when his party made him leader, still in his first term, and the youngest party leader the Senate had ever had.

The Senate's own historians describe how he worked. He would stand close to a senator's face and pull clippings, memos and statistics out of his pockets, and his tone "could be supplication, accusation, cajolery, exuberance, scorn, tears, complaint, the hint of threat." Running a majority of one or two, he kept his own side together and went looking for Republican votes. While he was away recovering from a heart attack his secretary Bobby Baker held bills back; when he came back he called up a run of them at once and passed them by unanimous consent, so everyone could see who was in charge.

In 1957 he pushed through the first civil rights law since Reconstruction. The Senate's history says he wanted the fact of passing it but feared a long filibuster would split his party, so he took the enforcement provisions out of the bill. It passed. It did very little.

As president he used the same arithmetic on bills that did a great deal. He signed the Civil Rights Act on 2 July 1964, after the first successful cloture vote on a civil rights bill in the Senate's history. He signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965, and this time the enforcement machinery stayed in: federal examiners who could register voters themselves, and the rule that covered states had to ask Washington before changing a voting rule.

On 11 April 1965 he signed the Elementary and Secondary Education Act at the one-room school he had attended near Stonewall, Texas, and said, "no law I have signed or will ever sign means more to the future of America."

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
How elections are run becomes one of the country's main political fights, and the courts sit at the centre of nearly every one of them. This section is written from records available on 10 September 2026, and every number in it carries the year it describes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bush v. Gore, 2000" -->
The 2000 presidential election came down to Florida, and Florida came down to how a machine had read a punched card. The Florida Supreme Court ordered hand recounts, including about 9,000 Miami-Dade ballots the machines had read as blank. The United States Supreme Court decided *Bush v. Gore* on 12 December 2000 in an unsigned opinion. Seven justices saw a constitutional problem: different counties were using different rules to decide what a ballot meant, so two identical ballots could be counted differently depending on where they were cast. Five justices then voted to stop the recount rather than send it back to be fixed, because there was no time left. Four dissented.
The Court wrote that the ruling covered this case and no other: "Our consideration is limited to the present circumstances, for the problem of equal protection in election processes generally presents many complexities."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money, and maps" -->
The Supreme Court decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* on 21 January 2010 by five votes to four. A non-profit corporation had made a film attacking Hillary Clinton and wanted to advertise it inside a period when federal law barred corporate election spending. The Court held that "the Government may not suppress political speech based on the speaker's corporate identity," and overruled a 1990 decision and part of a 2003 one. **`big-business` tells what the spending did after that; the ruling is this chapter's.**
The Court decided *Rucho v. Common Cause* on 27 June 2019, also five to four. Two maps were before it: North Carolina's congressional map of 2016, drawn by Republicans, and Maryland's of 2011, drawn by Democrats. The ruling was that "partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts." Justice Elena Kagan, dissenting: "For the first time ever, this Court refuses to remedy a constitutional violation because it thinks the task beyond judicial capabilities."
Since 2019 a voter who believes a map was drawn to favour one party cannot bring that complaint to a federal court. State courts, state constitutions and Congress are all still open. A map drawn by race is a different question, decided under the Voting Rights Act, and **`rights-movements` owns that thread and what the Supreme Court did to it in 2013 and again in 2026.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counting the votes, 6 January 2021" -->
Every four years the House and Senate meet in the House chamber to count the electoral votes. The Vice President presides, tellers open the states' certificates and read them out, and the presiding officer announces the result. Until 2022 a written objection to a state's votes needed only one member of each chamber to sign it; then the two houses separate, debate, and vote.
Two objections were made on 6 January 2021. The Senate rejected the objection to Arizona's votes 6 to 93 and the House rejected it 121 to 303. The Senate rejected the objection to Pennsylvania's 7 to 92 and the House rejected it 138 to 282. Congress finished the count and declared the result. No joint session has thrown out a state's electoral votes since the late nineteenth century.
The Capitol was attacked while that session was under way. The Government Accountability Office, reporting in 2022, found that over about seven hours roughly 140 police officers were assaulted, about 80 of them United States Capitol Police; that about 114 Capitol Police officers reported injuries; and that the damage came to about $1.5 million. The same reports found that Capitol Police planning for the day "did not reflect the potential for extreme violence aimed at the Capitol," although the force had information that protesters could be armed and were planning to target Congress.
Congress then changed the rules. The Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act became law on 29 December 2022 as part of a larger act. It raised the number of signatures needed to object from one member of each chamber to one fifth of each, about 87 representatives and 20 senators. It named the state's governor, or whichever official state law names, as the only one who may certify a state's electors, and required electors to be appointed under laws passed before election day. And it put the Vice President's role into the statute in plain words: "The President of the Senate shall have no power to solely determine, accept, reject, or otherwise adjudicate or resolve disputes over the proper certificate of ascertainment of appointment of electors, the validity of electors, or the votes of electors."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Impeached twice, convicted neither time" -->
Conviction in the Senate takes two thirds, which is 67 votes. No president has ever been convicted.
President Donald Trump was tried by the Senate on 5 February 2020 and acquitted: 48 guilty to 52 not guilty on the first article, 47 to 53 on the second. He was impeached again by the House on 13 January 2021 and tried after he had left office. On 13 February 2021 the Senate voted 57 guilty to 43 not guilty, ten short of conviction. Seven Republican senators voted guilty: Richard Burr, Bill Cassidy, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Mitt Romney, Ben Sasse and Pat Toomey.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who does not get a vote" -->
The 2020 census counted 689,545 people living in the District of Columbia. That is more than Wyoming, with 576,851, and more than Vermont, with 643,077. Each of those states sends two senators and a voting representative to Congress. The District sends a delegate who may not vote on the House floor, and no senators at all. It has three presidential electors, and only because of the 23rd Amendment.
The same census counted 3,285,874 people in Puerto Rico, more than lived in 21 of the 50 states. They are United States citizens. While they live there they cannot vote for president, and their Resident Commissioner in the House may not vote on the floor. Guam had 153,836 people, the United States Virgin Islands 87,146, American Samoa 49,710 and the Northern Mariana Islands 47,329 — the four island areas together just under 339,000 in 2020.
The Census Bureau states the rule in its own words: "The populations of the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico are excluded from the apportionment population because they do not have voting seats in Congress." **`america-world` carries how these places became American in the first place.**
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools stay in court" -->
The No Child Left Behind Act of 2001 was signed on 8 January 2002. Note the two years: it is named for 2001 and became law in January 2002. It was not a new law but a rewrite of the 1965 act, and it kept the 1965 machinery of federal money with conditions attached. It required yearly testing and set targets schools had to hit. Congress replaced it with the Every Student Succeeds Act on 10 December 2015, which kept the standards and the yearly tests and handed the states more say over what to do about the results. Federal money for all the programmes in that law came to $29.0 billion in the 2023 budget year.
Four rulings moved public money toward religious schools. *Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, 27 June 2002, upheld Cleveland's school vouchers, worth up to $2,250 a year for low-income families, even though 82 per cent of the private schools taking part were religious. The Court said an aid programme survives if it is neutral about religion and the money reaches a religious school only through a family's own choice. Then the question turned around. *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia v. Comer*, 26 June 2017, held that Missouri could not refuse a church preschool a playground-surfacing grant it would have given anyone else. *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, 30 June 2020, decided five to four, struck down Montana's rule keeping scholarship money out of religious schools. *Carson v. Makin*, 21 June 2022, held that Maine, which pays tuition for children in towns with no secondary school, may not require the school to be nonsectarian.
In *Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1*, decided 28 June 2007, the Court struck down two plans that used a child's race to decide which school the child attended: Seattle sorted pupils as white or nonwhite as a tiebreaker for high school places, and Jefferson County, Kentucky sorted them as black or other for elementary assignments. The best-known sentence in the case, "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race," was joined by four justices, not five. Justice Anthony Kennedy was the fifth vote and would not join it. He wrote that a district may still "consider the racial makeup of schools and ... adopt general policies to encourage a diverse student body," by choosing where to put new schools, by drawing attendance zones, by putting special programmes in particular places, and by recruiting. So the plain rule after 2007 is that a district may try to mix its schools and may not decide one child's school by that child's race.
State laws on curriculum, on which books a school library may hold, and on school sport have kept school boards in court since. School board elections, which used to draw few voters, have become contested politics. **`education` tells what all of this felt like inside a school.**

### Wandrea ArShaye "Shaye" Moss
> **Who:** A Fulton County election worker who was publicly accused of a crime she had not committed, and told a committee of Congress under oath what followed · **When and where:** Atlanta, Georgia, 2017 to 2022; sworn testimony in Washington, D.C., 21 June 2022

Shaye Moss worked in the Department of Registration and Elections in Fulton County, Georgia, from 2017 until 2022. She handled voter applications and absentee-ballot requests and helped process the count. Her mother, Ruby Freeman, worked as a temporary election worker in the same county for the 2020 election.

Moss told the House committee what she liked about the job. "I have always been told by my grandmother how important it is to vote and how people before me, a lot of people, older people in my family did not have that right." Older voters were her favourite part of it: "they like to call, they like to talk to you, they like to get my card, they like to know that every election I am here." She sent absentee ballots to elderly and disabled voters, and once drove an application to a woman in hospital.

About five million Georgians voted in 2020. The state then hand-counted every one of those ballots in an audit that ran from 11 to 19 November. Workers sorted 41,881 batches by hand. In no county did the result move by more than 0.73 per cent. The Secretary of State certified the count on 20 November, the governor certified it, and after a machine recount the state certified it again on 7 December.

On 3 December 2020 a lawyer working for the Trump campaign played clips of a security video from the State Farm Arena at a Georgia Senate hearing and said election workers there had pulled 18,000 hidden ballots from suitcases under a tablecloth. The two women in the video were Freeman and Moss.

Georgia's state election officials and the Georgia Bureau of Investigation watched the whole tape, which ran for hours, and interviewed everyone who had been in the room. They found no evidence at all for the claim. In the early hours of 4 December the state's Voting Implementation Manager posted that the video "Shows normal ballot processing." The containers held ballots that had been processed earlier that night, and no observer had been told to leave.

Asked at the hearing what her mother had actually handed her in the video, Moss answered: "A ginger mint."

The accusation kept going anyway. On 10 December 2020 Rudolph Giuliani told a Georgia state house hearing that the two women were "quite obviously surreptitiously passing around USB ports as if they are vials of heroin or cocaine," and said their homes and workplaces should have been searched. On 2 January 2021 President Trump telephoned Georgia's Secretary of State, Brad Raffensperger, and named Ruby Freeman again and again, calling her "a vote scammer, a professional vote scammer and hustler." Raffensperger answered him on that call: "You're talking about the State Farm video. And I think it's extremely unfortunate that Rudy Giuliani or his people, they sliced and diced that video and took it out of context."

Moss described the messages that arrived. There were "a lot of threats, wishing death upon me, telling me that, you know, I will be in jail with my mother, and saying things like, 'Be glad it's 2020 and not 1920.'" Many were racist. Strangers went to her grandmother's house and pushed their way in, saying they had come to make a citizen's arrest and needed to find Moss and her mother. The FBI told Freeman to leave her home around the week of 6 January 2021, and she stayed away about two months. "I've lost my name," Freeman said, "and I've lost my reputation." Moss said her own life had turned upside down: she stopped handing out her business cards, stopped transferring calls, stopped going to the shops.

Freeman and Moss sued Giuliani in a federal court in Washington, D.C. in December 2021 for defamation. On 30 August 2023 Chief Judge Beryl Howell entered judgment against him on all three of their claims as a punishment for refusing to hand over evidence, writing that he had "thwarted plaintiffs Ruby Freeman and Wandrea' ArShaye Moss's procedural rights to obtain any meaningful discovery in this case." A jury decided in December 2023 what he had to pay them. He asked the judge to throw the verdict out or change it, and on 15 April 2024 she refused.

Asked how many of the permanent election workers and supervisors visible in that video were still doing the job, Moss answered: "There is no permanent election worker or supervisor in that video that is still there." She had left too.
