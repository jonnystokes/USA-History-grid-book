## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People in this land got sick before 1500, but not from the diseases that later killed most of them. Smallpox, measles, influenza, mumps, chickenpox, cholera, typhus, malaria, yellow fever and bubonic plague had never reached the Americas. Nobody here wrote about illness before 1500. What is known comes from bones and teeth that archaeologists have studied.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the bones show" -->
- Tuberculosis was here. Tuberculosis is a disease caused by bacteria. It most often infects the lungs, and it can also destroy bone in the spine. Skeletons with tuberculosis damage appear after about the year 900, mostly at inland sites in the middle of the continent and the Southwest (Vågene and others, Nature Communications, 2022).
- Diseases in the same family as syphilis were here too. Yaws and bejel spread by skin contact, not only by sex, and they leave swollen, pitted marks on the leg bones. Ancient DNA studied in 2025 points to the bacteria behind all of these diseases starting in the Americas (Barquera and others, Nature, 2025).
- Other illnesses present, each probably rare in any one town: rabies, whooping cough, polio, hepatitis, herpes, tularemia, and gut infections from giardia and amoebas (Martin and Goodman, 2002).
- Farming changed people's health. At Dickson Mounds in Illinois, people in the 1100s went from hunting and gathering to growing mostly maize (corn). Maize has little iron the body can use. Signs of anemia rose sharply. Anemia means too few red blood cells, which leaves a person weak and tired. In a skull it shows as porous, spongy patches on the top of the head and the roofs of the eye sockets, where the marrow swelled to make more blood. Bone infections among the same people became nearly four times as severe (Lallo and others, 1977, reported in Martin and Goodman, 2002).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the crowd diseases were missing" -->
- Diseases like measles pass from person to person for less than two weeks, then the sick person dies or becomes immune. To keep going, measles needs a steady supply of new babies who have never had it. One scholar estimated that takes a population of 200,000 to 300,000 people living in contact. Another put it at 500,000 (Newson, British Academy, 1993).
- In small, spread-out towns, a disease like that infects everyone who can catch it and then disappears, because no one is left to infect. The diseases that stay in small towns are slow ones, like the yaws family and gut parasites (Newson, 1993).
- So when the Old World diseases arrived after 1492, nobody here had any protection. The historian Alfred Crosby called these "virgin soil epidemics": everyone in a town could be infected at the same time (Martin and Goodman, 2002, citing Crosby, 1976).
- How many people lived north of Mexico in 1492 is disputed. Scholars' estimates run from about 1 million (Steward, 1949) to about 18 million (Dobyns, 1983). Others fall between: about 1.85 million (Ubelaker, 1976) and about 3.8 million (Denevan, 1992). The size of the loss that followed depends on which starting number is right.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish and English expeditions brought new diseases into this land, and Native people began dying of them in large numbers. Three eyewitnesses wrote down what they saw: a Spanish castaway on the Texas coast in 1528, a Portuguese man in Hernando de Soto's army in 1540, and an English scientist on the Carolina coast in 1585 and 1586. None of the three could name the disease they saw.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Texas coast, 1528 and 1529" -->
- In November 1528 about 80 shipwrecked Spanish men of the Narváez expedition landed on an island on the Texas coast, on or near Galveston Island. Cabeza de Vaca, one of them, named the people living there the Capoque and the Han. Within months only 15 of the 80 were alive. Five Spaniards camped on the coast ate the bodies of their companions as each one died, and the islanders were angry when they learned of it.
- That winter a disease of the bowels killed half of the island's people, Cabeza de Vaca wrote. He did not name the disease. The islanders believed the Spaniards had caused the deaths and planned to kill the survivors. The Native man who had charge of Cabeza de Vaca talked them out of it. He pointed out that the Spaniards had not been able to save their own men from dying.
- The same islanders told the Spaniards to act as healers and gave them no food until they did. Cabeza de Vaca described how the island's own healers worked. A healer cut the skin over the place that hurt and sucked the cut. Healers also burned the skin at the painful spot on purpose, a treatment called cautery, which Cabeza de Vaca tried on himself and said helped him, and then blew on it. The Spaniards added the sign of the cross and Christian prayers.
- Later, on the long walk west (the exact route and year are uncertain), people brought Cabeza de Vaca a man with an arrowhead lodged in his chest above the heart. Cabeza de Vaca cut open the chest with his knife, pulled the arrowhead out, closed the cut with two stitches using a deer bone as a needle, and stopped the bleeding with hair from an animal skin. He wrote that the man said the next day he felt no pain.
Shared with: exploration (the journey and Estevanico, story `cabeza-de-vaca-estevanico`) · native-nations (the coastal nations).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Empty towns at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
- In the spring of 1540 de Soto's army reached Cofitachequi, a large town on a river in what is now South Carolina or Georgia (historians have placed it in different spots). A Portuguese member of the army, known only as the Gentleman of Elvas, wrote that there were large empty towns nearby, overgrown with grass. People at Cofitachequi told the Spaniards that two years before, a disease had struck the land, and the people of those towns had moved away to other towns.
- That disease came before de Soto's army. The Gentleman of Elvas did not name it, and he did not say how many people died.
Shared with: exploration and native-nations (de Soto's march, the violence at Mabila, and the captives his men took).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Roanoke, 1585 and 1586" -->
- In 1585 and 1586 English colonists sent by Sir Walter Raleigh lived on Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, in the land of the Carolina Algonquian peoples. The colonists visited Algonquian towns. Within a few days after they left a town, people there began to die quickly. In some towns about 20 people died, in some 40, in some 60, and in one town 120, the scientist Thomas Harriot wrote. The oldest people told him nothing like it had happened before.
- Harriot wrote that no English colonist died or became seriously sick during the same months.
- Harriot did not name the disease. Peter Mires, writing in the journal Historical Archaeology in 1994, argued that it was probably influenza. He called it probable, not certain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-harriot" name="Thomas Harriot" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Harriot
> **Who:** English mathematician and scientist who wrote down, town by town, an epidemic among the Carolina Algonquians. · **When and where:** Roanoke Island and the Carolina Algonquian towns, 1585 to 1586. His report was printed in London in 1588.
- Born about 1560, graduated from Oxford in 1580, and taught ocean navigation at Sir Walter Raleigh's London house. He sailed with the colony that reached Roanoke Island on 26 June 1585, and he and the artist John White mapped the area.
- He recorded the town-by-town deaths: about 20, 40, 60, and in one town 120. He wrote down what the Algonquians thought was killing them. Some believed the English God was killing people for the English. Others believed invisible spirits traveling with the English were shooting "invisible bullets" into them. Algonquian healers sucked "strings of blood" out of the sick and said these were the strings tied to the invisible bullets.
- The Roanoke leader Wingina, who was himself so sick twice that he nearly died, and others came to the English and asked them to pray that their enemies would die the same way. Harriot wrote that he told them this was ungodly. When their enemies did die, they thanked the English anyway.
- Harriot's own explanation was that the deaths were "the speciall worke of God" for the English. He also described how the Algonquians used uppowoc, the plant the Spanish called tobacco, as a medicine, breathing in the smoke through clay pipes. He believed it kept them healthy. He died in 1621.
- In 1994 Peter Mires compared Harriot's report with John Smith's later reports of an epidemic among the Accomac, a people the Roanoke colonists visited in the winter of 1585 to 1586. He concluded that the Roanoke colonists carried the disease.
