## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The land was already crossed by Native movement for thousands of years. Migration here does not begin with wagons. People moved to build, moved when places were left, followed the seasons, and traded along paths that laced the whole continent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia, Chaco, and the Athabascan migration" -->
Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, the largest town north of Mexico, peaked about 1100 at roughly 10,000–20,000 people, dispersed by about 1350, and was empty by the 1400s (causes debated — drought, floods, strain on wood and food; no settled answer). Chaco Canyon broke up after droughts around 1140 and was left by about 1200, its people regathering into the Pueblo communities. The long Athabascan (Dené) migration south — ancestors of the Navajo and Apache moving from western Canada and Alaska toward the Southwest; the arrival date is unsettled (about the 1200s to about 1525 — state the disagreement). Seasonal rounds, and long trade routes carrying turquoise, shells, copper, and obsidian hundreds of miles. No documented named individual survives from these journeys; zoom 3 stays empty on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Still Native land coast to coast, with no mass settler movement across the interior. Two changes mark the century's edges: the horse comes back, and in 1598 the first wagon-borne settler colony rolls north into New Mexico.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The horse returns" -->
The Spanish bring the horse back (Columbus 1493; onto the mainland with Coronado 1540–42). Horses had died out here after the ice age. The transformation of Plains life comes later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oñate up the Camino Real, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate leaves Santa Bárbara in northern New Spain in January 1598 with about 400 men — about 130 of them with families — plus 83 wagons and carts and more than 7,000 head of livestock: soldiers, friars, farmers, servants, and enslaved people, moving to stay. The column cuts across the Chihuahuan Desert, crosses the Rio Grande at El Paso del Norte in late April, claims New Mexico for Spain on April 30, 1598 (La Toma), and in July sets its headquarters at San Juan Pueblo (Ohkay Owingeh) — extending the road by more than 600 miles. The route becomes El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, the royal road of the interior. The land they moved into was Pueblo homeland; in 1599 Oñate's soldiers attacked Acoma Pueblo — that story belongs to `native-nations`. Shared with: `transportation` (the Camino Real as a road) · `native-nations` (the Pueblo world and Acoma).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="juan-de-onate" name="Juan de Oñate" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Juan de Oñate
Led the 1598 colony column up the Camino Real — about 400 men, 83 wagons and carts, 7,000 head of livestock, from Santa Bárbara to Ohkay Owingeh. The first documented overland settler colony to move into the future United States. (Luna's 1559 Pensacola colony and St. Augustine in 1565 came earlier, by sea.) His soldiers' 1599 attack on Acoma Pueblo goes with the nations' story (`native-nations`); this chapter says it plainly in one sentence and hands off.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Newcomers cling to the Atlantic coast; the Appalachians and powerful Native nations hold them near the ocean. The century's movement stories are the horse spreading among Native nations, and colonists' first short moves inland.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The horse spreads" -->
Traditional account: the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 frees perhaps more than 1,500 Spanish horses, which move north and east by trade to the Ute, Apache, Comanche, Lakota, and others. Newer account: a 2023 *Science* study (Taylor and Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee, and other partners) finds horses kept and cared for among Plains and Rockies nations by the early 1600s, matching Native oral histories. State both; the timing is unsettled. Effect: mounted nations could follow the buffalo and travel farther and faster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="First colonial moves inland" -->
Hooker's congregation walks to Hartford in 1636 (story below); Roger Williams leaves Massachusetts for Providence the same year (his banishment and ideas → `religion`); settlement crawls up the Chesapeake rivers. These are moves of miles, not continents — the interior stays Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hooker" name="Thomas Hooker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hooker
Pastor at Newtown (now Cambridge), Massachusetts. In May–June 1636 he led about 100 members of his congregation on a roughly two-week walk along Native trails to the Connecticut River, driving 160 cattle and living partly on the cows' milk — Winthrop's journal (May 31, 1636) records the departure, Hooker's wife carried on a horse litter. They settled Hartford; the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut followed in 1639 (the document → `government-politics`).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-hooker" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Coastal land fills and prices rise, so settler families push inland into the backcountry — while whole Native nations are also on the move, north and onto the Plains. Everything beyond the mountains is still Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Wagon Road and the backcountry" -->
The Great Wagon Road: about 730 miles from Philadelphia through the Shenandoah Valley to Augusta, Georgia, heavily used from the 1720s and busier after the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster opened the valley. Who moved: German and Scots-Irish families finding Pennsylvania land too costly (their arrival from Europe → `immigration`).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nations on the move" -->
The Tuscarora migration north, 1713–1722: after the Tuscarora War in North Carolina, about 1,500 survivors move roughly 500 miles in stages toward Pennsylvania and New York and, sponsored by the Oneida, become the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy in 1722 — a nation moving whole. Shared with: `native-nations` (the confederacy politics; the journey is ours). The Comanche move onto the southern Plains by the early 1700s (their power and economy → `native-nations`). No documented named traveler survives from this era's journeys; zoom 3 stays empty rather than invented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="migration" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first big crowds cross the Appalachians, Congress lays a grid over land it has not yet settled — and a whole deported people finds its way to Louisiana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Wilderness Road and the land ordinances" -->
Cumberland Gap and the Wilderness Road — Boone cut it in 1775 (`exploration`); as many as 300,000 settlers went through into Kentucky and beyond between 1775 and about 1810; Kentucky became a state in 1792. After the Revolution settlers pour into the Ohio Valley and Northwest Territory. The Land Ordinance of 1785 (townships six miles square, 36 sections of 640 acres, one reserved for schools) and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 (path to statehood; slavery barred north of the Ohio).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Acadian expulsion and the road to Louisiana" -->
Between 1755 and 1764 British forces deport about 11,500 Acadians — French-speaking Catholic farm families — from Nova Scotia and nearby areas, out of a population of about 14,000–18,000, scattering them to the thirteen colonies, England, and France. Death counts are disputed: one accounting says close to 5,000 died of disease, shipwreck, or violence; some sources say as many as half — state the range. Between 1764 and 1785 about 2,600–3,000 reach Louisiana; in 1785 seven Spanish-funded ships carry 1,596 Acadians from France, the first arriving at New Orleans on July 29, 1785. Their descendants become the Cajuns — the word comes from "Acadian." Shared with: `immigration` (the 1785 ships arrived from France — flag for the director) · `religion` (Catholic families in a new land).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-broussard" name="Joseph Broussard (Beausoleil)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Broussard (Beausoleil)
Acadian resistance leader, about 1702–1765. Fought the deportation, was held at Halifax until the 1763 peace, chartered a ship with about 600 Acadians to Saint-Domingue in late 1764, then led about 193–200 on to Louisiana, arriving at New Orleans in February 1765. Named militia captain and commander of the "Acadians of the Attakapas" on April 8, 1765; died that October in an epidemic. One of the first Cajun founders.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-broussard" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The country doubles in size and the trails fill. Most of this era's movement is toward the West — and some of it is at gunpoint: removal marches and a million people moved by the slave trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The trails" -->
Louisiana Purchase, 1803: about 827,000 square miles for $15 million. "Manifest Destiny" — a phrase coined by John O'Sullivan in 1845; an idea people held, not a force. The trails, most starting at Independence or St. Joseph, Missouri: the Santa Fe Trail (1821, William Becknell), mainly freight; the Oregon Trail, about 2,000–2,170 miles and four to six months, with the 1843 "Great Migration" of roughly 1,000 people and 120 wagons proving families could make it; the California Trail, which exploded after 1849; the Mormon Trail, whose 1847 vanguard company of about 148 people covered some 1,300 miles, with more than 70,000 following by 1869. Wagon-train life: the real killers were disease (cholera, typhoid), accidents, and river crossings, not attacks; roughly one in ten emigrants died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forced journeys: removal and the domestic slave trade" -->
The Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the Trail of Tears of 1838–39: more than 16,000 Cherokee driven from Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina; over 4,000 dead (the traditional estimate; some scholars argue higher); the overland walk about 800 miles. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — the nations broken, the treaties ignored) · `rights-movements` (the campaign against removal). The domestic slave trade — this chapter leads the journey (ruling 2): more than 1 million enslaved people moved from the Upper South to the Deep South, about 1790–1860, roughly 200,000 a decade from 1820 — marched overland in chained coffles or shipped down the coast to New Orleans, the country's largest slave market. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (lead on the system — people as property, families broken by sale).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gold Rush and the Mexican Cession" -->
Gold found at Sutter's Mill in 1848; about 80,000 forty-niners in 1849 and about 300,000 to California from 1848 to 1855, half by sea and half overland; California's non-Native population goes from about 14,000 to about 250,000. Shared with: `exploration` (the searching) · `economy` (the money) · `city-building` (San Francisco's leap). The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) opens the far West.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin
Cherokee child on the Trail of Tears, about three years old in 1838; rode in a wagon while her mother, father, and older brother walked the whole way. Her memory was recorded in 1932, near the end of her life — one of the last firsthand survivor voices of the removal journey. (The nation's story → `native-nations`, which features her separately.)
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-ball-migration" name="Charles Ball" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Ball
Enslaved man sold away from his wife and children in Maryland about 1805 and chained into a coffle of 52 people — iron collars padlocked to a hundred-foot chain — that walked more than a month to Columbia, South Carolina. He later escaped, walked back north by night, was seized again, escaped again, and dictated his story (published 1837). Dates approximate; the narrative passed through another man's pen — say so.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-ball-migration" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="brigham-young" name="Brigham Young" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Brigham Young
Led the 1847 Mormon vanguard company — about 148 people, some 1,300 miles from Winter Quarters to the Salt Lake Valley, arriving July 1847.

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Railroads and free land pull settlers onto the Plains, handcart companies walk to Utah, Black Southerners try Kansas as an escape, Native nations are marched onto reservations — and the Census declares the frontier line gone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homesteads, the railroad, and the cattle drives" -->
Homestead Act, 1862: up to 160 acres after five years' residence and farming; about 4 million claims and 270 million acres (roughly 10 percent of U.S. land) over its life. Transcontinental railroad completed May 10, 1869, cutting a coast-to-coast trip from about four months to about a week. Shared with: `transportation` (lead — the line) · `native-nations` · `immigration` · `work-workers`. Cattle drives north from Texas to Kansas railheads — more than 5 million head between 1866 and 1884, fading by the mid-1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The handcart companies, 1856" -->
More than 1,000 Latter-day Saint emigrants in the late-starting Willie company (about 500 people, left Florence, Nebraska Territory, August 17, 1856) and Martin company (left August 27) pull two-wheeled handcarts toward Salt Lake City. An early blizzard strikes October 19 in present-day Wyoming, just after the Martin company wades the North Platte. More than 300 rescuers with wagons bring the survivors in. Death counts differ — state the range: about 67–77 in the Willie company, about 103–150 in the Martin company; older accounts say 200–250 across the stranded companies and their wagon trains.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Exodusters and the land rush" -->
Exodusters, about 1879: up to 40,000 Black Southerners toward Kansas and the Plains, some 6,000 in the spring of 1879 alone. Oklahoma Land Rush, April 22, 1889: about 1.9 million acres opened, some 50,000 racers, about 2 million acres claimed in a day; the early sneaks were the "Sooners." The land had been taken from Native nations — `native-nations` carries that loss.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forced onto reservations, and the frontier line gone" -->
The Long Walk of the Navajo, 1863–64: about 8,500 people marched more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo; about 200 died on the march, thousands more at the reservation before the 1868 return home. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead). The 1890 Census found no continuous frontier line; Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 essay named the change.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-stewart-knight" name="Amelia Stewart Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Stewart Knight
Oregon Trail diarist, 1853 — Iowa to the Willamette Valley with her husband and seven children, pregnant the whole way; reached Oregon September 17 and gave birth the next day. Her diary records rain, dust, sick children, and river crossings. Placed here by director ruling 3 (1853 sits in 1850–1900).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amelia-stewart-knight" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="levi-savage" name="Levi Savage" movie="17 Miracles (2011)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Levi Savage
> **Movie:** 17 Miracles (2011) — feature film about the Willie handcart company, built around Savage's journal.
Sub-captain of the Willie handcart company, age 36. At Florence on August 13, 1856, he urged the roughly 500 emigrants to wait until spring, warning of snow and death with over 1,000 miles to go; he was overruled and reprimanded — then stayed with the company and worked through the blizzards to keep people alive. His journal anchors most modern tellings.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="levi-savage" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-pap-singleton" name="Benjamin 'Pap' Singleton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin "Pap" Singleton
Born enslaved near Nashville about 1809, escaped north and to Canada, returned about 1871, printed circulars and formed the Edgefield Real Estate and Homestead Association; testified that his work helped move more than 7,400 people and steered upward of 20,000 toward Kansas between 1877 and 1879 — the "Father of the Exodus."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-pap-singleton" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" name="Laura Ingalls Wilder" movie="Little House on the Prairie (NBC series, 1974-1983)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Laura Ingalls Wilder
> **Movie:** Little House on the Prairie (NBC series, 1974-1983) — nine seasons, 205 episodes, Michael Landon; loosely based on the books.
A real migrating family, documented move by move. Born February 7, 1867, near Pepin, Wisconsin; the family went to Kansas (1869–71) — the cabin stood on the Osage Diminished Reserve, land that still belonged to the Osage Nation — back to Pepin, then Walnut Grove, Minnesota (grasshopper plagues took the wheat, 1875–76), Burr Oak, Iowa, Walnut Grove again, and De Smet, Dakota Territory (1879, a railroad-camp job, then a homestead). In 1894 Laura, Almanzo, and Rose drove about 650 miles to Mansfield, Missouri; her trip diary was published as *On the Way Home* (1962). Tell honestly what the novels change: her Kansas age is raised, Burr Oak is left out, and the family was on Osage land it had no right to.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The two largest internal movements of the century begin: Black Americans out of the South, and Plains farm families to California.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Migration" -->
About 6 million people, roughly 1910–1970; the first wave to 1940 heads to Northern and Midwestern cities, mostly by train. City Black populations 1910–20: Chicago up 148 percent, Detroit up 611 percent, Philadelphia up 500 percent, New York roughly doubled. The South held about 90 percent of Black Americans in 1900 and under 55 percent by 1970. Shared with: `city-building` (where they settled) · `rights-movements` (what drove them out and met them) · `work-workers` (the jobs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dust Bowl move" -->
Drought and windstorms strip the southern Plains in the 1930s; more than 250,000 people go to California about 1935–40 (Oklahoma's net out-migration about 440,000 — a different measure; state both), many on US Route 66. "Okies" met hostility, including the 1936 "bum blockade." Los Angeles Police Chief James E. Davis sent 136 officers to 16 crossings on the state line from February 3 to early April 1936 to turn back poor migrants. Davis claimed about 11,000 turned away, and historians think the number was lower. Shared with: `land-environment` (the soil) · `disasters` (the storms) · `food-farming` (farm collapse) · `transportation` (Route 66 as a road).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-mae-brandon-gladney" name="Ida Mae Brandon Gladney" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ida Mae Brandon Gladney
A sharecropper's wife who left Chickasaw County, Mississippi, for Chicago in 1937 after a relative was badly beaten over a false accusation. One of the three documented lives in Isabel Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns* (2010).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-mae-brandon-gladney" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-swanson-starling" name="George Swanson Starling" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Swanson Starling
A Florida citrus picker who organized pickers for better wages and fled in 1945 after learning of a planned lynching, settling in Harlem and working the New York–Florida trains. From Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-swanson-starling" -->

### Florence Owens Thompson
Born in Indian Territory in 1903, photographed by Dorothea Lange as "Migrant Mother" at a pea-pickers' camp in Nipomo, California, in March 1936; not publicly identified until 1978. Her exact route west is less documented than the photograph — keep claims to what is verified.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The migration turns west and then south, the government moves Native families to cities, and where Americans lived is reset again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Second Great Migration and the Sun Belt" -->
The later Great Migration wave (about 1940–1970) was larger than the first and leaned west — Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle, Portland — for war and postwar jobs. The Sun Belt shift out of the Rust Belt: 1970–90 the South grew about 36 percent and the West about 51 percent; air conditioning made the boom livable. California became the most populous state in 1962 at about 17 million. The Interstate Highway System (1956 Act) made long moves and suburbs easier (`transportation` owns the roads). By the late 1990s the flow had begun to reverse: the South gained Black migrants, net, from all three other regions — the start of the New Great Migration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The government moves Native families to cities" -->
The federal Relocation program (begun in the BIA about 1948–52, backed by the Indian Relocation Act of 1956) used one-way tickets and short-term help: about 30,000 Native people moved to cities in the 1950s and more than 100,000 by the program's end in the 1970s — Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland and San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver. The movement is told here; the policy's aim and the urban Native communities' politics → `native-nations`. Native continuity past 1900, kept on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-joseph-pershing-foster" name="Robert Joseph Pershing Foster" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Robert Joseph Pershing Foster
A surgeon who left Louisiana in 1953 and drove west across the desert to California — the westward-leaning later wave. From Wilkerson's *The Warmth of Other Suns*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-joseph-pershing-foster" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="migration" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Sun Belt pull sharpens, the highest-cost metros lose people to the rest of the country, and two hurricanes move whole populations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sun Belt and remote-work moves" -->
The South gained about 2.7 million net domestic migrants from 2020 to 2024. Florida's net gain from other states fell from about 311,000 (2022) to about 184,000 (2023) and about 23,000 (July 2024 to June 2025). In that last year North Carolina (about 84,000), Texas (about 67,000) and South Carolina (about 67,000) gained the most, and 9 of the 15 biggest gainers were Southern states (Census Vintage 2025, released January 2026). From 2020 to 2022 California lost about 343,000 and New York about 300,000 to other states; the biggest out-migration metros were New York, Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Chicago. Remote work after 2020 let some people move by cost and space rather than job location. The New Great Migration continued: metro Atlanta's Black population grew about five-fold from 1970 to 2020, ranking second nationally by 2010, and Atlanta was the top destination for young Black adult movers from 2000 to 2018.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Storm moves: Katrina and María" -->
Hurricane Katrina, 2005: the storm and levee failures displaced more than 1 million people from the New Orleans metro and Gulf Coast; up to 600,000 households were still displaced a month later. New Orleans fell from 484,674 people (2000 census) to about 230,000 (2006) and stood near 363,000 in 2024 and about 362,000 in July 2025. Many never came back. About 250,000 evacuees reached Houston, and about 150,000 were still there a year later. On September 1, 2005, Gretna police, bridge police and Jefferson Parish deputies turned people walking out of New Orleans back at gunpoint on the Mississippi River bridge, on orders Gretna Police Chief Arthur Lawson and the parish sheriff's office agreed on. Shared with: `disasters` (lead — the storm, the levees, the rescue). Hurricane María, September 2017: about 130,000 people — almost 4 percent of Puerto Rico — left for the mainland within a year (Census estimate; other data sources give higher counts — state that the number depends on the data used), about a third of 2018's movers going to Florida. Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens; this is internal migration. Shared with: `america-world` (the territories thread) · `disasters` (the storm).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="kimberly-rivers-roberts" name="Kimberly Rivers Roberts" movie="Trouble the Water (2008)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kimberly Rivers Roberts
> **Movie:** Trouble the Water (2008), a documentary about Kimberly and Scott Roberts, directed by Tia Lessin and Carl Deal and built on her own video.

> **Who:** A 24-year-old from the Lower Ninth Ward of New Orleans who filmed the Katrina flood from inside it, with her husband Scott Roberts.

> **When and where:** New Orleans, August 2005, then Alexandria, Louisiana, then Memphis, Tennessee, then home to New Orleans.
- Someone had stolen the couple's car, so they could not leave before the storm. On August 28, 2005, Kimberly began filming with a camcorder she had bought for $20.
- The water rose and they climbed into the attic with neighbors. Police told them by phone they would not come yet. A neighbor floated people out on a punching bag.
- At a nearly empty Navy base, people at the gate held the group at gunpoint and turned them away. The film shows it, and the Navy denies it happened. The film does not name the people with the guns.
- Her grandmother died in a hospital that was never emptied. Her uncle was later found dead in his home.
- They went to a Red Cross shelter in Alexandria, Louisiana, then an uncle's trailer, then Memphis, where a cousin lived. FEMA, the federal office for disaster help, promised them a check. It did not come.
- A newspaper interview says they spent six months in Memphis and could not find work without high school diplomas. They went back to New Orleans, gutted flooded houses for pay, and Kimberly started a record label.
