## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The oldest built landmarks in the country are Native, and some are enormous. Centuries before any European arrived, people here raised earthen mounds bigger at the base than Egypt's Great Pyramid, stone buildings of hundreds of rooms, and villages built into cliff walls. These are American landmarks — the first ones.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Monks Mound and the mound builders" -->
- Monks Mound at Cahokia (Illinois), built mostly c. 1050–1200: about 100 feet tall with a base of about 14 acres — a bigger footprint than the Great Pyramid of Giza (about 13 acres), though the pyramid is far taller. The largest earthen structure in the Americas. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — the nation that built it) · `city-building` (Cahokia as a planned city).
- Poverty Point (Louisiana), c. 1700–1100 BC: five mounds and six concentric ridges built by hunter-fisher-gatherers; a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2014.
- Serpent Mound (Ohio): a quarter-mile earthen snake. Builder dispute stated honestly — radiocarbon tests point to the Fort Ancient culture (c. 1070 AD) in one study and the Adena culture (c. 300 BC) in another; it may have been built and rebuilt in more than one period. Nobody resolves this by picking a side.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone buildings of the Southwest" -->
- Chaco Canyon great houses (New Mexico, c. 850–1150): Pueblo Bonito rose four stories and held some 600–650 rooms.
- Mesa Verde's Cliff Palace (Colorado, c. 1190–1280): about 150 rooms and 23 kivas built under a cliff overhang.
- No individual builder's name survives from any of these places; the book does not invent people.

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Thin era, honestly: nothing the European newcomers built in this century still stands. Their forts were wood and earth, burned and rebuilt again and again — what survives of them is archaeology. The Native landmarks of the last era kept standing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine's founding site" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, founded September 1565 by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés — the oldest continuously occupied European-founded city in the continental U.S. Francis Drake burned it in 1586. The era's landmark is the site itself; the stone fort comes in the next century. Shared with: `city-building` (lead — the town and the Laws of the Indies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The oldest European-built structures still standing in the country date from this century: a Spanish stone fort in Florida, an adobe chapel in Santa Fe, a wooden meetinghouse in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fort that swallowed cannonballs" -->
Castillo de San Marcos, St. Augustine — begun October 1672, finished 1695; the oldest masonry fort in the continental U.S. Built of coquina, a soft stone made of packed seashells. When English cannons fired on it — a two-month siege in 1702, a 27-day siege in 1740 — the balls did not shatter the walls; the shell stone crushed where they hit and the balls sank in or bounced off. Both sieges failed. In 1702 the town burned; the fort and the people sheltering inside it survived. Now a National Monument.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The oldest churches, told honestly" -->
- San Miguel Chapel, Santa Fe: adobe church built c. 1610–1626 by Tlaxcalan workers from Mexico; burned in the 1680 Pueblo Revolt; rebuilt 1710 on the old foundations. Often called the oldest church in the country — honest version: the site and foundations are that old, the standing walls are mostly the 1710 rebuild.
- Old Ship Meetinghouse, Hingham, Massachusetts, 1681: the only surviving 17th-century Puritan meetinghouse, and the oldest church building in continuous use in the U.S. Its timber roof frame looked to the congregation like a ship's hull turned upside down.
- The Palace of the Governors in Santa Fe (c. 1610) is `city-building`'s story.

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial towns start building public structures meant to last: churches with tall steeples, a lighthouse at a harbor mouth, a statehouse of brick with a bell tower.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steeples and a statehouse" -->
- Old North Church, Boston, 1723 — the oldest church building standing in Boston; its steeple (finished 1740) was the tallest thing in town. Its fame came later: the two lanterns of April 18, 1775 (that night's events belong to `war`).
- Independence Hall, Philadelphia — built 1732–1753 as the Pennsylvania State House, designed by Andrew Hamilton with master carpenter Edmund Woolley; the bell inscribed "Proclaim liberty throughout all the land" hung in the new tower in 1753. What was signed inside is `government-politics`; the building is ours.
- The Alamo — Mission San Antonio de Valero, founded 1718 in San Antonio; stone church cornerstone laid May 8, 1744; that church collapsed in the 1750s, and the famous stone front begun in 1758 was never finished by the missionaries — no bell towers, no dome. The 1836 battle is `war`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first lighthouse" -->
Boston Light, on Little Brewster Island — first lit September 16, 1716, the first lighthouse in the colonies. The British blew it up in 1776; the tower standing today is the 1783 rebuild. Say both dates plainly.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-worthylake" name="George Worthylake" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Worthylake
> **Who:** the first lighthouse keeper in America, at Boston Light from 1716. · **When and where:** Little Brewster Island, Boston Harbor, 1716–1718.
Keeping the light meant living on a bare island with his family. On November 3, 1718, returning from a sermon in town, Worthylake's canoe capsized within sight of the lighthouse; he drowned with his wife Ann, his daughter Ruth, and three others, including Shadwell, a man he enslaved. A 12-year-old Boston boy named Benjamin Franklin printed a street ballad about the drowning, "The Lighthouse Tragedy," and sold it in the streets — Franklin later called his own verses "wretched stuff," and no copy survives. Days later the replacement keeper drowned too. The light stayed lit anyway; someone always kept it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-worthylake" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The new country starts building for itself — a president's house and a capitol modeled on ancient temples. Enslaved people did much of the building. That is a verified fact, and this chapter says it plainly.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Raising the capital's buildings" -->
- The President's House (the White House): cornerstone October 13, 1792; designed by Irish-born architect James Hoban. The U.S. Capitol: cornerstone September 18, 1793, laid by George Washington. Neither was finished by 1800 — John Adams moved into a house of wet plaster and bare stairs.
- Enslaved workers labored at every stage of both buildings: quarrying the sandstone, cutting timber, making bricks, sawing, carpentry. The government hired them from their owners, and the owners collected the pay. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the system itself) · `city-building` (L'Enfant's street plan, theirs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="white-house-enslaved-carpenters" name="Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel
> **Who:** five enslaved carpenters who helped build the White House, known by name from a surviving payroll. · **When and where:** the President's House construction site, Washington, May 1795.
The May 1795 carpenters' payroll lists them by first name only: Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, Daniel. Four were held by the architect James Hoban himself, one by his assistant Pierce Purcell. That month Peter and Ben each worked 28 days, Daniel 25, Harry 9 — and Hoban signed for their wages, because the pay went to the owners, not the workers. The building they raised became the most famous house in the country. Their last names, their lives before and after — not recorded.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="white-house-enslaved-carpenters" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Landmark houses" -->
Mount Vernon and Monticello — the era's most visited houses today, both built and run by enslaved workers. Monticello: Jefferson began it in 1769, tore into it again from 1796, called it done about 1809. (The households inside are `home-family`'s subject; these appear here as built landmarks only.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-jefferson-landmarks" name="Thomas Jefferson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Jefferson
> **Who:** author of the Declaration who was also the new country's most influential amateur architect. · **When and where:** Monticello, begun 1769, rebuilt 1796–1809; his temple-form Virginia Capitol design, 1785.
Jefferson designed Monticello himself and kept redesigning it for forty years — visitors found it half-built for most of his life. His Virginia State Capitol, modeled on a Roman temple, helped set the classical look that Washington's public buildings copied. The skilled hands executing his drawings were largely enslaved craftsmen; one of them, the joiner John Hemmings, carries the next era's story.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-jefferson-landmarks" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Thinner era: the country begins putting up monuments to its own short history — and shows it cannot yet finish them. Both of the era's big obelisks stalled for lack of money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two obelisks, slowly" -->
- Bunker Hill Monument, Charlestown: cornerstone June 17, 1825, with Lafayette present; funds ran out repeatedly; a women's fair organized by Sarah Josepha Hale in 1840 raised $30,000 of the cost; the 221-foot granite obelisk was finished in 1842 and dedicated in 1843.
- The Washington Monument: cornerstone July 4, 1848, before a crowd of about 20,000. By 1854 the money was gone and work stopped at about 150 feet. That year members of the anti-Catholic Know-Nothing movement stole the marble block donated by Pope Pius IX — the "Pope stone" — and by the usual account threw it in the Potomac; it was never found. Know-Nothings then seized the monument society itself (1855–58). The stub stood for a generation. The finish belongs to the next era.
- The Smithsonian's red sandstone Castle went up 1847–1855 (architect James Renwick Jr.).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-hemmings-landmarks" name="John Hemmings" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Hemmings
> **Who:** enslaved master joiner whose hands made Monticello's finest woodwork. · **When and where:** Monticello and Poplar Forest, Virginia; his lead years c. 1800–1826.
Trained from age 17 under hired white joiners, Hemmings (1776–1833) made the decorative moldings, doors, and furniture of the rebuilt Monticello and led the woodwork at Jefferson's Poplar Forest retreat. Jefferson's will freed him in 1826 — one of only five people it freed — and gave him the tools of the joiner's shop he had worked in all his life. The house tourists admire is, room after room, his craftsmanship. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the Hemings family's larger story, theirs).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-hemmings-landmarks" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1800-1850" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="landmarks" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Iron, steel, and stone make structures nobody could have built fifty years earlier: a bridge hung from steel cables, a copper woman taller than any building around her, a stone obelisk finished at last as the tallest structure on earth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Finishing the Capitol dome" -->
The Capitol's new iron dome rose through the Civil War, topped December 2, 1863 by the bronze Statue of Freedom — cast with the skill of an enslaved craftsman who was a free man by the day it was raised.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-reid-landmarks" name="Philip Reid" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Reid
> **Who:** enslaved foundry craftsman who helped cast the Statue of Freedom for the Capitol dome. · **When and where:** Clark Mills' foundry outside Washington, 1860–1863.
When the Italian sculptor-foreman demanded extra pay to reveal how to take apart the statue's plaster model, Reid — born enslaved in Charleston about 1820 — worked out how to do it himself with a pulley and tackle. He was paid $1.25 a day, more than the laborers around him, but only Sunday's pay was his; his owner took the other six days. Congress freed the capital's enslaved people on April 16, 1862, so when Freedom was bolted onto the dome in December 1863, Philip Reid was a free man. He stayed in Washington and worked as a plasterer under the name he chose to sign: Philip Reed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-reid-landmarks" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Brooklyn Bridge" -->
Opened May 24, 1883 — the longest suspension bridge in the world then, hung from steel cables between two stone towers. It cost the family that built it: at least 20 workers died, and caisson disease — "the bends," from working in compressed air underwater — crippled many more. Shared with: `technology` (suspension-bridge engineering as method) · `work-workers` (the caisson crews).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="roeblings" name="John, Washington, and Emily Roebling" movie="Brooklyn Bridge (1981)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John, Washington, and Emily Roebling
> **Who:** the designer of the Brooklyn Bridge, the son who built it, and the wife who carried the work when the son could not. · **When and where:** New York, 1869–1883. · **Movie:** *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981) — Ken Burns' first documentary, about the bridge; Academy Award nominee.
John Roebling designed the bridge but never built it: in 1869 a ferry crushed his foot during surveying, he refused doctors' treatment, and died of tetanus within weeks. His son Washington took over — then wrecked his health in the compressed-air caissons and directed the rest of the work part-paralyzed from a Brooklyn room, watching through a telescope. His wife Emily Warren Roebling became the connection: for 11 years she studied cable construction and bridge mathematics so she could carry accurate instructions to the engineers, answer their questions, and face down trustees who tried to fire her husband. Honest version of a popular claim: Washington kept the title and made the engineering decisions; Emily held the whole enterprise together, and the engineers dealt with her daily. At the opening she crossed first by carriage, carrying a rooster for victory.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roeblings" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Washington Monument, finished" -->
Congress took the stalled stub over in 1876; Army engineers restarted work in 1879 and set the capstone December 6, 1884 — with a 100-ounce aluminum tip, a metal then so rare it cost about a dollar an ounce, a day's wage. At 555 feet it was the tallest structure in the world until the Eiffel Tower (1889). About 150 feet up, the marble visibly changes shade — the restart used stone from different quarry beds. The 25-year stop is written on the monument itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the Statue of Liberty" -->
France gave the statue; America had to build the pedestal. Bartholdi's completed right arm and torch stood at the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial fair, then in Madison Square Park for five years, to coax donations. Gustave Eiffel engineered the iron skeleton inside (from 1879) — a copper skin about as thick as two pennies put together, hung on an iron frame. Pedestal money stalled until publisher Joseph Pulitzer's New York World campaign of 1885: about 120,000 donors, most giving less than a dollar — schoolchildren's pennies among them — every name printed in the paper. Dedicated October 28, 1886. Emma Lazarus's poem went up on a plaque in 1903; what the statue came to mean to arriving immigrants is `immigration`'s story. Shared with: `immigration` (lead on its meaning) · `news-communication` (Pulitzer's newspaper as engine).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" name="Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi
> **Who:** French sculptor of the Statue of Liberty. · **When and where:** Paris workshops, 1870s–1884; New York Harbor, dedicated October 28, 1886.
Bartholdi spent over 15 years on one statue: sketching the harbor figure by 1870, scaling plaster models up stage by stage, shipping the finished statue to New York in 350 pieces packed in 214 crates. He toured America selling the idea, put the torch arm in a Philadelphia fairground and the head at a Paris fair, and lived to see the statue lit in the harbor.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What else the era built" -->
Biltmore House, Asheville (1889–1895): George W. Vanderbilt's 250-room house, about 179,000 square feet — still the largest privately owned house in the country. The first steel-frame tall office buildings rose in Chicago from 1885 — as a change in how cities grew, they are `city-building`'s story. Yellowstone became the first national park in 1872 — parks as land policy are `land-environment`'s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1900-1950" order="08" chapter="landmarks" label="1900 to 1950" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The most photographed American structures nearly all go up in one stretch between the wars: a marble memorial, four faces on a mountain, the tallest building on earth, the longest bridge span, the biggest dam. The stories underneath them are harder than the postcards.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Lincoln Memorial" -->
Dedicated May 30, 1922 — Henry Bacon's marble temple, Daniel Chester French's seated Lincoln. The dedication crowd was segregated, and the one Black speaker, Robert Russa Moton, had his speech text vetted first. Seventeen years later, on Easter Sunday 1939, after the Daughters of the American Revolution barred her from Constitution Hall, Marian Anderson sang from its steps to about 75,000 people. The concert is `rights-movements`' story; the memorial that became the country's stage for it is ours. Shared with: `rights-movements` (lead on the concert and the 1963 March).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mount Rushmore — both halves of the story" -->
Carved October 1927 to October 31, 1941 by about 400 workers — 90 percent of the shaping done with dynamite, no worker killed. Both halves told plainly: the mountain is Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers, in the Black Hills — land guaranteed to the Lakota by the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty and taken after gold was found in 1874. In 1980 the Supreme Court ruled the taking violated the treaty; the Lakota refused the payment — over a billion dollars with interest now — because the land was never for sale. Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — the Black Hills and the treaty) · `art` (mountain sculpture as art).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="gutzon-borglum" name="Gutzon Borglum" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gutzon Borglum
> **Who:** the sculptor of Mount Rushmore — told honestly, including where he worked before. · **When and where:** Stone Mountain, Georgia, 1915–1925; the Black Hills, 1927–1941.
Before Rushmore, Borglum carved the Confederate memorial at Stone Mountain, a project funded partly by the revived Ku Klux Klan; historians document that he attended Klan meetings and was deep in Klan politics, though no membership record survives. Fired from Stone Mountain after quarrels, he took the South Dakota job and drove it for 14 years — engineering the pointing machine that scaled his models up, directing the dynamite crews, fighting for funding through the Depression. He died in March 1941, months before the money ran out and the carving stopped for good. Both facts stand together: the four faces are his work, and so is the company he kept.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gutzon-borglum" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="nick-clifford" name="Nick Clifford" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nick Clifford
> **Who:** the youngest Mount Rushmore worker, hired at 17 — and the last one living. · **When and where:** Keystone, South Dakota, 1938–1940; died 2019 at 98.
Donald "Nick" Clifford got the job partly because the Borglums wanted a pitcher for the crew's baseball team. He earned 55 cents an hour running the winch that lowered drillers down the faces, and drilled dynamite holes himself. He outlived every other carver, spent his last decades answering visitors' questions at the memorial, and died in November 2019 — the last person alive who had hung off that mountain.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nick-clifford" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Empire State Building" -->
Opened May 1, 1931 — 102 stories, built in about 410 days, the steel frame rising four and a half floors a week with up to 3,400 workers a day. Five workers died — the official count; the "hundreds" of legend is false. Lewis Hine photographed the riveting gangs for *Men at Work*; among them were Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake, whose community had built high steel since the 1880s — Native builders on the tallest thing in the world. Shared with: `work-workers` (lead on Hine's photographs and the trade) · `city-building` (the skyscraper as urban form).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Golden Gate Bridge" -->
Opened May 1937 — 200,000 people walked it on opening day. Credit told honestly: chief engineer Joseph Strauss got the statue and the plaque, but structural engineer Charles Ellis did the detailed design and calculations in 1929–31, was fired by Strauss before construction, and was only given principal design credit by the bridge district in 2007. Eleven workers died — ten of them on one day, February 17, 1937, when a falling work platform tore through the safety net. Shared with: `disasters` (the February 1937 collapse) · `technology` (suspension engineering).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-strauss" name="Joseph Strauss" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Strauss
> **Who:** chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge, whose real innovation was safety. · **When and where:** San Francisco, 1933–1937.
Strauss spent about $130,000 of Depression money on something no big American bridge job had used: a giant rope net slung under the whole deck. It caught 19 falling men over four years — they formed the "Halfway to Hell Club." He also required hard hats. The honest ledger: he took more design credit than he had earned (Charles Ellis's numbers held the bridge up), and his safety net saved more lives than any calculation he made.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-strauss" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="al-zampa" name="Al Zampa" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Al Zampa
> **Who:** ironworker who fell off the Golden Gate Bridge and lived — a founding member of the Halfway to Hell Club. · **When and where:** the Marin tower side, October 19, 1936.
Alfred Zampa, from Crockett, California, slipped on wet steel and fell — but near the shore the net sagged onto the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back. Twelve weeks in the hospital, two years in a steel brace, then back onto high steel for the rest of his career. In 2003 California named the new Carquinez Strait bridge the Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge — a major American bridge named for a working ironworker.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="al-zampa" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoover Dam" -->
Dedicated September 30, 1935. The deaths, honestly: the official count is 96 industrial fatalities — and it leaves out the tunnel men whose deaths were recorded as "pneumonia," which many historians read as carbon-monoxide poisoning, so the true toll is higher and not exactly known. And the legend, debunked plainly: nobody is buried in the concrete. The dam was poured as many small blocks in shallow layers with crews working over every pour — a body could not have been left inside. Shared with: `work-workers` (conditions in the canyon) · `energy` (the power it made) · `land-environment` (what damming the river did).

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="landmarks" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="researched" -->
## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The era builds a steel arch taller than any monument before it and twin towers taller than any building — and then, at its end, a memorial that is not a statue or a temple at all, but a wall of names cut into the ground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gateway Arch" -->
Eero Saarinen won the St. Louis design competition in 1948; he died in 1961, before a single piece was set. Built February 1963 to October 28, 1965: 630 feet tall, 630 feet wide, stainless steel. Insurers predicted 13 deaths; no worker died. On July 14, 1964, activists Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed 125 feet up the unfinished north leg to protest that no Black workers were on the job — the landmark's own construction became a civil-rights stage. Shared with: `rights-movements` (lead on the campaign) · `migration` (the westward-expansion meaning it memorializes).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="harold-mccomber" name="Harold McComber" movie="High Steel (1965)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harold McComber
> **Who:** Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake, one of the high-steel men who built New York's skylines. · **When and where:** Manhattan skyscrapers and the Kahnawake community near Montreal, filmed 1965. · **Movie:** *High Steel* (1965) — National Film Board short documentary about McComber himself.
Kahnawake men had worked high steel since the 1880s, through the 1907 Quebec Bridge collapse that killed 33 of them (that calamity is the disasters chapter's), and on to the Empire State Building and the towers after it. The 1965 documentary follows McComber at work on the beams above Manhattan and home in Kahnawake — Native builders, generations deep, still raising the country's landmarks. Native continuity past 1900, on film.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harold-mccomber" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The World Trade Center" -->
Ribbon-cut April 4, 1973 — Minoru Yamasaki's twin 110-story towers; the North Tower's 1,368 feet briefly the world's tallest, passed within weeks by Chicago's Sears Tower. The Space Needle (1962, 605 feet, built in about 400 days for Seattle's World's Fair) marks the same years' confidence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="philippe-petit" name="Philippe Petit" movie="Man on Wire (2008)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Philippe Petit
> **Who:** French high-wire walker who crossed between the Twin Towers on a cable. · **When and where:** New York, August 7, 1974. · **Movie:** *Man on Wire* (2008) — Academy Award winner, Best Documentary Feature; about the walk itself.
Petit and his friends spent months planning, smuggled their equipment to the top floors, and rigged a cable between the roofs overnight. At dawn he stepped out — about 1,350 feet up, no net — and crossed eight times in 45 minutes while police waited at both ends. Charges were dropped in exchange for a free children's show in Central Park. The stunt did what the buildings' owners had struggled to do: it made New Yorkers love the towers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philippe-petit" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Vietnam Veterans Memorial" -->
Dedicated November 13, 1982: two walls of polished black granite set into the Mall, carrying the names of the more than 58,000 dead in the order they fell. The design was attacked before it was built — one veteran called it "a black gash of shame" — and the compromise added Frederick Hart's Three Servicemen statue nearby in 1984. Then people came, touched the names, left letters and boots and photographs at its base, and it became the most visited memorial in Washington. Shared with: `war` (the war and its veterans, theirs).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="maya-lin" name="Maya Lin" movie="Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maya Lin
> **Who:** the 21-year-old college student whose design won the Vietnam Veterans Memorial competition. · **When and where:** Yale and Washington, D.C., 1981–1982. · **Movie:** *Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision* (1994) — Academy Award winner, Best Documentary Feature; about Lin herself.
Lin drafted the design for a college class, entered the blind competition, and her entry number 1,026 beat 1,420 others — the judges did not know the winner was a Chinese American undergraduate. Through months of public attack on her design and on her, she defended the idea that a memorial could be a quiet cut in the earth that hands you the names and lets you grieve. Her name went unmentioned at the 1982 dedication ceremony. The wall vindicated her; she spent the rest of her career building memorials and art on her own terms.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maya-lin" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Restoring the old landmarks" -->
The Statue of Liberty was stripped and restored for its centennial, its 1886 torch replaced with a gold-leafed replica, and rededicated July 4, 1986. Ellis Island — added to the Statue of Liberty National Monument in 1965 — had its ruined main building restored into the Immigration Museum by 1990. The arrival stories those buildings hold are `immigration`'s; their rescue as structures is ours.

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Current through 2026. The country builds memorials to loss — the towers' footprints turned to pools, a museum sheathed in bronze lattice — while taking down hundreds of older monuments and arguing about which should stand.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ground Zero rebuilt" -->
The National September 11 Memorial opened September 11, 2011: two recessed pools in the Twin Towers' footprints, the victims' names in bronze around the rims — "Reflecting Absence," by architect Michael Arad with Peter Walker, chosen from 5,201 competition entries. The museum below opened May 21, 2014. One World Trade Center opened November 3, 2014 — 1,776 feet to the spire, the number chosen for the Declaration's year. The attacks are `america-world`'s and `disasters`' to tell; what was built afterward is ours. Shared with: `america-world` (the attacks, lead) · `disasters` (the response).

<!-- hb-story:start slug="michael-arad" name="Michael Arad" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Michael Arad
> **Who:** the architect of the 9/11 Memorial. · **When and where:** New York, competition won 2004, memorial opened 2011.
Arad, a 34-year-old architect then working for a New York City government agency, first imagined the memorial as two voids torn in the Hudson River — absence made visible. His competition entry beat 5,200 others, then survived seven years of redesigns, budget fights, and grieving families' demands about how the names should be arranged. He held to the core: the footprints stay open, the water falls, the names surround it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="michael-arad" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New monuments on the Mall" -->
The Martin Luther King, Jr. Memorial was dedicated October 16, 2011 — the first memorial to an African American on the National Mall area. The National Museum of African American History and Culture opened September 24, 2016, its three-tiered bronze "corona" designed by architect David Adjaye, the lattice patterned on ironwork made by enslaved and free Black craftsmen in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Monuments coming down — ongoing history" -->
After the Charleston church murders (2015), the deadly Charlottesville rally held around a Lee statue (2017), and the George Floyd protests (2020), hundreds of Confederate monuments were removed — the Southern Poverty Law Center (an advocacy group's count, labeled as such) tallied at least 168 Confederate symbols removed in 2020 alone; Richmond's giant Robert E. Lee statue came down September 8, 2021 (news accounts: Washington Post, AP). Hundreds still stand, and the argument continues — what monuments are for is the `how-we-know` afterword's question. Also in these years: the Gateway Arch grounds became Gateway Arch National Park (2018), and Angel Island's poem-carved barracks — National Historic Landmark since 1997 — finished restoration in 2009.
