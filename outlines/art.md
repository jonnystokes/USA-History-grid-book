## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The oldest art made in this land that still survives was made to be used. Pots held food and water. Baskets carried it. Robes and blankets were worn. Carved and painted objects belonged to ceremonies. Almost none of the makers' names survive, because these nations kept their records in speech, in song, and in the objects themselves rather than in writing. The traditions did not stop: people are working in them today, and those living artists are named and documented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pottery, weaving, and carving" -->
Regional traditions to name with dates and places [VERIFY each]:
- Ancestral Puebloan black-on-white painted pottery in the Southwest.
- Mississippian shell gorgets and hammered copper plates from Cahokia and Etowah.
- Northwest Coast cedar carving, bentwood boxes, and painted house fronts.
- Southeastern river-cane double-weave basketry.
- Great Lakes and Northeast porcupine-quill embroidery.
- Effigy pipes and carved stone from the Ohio valley.

Say who holds these objects now and how they got them — the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian and the university collections [VERIFY holdings; `how-we-know` leads on the taking].

Petroglyphs pecked into stone and pictographs painted on it, across the Southwest, the Great Basin, and the Columbia Plateau [VERIFY sites and date ranges]. The Ohio and Mississippi valley earthworks are built form as well as engineering [VERIFY; `landmarks` leads on the mounds as places].

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Almost no Europeans lived in this land in the 1500s, so almost no European art was made here. What survives from them is a set of drawings and watercolors made to show people back in Europe what the place and its people looked like, and painted and carved decoration inside the first Spanish mission churches. Native artists went on working. Where Spanish missionaries took hold, they destroyed religious objects and forbade some ceremonies; this outline needs the documented cases, places, and dates rather than a general statement [VERIFY].

They were made for English readers and investors deciding whether to pay for a colony, not for the people in them, and the engravings later made from them in Europe changed what the pictures showed [VERIFY what changed].

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial painting in the 1600s was mostly plain portraits of people who could pay for them, and most of those painters' names were never written down. The other body of work that survives is the carved gravestones in New England burying grounds, and most of those carvers are unnamed too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Limners and gravestone carvers" -->
Traveling painters were called limners. The portrait known as *Mrs. Elizabeth Freake and Baby Mary*, painted in Massachusetts about 1671 to 1674, is by a painter whose name is not known [VERIFY dates and holding museum]. Slate headstones carved with winged skulls and soul effigies; needlework samplers stitched by girls; joined furniture and painted chests.

## 1700 to 1750

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
American painters found a subject of their own: the land. Large landscape paintings of the Hudson valley, the Catskills, and the western mountains sold to city buyers who would never stand in those places. Photography arrived in 1839, and within about ten years a portrait was something an ordinary family could pay for.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Painters stopped agreeing about what a picture was supposed to look like. Photographers argued that a photograph was art.
