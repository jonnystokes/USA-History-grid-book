## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the art made in this land was made by Native nations, and most of it was made to be used. Potters in the Southwest painted bowls and jars. Carvers in the Ohio valley shaped stone pipes into birds and animals. Workers in the Mississippian towns of the Southeast hammered copper into sheets and cut pictures into shell. People along the Northwest Coast carved bone, antler and stone. The names of the people who made these things were not written down, and none survive. Many of the objects that survive came out of graves and burial mounds. In the 1800s and 1900s diggers took them out and sold them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painted pottery in the Southwest" -->
- Ancestral Pueblo potters made black-on-white pottery: white clay painted with black designs. They built each pot from coils of clay, then scraped the coils smooth. The black paint came from boiled plants such as beeweed, or from crushed iron-rich rock. The brushes were yucca fibre (NPS). At Mesa Verde the style called Mesa Verde Black-on-white was made from about 1180 to the late 1200s.
- Mimbres potters in southwestern New Mexico painted bowls with people, animals and patterns from about 1000 to 1130 or 1150 (sources give both end dates). Many bowls were buried with the dead, turned over the head, with a hole punched through the bottom.
- Petroglyphs are pictures cut or pecked into rock. Pictographs are painted on it. On the cliffs west of Albuquerque, ancestral Pueblo people cut about 24,000 to 25,000 petroglyphs, most between 1300 and the 1680s, some as old as 2000 BC (NPS). Some Pueblo people use them in ceremonies today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pipes, copper and shell in the Ohio and Mississippi valleys" -->
- Hopewell carvers in Ohio, about 100 BC to AD 400 or 500, carved smoking pipes from stone in the shapes of hawks, ducks, frogs, otters and other animals. One mound, Tremper Mound, held 136 pipes. Most were Ohio pipestone. Some were red pipestone carried from quarries in Minnesota (Ohio History Connection).
- At Etowah, in Georgia, Mississippian metalworkers hammered copper sheets and pushed designs up from the back, about 1250 to 1325. The best-known plates show a winged man called the Birdman.
- Near Cahokia, in Illinois, a carver made a kneeling woman with a hoe from soft red flint clay, between 1050 and 1200. Archaeologists call it the Birger figurine.
- At Spiro, in Oklahoma, people built twelve mounds between 850 and 1450. One of them, Craig Mound, held engraved shell cups and gorgets (neck pieces), copper, baskets and cloth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carving on the Northwest Coast" -->
People on the Northwest Coast carved bone, stone and horn at least 5,000 years ago (Burke Museum). Most of their work was cedar, and wood rots, so little of it survives from before 1500. A woven cedar-bark hat found at Wapato Creek, in Washington, dates from 1450 to 1620. The carving tradition continues today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who dug these objects up, and where they went" -->
- 1846: Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis dug into the Mound City mounds in Ohio and took more than 200 stone pipes. The pipes had been broken and laid together under the floor of a ceremonial building (Ohio History Connection). In 1864 Davis sold his collection to William Blackmore, an Englishman, for $10,000. The British Museum in London bought the collection in 1931 (NPS).
- 1885: John Rogan, digging for the Smithsonian, took the Birdman copper plates from a stone-lined grave at Etowah. The Smithsonian in Washington holds them.
- 1933 to 1935: a group of men calling themselves the Pocola Mining Company leased Craig Mound at Spiro and dug into it for things to sell. In two years they destroyed about a third of the mound and sold thousands of objects to collectors around the world. Witnesses said the diggers crushed engraved shells underfoot and piled human bones at the edge of their camp, and that before they left they blew up the central burial chamber with black powder (David La Vere's history, as told in *405 Magazine*). Oklahoma lawmakers passed a licensing law, and the company was shut down in November 1935. University of Oklahoma archaeologists later took more than 600 burials out of the same mound (Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History).
- From the 1960s, looters used bulldozers and earth movers on Mimbres village sites to reach the bowls in the graves, and destroyed many sites completely. No Mimbres site escaped looting (Trafficking Culture).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (leads on the nations' claims and NAGPRA, 1990) · `landmarks` (leads on the mounds as places) · `science` (the collecting of the dead).

### Nathan Jackson
> **Who:** Tlingit carver of totem poles, masks, canoes and clan crest panels, of the Sockeye Clan on the Raven side of the Chilkoot Tlingit. · **When and where:** born August 29, 1938, in southeastern Alaska. Lives in Ketchikan.

- He learned his people's traditions from his clan uncle and grandfather, Jack David. He began carving small totem poles while in hospital with pneumonia for 54 days, He told the nurses, "Those totem poles there, they have a price on them" (KRBD, 2017). Sources differ on when this happened: after his Army service ended in 1959 (NEA), or in his mid-30s (KRBD).
- He studied at the Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe, and learned adze carving from Duane Pasco. He taught apprentices in Alaska. In 1995 he received a National Heritage Fellowship, the country's top award for traditional artists.
- In his words: "the traditional way of life is something I identify with" (NEA).
- In 2024, at 86, he carved an eight-foot pole of the Tlingit story of Raven and Fog Woman. He started it at Saxman, Alaska, and finished it at the Arts Council of Princeton, New Jersey (Jersey Arts, 2024).

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, two European artists drew this land and its people from life, and both did it for colonies that failed. Jacques Le Moyne drew French Florida in 1564. John White painted the coast of what is now North Carolina in 1585. Almost no Europeans lived here yet, so almost no other European art was made here. Native artists went on working in their nations' own traditions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jacques Le Moyne in Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
- The French brought Jacques Le Moyne to Fort Caroline, their colony in Florida, as its official artist in 1564. He drew the fort, the settlers, the land and the Native people the French met.
- When Spanish soldiers attacked the fort in 1565, Le Moyne escaped, but he lost all his drawings. Back in France he drew them again from memory (WUWF, 2024).
- After he died in 1588, his widow sold the pictures to Theodor de Bry, an engraver who printed them in 1591. De Bry changed the people and the landscapes to sell more books.
- One painting by Le Moyne survives, of the French commander René de Laudonnière with the Timucua leader Athore. The New York Public Library holds it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="John White's watercolors, 1585" -->
- John White sailed to Roanoke Island in 1585 with the English expedition and painted more than seventy watercolors in about thirteen months: Algonquian towns, people, dances, fishing, plants, birds and fish.
- In 1590 de Bry printed engravings of White's pictures of people in Thomas Harriot's book about the colony, for readers across Europe. He changed them. In White's watercolor, a Secotan girl holds a doll in English clothes. De Bry's copy also puts a small model of the globe and the heavens in her hands (Public Domain Review).
- The British Museum in London owns the originals. In the 1800s a fire at the Sotheby's auction house soaked them with water, and the silver paint has turned black (Smithsonian magazine, 2008).
- Shared with: `exploration` (leads on the Roanoke colony) · `news-communication` and `science` (lead on Harriot's book).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-white-roanoke-art" name="John White" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John White
> **Who:** English artist on the 1585 Roanoke expedition, and governor of the 1587 colony. · **When and where:** Roanoke Island and the Carolina coast, 1585 to 1586 and 1587. His birth and death years are not known. He was active from 1577 to 1593.

- He drew in black lead and filled the outlines with watercolor, with touches of gold and silver (NCpedia).
- His paintings are the first pictures of this land by an English artist, and the oldest surviving pictures of it painted on the spot. Le Moyne drew Florida twenty-one years earlier, but those drawings were lost.
- In 1587 he left the colonists on Roanoke to get supplies in England. When he got back in August 1590 they were gone, and the word "Croatoan" was carved on a tree (NCpedia). `exploration` tells the colony.

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most art made in the English colonies in the 1600s was made for use: portraits of rich families, carved gravestones, and needlework. Most of the painters' and carvers' names were never written down. In Massachusetts, printers produced the first book in the English colonies in 1640, and a colonist's poems were printed in London in 1650. In New Mexico, Spanish friars had Pueblo workers build mission churches, and other friars burned the masks Pueblo people used in their ceremonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portraits, gravestones and needlework in New England" -->
- A limner was a painter of portraits. The best-known New England portrait of the 1600s shows Elizabeth Freake holding her baby, Mary, painted in Boston between 1671 and 1674. Nobody knows the painter's name. About ten Boston portraits from 1670 to 1674 are by the same hand. The Freakes were one of the richest families in Boston (Worcester Art Museum holds it).
- Puritan gravestones carried winged skulls, crossed bones and hourglasses, often with the Latin words "Memento Mori," meaning "remember you will die." Joseph Lamson (1658 to 1722) of Charlestown, Massachusetts, is one carver whose name is known. He carved skulls with hooked eyebrows and borders of fruit, vines and flowers. His sons and grandson carved gravestones after him (Woburn Historical Society).
- Girls learned needlework from their mothers and stitched samplers, long strips of cloth that recorded stitches and patterns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first books printed and written here" -->
- In 1638 the Reverend Jose Glover sailed for Massachusetts with a printing press and died on the voyage. His widow, Elizabeth Glover, set the press up in Cambridge, Massachusetts, under Stephen Daye, a locksmith. In 1640 Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes*, called the *Bay Psalm Book*, the first book printed in the English colonies. About 30 ministers had put the psalms into English verse for singing. Daye printed about 1,700 copies at twenty pence each. Eleven survive (Library of Congress).
- Anne Bradstreet's *The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America* was printed in London in 1650, the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies. The title page calls the writer only "a Gentlewoman of those parts."
- Shared with: `news-communication` (leads on the printing trade) · `music` (the psalm singing) · `religion` (the psalms in church).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mission churches and burned masks in New Mexico" -->
- Spanish soldiers under Juan de Oñate took Acoma Pueblo by force in 1599 (`native-nations` tells it). From about 1629 the friar Juan Ramírez had Acoma workers build the church of San Esteban del Rey on top of their mesa. They carried every load up the cliffs, including 40-foot roof beams cut in mountains 30 to 40 miles away (NPS). One source calls the workers enslaved. The records reached here do not say whether they were paid. It is the oldest surviving European church in New Mexico.
- From 1656 to 1665, the Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies and ordered friars to go into the kivas, the Pueblo ceremonial rooms, and burn the masks and other sacred objects they found there (NMAI). Spanish soldiers raided kivas and burned masks too (National Library of Medicine). No surviving record counts the masks. In 1680 Pueblo people rose up, killed 21 friars and burned the mission churches.
- Shared with: `religion` (leads on the missions and the suppression) · `native-nations` (leads on Acoma in 1599 and the 1680 revolt) · `landmarks` (the churches as places).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-bradstreet" name="Anne Bradstreet" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Bradstreet
> **Who:** Poet in the Massachusetts Bay colony. · **When and where:** born in England in 1612, came to Massachusetts in 1630, died in 1672.

- She married Simon Bradstreet about 1628, and they had eight children (New-York Historical Society).
- Her brother-in-law, the Reverend John Woodbridge, took her poems to London, where they were printed in 1650. Woodbridge's preface said she had not known. Later she wrote a poem calling him a "friend more loving than true" for taking them.
- The biographer Charlotte Gordon studied the poems and the letters and found that Bradstreet knew of the plan and helped with it from the start. One poem in the book is about events of 1649, after Woodbridge had sailed, so someone sent it on to him (Commonplace).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-bradstreet" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="loara-standish-sampler" name="Loara Standish" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Loara Standish
> **Who:** Daughter of Myles and Barbara Standish of Plymouth Colony, and maker of the oldest known sampler made in America. · **When and where:** Duxbury, Plymouth Colony, in the 1640s or about 1653. Born after 1627, died before 1655.

- She stitched the sampler in silk on linen, probably in her teens, in bands of roses, carnations, oak leaves and acorns (Pilgrim Hall Museum).
- She stitched a verse at the bottom, the first known on an American sampler. It begins "Loara Standish is my name Lorde guide my hart that I may doe thy will."
- The Standish and Alden families kept the sampler until 1844, when Lucius Alden gave it to Pilgrim Hall Museum in Plymouth. It is faded from years of hanging. Sources date it to the 1640s (Textile Research Centre, Leiden) or about 1653 (Pilgrim Hall).

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, a few trained painters from Europe settled in the colonies and made a living painting portraits of merchants, planters and officials. Only families with money could pay for one. Some of those families were rich from slavery, and two portraits of this period show that. One painter painted two Lenape leaders just before William Penn's sons took a large part of their people's land. Native art also crossed the ocean the other way: in 1725 Cherokee river-cane baskets were taken to London, where they are still kept.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portrait painters in the colonies" -->
- Henrietta Johnston drew pastel portraits in Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708. A pastel is a stick of powdered color. She was the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies (New-York Historical Society).
- John Smibert, trained as a painter in Europe, came to Boston in 1729 or 1730 and painted its merchants.
- Justus Engelhardt Kühn, from Germany, painted the children of rich Maryland families in Annapolis from about 1708 until he died in 1717.
- Gustavus Hesselius (about 1682 to 1755), from Sweden, painted portraits in Philadelphia and built church organs.
- Robert Feke painted in Newport, Rhode Island, and Boston.
- A painter's customers were merchants, planters and officials. Smibert still had to open a shop selling paint and prints to earn enough (Britannica).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portraits paid for with slavery's money" -->
- About 1710, Kühn painted Henry Darnall III, the son of a rich Maryland family, at about eight years old, in fine clothes with a bow. Beside him stands a Black boy wearing a metal collar around his neck. The boy was enslaved, and his name was not written down. The museum that holds the picture, in Baltimore, calls him "Unnamed attendant." The painting is called the earliest known formal painting of an enslaved African in America (Sartle).
- In 1741 Feke painted Isaac Royall Jr., 22, with his wife, baby daughter, sister and sister-in-law. His father got rich on a sugar plantation on Antigua, worked by enslaved people, and by trading in sugar, rum and enslaved people. In 1737 the family moved to Medford, Massachusetts, and brought at least 27 enslaved Africans with them (Royall House and Slave Quarters). Harvard Law School owns the painting.
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (leads on slavery in the North and in Maryland).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two Lenape leaders painted, 1735" -->
- In 1735 Hesselius painted the Lenape leaders Lapowinsa and Tishcohan for John Penn, one of William Penn's sons. They are the only two Lenape chiefs whose portraits are known to survive (Wikipedia). The Historical Society of Pennsylvania owns them (Colonial Society of Massachusetts).
- In 1737 both men signed the Walking Purchase with John and Thomas Penn. Under that deal the Penns took more than a thousand square miles of Lenape land, and the Lenape leader Nutimus and his people had to move (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia).
- Shared with: `native-nations` (leads on the Walking Purchase).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cherokee baskets taken to London, 1725" -->
- Cherokee weavers split river cane into thin strips for baskets and dyed some strips with walnut and pokeberry.
- In 1725 a South Carolina colonial governor took a set of Cherokee double-weave baskets to London. Double weave means two baskets woven one inside the other from the same strips, so both the inside and the outside show a finished pattern. A note tied to them says, "They will keep anything in them from being wetted by rain." The British Museum holds them (Western Carolina University).
- Printed almanacs, such as Benjamin Franklin's *Poor Richard's Almanack*, belong to `news-communication`.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="henrietta-johnston" name="Henrietta Johnston" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henrietta Johnston
> **Who:** Pastel portrait artist, the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies. · **When and where:** born in northern France about 1674. Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708 until she died there on March 9, 1729.

- Her family fled France for London in 1687 to escape religious persecution. She learned pastel portraits in Ireland, where she lived with her first husband. He died in 1704 and left her with two small children.
- Her second husband, Gideon Johnston, an Anglican minister, moved the family to Charleston in 1708. They were short of money, so she drew portraits of the town's leading families for pay.
- Pastels could only be bought in England, so in 1711 she sailed to London for supplies. Pirates attacked her ship on the way back, and she survived.
- After Gideon died in 1716 she supported the family alone. About three dozen of her portraits survive (New-York Historical Society).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henrietta-johnston" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-smibert" name="John Smibert" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Smibert
> **Who:** Scottish portrait painter, and later designer of Faneuil Hall in Boston. · **When and where:** born in Edinburgh in 1688. Newport, Rhode Island, in 1729, then Boston until he died in 1751.

- He came with George Berkeley, a minister who planned a college in Bermuda where Smibert would teach art. The college was never founded. Smibert painted Berkeley and his party, a picture now at Yale.
- In Boston he painted merchants such as Peter Faneuil, and Judge Samuel Sewall.
- In 1734 he opened a shop selling paint and prints. Upstairs he hung copies he had painted of European masterpieces and plaster casts of statues. The historian Richard Saunders calls it "America's first art gallery." John Singleton Copley, John Trumbull and other younger painters learned from it (Wikipedia, Encyclopedia.com).
- He drew the plans for Faneuil Hall in 1742 (Britannica).

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, American painters made the portraits of the men who led the Revolution, and several of them trained in London. The first book of poems by an African American, Phillis Wheatley's *Poems*, was printed in London in 1773 while she was still enslaved in Boston. Enslaved Black artists worked in these same years, and a few records keep their names. Mercy Otis Warren wrote plays against British rule, and Patience Wright modelled portraits in wax.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters of the Revolution" -->
- John Singleton Copley was born in Boston in 1738. In 1766 he sent a painting of a boy with a pet flying squirrel to London, where the painters Joshua Reynolds and Benjamin West praised it. In 1774 he left America for England, and he died in London in 1815 (Britannica).
- Benjamin West, born in Pennsylvania, settled in London in 1763 and became painter to King George III. He took in young American painters, among them Gilbert Stuart, and gave Stuart work, a studio and a place to live (Mount Vernon).
- Charles Willson Peale, a saddle maker from Maryland who studied painting in England for three years, painted George Washington from life seven times, the first time at Mount Vernon in May 1772 (Mount Vernon).
- In 1784 Peale opened a paid exhibit of 44 portraits of Revolutionary leaders in his Philadelphia home and called it the Philadelphia Museum. He later added animals, fossils, and clothing, weapons and tools from Native nations (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia). `science` leads on the museum's mastodon, dug up in 1801.
- Patience Wright (1725 to 1786), from New Jersey, modelled portraits in colored wax. In 1772 she moved to London and showed wax figures of politicians, actors and nobles (Britannica).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved artists, and people used as payment" -->
- By 1776 a Maryland planter had paid Peale for portraits by giving him two enslaved people, Scarborough and Lucy. Peale freed them in 1786. Their son, Moses Williams, born about 1777, was held in Peale's household under Pennsylvania's 1780 gradual abolition law until about 1802 (Davis Art). The planter's name is not recorded in the source.
- Scipio Moorhead was enslaved by the Reverend John Moorhead of Boston. Scholars think the engraved portrait of Phillis Wheatley at the front of her book is based on a painting by him. None of his signed work survives. Wheatley wrote him a poem, "To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works."
- Joshua Johnson was born enslaved in Maryland in 1763. In 1782 his white father, George Johnson, bought him for £25 and signed a manumission, a legal paper that freed him. By 1796 he was listed in Baltimore as a portrait painter. In 1798 he advertised himself as a "self-taught genius" who had faced "many insuperable obstacles" (Maryland State Archives).
- Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (leads on slavery in the North and on gradual abolition).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Writers of the Revolution" -->
- Phillis Wheatley's *Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral* came out in London in September 1773. The title page calls her "Negro Servant to Mr. John Wheatley, of Boston."
- Mercy Otis Warren (1728 to 1814) of Massachusetts wrote plays that mocked the British governor and other British colonial leaders, from *The Adulateur* in 1772 to *The Group* in 1775. All were printed without her name. The National Women's History Museum says she likely did this to avoid punishment and so that her work would be taken seriously. `storytelling-evolution` takes the plays as performance.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="phillis-wheatley" name="Phillis Wheatley" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Phillis Wheatley
> **Who:** Poet, the first African American to publish a book of poems, enslaved in Boston when it was printed. · **When and where:** taken from West Africa to Boston in 1761, aged seven or eight. Book printed in London in 1773. Died in Boston in 1784, about 31 years old.

- John Wheatley, who bought her, wrote that she learned English in sixteen months and was taught at home, with no school (the book's preface).
- The publisher expected readers to doubt that an enslaved African girl could write the poems. So 18 Boston men signed a statement printed at the front of the book. It says she "has been examined by some of the best Judges," and that the poems were "written by PHILLIS, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and ... now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave." The signers included Governor Thomas Hutchinson, John Hancock, the Reverend John Moorhead, and John Wheatley, "her Master."
- The Wheatleys freed her by November 1773, weeks after the book appeared. She married John Peters in 1778 and died poor in 1784 (Museum of the American Revolution). `slavery-freedom` tells her enslavement and her life in freedom.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="phillis-wheatley" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="prince-demah" name="Prince Demah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Prince Demah
> **Who:** Enslaved portrait painter in Boston, and later a free militia soldier. · **When and where:** baptized in Boston in 1745. Worked in Boston and London, 1769 to 1778. Died in Boston in March 1778.

- In 1769 the merchant Henry Barnes of Marlborough, Massachusetts, bought Prince. Prince's mother, Daphney, was already enslaved by the Barnes family. Barnes's wife Christian wrote that they planned on "improving his genius in painting."
- In 1770 Barnes took him to London, where a painter named Pine, probably Robert Edge Pine, gave him lessons. Barnes feared he would "attempt his freedom" and wrote, "I do not let [Prince] converse with any of his own colour here."
- Back in Boston in 1773, a newspaper advertisement offered a "Negro Artist" who "takes Faces at the lowest Rates." That year he painted the merchant William Duguid and signed it "Prince Demah Barnes." It is now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
- The Barneses sided with the king and left for England in 1775. In April 1777 Prince enlisted in a Massachusetts artillery regiment. On March 11, 1778, as "Prince Demah, limner" and "free Negro," he wrote a will leaving everything to his mother. He died a week later (Peck and Bagger, *The Magazine Antiques*, 2015).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="prince-demah" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-willson-peale" name="Charles Willson Peale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Willson Peale
> **Who:** Painter of the Revolution's leaders and founder of the Philadelphia Museum. · **When and where:** born in Chester, Maryland, in 1741. Annapolis, then Philadelphia. Died in 1827.

- He trained as a saddle maker, then studied painting in England for three years. In Annapolis he joined the Sons of Liberty (Mount Vernon).
- Washington sat for him seven times, starting in 1772 (Mount Vernon).
- He turned his portrait gallery into a paid museum in 1784. His family ran it for about 50 years and sold the collection in 1849 and 1854.
- He was paid for portraits with two enslaved people, Scarborough and Lucy, and freed them in 1786 (Davis Art).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-willson-peale" -->

### Gilbert Stuart
> **Who:** Portrait painter whose unfinished picture of George Washington is the model for the face on the one-dollar bill. · **When and where:** born in Rhode Island on December 3, 1755. London and Dublin, then the United States from 1793. Died in 1828.

- In London, Benjamin West gave him work and a studio. He left London for Dublin in 1787, and Dublin for New York in 1793, both times to get away from people he owed money (Mount Vernon).
- In 1796 Martha Washington ordered portraits of her husband and herself. Stuart left Washington's unfinished, kept it, and never gave it to her, though she asked for it again after Washington died in 1799.
- He used the head to paint more than a hundred copies, 130 by one count, and sold them for $100 each. An engraving of it, flipped left to right, is on the one-dollar bill. The Museum of Fine Arts in Boston and the National Portrait Gallery in Washington have owned the original together since 1980.

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Painters stopped agreeing about what a picture was supposed to look like. Photographers argued that a photograph was art.
