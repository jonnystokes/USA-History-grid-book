## Before 1500
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People here worked metals for thousands of years without any idea of an element — because nobody on Earth had that idea yet. The land was entirely Native land, and its metalworkers are known through their pits and tools, not their names.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="among the world's first coppersmiths" -->
- The Old Copper Complex of the Lake Superior country (present-day Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota): Native miners dug veins of pure "native" copper and hammered it into spearpoints, knives, awls, fishhooks, and ornaments. A 2021 re-dating (53 radiocarbon dates, Pompeani et al., *Radiocarbon*) puts the start **at least 9,500 years ago** — about 3,500 years earlier than once thought, peak about 7,000–5,000 years ago. Say "among the world's first coppersmiths," not "the first" — the oldest documented Middle East copper pendant is about 8,700 years old (*Science* news, Mar 2021; Archaeology Magazine).
- Lake Superior copper moved hundreds of miles on Native trade networks, the same routes that carried shells, turquoise, and obsidian. Shared with: `native-nations` (the nations and their trade).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="iron from the sky" -->
- Before smelting, the only workable iron metal on the continent fell from space. Twenty-two iron beads from Hopewell burial mounds near Havana, Illinois (excavated 1945), made about 2,000 years ago, came from the Anoka, Minnesota meteorite — metal carried or traded several hundred miles and worked with the same hammering skills used on copper (McCoy et al., *Journal of Archaeological Science* 2017; Smithsonian).
- Also in use: gold and silver ornaments in some regions, red and yellow ochre pigments (iron oxides), salt gathered and traded, flint and chert tools — materials skill without atomic theory. No named individuals are documented this far back; this era has no featured story on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A thin era, said plainly: element science existed nowhere on Earth yet — Europe was still in the age of alchemy, and the metals in use here were the same copper, gold, and meteoritic iron as before.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="gold hunts that found nothing" -->
- Spaniards came hunting two elements. De Soto (1539–43) crossed the Southeast and Coronado (1540–42) the Southwest chasing stories of golden cities; neither found gold or silver in the future United States. The century's great silver strikes were in Mexico and South America. Shared with: `exploration` (the journeys themselves).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first American industry that made a metal was iron, and it took two tries — one ended in an attack, one ran for about two decades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Falling Creek and Saugus" -->
- Falling Creek, Virginia: the Virginia Company began building an ironworks in 1619 near present-day Richmond; on March 22, 1622, forces coordinated by the Powhatan leader Opechancanough destroyed the works and killed twenty-seven people there, part of the wide 1622 attacks. It was never successfully rebuilt (Encyclopedia Virginia; Virginia DHR). Shared with: `native-nations` (the 1622 war from the Powhatan side).
- Saugus, Massachusetts: the works called Hammersmith, begun 1646 under Richard Leader on the Saugus River — the first integrated ironworks in North America: blast furnace, forge, and a rolling and slitting mill on one site, powered by seven waterwheels, fed on bog iron scooped from swamps and ponds. It ran until about 1668–1670 and is now Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site (NPS; ASME).
- The iron story here is use and industry, not discovery — iron had been smelted in the Old World for thousands of years. Abroad, chemistry began separating from alchemy (Boyle's *Sceptical Chymist*, 1661); every element discovery of the century (phosphorus, Hamburg, 1669) happened in Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonial iron grew from a few furnaces into a world-scale industry — everyday metals, no discoveries. Element science was still entirely European.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="iron third in the world, and the Iron Act" -->
- Ironworks spread through New England, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, and New Jersey; by mid-century the colonies exported raw iron to Britain. By the Revolution colonial iron was commonly ranked third in the world, ahead of England and Wales (Britannica; Penn Libraries).
- Parliament's Iron Act of 1750 welcomed colonial raw iron but banned new colonial mills for finished ironware. Colonists ignored it — more than sixty new works were built in defiance, about twenty in Pennsylvania (Britannica "Iron Act").
- Other elements in colonial life: lead for shot and pewter, copper and brass for kettles and buttons, silver and gold arriving as Spanish coin. Shared with: `money` (coin as a money system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In Europe, chemistry became a science and defined what an element is (Lavoisier, Scheele, and others). The American contribution was use, ore — and one immigrant who had already made his discovery before he arrived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="three elements in every pocket" -->
- The Coinage Act of April 2, 1792 created the U.S. Mint and a coinage of gold (eagles), silver (dollars and smaller), and copper (cents) — element chemistry as daily money (U.S. Mint history). Shared with: `money` (lead — the money system).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-priestley" name="Joseph Priestley" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Priestley
> **Who:** the English chemist credited with discovering oxygen, who spent his last decade as an immigrant in Pennsylvania. · **When and where:** discovered oxygen August 1, 1774, in England; emigrated 1794; Northumberland, Pennsylvania, until his death February 6, 1804.
Heated mercuric oxide with a burning lens and isolated the gas later named oxygen — state the shared history honestly: Scheele in Sweden found it earlier but published later, and Lavoisier named it. Priestley also invented carbonated water (Leeds, 1767) and gave rubber its English name in 1770, for rubbing out pencil marks. A dissenting minister, he saw a mob burn his Birmingham home and laboratory in 1791 and left England in 1794. At Northumberland he built the best chemical laboratory in the young country (house finished 1798) and identified carbon monoxide there. American chemists met at his house in 1874, on the oxygen centennial — a gathering that led toward the American Chemical Society (1876); the house is a National Historic Landmark (ACS landmark; Science History Institute; Joseph Priestley House). **No false first: the discovery was English.** Shared with: `immigration` (the arrival).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-priestley" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="conrad-reed" name="Conrad Reed" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Conrad Reed
> **Who:** the 12-year-old farm boy who found the first authenticated gold in the United States. · **When and where:** Little Meadow Creek, Cabarrus County, North Carolina, 1799.
Pulled a heavy yellow rock — a 17-pound gold nugget — out of the creek on his father John Reed's farm. The family did not know what it was and used it as a doorstop for about three years, until a jeweler identified it in 1802 and bought it from John Reed for $3.50, roughly a week's wages, when it was worth about $3,600. The first authenticated U.S. gold discovery; the mining it set off unfolds in the next era (NCpedia; Reed Gold Mine state historic site). By the boundary rule he stays here in 1750–1800.

## 1800 to 1850
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
America's first mineral rushes began — and the first big one was copper, not gold. Still no American element discovery: while Davy, Berzelius, and others isolated element after element in Europe, none was found here. An honest "not yet."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="gold in the South, copper in the North" -->
- Mining grew at Reed's farm and neighboring North Carolina creeks from 1803; the Georgia gold rush followed in 1829. North Carolina led U.S. gold production until California (NCpedia). Shared with: `migration` (the 1849 crowds) · `economy` (the money).
- The nation's first mineral rush was Michigan copper. After state geologist Douglass Houghton reported on the Keweenaw Peninsula's pure native copper in the early 1840s, thousands of prospectors poured into the Upper Peninsula from 1843 — five years before California gold. By 1849 the district produced 96 percent of U.S. copper, dug from veins Native miners had worked for thousands of years (NPS Keweenaw NHP).
- Lead mining grew in the Galena district of Illinois and Wisconsin — the town of Galena is named for the lead ore — and in Missouri. Shared with: `economy` (mining as business).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Ontonagon Boulder" -->
- A 3,708-pound boulder of pure native copper from the Ontonagon River country, long known and held sacred by the Ojibwe, was hauled away in 1843 by Detroit merchant Julius Eldred, seized by the War Department, and placed in the Smithsonian in 1860. The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community formally asked for its return in 1991; the Smithsonian refused. State the taking and the still-open request plainly (Smithsonian records; The Mining Gazette 2025). Shared with: `native-nations` (lead — the taking and the refused repatriation).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The periodic table was published abroad, and the first great American element story was not a discovery but a process — a woodshed experiment that cut aluminum's price roughly ninety percent within a decade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="silver underfoot, and a table from Russia" -->
- The Comstock Lode, Virginia City, Nevada, 1859 — the richest silver deposit found in North America (Britannica). Shared with: `migration` (the rush) · `money` (the silver question).
- Dmitri Mendeleev published the periodic system in Russia in 1869 and used it to predict undiscovered elements. Context the chapter needs: the table every later American discovery slotted into was a Russian invention — a fact that pays off in 1955 when Berkeley names element 101 for him.
- Copper became the nerve metal of cities as electric light and the telephone spread; Michigan, then Butte, Montana, supplied the wire. Shared with: `technology` (the inventions) · `economy` (the copper business).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="aluminum: from Tiffany's showpiece to everyday metal" -->
- In 1884 the government capped the finished Washington Monument with a 100-ounce aluminum pyramid — aluminum then a semiprecious rarity at about $1 an ounce, the price of silver. Philadelphia foundryman William Frishmuth cast it for $225, the largest aluminum casting yet made; before installation it sat on the floor at Tiffany's in New York so visitors could step over the top of the Washington Monument. Set in place December 6, 1884 (NPS; TMS/JOM; National Archives *Prologue*). Shared with: `landmarks` (the monument itself).
- After Hall's 1886 process reached industry, aluminum fell from about $12 a pound in the early 1880s to about $2, then under $1 by the mid-1890s (about 78 cents by 1893). His backers' Pittsburgh Reduction Company (1888) became the Aluminum Company of America — Alcoa — in 1907. A metal displayed at Tiffany's became foil and pots-and-pans cheap (Science History Institute; ACS landmark). Shared with: `economy` (Alcoa as an industry).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" name="Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall
> **Who:** the 22-year-old Ohio college graduate who invented the cheap way to make aluminum, and the chemist sister whose notes helped win the patent fight. · **When and where:** the family woodshed, Oberlin, Ohio, February 23, 1886.
Fresh out of Oberlin College, working with homemade batteries, Hall dissolved aluminum oxide in molten cryolite, ran electricity through it, and got beads of aluminum metal. His older sister Julia Brainerd Hall, an Oberlin chemistry graduate, assisted, witnessed, and documented the experiments — her notes later mattered in the patent fight. The Frenchman Paul Héroult hit the same process independently the same year (both men born 1863, both discovered it 1886, both died 1914), so it is fairly called the Hall–Héroult process — it still makes the world's aluminum (ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark, dedicated at Oberlin 1997; Science History Institute). **No false first on the element: aluminum was known abroad; the American story is the process.**
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the helium near-miss" -->
- About 1890, USGS chemist William Hillebrand extracted an unreactive gas from uranium ore and recorded it as nitrogen. In 1895 William Ramsay in London, following up that paper, ran the gas through a spectroscope and saw the yellow line of helium — known in the sun since 1868, never before found on Earth. The element credit is British; the ore-gas trail ran through American rock, and the American helium story proper starts in the next era (Chemistry World; Ramsay biographies).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1900 to 1950
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In fifty years the United States went from no element discoveries to making elements that had never existed — and part of the era's element story was paid for in workers' bodies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the well that would not burn" -->
- May 1903: a drilling company hit a roaring gas well on William Greenwell's farm near Dexter, Kansas. The town pushed a burning hay bale toward the gusher to light a celebration flame — and the gas put the fire out. Locals scoffed at the "wind gas"; geologist Erasmus Haworth carried samples to the University of Kansas, where Hamilton P. Cady and David F. McFarland showed on December 7, 1905 that it was 1.84 percent helium — an element thought vanishingly rare on Earth, sitting in quantity under the Great Plains (ACS landmark 2000; KU History).
- Helium went strategic: the Helium Act of 1925 created federal production and the Federal Helium Reserve, stored underground in the Bush Dome at the Cliffside field near Amarillo, Texas — for decades nearly all the world's helium. A 1927 law barred exports, which is why Germany's Hindenburg flew on flammable hydrogen and burned at Lakehurst on May 6, 1937. After the disaster FDR weighed selling helium to Germany; Interior Secretary Harold Ickes refused after Hitler annexed Austria in 1938 (Texas State Historical Association; State Dept. FRUS 1938). Shared with: `economy` (lead — the helium industry) · `war` (strategic supply) · `disasters` (the Hindenburg fire).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the radium craze" -->
- Radium — discovered by the Curies in France in 1898, no false first — was painted onto watch dials, sold in quack tonics, and marketed as health itself. This chapter's angle is the element: what radium is, why it glows, and how it settles in bone.
- Wealthy Pittsburgh industrialist Eben Byers drank about 1,400 bottles of Radithor, radium dissolved in water, from 1927–30; it destroyed his bones and killed him in 1932, and his death pushed the government to act against radioactive cures. (Writer's caution from the bank: the famous "jaw came off" headline is a 1990 retrospective, not from 1932.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="radium-girls-elements" name="The Radium Girls" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The Radium Girls
> **Who:** young women — many teenagers — who painted glowing radium numbers on watch and instrument dials and were poisoned by the paint. · **When and where:** U.S. Radium Corporation, Orange, New Jersey, from 1917; Radium Dial, Ottawa, Illinois, into the 1930s.
Taught to point their brushes with their lips — "lip, dip, paint" — they went home glowing; some were called ghost girls. The radium settled in their bones like calcium; jaws crumbled ("radium jaw"), and women in their twenties began dying. Grace Fryer, painting from April 10, 1917, led four dying co-workers into court in 1927; the company settled in June 1928 — $10,000 each, a $600-a-year pension, and medical bills. In Illinois, Catherine Wolfe Donohue, so ill she collapsed testifying, won in 1938 and died within months (Library of Congress "Living Dead Women"; History.com). The court fight and what it changed for workers leads in `work-workers`, which carries the film *Radium Girls* (2018) with Fryer's story; the health science is `health`'s. This chapter leads on the element itself.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="radium-girls-elements" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="a false first, and the machine that opened the door" -->
- 1931: physicist Fred Allison of Alabama Polytechnic (now Auburn) announced element 85 ("alabamine") and element 87 ("virginium"), found with his "magneto-optical" apparatus. No other lab could reproduce it; the method was an illusion of the equipment, and the claims died — a false American first the chapter states plainly. Element 85 was really made at Berkeley in 1940 (Encyclopedia of Alabama; *Time* 1932).
- Ernest Lawrence's first cyclotron (Berkeley, 1930–31) was glass, sealing wax, and bronze, small enough to hold in one hand; it whirled charged particles to high speed in a spiral. Bigger versions followed — the 60-inch of 1939 had a 220-ton magnet — and turned the Radiation Laboratory into an element factory. Nobel Prize in Physics, 1939 (LBL; APS News). Shared with: `technology` (lead — the machine itself) · `science` (the Rad Lab as institution).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="ernest-lawrence-elements" name="Ernest Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ernest Lawrence
> **Who:** inventor of the cyclotron, the machine behind nearly every American element discovery. · **When and where:** Berkeley, California, 1930s–1950s; Nobel Prize 1939; died 1958.
This chapter keeps the element-factory framing: his machines made technetium's foil, astatine, and plutonium's ingredients, and element 103 — lawrencium — carries his name. The machine story itself belongs to `technology`. No film about him exists; he appears as a character in *Oppenheimer* (2023), which belongs to `war` — flagged, not adopted.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ernest-lawrence-elements" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="elements made by people" -->
- Technetium, 1937 — the first artificial element. Lawrence mailed a molybdenum deflector foil from the 37-inch cyclotron to Palermo, Sicily, where Emilio Segrè and Carlo Perrier dug a few billionths of a gram of element 43 out of it and named it "the artificial one." Honest split credit: Italian chemistry, American machine. (Technetium-99m later became a workhorse of hospital imaging — `health`'s slice.) (LANL; Guinness.)
- 1940: astatine — element 85, the one "alabamine" was supposed to be — made by bombarding bismuth in the 60-inch cyclotron (Corson, MacKenzie, Segrè); and neptunium, the first element past uranium (McMillan and Abelson), named for the next planet out (Nobel Foundation; OSTI).
- Plutonium: produced December 14, 1940 (Seaborg, McMillan, Kennedy, Wahl) and chemically proved the night of February 23–24, 1941, in Room 307 of Gilman Hall, now a National Historic Landmark. Seaborg picked the symbol Pu instead of "Pl" as a joke — "pee-yoo," like a smell — and the committee accepted it. Wartime secrecy fell over the work at once; the bomb is `war`'s story (Atomic Heritage; symmetry magazine). Shared with: `war` (lead — the Manhattan Project and the weapons).
- Curium (summer 1944) and americium (1944–45), made in the wartime Met Lab circle, were revealed when a boy on the radio show *Quiz Kids* asked Seaborg on November 11, 1945 whether any new elements had turned up during the war. He said yes — five days before his formal announcement (LANL; RSC podcast).
- Promethium, 1945: Marinsky, Glendenin, and Coryell fished the last missing rare earth out of uranium fission products at Oak Ridge; announced 1947. Grace Mary Coryell suggested naming it for Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods — a pointed name from inside the bomb project. Still the only element ever discovered in Tennessee (Britannica; ORNL).
- December 1949: berkelium — inside this era by two weeks (boundary rule).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-segre-elements" name="Emilio Segrè" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Segrè
> **Who:** the Italian physicist stranded by Mussolini's anti-Jewish laws who stayed in America and co-discovered two elements. · **When and where:** Palermo and Berkeley, 1937–1940s; stayed in the U.S. from 1938; died 1989.
Co-identified technetium in Palermo from Lawrence's mailed foil (1937). Visiting Berkeley in summer 1938, he learned Italy's new racial laws had stripped Jews like him of university posts; he stayed, became an American, helped find astatine (1940), and showed with Seaborg that plutonium-239 was fissile. Nobel Prize 1959, for the antiproton (Nobel Foundation; Britannica). Shared with: `immigration` (the immigrant-scientist thread) · `science` (features him separately).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilio-segre-elements" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="glenn-seaborg" name="Glenn Seaborg" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Glenn Seaborg
> **Who:** the chemist whose teams found plutonium and nine more elements — and the first living person to have an element named for him. · **When and where:** Berkeley and wartime Chicago, 1940–1974; died 1999.
Led plutonium's discovery (produced December 14, 1940; proved February 23–24, 1941, Gilman Hall 307) and in all co-discovered ten elements — with the honest footnote that one of the ten, nobelium, was later credited to Dubna. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1951, shared with McMillan. The named stories ride with him: the Pu symbol joke, the *Quiz Kids* reveal, mendelevium as Cold War diplomacy, and seaborgium in his lifetime (1997) — he could receive mail addressed entirely in elements: Seaborgium, Lawrencium, Berkelium, Californium, Americium (Nobel Foundation; Science History Institute). His seaborgium naming fight sits in the next era's span; he is placed once, here, at the plutonium anchor.

## 1950 to 2000
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For four decades American labs made new elements almost faster than anyone could name them — and fought Soviet labs for decades over who got there first. The disputes are stated, not resolved: some credits went to Berkeley, some to Dubna, some were split.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="elements from a hydrogen bomb" -->
- Californium: made at Berkeley in early 1950, announced March 17, 1950 — in this era by the boundary rule, named for the state and the university (Britannica).
- The Ivy Mike test (November 1, 1952, Enewetak Atoll) vaporized an island; drone planes flew through the mushroom cloud trailing filter papers, and tons of blasted coral were shipped to Berkeley. In the debris, Ghiorso and teams from Berkeley, Argonne, and Los Alamos found elements 99 and 100, where uranium had swallowed floods of neutrons. The discoveries were classified until August 1, 1955; the names — einsteinium and fermium — went public only after both namesakes had died (Fermi November 1954, Einstein April 1955), neither told an element would bear his name (Science History Institute "Element Hunting in a Nuclear Storm"; LANL). Shared with: `war` (the H-bomb test itself).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="one atom at a time" -->
- Mendelevium, 1955: the Berkeley team bombarded a target of about a billion einsteinium atoms — all that existed — and caught 17 atoms of element 101 with Ghiorso's new recoil method, the first element identified atom by atom. Ghiorso wired the detector to the building fire alarm as a celebration bell — and, left connected, it set off a late-night evacuation (anecdote-grade sourcing, flagged in the bank). Naming it for Mendeleev, a Russian, at the height of the Cold War took U.S. government approval (LANL; Britannica; Atomic Heritage).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="the Transfermium Wars" -->
- Nobelium, an honest American miss: a Stockholm claim (1957) was retracted, Berkeley's 1958 claims were judged insufficient, and the decisive 1966 work was Dubna's — the IUPAC/IUPAP Transfermium Working Group credited Dubna, and the Swedish name stuck anyway (TWG report, *Pure Appl. Chem.* 1993).
- Lawrencium (Berkeley 1961, Dubna 1965–67): credit ruled shared; the name honors Ernest Lawrence — the cyclotron man got element 103.
- Berkeley and Dubna both claimed elements 104 (1964/1969) and 105 (1968/1970); the naming fight ran for decades in dueling journals. The Working Group (1992–93) split the credit for both and credited Berkeley for 106 (1974). The final 1997 package: rutherfordium, dubnium — putting the Russian lab's town on the table — and seaborgium, the first element named for a living person, after IUPAC first tried to ban living namesakes in 1994. Seaborg kept a periodic table with Sg on it until his death in 1999 (IUPAC 1997; Science History Institute "The Transfermium Wars").
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-ghiorso" name="Albert Ghiorso" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Ghiorso
> **Who:** the instrument-builder who co-discovered more elements than anyone in history — without a doctorate. · **When and where:** Berkeley, 1944–1974 (career into the 1990s); died 2010.
Co-discovered 12 elements, americium through seaborgium — the Guinness World Record. He was the gadget man: the detectors, the recoil catch, the HILAC accelerator, the mendelevium counter wired to a fire alarm (Guinness; Physics Today obituary; LBL).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-ghiorso" -->
<!-- hb-story:start slug="darleane-hoffman-elements" name="Darleane Hoffman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Darleane Hoffman
> **Who:** the nuclear chemist who proved a "man-made" element occurs in nature. · **When and where:** Los Alamos from 1953; Berkeley from 1984; died 2025, age 98.
In 1971 she detected traces of plutonium-244 in ancient rock from California's Mountain Pass mine — a man-made element found occurring naturally on Earth in tiny amounts. She showed fermium-257 could split spontaneously, led one-atom-at-a-time chemistry of the heaviest elements at Berkeley, was the first woman to lead a Los Alamos scientific division, and won the National Medal of Science (1997) and the ACS Priestley Medal (2000, the second woman ever) (ACS; Physics Today; *Nature* obituary). `science` features her separately.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="darleane-hoffman-elements" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="elements in daily life" -->
- Americium-241 — a Manhattan Project element — sits in household ionization smoke detectors, about a third of a millionth of a gram in each, its alpha particles ionizing the air in a small chamber until smoke trips the alarm (EPA RadTown; LANL). Shared with: `technology` (the device).
- Silicon, one line and no false first (discovered in Sweden, 1824): the transistor age made it the emblem of American technology — "Silicon Valley" is named for the chip element. Shared with: `technology` (the chips) · `economy` (the industry).

## 2000 to Today
<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
International teams finished the last row of the periodic table, and the American share is stated honestly: target material, chemistry, detectors, and shared credit — the atoms themselves formed in Russia. Current through 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="finishing the seventh row" -->
- Between 1998 and 2010, joint teams at Dubna (JINR) and Livermore (LLNL) — for elements 115 and 117 also Oak Ridge and Vanderbilt — made elements 114–118. IUPAC recognized 113, 115, 117, and 118 on December 30, 2015, completing period 7; the names came November 28, 2016: nihonium (113, Japan's RIKEN — the first element discovered in Asia), moscovium, tennessine, and oganesson. Flerovium (114) and livermorium (116), named May 2012, honor Russia's Flerov Laboratory and the Livermore lab and city. Frame all of it as "made together": IUPAC credits the collaborations, not one country (IUPAC press releases; LLNL).
- Oganesson, element 118, is named for Yuri Oganessian, the Dubna physicist who led the superheavy hunt — only the second element named for a living person, after seaborgium; his colleagues voted after asking him to leave the conference call. He is Russian: the era's emblem in the history prose, not a featured American story (IUPAC; sciencenotes).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="tennessine: Tennessee on the table" -->
- Oak Ridge's HFIR reactor made the only possible target material on Earth: 22 milligrams of berkelium-249, 250 days in the reactor plus 90 days of chemistry. Shipped in June 2009, the package crossed the Atlantic five times because Russian customs twice rejected the paperwork. Six months of calcium-48 beam at Dubna yielded six atoms of element 117 — published April 2010, named tennessine November 28, 2016. Tennessee became the second U.S. state on the table (after californium) — but promethium (Oak Ridge, 1945) is still the only element actually discovered in Tennessee; tennessine's atoms were made in Russia. The collaboration began when Vanderbilt's Joe Hamilton introduced ORNL's Jim Roberto to Yuri Oganessian (ORNL element-117 timeline; "Charmed half-life").

### Clarice Phelps
> **Who:** Oak Ridge nuclear chemist, U.S. Navy veteran, and the first Black woman to help discover a chemical element. · **When and where:** Oak Ridge National Laboratory, from 2009; tennessine target work 2008–09; recognition 2016–19.
Worked on purifying the berkelium-249 used as the element-117 target; the recognition as the first African-American woman on an element-discovery team came after tennessine was confirmed (ORNL staff profile; CNN; Tennessee State University).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarice-phelps" -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="toward row 8, and the end of federal helium" -->
- In 2024, Berkeley Lab's Heavy Element Group made two atoms of livermorium a new way — a titanium-50 beam on plutonium-244 at the 88-Inch Cyclotron (announced July 23, 2024) — the proof of principle needed to attempt element 120, which would open row 8 and be the heaviest atom ever made. The attempt was under way as of 2026, with no discovery announced (Berkeley Lab News; DOE Office of Science).
- The Federal Helium System — the reserve, the Cliffside field, the 423-mile pipeline — was sold to the industrial-gas company Messer in a sale completed in June 2024; the sale sent about $460 million to the U.S. Treasury (accepted bids totaled $423.35 million), ending nearly a century of federal helium. Helium remains scarce and strategic: MRI machines, rockets, chips (BLM press releases 2024). Shared with: `economy` (lead — the supply story).
