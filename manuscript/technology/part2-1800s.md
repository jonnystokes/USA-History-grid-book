## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1831 and 1846, inventors in the United States built machines to cut grain, plow prairie soil and sew cloth. Charles Goodyear found a way to treat rubber with sulfur so that heat did not melt it. In 1839 people in Philadelphia made a new French way of taking photographs work within weeks of hearing about it. On May 24, 1844, Samuel Morse sent a message by electric telegraph from Washington to Baltimore, 40 miles away. Morse, Goodyear, the sewing machine maker Elias Howe and the reaper maker Cyrus McCormick each held a patent, a government grant that gives an inventor the only right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. The men whose names went on the patents had help. Morse's partner Alfred Vail built the telegraph instruments. Jo Anderson, a man enslaved by the McCormick family, worked on the first McCormick reaper.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telegraph, 1844" -->
The electric telegraph sent messages as pulses of electricity through a wire. At the receiving end, each pulse worked an electromagnet, a magnet that works only while electricity flows through it. The magnet moved an arm with a pen-like point, called a stylus. A clockwork motor pulled a strip of paper tape past the stylus. For each pulse, the stylus pressed a small dent into the tape, a short one for a dot or a longer one for a dash. Every letter and number had its own pattern of dots and dashes, and that pattern is called Morse code.

In 1843 Morse asked Congress for $30,000 to build a telegraph line from Washington to Baltimore. Members of the House and then senators voted for the money, and President John Tyler signed the bill. Ezra Cornell was hired to lay the wire underground. When Cornell ran into bad wire, he suggested stringing the wires overhead on trees and poles instead. A signal in a long wire grows weaker as it goes. Leonard Gale showed Morse how to boost it at points along the line with a device called a relay.

On May 24, 1844, Morse sent the first message over the line from the US Capitol in Washington. Alfred Vail received it in Baltimore. The words were "What hath God wrought," from the Bible, in the book of Numbers, chapter 23, verse 23. They mean "What has God done." Annie G. Ellsworth chose them. She was the daughter of the commissioner of the Patent Office, the head of the government office that grants patents. The paper tape with the message still exists. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-morse" name="Samuel Morse" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Morse

> **Who:** The inventor who held the patents on the electric telegraph and sent the first message over the Washington to Baltimore line.
> **When and where:** The idea came to him in 1832. The first message went from Washington to Baltimore on May 24, 1844.

Samuel Morse held the patents on the American electric telegraph and got the fame for it. The story told in a Library of Congress history is that the idea of sending messages over a distance by electricity came to Morse in 1832. He was on a ship coming home from Europe, during a conversation on board.

Other men helped Morse build the telegraph. Leonard Gale showed him how to boost a weakening signal with a relay. Alfred Vail built and improved the instruments in the shop at the Speedwell Ironworks. The same Library of Congress history identifies hiring Vail as one of the keys to Morse's success. Vail received the first message in Baltimore on May 24, 1844.

Accounts differ about who worked out the dot and dash code. Some say that Morse invented it and Vail improved it. Others give Vail credit for the practical version, which uses the shortest codes for the letters people use most. The records leave the question open. Morse held the patents, and people call the code Morse code.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-morse" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sewing machines, rubber and the camera" -->
Elias Howe patented a sewing machine on September 10, 1846. It sewed a lockstitch, in which two threads lock together through the cloth. Isaac Singer made a more practical machine, and it sold better than Howe's. Howe sued the makers who used his invention. In 1854 judges upheld Howe's patent, and the makers had to pay him royalties, which are fees for using a patent. At the peak, the fees paid to Howe reached as much as $4,000 a month.

In 1856 Howe and the biggest makers, Singer, Wheeler & Wilson, and Grover & Baker, pooled their patents. That meant each member could use the others' patented inventions. Their group was called the Sewing Machine Combination. Its members controlled the sewing machine trade until 1877.

In 1839, in Woburn, Massachusetts, a piece of Charles Goodyear's mix of rubber and sulfur landed on a hot stove. The rubber charred like leather instead of melting. Accounts differ on how it reached the stove. Some say he dropped it. Others say it flew from his hand while he was trying to sell it. It took Goodyear five more years to learn to make the change happen every time. He patented the process in 1844. Rubber treated this way is called vulcanized rubber.

In late August and September of 1839, news of the daguerreotype reached the United States. It was an early way of making photographs, invented by Louis Daguerre, a French artist, and made public in Paris on August 19, 1839. Within weeks, people in Philadelphia had made the process work for themselves. In October or November of 1839, Robert Cornelius of Philadelphia made a photograph of himself. It is the oldest surviving portrait photograph made in America. The Library of Congress holds it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mccormick-deere" name="Cyrus McCormick and John Deere" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cyrus McCormick and John Deere

> **Who:** Two makers of farm machines. McCormick's reaper cut grain, and Deere's steel plow cut through prairie soil.
> **When and where:** The reaper at Walnut Grove and Steele's Tavern, Virginia, 1831. The plow at Grand Detour, Illinois, 1837.

A reaper is a machine that cuts ripe grain. Cyrus McCormick held the patent on the McCormick reaper and built the business that sold it. Other people also worked on the first one.

His father, Robert McCormick, tried for several years to build a reaper at Walnut Grove, the family farm in Rockbridge County, Virginia. In 1831 he gave up. Within six weeks, Cyrus showed a working reaper by cutting oats at nearby Steele's Tavern. A blacksmith named John McCown worked on the machine. So did Jo Anderson, a man the McCormicks enslaved.

Anderson was born on Robert McCormick's plantation in about 1807. Robert McCormick held him in slavery from birth, and Anderson was given to Cyrus as a baby. In 1885 a white interviewer, William J. Hanna, wrote down Anderson's words, spelled the way Hanna heard them. Hanna recorded Anderson as 78 years old and unable to walk. Anderson said he carried grain to the blacksmith shop to test the machine: "I fotched loose grain to the shop so he could see how it cut it." He also worked as "blower and striker for the blacksmith." That is the helper who pumps the bellows, a big bag that blows air onto the fire, and swings the heavy hammer. Anderson gave Cyrus the credit for the reaper and said Robert "tried it and gave it up." He said Cyrus freed him before the Civil War.

In December 1833 another inventor, Obed Hussey, patented a reaper. McCormick patented his own in 1834 and printed a warning against Hussey's machine in *Mechanics' Magazine*. McCormick made his first sales in 1840 and moved to Chicago in 1847. In 1859 he and his brothers made and sold more than 5,000 reapers.

John Deere made plows at Grand Detour, Illinois. The sticky prairie soil there clogged iron plows. In 1837 Deere built a plow with a polished steel moldboard, the curved blade that turns the soil over. The sticky soil slid off the smooth steel, so the plow scoured, or cleaned, itself as it moved. By 1846 Deere was selling nearly 1,000 plows a year.

Ho-Chunk people, long called the Winnebago in English, had lived in a village at the bend of the Rock River where Grand Detour stands. Settlers who came later said they could still see signs of that village. In 1832, at Fort Armstrong in Illinois, Ho-Chunk leaders signed a treaty, a written agreement between nations, with two men sent to make it for the United States. They were General Winfield Scott and John Reynolds, the governor of Illinois. In the treaty the Ho-Chunk signed over to the United States their lands south and east of the Wisconsin River and the Fox River of Green Bay, along a boundary that ran up the Rock River to its source. In return, US officials agreed to pay the Ho-Chunk $10,000 a year for 27 years. The records do not make clear which treaty covered the exact spot where Grand Detour stands.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1850, some inventors worked with patent lawyers and with teams of workers. On February 14, 1876, Alexander Graham Bell's lawyer filed his application for a telephone patent a few hours before the papers of a rival, Elisha Gray, arrived. Thomas Edison's team at Menlo Park, New Jersey, made a long-lasting electric lamp in 1879, building on 40 years of work by other inventors. Not everyone could own an invention. In 1858 the US attorney general ruled that an invention by an enslaved person could not be patented. According to the law scholar Kara Swanson, the ruling also shut free Black inventors out of the patent system.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who could own an invention" -->
By August 1857 an enslaved blacksmith named Ned, near Holmesville in Pike County, Mississippi, had invented a new farm tool. It was a "double Cotton Scraper, and two plows" that could "Scrape both Sides of the Cotton ridge at the same time, and plough out the middles." A cotton ridge is a raised row of soil where the cotton grows, and the middles are the low strips between the rows. Everything known about Ned comes from the letters of Oscar J. E. Stuart, a white planter and lawyer who claimed to own him. Ned's second name, if he had one, is not recorded. How his life ended is not known.

On November 15, 1857, Stuart applied for a patent on Ned's invention. He swore that he owned Ned and that Ned was the inventor. Stuart also threatened Ned. If Ned wrote to the Patent Office himself, Stuart said, he would "correct" Ned "according to our Southern usage." To correct meant to punish. Stuart made the threat in a letter he later sent to the secretary of the interior. He did not write in it what the punishment would be.

The patent commissioner, Joseph Holt, sent the application back. The law required the inventor to swear an oath, a solemn promise before the law, that he had made the invention. Holt decided that an enslaved man could not take the oath. Stuart complained to Jacob Thompson, the secretary of the interior. Thompson sent the question to the attorney general, Jeremiah S. Black, the government's chief lawyer.

In 1858 Jeremiah Black ruled that "a machine invented by a slave, though it be new and useful, cannot, in the present state of the law, be patented." He based his ruling on the *Dred Scott* decision of 1857. In that case, the judges of the Supreme Court, called justices, ruled that African Americans were not citizens. Without citizenship, Jeremiah Black reasoned, an enslaved inventor could not swear the oath. The owner could not get the patent either, because only the "original and first inventor" could. Free Black inventors had received patents before this. The law scholar Kara Swanson writes that Jeremiah Black's opinion "created a formal racial barrier" that shut them out too.

Stuart asked Congress twice for a patent and failed both times. In 1859 Senator Albert Gallatin Brown of Mississippi backed a bill that would let enslavers patent the inventions of the people they enslaved. Senators never moved the bill forward. Stuart sold the "Stuart Double Plough and Scraper" anyway, without a patent. In 1861 Southern states broke away from the United States and formed the Confederacy. Its patent law of May 21, 1861 let enslavers patent the inventions of enslaved people.

Benjamin Montgomery was enslaved in Mississippi by Joseph Davis. Montgomery invented a better propeller for boats. Joseph Davis tried to patent it through his brother, Senator Jefferson Davis, and failed. After slavery ended, Montgomery tried to patent the propeller in his own name, and that failed too, according to his son Isaiah Montgomery. After the Civil War, slavery ended, and Congress and the states added amendments, or changes, to the Constitution. Under those changes, Black inventors could get patents again. In 1913 Henry E. Baker, a Black patent examiner who checked patent applications for the government, published *The Colored Inventor* to mark 50 years since the end of slavery. It appeared in *The Crisis*, the magazine of the NAACP, a group that works for the rights of Black Americans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telephone, 1876" -->
A telephone turns the sound of a voice into electric current and back into sound. In Bell's own description from 1876, the voice makes a thin sheet, called a membrane, shake in time with the sound. The shaking makes the electric current in the wire grow stronger and weaker in the same pattern. At the other end, the changing current makes a second sheet shake, and the listener hears the voice.

On the morning of February 14, 1876, Bell's lawyer filed an application for a patent called "Improvement in Telegraphy." It was the fifth filing at the Patent Office that day. A few hours later, papers from Elisha Gray arrived as the 39th filing. Gray filed a caveat, a notice that he was working on an invention and planned to apply for a patent. Both papers described a liquid transmitter, a sending part that used a liquid. On the advice of his lawyers, Gray withdrew.

Patent Office officials issued patent number 174,465 to Bell on March 7, 1876. On March 10 Bell spoke the first sentence ever sent by telephone. He spoke to his assistant, Thomas A. Watson, who was in the next room of Bell's laboratory in Boston. Bell's notebook records the words "Mr. Watson," then "come here," then "I want to see you."

Bell filed first and held the patent. People have argued ever since about whether Bell or Gray deserved the credit. In 2020 the authors of an article in an engineering journal, the *Proceedings of the IEEE*, took a new look at the case.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Alexander Graham Bell

> **Who:** An inventor in Boston whose lawyer filed for the telephone patent a few hours before a rival's papers arrived. He received the patent.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1876.

The first telephone patent, number 174,465, went to Alexander Graham Bell on March 7, 1876. His lawyer had filed the application on February 14, a few hours before Elisha Gray's papers for a similar instrument reached the Patent Office.

Gray withdrew on the advice of his lawyers, and the patent stayed with Bell. People have argued ever since about who deserved the credit.

Lewis Latimer, a draftsman at a patent law firm, made the drawings for Bell's patent application. A draftsman makes exact drawings of machines and buildings. Latimer later worked on the electric lamp.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-graham-bell" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emma-nutt" name="Emma Nutt" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emma Nutt

> **Who:** The first woman telephone operator.
> **When and where:** Boston, hired September 1, 1878.

On September 1, 1878, Emma Nutt left a job at a telegraph office to work for the telephone dispatch company in Boston. She became the first woman to work as a telephone operator. An operator connected each caller to the person being called. Her pay was $10 a month for a 54-hour week. Her sister, Stella, started work as an operator the same day.

The first operators had been teenage boys, who were rude to callers and played pranks on them. Women took their places. By the late 1880s, almost every telephone operator was a woman. It was one of the first big jobs in a new technology open to women.

Emma Nutt worked as an operator for more than 30 years. Accounts of the exact length differ, from about 33 to about 37 years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emma-nutt" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Edison's invention factory" -->
An electric lamp makes light when an electric current heats a thin thread inside it, called a filament, until the filament glows. Inventors had worked on electric lamps for 40 years before Edison's team made its lamp in 1879. British inventors showed arc lamps, which make light from a spark between two rods, starting in 1835. Joseph Swan in England patented a lamp design before Edison did, and so did William Sawyer and Albon Man.

Edison ran a laboratory at Menlo Park, New Jersey, with a team of workers. Among them were Charles Batchelor, John Kruesi and Francis Upton. In October 1879 the team made a lamp with a filament of cotton thread that had been baked into carbon. It burned for about 14.5 hours. Edison showed the lamps to the public at Menlo Park on New Year's Eve, 1879. The team kept testing filaments and settled on one made of bamboo. It let Edison's lamps burn for up to 1,200 hours.

Edison's team also built the other parts needed to light a whole district. These included the dynamo, a machine that makes electricity when it is turned, along with the wiring and a meter to measure how much electricity each customer used. On September 4, 1882, the Pearl Street station at 257 Pearl Street in Manhattan began supplying electricity. It lit the first district of paying customers.

The Menlo Park team built other machines too. Two years before the lamp, in early December 1877, John Kruesi built a phonograph to Edison's design. A phonograph records sound and plays it back. The first one recorded on a sheet of tinfoil. On December 6, 1877, Edison recorded himself saying "Mary had a little lamb."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-edison-technology" name="Thomas Edison" movie="Edison (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Edison

> **Who:** An inventor who ran a laboratory with a team of workers. His team made a long-lasting electric lamp, the phonograph and the system that brought electric light to paying customers.
> **When and where:** Menlo Park and West Orange, New Jersey. Born 1847, died 1931.
> **Movie:** *Edison* (2015), a documentary in the PBS series *American Experience*. It is about Edison's life and work.

In 1879 Thomas Edison's team made an electric lamp that burned for about 14.5 hours, and later lamps burned for up to 1,200 hours. Other inventors had built electric lamps for 40 years before that. Edison was born in Milan, Ohio, on February 11, 1847.

Edison and his team at Menlo Park found a filament that lasted. They also built the dynamos that made the electricity, the wires that carried it to customers and the meters that measured how much each customer used. In 1882 Edison's company began selling electricity from the Pearl Street station in Manhattan to the first district of paying customers.

Edison worked with a team. John Kruesi built the phonograph that Edison designed. Charles Batchelor and Francis Upton worked with him on the lamp. Lewis Latimer made patent drawings and gave expert testimony for Edison's company from 1884.

In November 1887 Edison opened a new laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey. He died at his home in West Orange on October 18, 1931.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-edison-technology" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lewis-latimer" name="Lewis Latimer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lewis Latimer

> **Who:** A self-taught draftsman who later oversaw the building of electric lighting systems. He patented a better way to make the carbon filaments inside electric lamps.
> **When and where:** Boston and New York. Born 1848, died 1928.

A patent of 1882 gave Lewis Latimer the rights to a better way of making the carbon filaments that glow inside electric lamps. The patent, number 252,386, was issued on January 17, 1882, for a "Process of Manufacturing Carbons." His method made filaments that were more even and lasted longer.

Latimer was born in Chelsea, Massachusetts, on September 4, 1848. His parents had freed themselves from slavery in Virginia by escaping. He taught himself to be a draftsman. While he worked at a patent law firm, he made the patent drawings for Bell's 1876 telephone application.

He received his filament patent while working at the U.S. Electric Lighting Company of Hiram Maxim. In 1884 he went to work for Edison's company. He made patent drawings and testified in court as an expert on patents. He also oversaw lamp factories and the setting up of electric lighting in New York, Philadelphia, Montreal and London.

In 1918 Latimer was named one of the 28 first members of the Edison Pioneers, a group of people who had worked closely with Edison before 1885. He was its only Black member. From 1903 he lived in a house in East Flushing, in Queens, New York. He died in Flushing on December 11, 1928, at the age of 80. The house is now the Lewis Latimer House Museum.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lewis-latimer" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="granville-t-woods" name="Granville T. Woods" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Granville T. Woods

> **Who:** A Black inventor who patented a way to send messages between moving trains and railroad stations.
> **When and where:** Cincinnati, Ohio. Patent issued November 29, 1887.

Granville T. Woods invented a way for moving trains and stations to send messages to each other. He called it the Synchronous Multiplex Railway Telegraph. He patented it on November 29, 1887, while working in Cincinnati. With it, the people who directed train traffic, called dispatchers, could know where the trains were.

Woods's telegraph worked by induction. In induction, a changing electric current in one wire makes a current flow in another wire nearby, without the two touching. Woods used it to carry messages between moving trains and stations.

Twice, people on Edison's side claimed that inventions Woods had patented were theirs. Each time Patent Office officials held a hearing, called an interference proceeding, to decide who had invented first. Woods won both times. Edison offered him a job, and Woods turned it down. Over his career Woods received dozens of patents for electrical and railroad inventions.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="granville-t-woods" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nikola-tesla-technology" name="Nikola Tesla" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nikola Tesla

> **Who:** A Serbian immigrant inventor whose induction motor used alternating current to make a shaft turn.
> **When and where:** New York. Patent issued May 1, 1888.

Alternating current, or AC, is electric current that switches direction back and forth many times each second. Nikola Tesla patented a motor that used AC to turn a shaft. His motor used induction, in which a changing current in one coil of wire makes a current flow in a part nearby without the two touching. The motor worked without brushes, the sliding electrical contacts that other motors used to carry current to their turning part.

Tesla was a Serbian immigrant who came to the United States in 1884. He filed for the motor patent in October 1887. The patent, number 381,968, was issued on May 1, 1888.

In July 1888 the Westinghouse company, a maker of electrical equipment, bought a license to Tesla's polyphase patents. A license is the right to use someone else's patent. Polyphase means his motors ran on two or more alternating currents at once. The company paid him $60,000 in cash and stock, which are shares in owning the company. It also agreed to pay him $2.50 for each horsepower of the motors it sold. Horsepower is a measure of how much work a motor can do.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nikola-tesla-technology" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The office and the fence line" -->
Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden and Samuel Soule patented a typewriter on June 23, 1868. E. Remington & Sons began making it on March 1, 1873. The Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer went on sale in 1874 for $125. Its keyboard began with the letters Q, W, E, R, T and Y across the top row of letters. That arrangement, called QWERTY, is still used on keyboards today.

On November 24, 1874, Joseph Glidden, a farmer from De Kalb, Illinois, received patent number 157,124 for barbed wire. He was a different man from the Carlos Glidden of the typewriter. Joseph Glidden's barb was a short piece of wire that locked onto a wire of two strands. He also built machines to make the wire in large amounts. Nine wire-fence designs were patented, and Glidden's was the one that sold well.

With barbed wire, farmers and ranchers could fence land where there was little wood to build fences. In the West, ranchers and homesteaders, families who had claimed farms, fenced off land and water that had been open to all. The open range, the unfenced land where cattle had grazed freely, came to an end. In Texas, New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming, cattlemen who found their old grazing land fenced off first asked officials for the right to use it. Then they fought range wars, fights against the farmers who owned the fenced land. The fences also blocked Native peoples who moved with the seasons from more of the lands they had always used, according to the National Archives. They called barbed wire "the Devil's rope."
