## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 10 minutes**

Americans brought machines into their factories, farms and homes in these fifty years. Workers at Henry Ford's car plant in Michigan started building car frames on a moving assembly line on October 7, 1913. Electric refrigerators and radios went into American homes, tractors began to replace horses on farms, and chemists made new materials, starting in 1907 with Leo Baekeland's Bakelite, the first plastic made entirely from chemicals.

The airplane and the electronic computer both date from these years. Wilbur and Orville Wright flew a powered airplane in North Carolina on December 17, 1903. In February 1946 engineers at the University of Pennsylvania showed ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, one that could be set up to solve many kinds of problems.

Scientists at Bell Labs, the research laboratory of the telephone company AT&T, made the first working transistor in December 1947. It was a small part that could control the flow of electricity and make a weak signal stronger. Transistors later replaced vacuum tubes, glass tubes with the air taken out, like light bulbs, in machines like ENIAC.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-technology" name="The Wright brothers" movie="Wright Brothers' Flying Machine (2003)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers

> **Who:** Wilbur and Orville Wright, bicycle builders from Dayton, Ohio. They invented an airplane with an engine that a pilot could steer and control.
> **When and where:** Near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, December 17, 1903.
> **Movie:** *Wright Brothers' Flying Machine* (2003), a documentary in the PBS series *NOVA*, first shown on November 11, 2003. It is about the brothers' flying machine.

**The first photograph of a flight was the photographer's first photograph ever.**

The Wright brothers made four flights in an airplane with an engine near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, on December 17, 1903. Orville flew first. He stayed in the air for 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. On the fourth flight of the day, Wilbur flew 852 feet in 59 seconds.

Five people watched. One of them, John T. Daniels of the Life-Saving Station at Kill Devil Hills, squeezed the camera's shutter bulb and took the photograph of the first flight. It was the first photograph he had ever taken.

The brothers worked out how to steer an airplane on gliders, which have no engine, in 1901 and 1902. Besides going forward, an airplane can move in three other ways, and the Wrights built a control for each.

When the wing tips move up and down, the airplane rolls. The Wrights controlled roll with cables that twisted the tips of the wings. To control pitch, the nose moving up and down, they added a movable part at the front, called an elevator, in 1901. Yaw is the nose swinging from side to side. In 1902 they added a movable upright part at the back, called a rudder, to control it.

To test the shapes of wings, they built their own wind tunnel, in which a fan blows a steady stream of air past a model. They built a light gasoline engine, too, in the workshop of their bicycle shop in Dayton.

**The first death.** Army officers were testing the Wright airplane at Fort Myer, Virginia, on September 17, 1908. Orville flew it with Lieutenant Thomas E. Selfridge of the Army Signal Corps as his passenger. The Signal Corps, the Army's communications branch, was then in charge of the Army's first aircraft.

One of the propellers split and hit a wire, and the plane dived into the ground. Selfridge's skull was broken. He never woke up, and he died that evening, the first person killed in an airplane crash. Orville broke a leg and several ribs and spent seven weeks in the Army hospital.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-technology" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-technology" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford

> **Who:** The carmaker whose workers at Highland Park, Michigan, built the Model T on a moving assembly line.
> **When and where:** Highland Park, Michigan. The moving chassis line started on October 7, 1913.
> **Movie:** *Henry Ford* (2013), a documentary in the PBS series *American Experience*, directed by Sarah Colt. It is about Ford's life and his company.

**A motor and a rope pulled each car frame past 140 workers.**

At Henry Ford's plant in Highland Park, Michigan, the time it took to build the frame of a car fell from more than 12 hours to about 93 minutes. The workers got that speed by building on a moving assembly line. On a moving line, a motor pulls the car or part past the workers, and each worker adds parts or does one task as it goes by.

Ford's workers tried the line first on one part. In the spring of 1913 they put together the flywheel magneto, an engine part that makes electricity, on a line, with the job split into 29 steps done by 29 men along a moving belt.

The chassis line started on October 7, 1913. Each chassis, the frame of the car with its wheels, moved along a line 150 feet long. At first the time to build one fell to under 3 hours.

**Five dollars a day.** Ford announced a pay of $5 for an eight-hour day in January 1914. Before that, workers had earned about $2.34 for nine hours, so the new pay was more than double. Part of his reason was to keep workers who hated the pace of the line.

Ford's workers built cars in a year when about 23,000 workers in American industry died on the job, about 63 a day. Staff at the Bureau of Labor Statistics, a federal office that gathers facts about work, made that count for 1913. It covers every kind of industrial work across the country and gives no number for Ford's plant.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-technology" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radios, refrigerators and plastic" -->
Lee de Forest's Audion of 1906 made weak radio signals stronger. The Audion was a vacuum tube. Inside its glass were a thin wire called a filament, a metal plate, and a bent wire between them called the grid. A small electric current on the grid controlled a bigger current between the other two parts. The Audion could pick up radio signals too, and tubes like it made radios for the home practical.

Philo Farnsworth, age 21, sent the first all-electronic television picture from his lab on Green Street in San Francisco on September 7, 1927. The picture was a straight line, made with his own camera tube, called the image dissector.

**The refrigerator.** The General Electric company began selling the Monitor Top, an electric refrigerator for the home, the same year. It cost about $300, and by the middle of 1929 buyers had bought more than a quarter of a million of them. It was the first home electric refrigerator sold in large numbers.

The researcher Emanuela Cardia studied what American households owned, using the census, the government's count of everyone in the country, taken every ten years. In 1930, 14 percent of households had a mechanical refrigerator, one that kept itself cold with a motor instead of a block of ice. By 1940 the share was 44 percent, and by 1950 it was 80 percent, four homes in five.

**Plastic.** Leo Baekeland, a chemist born in Belgium who lived in the United States, made Bakelite in 1907 from two chemicals, phenol and formaldehyde. It was the first fully synthetic plastic, made entirely from chemicals rather than from anything that grew. He filed for a patent in July 1907 and received it on December 7, 1909.

Nylon, a strong thread-like material made from chemicals, came from Wallace Carothers's work at the DuPont chemical company, which he joined in 1928. Gerard Berchet made the first half ounce of nylon on February 28, 1935, and DuPont officials announced it to the public on October 27, 1938.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tractors replace horses" -->
Census takers counted 246,000 tractors on American farms in 1920, and about 26 million horses and mules, more than 100 for every tractor. The Fordson tractor of 1917 was cheap and made in large numbers. About three out of every four American tractors built in the 1920s were Fordsons.

The International Harvester company began selling the Farmall in 1924. It could do the jobs other tractors did, and it could also cultivate, breaking up the soil and clearing weeds between rows of growing crops. Cultivating was the last farm job that horses still did.

The Census of Agriculture of 1954 was the first to record more tractors than horses and mules on American farms: about 4.6 million tractors and about 4 million horses and mules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first computers and the transistor" -->
ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, was shown to the public at the University of Pennsylvania in February 1946. John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert designed it at the university's Moore School, and the US Army paid to build it. It was built to work out artillery firing tables, charts that told soldiers how to aim their big guns.

ENIAC had 17,468 vacuum tubes, weighed more than 27 tons, and filled 1,800 square feet. It could not store a program. For each new set of calculations, its programmers, a team of six women, moved its switches and plugged its cables into new places by hand.

**The transistor.** At Bell Labs, a group of scientists led by William Shockley looked for a replacement for vacuum tubes, which often failed. On December 16, 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain placed two gold contacts, very close together, on a small slab of germanium, a chemical element. The electric push, or voltage, on one contact controlled the flow of electricity, or current, through the other.

The device made a signal up to 100 times stronger. It was the first transistor. Workers at Bell Labs chose the name "transistor" in a vote, and the company's officials announced it to the public on June 30, 1948. Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eniac-programmers" name="The six ENIAC programmers" movie="The Computers (2014)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The six ENIAC programmers

> **Who:** The six women who programmed ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, and were left out of its celebration.
> **When and where:** University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, 1945 and 1946.
> **Movie:** *The Computers* (2014), a short documentary first shown at the Seattle International Film Festival in 2014. The six programmers are its subject.

**Six women programmed ENIAC. None was invited to its dinner.**

The six were Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty Mauchly Antonelli, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence and Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum. No one invited them to the dinner held for the machine's public showing in 1946. The records do not say who made the guest list.

At first the six had only drawings of the machine's wiring to learn from. They were hired to work out the paths of artillery shells for the Army, and later they also ran calculations for the scientists at Los Alamos, the laboratory in New Mexico where the atomic bomb was built.

Their story went untold for decades, until Kathy Kleiman learned about the six while she was researching her college thesis, a long paper written at the end of college. She started the ENIAC Programmers Project to record their story. In 1997 the group Women in Technology International named all six to its Hall of Fame. Four of them were there to accept. Ruth Teitelbaum had died, and her husband, Adolph, accepted for her. Frances Spence is not named among those who were there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eniac-programmers" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ernest-lawrence-technology" name="Ernest Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ernest Lawrence

> **Who:** A scientist who built the cyclotron, a machine that whirls particles in a spiral and uses them to smash atoms.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from 1930 and 1931.

**One of his machines had a magnet that weighed 220 tons.**

Ernest O. Lawrence's first cyclotron was small enough to hold in one hand. He built it at Berkeley, California, in 1930 and 1931, out of glass, sealing wax and bronze. It sped up hydrogen ions, hydrogen atoms with an electric charge, to an energy of 80,000 electron volts, a very small unit of energy used for particles.

Each machine Lawrence built after that was bigger. The first model was followed by machines 11 inches, 27 inches and 37 inches across. The 60-inch cyclotron of 1939 had a magnet that weighed 220 tons. Inside each machine, particles whirled in a spiral and then struck atoms and broke them apart.

Scientists used cyclotrons to make elements that do not occur in nature. They made radioactive materials for use in medicine, too, materials whose atoms break down and give off energy and tiny particles. Lawrence won the Nobel Prize in 1939. One later machine at Berkeley, the 88-Inch Cyclotron, still runs.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ernest-lawrence-technology" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 11 minutes**

Computers shrank from machines that filled a room to machines that sat on a desk at home, and people linked them into networks. Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments built the first integrated circuit, a whole electronic circuit on one small chip, in 1958. The Altair 8800, a computer sold as a kit of parts, was announced in the magazine *Popular Electronics* in January 1975, and IBM began selling the IBM PC in 1981. The IBM PC became the standard office computer.

A student at the University of California, Los Angeles, or UCLA, sent the first message between two computers on ARPANET, a network that linked computers at universities, on October 29, 1969. Martin Cooper of Motorola made the first call on a handheld cell phone four years later. All 24 satellites of the Global Positioning System, or GPS, were working by 1995.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="grace-hopper" name="Grace Hopper" movie="The Queen of Code (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Grace Hopper

> **Who:** A mathematician and Navy officer whose A-0 compiler let people write instructions for a computer in words closer to English.
> **When and where:** Harvard University from 1944, then Remington Rand, the company that made the UNIVAC computer. Born in New York in 1906, died in 1992.
> **Movie:** *The Queen of Code* (2015), a short documentary directed by Gillian Jacobs, part of the FiveThirtyEight series *Signals*. It is about Hopper's life and work.

**At 79 she was the oldest officer still serving in the Navy.**

Grace Hopper's A-0 compiler, built in 1951 and 1952, was a program that takes instructions written closer to human language and translates them into instructions the computer can carry out.

Hopper trained as a mathematician. During World War II she served as a lieutenant in the WAVES, the women's branch of the Navy. In July 1944 she was assigned to the Harvard Mark I computer, and she became one of its first programmers. In 1946 she wrote the first long manual on how to program a computer.

She built the A-0 compiler while working on the UNIVAC computer at the Remington Rand company. From 1955 to 1959 she worked on FLOW-MATIC, a programming language for business. Like other programming languages, it was a set of words and rules for writing instructions a computer can follow, and its words were English words. Later, the designers of COBOL, another business programming language, drew on FLOW-MATIC.

Hopper retired from the Navy in 1986 as a rear admiral, one of the Navy's highest ranks. She was 79, and at that time she was the oldest officer still serving in the Navy.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="grace-hopper" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who invented the chip?" -->
Two engineers at two different companies, Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce, each made a chip. The encyclopedia Britannica and the German Patent and Trade Mark Office both name the two men as its inventors. A chip, or integrated circuit, puts a whole electronic circuit, the path electricity flows through and the parts along it that do jobs such as switching it on and off, onto one small piece of material.

Kilby, of Texas Instruments, showed the first integrated circuit on September 12, 1958. He made it from germanium and connected its parts with wires by hand.

Noyce, of Fairchild Semiconductor, designed a chip made of silicon, another chemical element, in 1959. On Noyce's chip the wiring lay flat and was printed onto the surface, so factories could make chips in large numbers. Every later chip was built in Noyce's flat form. Silicon Valley, the area south of San Francisco where many computer companies started, is named for this element.

Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000. Noyce had died in 1990. Kilby said in public that Noyce deserved to share the prize.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marty-cooper" name="Martin Cooper" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Martin Cooper

> **Who:** A Motorola engineer who made the first call on a handheld cell phone.
> **When and where:** A street in Manhattan, New York City, April 3, 1973.

**The first handheld cell phone call went to his rival.**

Martin Cooper, an engineer for the Motorola company, made the first call on a handheld cell phone while walking along a street in Manhattan on April 3, 1973. He called Joel Engel, his rival at Bell Labs.

The phone Motorola sold to the public came 10 years later. The DynaTAC 8000X went on sale in 1983. It cost $3,995 and weighed 28 ounces, almost 2 pounds. One charge of its battery gave about 30 minutes of talking.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marty-cooper" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The personal computer" -->
The Altair 8800, a computer sold as a kit, a box of parts that the buyer puts together, was announced in the magazine *Popular Electronics* in January 1975. About 30 people came through a rainstorm to Gordon French's garage in Menlo Park, California, on March 5, 1975, to see an Altair. That meeting was the first of the Homebrew Computer Club, a group of people who built their own computers.

Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak and, for a short time, Ron Wayne founded Apple Computer on April 1, 1976. The Apple II, which came out in June 1977, was a finished computer that people could plug in at home. IBM brought out the IBM PC on August 12, 1981, and its design became the standard for computers in offices.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-felsenstein" name="Lee Felsenstein" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lee Felsenstein

> **Who:** An engineer who ran the meetings of the Homebrew Computer Club and designed two early small computers, the Sol-20 and the Osborne 1.
> **When and where:** Berkeley and the San Francisco Bay Area, California, from 1973. Born in Philadelphia in 1945.

**He put a public computer terminal in a Berkeley record store.**

**Arrested at Sproul Hall.** Nine years before he set up that terminal, Lee Felsenstein was a student at the University of California, Berkeley. He took part in the Free Speech Movement of 1964, a protest by Berkeley students for the right to speak and organize about politics on campus. He was at the Sproul Hall sit-in, where students sat inside a campus building and refused to leave. On December 3, 1964, hundreds of police officers went into the building and arrested nearly 800 students, Felsenstein among them.

**Community Memory.** Felsenstein earned a degree in electrical engineering and computer science at Berkeley in 1972. The next year he and four partners, among them Efrem Lipkin and Jude Milhon, set up a computer terminal that anyone could use, a keyboard and a printer or screen connected to a computer somewhere else. They called the project Community Memory and put the terminal in a Berkeley record store, Leopold's Records, next to the store's paper bulletin board.

People typed notes about music, politics, housing, services, women's groups and technology, and other people read them. The Computer History Museum's profile of Felsenstein describes it as one of the earliest online communities. The last version of the project closed in 1992.

Community Memory ran on a large computer called an SDS-940, which was donated for the project. It was a time-sharing computer, one that many people could use at once from different terminals. The project's team worked with Resource One, a nonprofit group, run for a cause rather than for profit. Three students who had dropped out of computer science at Berkeley had started Resource One.

**Homebrew.** Felsenstein was one of the first members of the Homebrew Computer Club, and he ran its open meetings, keeping order with a blackboard pointer.

With Gordon French and Bob Marsh, at a company called Processor Technology, he designed the Sol-20, one of the first small computers sold with its own case and keyboard. More than 10,000 were sold from 1977 to 1979. He then designed the Osborne 1 for Adam Osborne's company, a portable computer with a 5-inch screen. It cost $1,795, and more than 125,000 were sold in 1982.

His motto, as the Computer History Museum records it, is "To change the rules, change the tools." In 2016 the museum's leaders gave him their Fellow Award for his influence on early personal computing.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-felsenstein" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jobs-wozniak" name="Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak" movie="Steve Jobs (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak

> **Who:** The founders of Apple Computer: Wozniak designed its first two computers, and Jobs made the first sale.
> **When and where:** Jobs's garage in California, from 1976.
> **Movie:** *Steve Jobs* (2015), directed by Danny Boyle and written by Aaron Sorkin. It is a drama, not a documentary. Actors play the real people, and it is based on Walter Isaacson's biography of Jobs. *Steve Jobs: The Man in the Machine* (2015), directed by Alex Gibney, is a documentary about Jobs.

**A Volkswagen and a calculator paid for the first Apple computer.**

Steve Wozniak designed Apple's first two computers, and Steve Jobs found the first buyer. The two met while they were both working at the Hewlett-Packard company. In 1975 Wozniak began developing the Apple I. He later said that the Homebrew Computer Club was where the Apple I got its start.

To pay for the first working model, Jobs sold his Volkswagen and Wozniak sold his scientific calculator, for $1,300 in all. On April 1, 1976, they founded Apple Computer, with Ron Wayne as a partner for a short time. Jobs and Wozniak built the first Apple I computers in Jobs's garage.

On his first sales call, Jobs got an order for 50 of them at $500 each, $25,000 in all, from a store called the Byte Shop. The Apple I came without a case, a keyboard, a screen or a power supply. Buyers added their own.

**The Apple II.** Wozniak designed the Apple II in 1977. Its electronics, keyboard and power supply came together in one case, and it could show color pictures. The programming language BASIC came stored in its permanent memory, a part that keeps what is stored in it even when the power is off.

A floppy disk drive for storing files became available for it in 1978, and in 1979 VisiCalc came out, a spreadsheet program for working with tables of numbers. With those two, the Apple II sold in large numbers. Apple's sales grew from $7.8 million in 1978 to $117 million in 1980, 15 times as much in two years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jobs-wozniak" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the internet began" -->
The internet began with ARPANET, a computer network paid for by the Department of Defense to link computers at universities. On October 29, 1969, Charley Kline, a student programmer in Leonard Kleinrock's lab at UCLA, tried to log in to a computer at Stanford Research Institute. He typed "L" and "O," the first two letters of LOGIN, and then the system crashed. So the first message ever sent on the network was "LO." About an hour later the full login worked.

The computers on ARPANET began using TCP/IP, the internet's shared set of rules for moving data between computers, on January 1, 1983.

**The web.** Many people use "the web" and "the internet" to mean the same thing, but they are two different things. The internet is the network of linked computers. The World Wide Web is a system of pages linked to one another, which people read over the internet.

A British computer scientist, Tim Berners-Lee, invented the web at CERN, a European physics laboratory in Switzerland. He proposed it in 1989 and opened it to the public on August 6, 1991. Americans built the program that let ordinary people use the web easily. Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina and others at the University of Illinois released Mosaic, the first widely used graphical browser, a program that shows web pages with pictures, on April 22, 1993.

**GPS.** The Global Positioning System, or GPS, is a set of satellites owned by the United States. Each satellite sends out a signal that gives its position and the time, and a GPS receiver picks up these signals and uses them to work out where it is. The first GPS satellite went up on February 22, 1978, and the full set of 24 was working in 1995.

Until 2000, under a policy called Selective Availability, US government officials made the GPS signals for the public less exact on purpose, for national security. President Bill Clinton ordered the policy ended in May 2000. After that, ordinary GPS receivers could find their place to within about 10 to 20 meters (about 30 to 65 feet) or better, close enough to give driving directions turn by turn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What is inside a smoke detector?" -->
The ionization smoke detector, a cheap alarm made in large numbers for homes, holds about a third of a millionth of a gram of americium-241. Americium is a radioactive element, one whose atoms break down and give off energy and tiny particles, and scientists discovered it in 1944 and 1945.

The americium gives off tiny particles called alpha particles. The particles knock bits of electric charge loose from the air in a small chamber inside the detector, so a very small electric current can flow through the air. When smoke enters the chamber, it disturbs the current, and the alarm goes off.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 11 minutes**

Nine in ten American adults carried a smartphone by 2025, a survey by the Pew Research Center found, and new programs could write answers and match faces, sometimes wrongly. Apple began selling the iPhone in 2007. In 2022 the company OpenAI released ChatGPT, a program that writes answers to typed requests, and by February 2026, 44 percent of American adults told Pew they had used it.

ChatGPT is an AI program. AI, short for artificial intelligence, is a name for computer programs that do tasks such as recognizing a face or writing a paragraph, and these programs make mistakes. In 2020 Detroit police arrested the wrong man after a face-recognition search, a computer comparison of a photo of a face with a collection of photos, returned a false match.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The smartphone" -->
Steve Jobs of Apple announced the iPhone on January 9, 2007. It went on sale on June 29, 2007, for $499 or $599, with a phone contract, a promise to pay a phone company for service every month. On September 10, 2007, Apple officials announced that the company had sold its one millionth iPhone the day before, 74 days after the phone went on sale.

A smartphone puts a computer, a camera, a phone, a GPS receiver and a music player into one machine that fits in a pocket. Social media apps run on these phones.

Researchers at the Pew Research Center, a group that runs large surveys, have asked Americans whether they own a smartphone since 2011. In their first count, in 2011, 35 percent of adults owned one, about one in three. In a survey of 5,022 adults from February to June 2025, 91 percent did, and 98 percent owned some kind of cell phone.

Older people were less likely to own a smartphone. Among adults aged 18 to 29, 97 percent had one, against 78 percent of those 65 and older.

For some Americans, the phone is the only way to get online. In the same 2025 survey, 78 percent of adults had broadband at home, a fast internet connection that is always on. Among adults in households earning less than $30,000 a year, 34 percent reached the internet only through a smartphone. Among those earning $100,000 or more, 4 percent did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Batteries, printers and gene tools" -->
The battery in an electric car is a lithium-ion battery, a kind that can be recharged many times. Scientists in three countries worked on it, starting in the 1970s, when M. Stanley Whittingham began work on rechargeable lithium batteries for the company Exxon in New Jersey.

John Goodenough, an American chemist then working at Oxford in England, found the material for the battery's cathode, its positive end, in 1980: lithium cobalt oxide. Akira Yoshino in Japan built a safe early model with a carbon anode, the negative end, in 1985. The Sony company in Japan began selling the first lithium-ion batteries in 1991. Whittingham, Goodenough and Yoshino shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2019.

**3D printing.** A 3D printer builds a solid object out of many thin layers, one on top of another. Chuck Hull invented stereolithography, a method of 3D printing in which the machine hardens each thin layer in turn, in 1983 and 1984. He applied for a patent on August 8, 1984, and received it on March 11, 1986. In the 2010s, 3D printers spread to factories and to hobbyists' desks.

**Gene tools.** CRISPR is a tool for changing genes, the instructions inside living cells that shape how a living thing grows and works. Chapter 9 tells how the scientists Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier made it into that tool in 2012.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="limor-fried" name="Limor Fried" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Limor Fried

> **Who:** An engineer who founded Adafruit Industries, a company that makes electronics kits and teaches people to build with them.
> **When and where:** MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, then New York City, from 2005.

**Adafruit's designs are open source: anyone may copy and change them.**

Adafruit Industries began in 2005 in Limor Fried's dorm room at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, or MIT, a university in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She had earned a bachelor's degree from MIT in 2003, and she finished a master's degree in engineering there in 2005. Adafruit sells electronics kits along with lessons on how to build them, and it shares its designs as open source, free for anyone to copy and change.

According to the company's website, Fried was the first woman engineer on the cover of *Wired* magazine, and women own all of the company. Adafruit's workers make its products in the company's factory in New York City. In 2024 Adafruit moved to Industry City in Brooklyn. The company's website states that it has more than 100 employees.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="limor-fried" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How AI programs work, and why they make mistakes" -->
One kind of AI program, a neural network, learns from examples instead of following step-by-step instructions written by a person. To train a network to name what is in photographs, a computer shows it a huge number of photos whose right answers are already known.

Each time the network gives an answer, the computer compares it with the right answer and adjusts the network's settings a little, so that the next answer comes closer. After enough examples, the network can name what is in photos it has never seen, though it still makes mistakes.

**A contest.** Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton of the University of Toronto won ImageNet in 2012 with a neural network called AlexNet. ImageNet was a contest that scored computer programs on naming what is in photographs. Each program gave its five best guesses for each picture. AlexNet missed with all five on about 15 of every 100 pictures, and the second-place program missed on about 26 of every 100. After AlexNet's win, neural networks came into wide use, a time called the deep-learning era.

ChatGPT and programs like it produce text, pictures and computer code from requests typed in plain language. OpenAI released ChatGPT on November 30, 2022. Five days later, OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, posted online that it had "crossed 1 million users." Analysts at the bank UBS estimated that by the end of January 2023, about 100 million people a month were using it.

**Why they make mistakes.** Staff at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a federal agency, published a report in July 2024 on how large language models, programs that write text, work and why they make mistakes. A program of this kind predicts the next word in a sentence, over and over. That kind of guessing can produce true statements, and it can also produce false ones. The report states that the programs give false answers in a confident way, and that people who believe them can be misled.

**Fake cases in court.** Two lawyers in a federal court case in New York cited court decisions in 2023 that ChatGPT had made up. Lawyers cite, or point to, earlier decisions to show a judge how courts have ruled in cases like theirs.

Roberto Mata had sued the airline Avianca, saying a metal serving cart struck his knee on a flight. On March 1, 2023, his lawyers, Steven A. Schwartz and Peter LoDuca, filed a court paper that cited earlier decisions to support Mata's side. ChatGPT had made up those decisions and the quotes from them.

Schwartz later said that he asked ChatGPT whether one case was real, and it answered that the case "does indeed exist." In written orders, the judge then asked whether the cases existed, and the lawyers kept standing by them. On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel found that the lawyers had acted in bad faith, meaning dishonestly. He ordered the two lawyers and their law firm to pay a penalty of $5,000.

### Karla Ortiz

> **Who:** A San Francisco concept artist whose designs went into Marvel films and who told US senators how AI image programs affected artists.
> **When and where:** San Francisco and Washington, D.C., from 2022.

**"I am no longer certain of my future as an artist."**

Karla Ortiz is a concept artist, an artist who draws the first designs for characters and scenes. Her designs went into Marvel films, including *Doctor Strange*, *Black Panther* and *Avengers: Infinity War*. She worked on the video game *Final Fantasy 16* and the card game *Magic: the Gathering*, too.

Ortiz first saw an AI image program in April 2022. A website showed pictures made by a machine that copied the styles of named artists, her friends among them. No one had asked them. By August and September of 2022, the programs Midjourney, Stable Diffusion and DALL-E were in wide use.

**The lawsuit.** Ortiz, Sarah Andersen, Kelly McKernan and other artists sued Stability AI, Midjourney and DeviantArt in federal court on January 12, 2023. They said the companies had broken copyright law, the law that protects an artist's right to decide who copies their work.

**Her testimony.** Ortiz gave written testimony, a statement to lawmakers, to a subcommittee of the US Senate Judiciary Committee on July 12, 2023. In it she explained how the popular image programs were built. Their makers trained them on a collection of 5.8 billion pictures with captions, "taken indiscriminately from the web," meaning without anyone choosing which ones.

People at a nonprofit group called LAION, which the company Stability AI funded, built the collection. Ortiz wrote that the collection "includes almost all of my fine art work."

She described what she had seen in her own work. She had been part of three productions where generative AI, AI that makes new pictures, text or sound, had been "utilized," or used, "at different levels." "Some of my peers have lost jobs, some have had their duties lessened (and of course, their proposed pay)," she wrote.

She also wrote, "I am no longer certain of my future as an artist." She worked with researchers at the University of Chicago on Glaze, a program meant to protect artists' pictures from having their style copied.

US District Judge William Orrick ruled on August 12, 2024, that the artists' main copyright claims could go forward toward a trial. As of October 2, 2026, the case had not gone to trial. On September 28, 2026, Judge Orrick changed the schedule so that the next major motions in the case, formal requests to the judge, are to be heard by September 29, 2027.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Arrested by a false match" -->
Detroit police officers arrested Robert Williams, a Black man, at his home in Farmington Hills, Michigan, in January 2020, after a face-recognition search matched him by mistake. They arrested him in front of his wife and two young daughters and in view of his neighbors.

The software, which names possible matches for a face from a collection of photos, had matched a blurry picture from a store's security video to his expired driver's license photo. The video showed a theft of watches from a Shinola store in Detroit in 2018. Williams was not the man in the video.

Police used the match to build a photo lineup, a set of photos shown to someone who might pick out the thief. They showed it to a contractor hired to prevent theft at the store, who had not seen the theft and knew it only from the video.

**Thirty hours.** Detroit police held Williams for 30 hours. The ACLU, the civil rights group whose lawyers represented him, described the place he was held as "an overcrowded, dirty cell." Wayne County prosecutors dropped the charges, but they kept the right to bring them again.

The county's prosecutor, Kym Worthy, apologized. "This case should not have been issued based on the DPD investigation, and for that we apologize," she said, using the initials of the Detroit Police Department. His was the first publicly reported case in which police wrongly arrested someone after a false face-recognition match.

At least three Detroit arrests of this kind are publicly known, according to reporting by Michigan Public in 2024. In 2023 Detroit police wrongly arrested Porcha Woodruff, who was pregnant, for a carjacking, stealing a car from its driver.

**The settlement.** Williams and lawyers for the City of Detroit settled his lawsuit on June 28, 2024. Under the settlement, Detroit police may not arrest anyone based on a face-recognition result without other evidence, and they may not build a photo lineup from a face-recognition match alone. They must train officers, including on the higher rate of wrong matches for people of color, and they must review cases back to 2017.

The share of Americans who have used ChatGPT has grown in each Pew survey. Researchers at the Pew Research Center found in February 2026, in a survey of 5,119 American adults, that 44 percent had used it, up from 18 percent in 2023 and 34 percent in 2025.

About half of adults in the 2026 survey used some AI chatbot, a program that answers typed questions in sentences. Among adults with jobs, 38 percent used chatbots for tasks at work. Forty percent of adults expected AI to have a bad effect on society over the next 20 years.

Some employers say AI is the reason they are cutting jobs. Staff at Challenger, Gray & Christmas, a company that helps laid-off workers find new jobs, count the job cuts that American employers announce and the reasons the employers give.

From January through August 2026, employers named AI as the reason for 116,175 announced job cuts. That was about 22 percent of all the cuts they announced, more than one in five and more than for any other reason. These numbers are employers' own statements, not a government count of jobs lost to AI.

**The three questions, answered**
- Why did planters enslave far more people after the cotton gin? One gin cleaned about 50 pounds of cotton a day, work that took several hundred hours by hand. Planters grew far more cotton and enslaved far more people to grow and pick it, nearly 4 million by 1860.
- Why could an enslaved inventor not get a patent? Only the inventor could get a patent, and only by swearing an oath. In 1858 Attorney General Jeremiah Black ruled that an enslaved man was not a citizen and could not swear it, and that the enslaver could not take the patent instead.
- What went wrong when people trusted a computer's answer? In 2023 two lawyers cited court decisions that ChatGPT had made up, and a judge ordered them and their law firm to pay a $5,000 penalty. In 2020 Detroit police held Robert Williams for 30 hours after a face-recognition search matched him by mistake.

**What changed:** Work done by hand moved to machines run by water, then steam and electricity, then pocket-sized computers.
