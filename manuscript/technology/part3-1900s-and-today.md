## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Wilbur and Orville Wright flew a powered airplane in North Carolina on December 17, 1903. Ten years later, on October 7, 1913, workers at Henry Ford's car plant in Michigan started a moving assembly line. A motor and a rope pulled each car frame past 140 workers, who added parts as it moved. Over the next few decades, electric refrigerators and radios went into American homes, and chemists made the first plastics. In February 1946 engineers at the University of Pennsylvania showed ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer. In December 1947 scientists at Bell Labs made the first transistor work. A transistor is a small part that controls the flow of electricity and can make a weak signal stronger. Transistors later replaced the glass vacuum tubes that machines like ENIAC used.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-technology" name="The Wright brothers" movie="Wright Brothers' Flying Machine (2003)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers

> **Who:** Wilbur and Orville Wright, bicycle builders from Dayton, Ohio. They invented an airplane with an engine that a pilot could steer and control.
> **When and where:** Near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, December 17, 1903.
> **Movie:** *Wright Brothers' Flying Machine* (2003), a documentary in the PBS series *NOVA*, first shown on November 11, 2003. It is about the machine and how the brothers built it.

On December 17, 1903, near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, the Wright brothers made four flights in an airplane with an engine. Orville flew first. He stayed in the air for 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. On the fourth flight of the day, Wilbur flew 852 feet in 59 seconds. Five people watched. One of them, John T. Daniels of the Life-Saving Station at Kill Devil Hills, squeezed the camera's shutter bulb and took the photograph of the first flight. It was the first photograph he had ever taken.

The brothers worked out how to steer an airplane on gliders, which have no engine, in 1901 and 1902. An airplane can move in three ways besides going forward. Its wing tips can move up and down, which is called roll. Its nose can move up and down, which is called pitch. The nose can also swing from side to side, which is called yaw. The Wrights used cables that twisted the tips of the wings up or down to control roll. They added a movable part at the front, called an elevator, in 1901 to control pitch, and a movable upright part at the back, called a rudder, in 1902 to control yaw.

To test the shapes of wings, the brothers built their own wind tunnel, in which a fan blows a steady stream of air past a model. They also built a light gasoline engine in the workshop of their bicycle shop in Dayton.

On September 17, 1908, Army officers were testing the Wright airplane at Fort Myer, Virginia. Orville flew it with Lieutenant Thomas E. Selfridge of the Army Signal Corps as his passenger. The Signal Corps was the Army's communications branch, and it was then in charge of the Army's first aircraft. One of the propellers split and hit a wire, and the plane dived into the ground. Selfridge's skull was broken. He never woke up, and he died that evening. He was the first person killed in an airplane crash. Orville broke a leg and several ribs and spent seven weeks in the Army hospital.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-technology" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-technology" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford

> **Who:** The carmaker whose workers at Highland Park, Michigan, built the Model T on a moving assembly line.
> **When and where:** Highland Park, Michigan. The moving chassis line started on October 7, 1913.
> **Movie:** *Henry Ford* (2013), a documentary in the PBS series *American Experience*, directed by Sarah Colt. It is about Ford's life and his company.

At Henry Ford's plant in Highland Park, Michigan, the time it took to build the frame of a car fell from more than 12 hours to about 93 minutes. The workers got that speed by building on a moving assembly line. On a moving line, a motor pulls the car or part past the workers, and each worker adds parts or does one task as it goes by.

The line was tried first on one part. In the spring of 1913 workers put together the flywheel magneto, an engine part that makes electricity, on a line. The job was split into 29 steps done by 29 men along a moving belt. On October 7, 1913, the chassis line started. The chassis is the frame of the car, with its wheels. A motor and a rope pulled each chassis along a line 150 feet long, past 140 workers. At first the time to build one fell to under 3 hours, and later to about 93 minutes.

In January 1914 Ford announced a pay of $5 for an eight-hour day. Before that, workers had earned about $2.34 for nine hours. Part of his reason was to keep workers who hated the pace of the line.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics is a federal office whose staff gather facts about work. Its count for 1913 recorded about 23,000 deaths of workers in industry, among 38 million workers. That was 61 deaths for every 100,000 workers. The count covers all kinds of industrial work, not only work at machines.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-technology" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wired home" -->
In 1906 Lee de Forest invented the Audion, a part that made weak radio signals stronger. The Audion was a vacuum tube, a glass tube with the air taken out, like a light bulb. Inside it were a thin wire called a filament, a metal plate, and a bent wire between them called the grid. A small electric current on the grid controlled a bigger current between the other two parts. The Audion could also pick up radio signals. Tubes like it made radios for the home practical.

On September 7, 1927, Philo Farnsworth, age 21, sent the first all-electronic television picture from his lab on Green Street in San Francisco. The picture was a straight line. He made it with his own camera tube, called the image dissector.

The same year, the General Electric company began selling the Monitor Top, an electric refrigerator for the home. It cost about $300. By the middle of 1929, buyers had bought more than a quarter of a million of them. It was the first home electric refrigerator sold in large numbers. The economist Emanuela Cardia studied census counts of what American households owned. In 1930, 14 percent of households had a mechanical refrigerator, one that kept itself cold with a motor instead of a block of ice. By 1940 the share was 44 percent, and by 1950 it was 80 percent.

A plastic is a material that can be shaped while it is soft and then sets hard. In 1907 Leo Baekeland, a chemist born in Belgium who lived in the United States, made Bakelite from two chemicals, phenol and formaldehyde. It was the first fully synthetic plastic, which means it was made entirely from chemicals rather than from anything that grew. He filed for a patent in July 1907 and received it on December 7, 1909. Nylon, a strong thread-like material made from chemicals, came from Wallace Carothers's work at the DuPont chemical company, which he joined in 1928. On February 28, 1935, Gerard Berchet made the first half ounce of nylon. DuPont officials announced it to the public on October 27, 1938.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tractors replace horses" -->
In 1920 census takers counted 246,000 tractors on American farms, and about 26 million horses and mules. The Fordson tractor of 1917 was cheap and made in large numbers. About three out of every four American tractors built in the 1920s were Fordsons.

In 1924 the International Harvester company began selling the Farmall. It could do the jobs other tractors did, and it could also cultivate, which means breaking up the soil and clearing weeds between rows of growing crops. Cultivating was the last farm job that horses still did. The Census of Agriculture of 1954 was the first to record more tractors than horses and mules on American farms. It recorded about 4.6 million tractors and about 4 million horses and mules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first computers and the transistor" -->
ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, was shown to the public at the University of Pennsylvania in February 1946. General-purpose means it could be set up to solve many kinds of problems, not only one. John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert designed it at the university's Moore School, and the US Army provided what it took to build it. It was built to work out artillery firing tables, charts that told soldiers how to aim their big guns. ENIAC had 17,468 vacuum tubes, weighed more than 27 tons, and filled 1,800 square feet. It could not store a program. For each new set of calculations, its programmers moved its switches and plugged its cables into new places by hand.

At Bell Labs, a group of scientists led by William Shockley looked for a replacement for vacuum tubes, which often failed. On December 16, 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain placed two gold contacts, very close together, on a small slab of germanium, a chemical element. The electric push, or voltage, on one contact controlled the flow of electricity, or current, through the other. The device made a signal up to 100 times stronger. It was the first transistor. Workers at Bell Labs chose the name "transistor" in a vote, and the company's officials announced it to the public on June 30, 1948. Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eniac-programmers" name="The six ENIAC programmers" movie="The Computers (2014)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The six ENIAC programmers

> **Who:** Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty Mauchly Antonelli, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence and Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum. They were the six women who programmed ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer.
> **When and where:** University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, 1945 and 1946.
> **Movie:** *The Computers* (2014), a short documentary first shown at the Seattle International Film Festival in 2014. The six programmers are its subject.

Six women programmed ENIAC, but no one invited them to the dinner held for the machine's public showing in 1946. The records do not say who made the guest list. Their names were Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty Mauchly Antonelli, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence and Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum.

ENIAC had no way to store a program. No one taught the six how to program it. They learned at first by studying drawings of the machine's wiring. To give ENIAC a new problem, they moved its switches and plugged its cables into new places by hand. They were hired to work out the paths of artillery shells for the Army, and later they also ran calculations for the scientists at Los Alamos, the laboratory in New Mexico where the atomic bomb was built.

Their story stayed untold for decades. Kathy Kleiman learned about the six while she was researching her college thesis, a long paper written at the end of college. She started the ENIAC Programmers Project to record their story. In 1997 the six were named to a hall of fame for their work.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eniac-programmers" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ernest-lawrence-technology" name="Ernest Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ernest Lawrence

> **Who:** A scientist who built the cyclotron, a machine that whirls particles in a spiral and uses them to smash atoms.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from 1930 and 1931.

Ernest O. Lawrence's first cyclotron was small enough to hold in one hand. He built it at Berkeley, California, in 1930 and 1931, out of glass, sealing wax and bronze. It sped up hydrogen ions, which are hydrogen atoms with an electric charge, to an energy of 80,000 electron volts. An electron volt is a very small unit of energy used for particles.

Each machine Lawrence built after that was bigger. The first model was followed by machines 11 inches, 27 inches and 37 inches across. The 60-inch cyclotron of 1939 had a magnet that weighed 220 tons. Inside each machine, particles whirled in a spiral and then struck atoms and broke them apart.

Scientists used cyclotrons to make elements that do not occur in nature. They also made radioactive materials for use in medicine. Lawrence won the Nobel Prize in 1939. One later machine at Berkeley, the 88-Inch Cyclotron, still runs today.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ernest-lawrence-technology" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments built the first integrated circuit in 1958. It put a whole electronic circuit on one small chip. On October 29, 1969, a student at the University of California, Los Angeles, or UCLA, sent the first message between two computers on ARPANET, a network that linked computers at universities. Martin Cooper of Motorola made the first call on a handheld cell phone four years later. The magazine *Popular Electronics* announced the Altair 8800, a computer sold as a kit of parts, in January 1975, and IBM began selling the IBM PC in 1981. The IBM PC became the standard office computer. All 24 satellites of the Global Positioning System, or GPS, were working by 1995.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="grace-hopper" name="Grace Hopper" movie="The Queen of Code (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Grace Hopper

> **Who:** A mathematician and Navy officer who built the A-0 compiler, a program that lets people write instructions for a computer in words closer to English.
> **When and where:** Harvard University from 1944, then Remington Rand, the company that made the UNIVAC computer. Born in New York in 1906, died in 1992.
> **Movie:** *The Queen of Code* (2015), a short documentary directed by Gillian Jacobs, part of the FiveThirtyEight series *Signals*. It is about Hopper's life and work.

Grace Hopper built the A-0 compiler in 1951 and 1952. A compiler is a program that takes instructions written closer to human language and translates them into instructions the computer can carry out.

Hopper was born in New York on December 9, 1906, and trained as a mathematician. During World War II she served as a lieutenant in the WAVES, the women's branch of the Navy. In July 1944 she was assigned to the Harvard Mark I computer, and she became one of its first programmers. In 1946 she wrote the first long manual on how to program a computer.

She built the A-0 compiler while working on the UNIVAC computer at the Remington Rand company. From 1955 to 1959 she worked on FLOW-MATIC, a programming language for business that used English words. A programming language is a set of words and rules for writing instructions a computer can follow. Later, the designers of COBOL, another business programming language, drew on FLOW-MATIC.

Hopper retired from the Navy in 1986 as a rear admiral. She was 79, and at that time she was the oldest officer on active duty in the Navy. She died on January 1, 1992.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="grace-hopper" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The chip and its two inventors" -->
An integrated circuit, or chip, is a whole electronic circuit built on one small piece of material. A circuit is a path that electricity flows through, with parts along the way that do jobs such as switching it on and off. Two engineers working at two different companies each made a chip, and histories of the chip identify both men as its inventors.

Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments showed the first integrated circuit on September 12, 1958. He made it from germanium, and he connected its parts with wires by hand. In 1959 Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor designed a chip made of silicon, another chemical element. On Noyce's chip the wiring lay flat and was printed onto the surface. That meant factories could make chips in large numbers. Every later chip was built in Noyce's flat form. Silicon Valley, the area south of San Francisco where many computer companies started, takes its name from this element.

Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000. Noyce had died in 1990. Kilby said in public that Noyce deserved to share the prize.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marty-cooper" name="Martin Cooper" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Martin Cooper

> **Who:** A Motorola engineer who made the first call on a handheld cell phone.
> **When and where:** A street in Manhattan, New York City, April 3, 1973.

The first call on a handheld cell phone was made on a street in Manhattan on April 3, 1973. Martin Cooper, an engineer for the Motorola company, made it while walking. He used the phone to call Joel Engel, his rival at Bell Labs.

The phone Motorola sold to the public came 10 years later. The DynaTAC 8000X went on sale in 1983. According to an EBSCO reference article and news reports on the call's 50th anniversary in 2023, it cost $3,995 and weighed 28 ounces, almost 2 pounds. One charge of its battery gave about 30 minutes of talking.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marty-cooper" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The personal computer" -->
In January 1975 the Altair 8800, a computer sold as a kit, was announced in the magazine *Popular Electronics*. A kit is a box of parts that the buyer puts together. On March 5, 1975, about 30 people came through a rainstorm to Gordon French's garage in Menlo Park, California, to see an Altair. That meeting was the first of the Homebrew Computer Club, a group of people who built their own computers.

Apple Computer was founded on April 1, 1976. Its founders were Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak and, for a short time, Ron Wayne. The Apple II came out in June 1977. It was a finished computer that people could plug in at home. On August 12, 1981, IBM brought out the IBM PC, and its design became the standard for computers in offices.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-felsenstein" name="Lee Felsenstein" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lee Felsenstein

> **Who:** An engineer who ran the meetings of the Homebrew Computer Club and designed two early small computers, the Sol-20 and the Osborne 1.
> **When and where:** Berkeley and the San Francisco Bay Area, California, from 1973. Born in Philadelphia in 1945.

In 1973, Lee Felsenstein and four other people set up a computer terminal that anyone could use, in a record store in Berkeley, California. A terminal is a keyboard and a printer or screen connected to a computer somewhere else. His partners were Efrem Lipkin, Ken Colstad, Jude Milhon and Mark Szpakowski. They called the project Community Memory. They put the terminal at Leopold's Records, next to the store's paper bulletin board. People typed notes about music, politics, housing, services, women's groups and technology, and other people read them. The Computer History Museum's profile of Felsenstein describes it as one of the earliest online communities. The last version of the project closed in 1992.

Community Memory ran on a large computer called an SDS-940, which was donated for the project. It was a time-sharing computer, which means many people could use it at once from different terminals. The project worked with Resource One, a nonprofit group, one run for a cause rather than for profit, started by three students who had dropped out of computer science at Berkeley.

Felsenstein was born in Philadelphia in 1945. In 1964, as a student at the University of California, Berkeley, he took part in the Free Speech Movement, a protest by Berkeley students for the right to speak and organize about politics on campus. Police arrested him at the Sproul Hall sit-in, a protest in which students sat inside a campus building and refused to leave. He earned a degree in electrical engineering and computer science at Berkeley in 1972.

He was one of the first members of the Homebrew Computer Club. He ran its open meetings, keeping order with a blackboard pointer. With Gordon French and Bob Marsh, at a company called Processor Technology, he designed the Sol-20. It was one of the first small computers sold with its own case and keyboard. More than 10,000 were sold from 1977 to 1979. He then designed the Osborne 1 for Adam Osborne's company. It was a portable computer with a 5-inch screen. It cost $1,795, and more than 125,000 were sold in 1982.

His motto, as the Computer History Museum records it, is "To change the rules, change the tools." In 2016 he was honored as a Fellow of the museum for his influence on early personal computing.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-felsenstein" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jobs-wozniak" name="Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak" movie="Steve Jobs (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak

> **Who:** The two founders of Apple Computer. Wozniak designed the Apple I and the Apple II. Jobs made the first sale.
> **When and where:** Jobs's garage in California, from 1976.
> **Movie:** *Steve Jobs* (2015), directed by Danny Boyle and written by Aaron Sorkin. It is a drama, not a documentary. Actors play the real people, and it is based on Walter Isaacson's biography of Jobs. *Steve Jobs: The Man in the Machine* (2015), directed by Alex Gibney, is a documentary about Jobs.

Steve Wozniak designed Apple's first two computers, and Steve Jobs found the first buyer. The two met while they were both working at the Hewlett-Packard company. In 1975 Wozniak began developing the Apple I. He later said that the Homebrew Computer Club was where the Apple I got its start.

To pay for the first working model, Jobs sold his Volkswagen and Wozniak sold his scientific calculator, for $1,300 in all. On April 1, 1976, they founded Apple Computer. Ron Wayne was a partner for a short time. Jobs and Wozniak built the first Apple I computers in Jobs's garage. On Jobs's first sales call, a store called the Byte Shop ordered 50 of them at $500 each. The Apple I came without a case, a keyboard, a screen or a power supply. Buyers added their own.

Wozniak designed the Apple II in 1977. It came in one case, with the electronics, the keyboard and the power supply inside. It could show color pictures. It also came with the programming language BASIC stored in its permanent memory, a part that keeps what is stored in it even when the power is off. A floppy disk drive for storing files was added in 1978, and VisiCalc, a spreadsheet program for working with tables of numbers, came out in 1979. The Apple II then sold in large numbers. Apple's sales grew from $7.8 million in 1978 to $117 million in 1980.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jobs-wozniak" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The network" -->
ARPANET was a computer network paid for by the Department of Defense to link computers at universities. On October 29, 1969, Charley Kline, a student programmer in Leonard Kleinrock's lab at UCLA, tried to log in to a computer at Stanford Research Institute. He typed "L" and "O," the first two letters of LOGIN, and then the system crashed. So the first message ever sent on the network was "LO." About an hour later the full login worked. On January 1, 1983, the computers on ARPANET began using TCP/IP, the internet's shared set of rules for moving data between computers. ARPANET was the network from which the internet began.

The World Wide Web is a different thing from the internet. The web is a system of pages linked to one another, which people read over the internet. A British computer scientist, Tim Berners-Lee, invented it at CERN, a European physics laboratory in Switzerland. He proposed it in 1989 and opened it to the public on August 6, 1991. Americans built the program that let ordinary people use the web easily. On April 22, 1993, Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina and others at the University of Illinois released Mosaic. It was the first widely used graphical browser, a program that shows web pages with pictures.

GPS, the Global Positioning System, is a set of satellites owned by the United States. Each satellite sends out a signal that gives its position and the time. A GPS receiver picks up these signals and uses them to work out where it is. The first GPS satellite went up on February 22, 1978. The full set of 24 satellites was working in 1995. Until 2000, under a policy called Selective Availability, US government officials made the GPS signals for the public less exact on purpose, for national security. In May 2000 President Bill Clinton ordered the policy ended. After that, ordinary GPS receivers could find their place to within about 10 to 20 meters, close enough to give driving directions turn by turn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The smoke detector" -->
The ionization smoke detector, a cheap alarm made in large numbers for homes, holds about a third of a millionth of a gram of americium-241. Americium is a radioactive element that scientists discovered in 1944 and 1945. It gives off tiny particles called alpha particles. The particles knock bits of electric charge loose from the air in a small chamber inside the detector, so a very small electric current can flow through the air. When smoke enters the chamber, it disturbs the current, and the alarm goes off.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 2025, 91 percent of American adults owned a smartphone, a pocket-sized computer that is also a phone, according to a survey by the Pew Research Center. Apple began selling the iPhone in 2007. In 2022 the company OpenAI released ChatGPT, a program that writes answers to typed requests. By February 2026, 44 percent of American adults told Pew they had used it. AI programs also make mistakes. In 2020 Detroit police arrested the wrong man after a face-recognition search, a computer comparison of a photo of a face with a collection of photos, returned a false match.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The smartphone" -->
Steve Jobs of Apple announced the iPhone on January 9, 2007. It went on sale on June 29, 2007, for $499 or $599, with a phone contract. More than a million were sold in the first 74 days, according to news reports at the time. A smartphone puts a computer, a camera, a phone, a GPS receiver and a music player into one machine that fits in a pocket. Social media apps, programs for posting and sharing with other people, run on these phones.

The Pew Research Center is a research group that runs large surveys. Its researchers have asked Americans whether they own a smartphone since 2011. In their first count, in 2011, 35 percent of adults owned a smartphone. In a survey of 5,022 adults from February to June 2025, 91 percent did, and 98 percent owned some kind of cell phone. Older people were less likely to own one. Among adults aged 18 to 49, 97 percent had a smartphone, against 76 percent of those 65 and older.

For some Americans, the phone is the only way to get online. In the same 2025 survey, 78 percent of adults had broadband at home, a fast internet connection that is always on. Among adults in households earning less than $30,000 a year, 34 percent reached the internet only through a smartphone. Among those earning $100,000 or more, 4 percent did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Batteries, printers and gene tools" -->
The battery in an electric car is a lithium-ion battery, a kind that can be recharged many times. Scientists in three countries worked on it, starting in the 1970s. M. Stanley Whittingham began work on rechargeable lithium batteries then, working for the company Exxon in New Jersey. John Goodenough, an American chemist then working at Oxford in England, found the material for the battery's cathode, its positive end, in 1980. The material was lithium cobalt oxide. Akira Yoshino in Japan built a safe early model with a carbon anode, the negative end, in 1985, and the Sony company in Japan began selling the first lithium-ion batteries in 1991. Whittingham, Goodenough and Yoshino shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2019.

A 3D printer builds a solid object out of many thin layers, one on top of another. Chuck Hull invented stereolithography, a method of 3D printing in which the machine hardens each thin layer in turn, in 1983 and 1984. He applied for a patent on August 8, 1984, and received it on March 11, 1986. In the 2010s, 3D printers spread to factories and to hobbyists' desks.

On June 28, 2012, the scientists Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier published a paper on CRISPR, a tool for changing the genes of living things. Genes are the instructions inside living cells that shape how a living thing grows and works. The two shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="limor-fried" name="Limor Fried" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Limor Fried

> **Who:** An engineer trained at MIT who founded Adafruit Industries, a company that makes electronics kits and teaches people to build with them.
> **When and where:** MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, then New York City, from 2005.

Adafruit Industries began in 2005 in Limor Fried's dorm room at MIT. She had earned a bachelor's degree from MIT in 2003, and she finished a master's degree in engineering there in 2005. Adafruit sells electronics kits along with lessons on how to build them. Its designs are open source, which means anyone may copy them and change them.

According to the company's website, Fried was the first woman engineer on the cover of *Wired* magazine, and women own all of the company. Adafruit's workers make its products in the company's factory in New York City. In 2024 Adafruit moved to Industry City in Brooklyn. In about 2020, the company's website stated that it had more than 100 employees.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="limor-fried" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="AI, plainly" -->
AI, short for artificial intelligence, is a name for computer programs that do tasks such as recognizing a face or writing a paragraph. A neural network is one kind of AI program. A computer tunes its settings automatically by running it on a huge number of examples, so no person writes out its rules step by step. People call this tuning learning.

In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet won ImageNet, a contest in which computer programs try to name what is in photographs. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton built it at the University of Toronto. AlexNet's top five guesses missed the right answer for 15.3 percent of the pictures. The second-place program missed for 26.2 percent. After AlexNet's win, neural networks came into wide use, a time called the deep-learning era.

On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. According to news reports, about a million people used it in its first five days. Within two months, an estimated 100 million people a month were using it. ChatGPT and programs like it produce text, pictures and computer code from requests typed in plain language.

In July 2024 the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a federal agency, published a report on how large language models, programs that write text, work and why they make mistakes. A program of this kind predicts the next word in a sentence, over and over. That kind of guessing can produce true statements, and it can also produce false ones. The report states that the programs give false answers in a confident way, and that people who believe them can be misled.

In 2023, in a federal court case in New York, lawyers filed court decisions that ChatGPT had made up. Roberto Mata had sued the airline Avianca, saying a metal serving cart struck his knee on a flight. On March 1, 2023, his lawyers Steven A. Schwartz and Peter LoDuca filed a court paper that cited earlier court decisions. Those decisions did not exist. ChatGPT had produced them, with fake quotes.

Schwartz later said that he asked ChatGPT whether one case was real, and it answered that the case "does indeed exist." The judge then issued orders that questioned whether the cases existed, and the lawyers kept standing by them. On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel found that the lawyers had acted in bad faith, which means dishonestly. He ordered the two lawyers and their law firm to pay a penalty of $5,000.

### Karla Ortiz

> **Who:** A concept artist and illustrator in San Francisco whose designs went into Marvel films. She told the US Senate how AI image programs affected artists.
> **When and where:** San Francisco and Washington, D.C., from 2022.

On July 12, 2023, Karla Ortiz gave written testimony to a subcommittee of the US Senate Judiciary Committee about AI programs that make pictures. Testimony is a statement given to a court or to lawmakers. Ortiz is a concept artist, an artist who draws the first designs for characters and scenes. Her designs went into Marvel films, including *Doctor Strange*, *Black Panther* and *Avengers: Infinity War*. She also worked on the video game *Final Fantasy 16* and the card game *Magic: the Gathering*.

Ortiz first saw an AI image program in April 2022. A website showed pictures made by a machine that copied the styles of named artists, her friends among them. No one had asked them. By August and September of 2022, the programs Midjourney, Stable Diffusion and DALL-E were in wide use.

In her testimony, Ortiz explained how the popular image programs were built. Their makers trained them on a collection of 5.8 billion pictures with captions, "taken indiscriminately from the web." A nonprofit group called LAION, which the company Stability AI funded, built the collection. Ortiz wrote that the collection "includes almost all of my fine art work."

She described what she had seen in her own work. "I have personally been a part of three productions where Generative AI has been utilized at different levels," she wrote. "Some of my peers have lost jobs, some have had their duties lessened (and of course, their proposed pay)." She also wrote, "I am no longer certain of my future as an artist." She worked with researchers at the University of Chicago on Glaze, a program meant to protect artists' pictures from having their style copied.

On January 12, 2023, Ortiz, Sarah Andersen, Kelly McKernan and other artists sued Stability AI, Midjourney and DeviantArt in federal court. They said the companies had broken copyright law, the law that protects an artist's right to decide who copies their work. On August 12, 2024, US District Judge William Orrick ruled that their main copyright claims could go forward toward a trial. No verdict had been reported by late September 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A wrongful arrest and face recognition" -->
Face-recognition software compares a picture of a face with a collection of photos and names possible matches. In January 2020, Detroit police officers arrested Robert Williams, a Black man, at his home in Farmington Hills, Michigan. They arrested him in front of his wife and two young daughters and in view of his neighbors. A face-recognition search had matched a blurry picture from a store's security video to his expired driver's license photo. The video showed a theft of watches from a Shinola store in Detroit in 2018. Williams was not the man in the video.

Police used the match to build a photo lineup, a set of photos shown to someone who might pick out the thief. They showed it to a contractor hired to prevent theft at the store, who had not seen the theft and knew it only from the video. Williams was held for 30 hours in what the ACLU, the civil rights group whose lawyers represented him, called "an overcrowded, dirty cell." Wayne County prosecutors dropped the charges. The county's prosecutor, Kym Worthy, apologized. "This case should not have been issued based on the DPD investigation, and for that we apologize," she said. DPD is the Detroit Police Department. His was the first publicly reported case in which police wrongly arrested someone after a false face-recognition match.

At least three Detroit arrests of this kind are publicly known, according to reporting by Michigan Public in 2024. In 2023 Detroit police wrongly arrested Porcha Woodruff for a carjacking. She was pregnant. On June 28, 2024, Williams and lawyers for the City of Detroit settled his lawsuit. Under the settlement, Detroit police may not arrest anyone based on a face-recognition result without other evidence. They may not build a photo lineup from a face-recognition match alone. They must train officers, including on the higher rate of wrong matches for people of color, and they must review cases back to 2017.

In a survey of 5,119 American adults in February 2026, researchers at the Pew Research Center found that 44 percent had used ChatGPT. The share was 18 percent in 2023 and 34 percent in 2025. About half of adults in the 2026 survey used some AI chatbot, a program that answers typed questions in sentences. Among adults with jobs, 38 percent used chatbots for tasks at work. Forty percent of adults expected AI to have a bad effect on society over the next 20 years.

Some employers say AI is the reason they are cutting jobs. Challenger, Gray & Christmas is an outplacement firm, a company that helps laid-off workers find new jobs. Its staff count the job cuts that American employers announce, and the reasons the employers give. From January through August 2026, employers named AI as the reason for 116,175 announced job cuts. That was about 22 percent of all the cuts they announced, more than for any other reason. These numbers are employers' own statements. They are not a government count of jobs lost to AI.
