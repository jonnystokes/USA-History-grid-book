## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, people in North America made their tools from wood, stone, bone, clay, animal skin and, near Lake Superior, copper. They built no metal machines. They did build traps that caught fish with the tide, fields that grew three crops together, and boats made of birch bark. The names of the people who first worked out these methods are not recorded. Some of the methods are still practiced today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Weapons and food machines" -->
Before the bow and arrow, hunters in western North America threw spears with an atlatl. An atlatl is a spear-thrower, a stick that holds the back end of the spear while the hunter throws it.

About 1,400 years ago, around the years A.D. 600 to 700, the bow and arrow reached hunters across the West. A study published in 2026 in the *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* gives that date. South of an east-west line called the 55th parallel, archaeologists stop finding atlatls soon after the bow appears. Archaeologists are scientists who dig up and study objects people left behind. North of that line, hunters used both weapons side by side for more than 1,000 years.

People also built fixed traps to catch fish. A fish weir is a fence of wooden stakes set across the water, with bundles of brush woven between the stakes. Fish swim in over the mud flats with the rising tide, and the fence traps them when the water goes out. People then scooped them up with baskets and nets, or caught them by hand. Lines of wooden stakes from fish weirs lie under Boylston Street in Boston. People built these weirs in tidal channels between about 4,700 and 3,700 years ago. They used them for more than a thousand years, until mud filled the bay around 1550 B.C., about 3,500 years ago.

Native farmers planted corn, beans and squash together in the same field. These three crops are called the Three Sisters. People also set controlled fires, which means fires they started on purpose and kept in bounds, to shape the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Built crafts and a calendar in wood" -->
People made pottery from clay and wove baskets. They tanned animal hides, which means they treated the skins so they would not rot and would stay soft, and they made canoes from the bark of birch trees.

At Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, people built the largest city north of Mexico before 1500. Between about A.D. 900 and 1100, builders there raised five circles of upright wooden posts, one after another. Each circle had between 24 and 72 posts and measured about 240 to 475 feet (73 to 145 meters) across. The posts marked the places on the horizon where the sun rose at different times of the year, so the circles worked as a calendar.

Near Lake Superior, people worked copper for thousands of years. They worked the copper cold, which means they shaped it without melting it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wayne-valliere" name="Wayne Valliere" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Wayne Valliere

> **Who:** An Ojibwe builder of birchbark canoes and a teacher of the Ojibwe language. A National Heritage Fellow of the National Endowment for the Arts in 2020.
> **When and where:** Lac du Flambeau, Wisconsin, in the present day.

Birchbark canoes were among the boats people built before 1500, and Wayne Valliere builds them today. He is a member of the Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Ojibwe, in Wisconsin. The National Endowment for the Arts is a US government agency that gives money and honors to artists. Its profile of Valliere describes the Ojibwe way of building canoes as handed down over many generations.

According to the profile, Valliere began learning at 16. He learned from elders, among them Joe Chosa, Marvin DeFoe and Ojaanimigiizhig. He also studied old objects kept in museums, along with writings and photographs.

Valliere builds each canoe from birchbark, cedar, spruce roots and pitch. The hull, the body of the boat, is thick birchbark. Ribs of straight-grained cedar hold the hull in shape, and spruce roots serve as thread to sew the pieces of bark together. Pitch, a sticky tree sap, is mixed with oak ash and deer tallow, which is fat from a deer, to seal the seams against water. The builder gathers each material in a different season of the year.

Valliere has taught more than 100 students. At least one of them now builds canoes without his help. A second is learning as an apprentice, a beginner who learns a craft by working beside a master. Valliere also teaches the Ojibwe language at Lac du Flambeau Public School. In 2020 he received a National Heritage Fellowship from the National Endowment for the Arts. It is the highest honor in the United States for folk and traditional arts, the crafts and music that families and communities hand down.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
European metal reached Native towns on the East Coast in the 1500s, before most Europeans did. Native smiths, people who shape metal into tools, cut European copper and brass into tools of their own design. Brass is a yellow metal made by melting copper and zinc together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kettle metal, new tools" -->
Copper and brass from Europe reached the East Coast through trade with Europeans along the shore. Both metals had been made in European furnaces from rock dug out of the ground. Native traders passed the metal from one group to the next, so it reached people inland before most Europeans did. In 1524 Verrazzano, a European sailing along the Northeast coast, wrote that the Native people there had sheets of worked copper.

Some of the metal came as kettles, large cooking pots. Native smiths cut the kettles apart and hammered the pieces into arrow points, knives, pipe stems, bells and ornaments. They took the new metal and made it into the tools and goods they already used.

Spanish expeditions also crossed parts of what is now the United States in the 1500s, through lands where Native nations lived. The men on these expeditions carried iron weapons and tools. No lasting trade in tools followed their visits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists built water-powered mills and ironworks here in the 1600s. An ironworks is a place where workers make iron from rock dug out of the ground. Colonists dammed streams to turn waterwheels. The wheels drove grist mills, which ground grain into flour, and sawmills, which cut logs into boards. At the ironworks they worked the bellows and hammers. The first ironworks begun in the English colonies stood on Powhatan land in Virginia. The first one that ran for years stood on Pawtucket land in Massachusetts. Its workers were skilled English ironworkers, nearby farmers, at least two Native woodcutters and Scottish prisoners of war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Falling Creek, 1619 to 1622" -->
At Falling Creek, Virginia, colonists began the first ironworks in the English colonies. They began building it on Powhatan land at the falls of the James River, where the river drops over rocks, near present-day Richmond. The Virginia Company was the English company that ran the colony. Its officers hired Captain Benjamin Bluett in 1619 to run the ironworks. Chesterfield County's history of the site says he sailed with 80 men. A county tourism page says 150.

Bluett died on the voyage or soon after he arrived. Others of his men died too, of what colonists called seasoning. Seasoning was the sickness that killed between 50 and 90 percent of early newcomers, according to the county's history. The men who lived could not finish the furnace, the tall oven where iron is melted out of rock. In 1621 company officers hired John Berkeley to take over. He sailed with his son Maurice and 25 other men, according to the county. Another account gives his party as 20 ironworkers. He promised iron by June 9, 1622.

Opechancanough, a Powhatan leader, planned an attack on English settlements across the colony. From 1618 to 1620, English tobacco planters had taken Powhatan land without paying for it. Virginia Company officials had also pressed to take Powhatan children for English schooling.

On March 22, 1622, Powhatan warriors attacked the ironworks. They killed 27 English people there, John Berkeley among them. Two children at Falling Creek survived. The warriors left the works in ruins. Across the colony that day, Powhatan warriors killed about 350 English colonists. Maurice Berkeley was away from the works and lived. He tried to start the ironworks again and failed. Sir John Zouch tried again from 1634 to 1636 and also failed.

The records disagree about whether Falling Creek ever made iron. Virginia's official list of historic places states that workers produced iron there in 1620. The county's history states that Bluett's men could not finish the furnace, and in 1621 Berkeley was still promising iron for the next year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Iron from water power at Saugus" -->
John Winthrop Jr. started the first ironworks in the English colonies that ran for years, on the Saugus River in Massachusetts. He gathered about two dozen investors, people who put in money hoping for a profit, by 1643. They formed the Company of Undertakers of the Iron Works in New England. Undertakers here meant people who take on a big job. The works ran from 1646 to about 1670.

Seven waterwheels drove three kinds of machine. The first was a blast furnace, a tall furnace where air is blown into the fire to make it hot enough to melt iron out of rock. Bellows, giant bags that squeeze air into a fire, pumped the air. They were 18 feet long, and the fire reached about 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit. Next came a forge, where 10 to 12 men hammered the iron. The last was a rolling and slitting mill, which pressed iron into flat sheets and cut it into thin rods.

The ironworks burned more than 3,600 cords of wood a year. A cord is a stack of wood 8 feet long, 4 feet wide and 4 feet high. Workers cut the wood with axes and saws made at the works.

The Saugus works closed because of debt, high costs and competition from iron brought in from overseas. The men who had lent money sued. Some of the ironworkers, such as James Leonard, went on to run forges in other places. The works have been rebuilt on the old site, which is now the Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who did the work at Saugus" -->
The Saugus works stood on the land of the Pawtucket, the Native people who lived there when the English arrived. Saugus had been a Native settlement. The English first called it Lynn. About 100 people worked at the ironworks. They lived in a village the company owned, called Hammersmith after the English town that many of the ironworkers came from.

Skilled ironworkers were hired from the iron-making parts of England. Colonists from nearby farms did the unskilled work. Company records show that at least two Native people cut wood for the same wages as the other workers. The records do not give their names.

Some of the workers were prisoners of war. In September 1650, Oliver Cromwell's English soldiers defeated a Scottish army at Dunbar, in Scotland, and captured Scottish soldiers. Members of England's Council of State, the group of leaders that ran England's government at the time, ordered 150 of the prisoners handed over. Sir Arthur Haselrig gave them to agents of John Becx and Joshua Foote, two of the company's investors. The agents shipped the prisoners across the ocean on the ship *Unity*, which left Gravesend, England, on November 11, 1650. How many died on the voyage is not known.

The company's agents kept 62 of the Scots. About 36 of them reached Saugus in December 1650, and the agents put them to work there. A National Park Service history states the number as about 35, arriving in 1651. As prisoners, the Scots were bound to seven years of forced labor. The papers that bound them were called indentures. An indenture binds a person to work for a master for a set number of years. The Scots cut wood, worked as colliers, who make charcoal, and did other labor. They received clothing, food and tobacco.

Some of the Scots are known by name. A list of 35 Scots at the works in 1653 includes James Adams, John Clark, Alexander Innes, Peter Grant, John Stewart and Thomas Tower. A November 1653 list of the property of the works includes Alexander Innes, with a value of 10 pounds. The Scottish Prisoners of War Society prints these lists from the company's record books.

At the forge, flying sparks and pieces of hot metal were a constant danger to the men. The forge hammer weighed 500 pounds. Many workers lost their hearing from its noise. The surviving records of the works do not give a count of deaths.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cambridge printing press" -->
The first printing press in the English colonies arrived in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1638. A printing press is a machine that presses paper down hard onto metal letters covered in ink. The Reverend Jose Glover, a minister, bought the press and hired a locksmith named Stephen Daye to run it. Glover died on the voyage across the ocean. His widow, Elizabeth Glover, owned the press, and Daye ran it.

In 1640 Daye printed the *Bay Psalm Book* on the press. It was a book of psalms, the religious songs of the Bible, and it was the first book printed in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-jenks" name="Joseph Jenks" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Jenks

> **Who:** A sword maker from London who had worked at a water-powered sword mill at Hounslow, England, and then ran the blacksmith shop at the Saugus ironworks. US Patent Office officials credit him with the first patent for a machine in what is now the United States.
> **When and where:** Saugus, then part of Lynn, Massachusetts. At the works from 1646. Died in 1683.

US Patent Office officials credit Joseph Jenks with the first patent for a machine in what is now the United States, according to a National Park Service page about his shop. A patent is a government grant that gives an inventor the only right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. Massachusetts leaders granted Jenks his patent in 1646.

Jenks was born in about August 1599 and baptized in a church in London. His father was a cutler, a maker of knives. In the 1630s Jenks worked for a sword maker named Benjamin Stone, who ran a sword mill powered by water at Hounslow, England. A sword marked with Jenks's name as its maker survives in a museum in Wales. He next appears in court records in the Kittery area of Maine.

In May 1646 Jenks asked the Massachusetts General Court for the only right, for 14 years, to build water-driven mills. The General Court was the group of leaders who made the colony's laws. The mills would make scythes, long curved blades for cutting grass. He also asked to build "a new Invented Saw Mill" and other machines for making tools with edges. He explained why he wanted protection. Without it, he wrote, "Another when he seeth it makes the like." He went on, "and soe I loose the benefit." He meant that another man would see his machine and copy it, and Jenks would lose the profit. The court's magistrates, the leaders who sat as its judges, granted his request. His petition, the written request, survives in the Massachusetts Archives. The copy there is dated May 10, 1646.

On January 20, 1647, Richard Leader, the agent in charge of the ironworks, let Jenks build a water-driven hammer at the tail of the blast furnace. It ran on the water that had already turned the furnace wheel. The company owned the shop, and Jenks paid rent. There he made saw blades, felling saws for cutting down trees, and steelyards, which are weighing scales. He also drew iron into fine wire. In 1652 he sent two saw blades for a water-powered sawmill that John Winthrop Jr. ran in Connecticut.

The ironworks company went bankrupt, which means it could not pay its debts. In September 1656 Jenks bought a share of his shop, and in October the rolling and slitting mill, with a loan from Simon Bradstreet. By October 1667 he was "very weak in estate," which meant he had little money. He asked the court for 50 pounds to start making iron wire. He died in March 1683. His son, Joseph Jenks Jr., began working at Saugus at 16 in 1649 and built his own forge in the town of Pawtucket, Rhode Island, in 1672. That town is a different place from the land of the Pawtucket people at Saugus.

In 1952 the archaeologist Roland Robbins dug up the site of Jenks's shop. He found waterwheels, the supports of a large hammer, an anvil base and a stone forge. A marker put up in 1898 states that Jenks made the dies, the stamps, for the first silver coins made in New England in 1652. No document from the time shows that he did.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Colonists built iron furnaces and forges in Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia between 1700 and 1750. Waterwheels still drove the machines, and the furnaces still burned charcoal. At the Baltimore Iron Works in Maryland, which opened in 1731, 42 of the first 89 workers were enslaved. In Philadelphia, the printer Benjamin Franklin designed a new kind of stove in 1741 or 1742 and refused to patent it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Franklin stove" -->
Benjamin Franklin designed a stove that sent more of a fire's heat into the room and less of it up the chimney. Accounts of his life give the year as 1741 or 1742. The Franklin Institute, a science museum in Philadelphia, states 1742 in its history of his inventions.

Franklin's stove was a fireplace lined with metal. It stood a few inches out from the chimney. At the back was a hollow iron wall, called a baffle. Air from the room flowed into the baffle, picked up the fire's heat and came back out into the room. Behind the baffle was a U-shaped passage called an inverted siphon. The smoke had to go up over the baffle, down behind it and only then up the chimney, so the iron took in more of its heat on the way. The stove also made less smoke than an ordinary fireplace.

Franklin refused to patent the stove. He gave his reason later in his autobiography, the story of his life that he wrote himself: "That as we enjoy great Advantages from the Inventions of others, we should be glad of an Opportunity to serve others by any Invention of ours." He went on: "and this we should do freely and generously." He meant that everyone gains from other people's inventions, so people should share their own inventions for free. He never patented any of his inventions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who worked the iron furnaces" -->
Many of the workers at the Maryland ironworks were enslaved people or convicts. In 1719, investors in Britain set up the Principio Company in Cecil County, Maryland. In 1731 five Maryland planters founded the Baltimore Iron Works on the Patapsco River. They were Dr. Charles Carroll, another man named Charles Carroll, Daniel Carroll, Daniel Dulaney and Benjamin Tasker. Dr. Carroll put in 2,200 acres of land and served as the first manager. The five partners supplied the money and the enslaved workers.

The Patapsco valley lay in a region where the Susquehannock and the Piscataway lived. In 1652 five Susquehannock war chiefs had signed a treaty, a written agreement between nations, with Maryland colonial officials. In it they gave up their claim to the western shore of the Chesapeake Bay from the Patuxent River north to Palmer's Island. That stretch of shore includes the Patapsco. Emma Bilski, a history graduate student at Johns Hopkins University, points out that the land was not really the Susquehannock's to give away. Other nations, such as the Piscataway, lived in the region too.

The Baltimore Iron Works opened with 89 workers. Forty-two of them were enslaved. Over the years, many other workers there were convicts. They were people found guilty of crimes in Britain. Under a British law of 1718, English judges sentenced many such people to be shipped to the colonies. Merchants who held contracts with the British government carried them across the ocean and sold their labor to colonists, most often for seven years. Iron makers were among the buyers. The managers of the Baltimore Iron Works preferred enslaved workers to convicts. Many of the enslaved ironworkers were Igbo people from West Africa, who brought their own skill at working metal. By 1785 an advertisement for the works listed a workforce of "more than 200 Negroes." Negro was a word used then for a Black person.

The workers hauled charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore to the top of the furnace. They worked 12-hour shifts, day and night, six or seven days a week. The owners did not send enough food. In 1775 the manager, Clement Brooke, wrote to the partners: "I cannot do my job unless you send more slaves and more corn." The Mount Clare Museum's history of the works states that the workers and the animals were starving.

Each of the five owners took one fifth of the iron. In good years the partners earned 55 percent on their money each year. The works closed in 1799. In 1810 the 30 heirs of the original owners, the people who had inherited their shares, had the land that was left sold at auction. It came to more than 30,000 acres. The heirs had 200 enslaved people sold at the same auction.

Some workers ran away. In August 1754 someone placed an advertisement in the *Maryland Gazette* newspaper asking for the capture of a worker who had escaped. Others worked slowly. In the spring of 1771, an enslaved blacksmith named Anthony and an indentured servant named Thomas Livy were jailed for robbing a store, most likely for food. The record does not say who jailed them. The overseer, the man in charge of the workers, was William Hammond. He wrote about Anthony: "I cannot tell how we can carry on the Forge business without him."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-technology" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Philadelphia printer who designed the Franklin stove and later the lightning rod. He refused to patent any of his inventions.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston in 1706. Worked in Philadelphia. Died there in 1790.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a two-part documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about Franklin's life.

The printer Benjamin Franklin also designed devices for homes and buildings. He was born in Boston on January 17, 1706. Under the calendar used in the colonies at the time, the date was January 6. He made his living in Philadelphia, where he printed a newspaper and a yearly almanac, a book of calendars, weather guesses and sayings.

In 1741 or 1742 he designed the Franklin stove, a metal fireplace that put more of a fire's heat into the room. In the 1750s he designed the lightning rod, an iron rod that protects a building from lightning. He also ran experiments with electricity.

Franklin took out no patent on any of these inventions. In his autobiography he wrote that people gain from the inventions of others, so they should share their own inventions "freely and generously."

Franklin died in Philadelphia on April 17, 1790.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Benjamin Franklin's lightning rods went up on Philadelphia roofs in the 1750s, and the first steam engine in America began pumping water from a New Jersey mine in 1755. In 1790 Samuel Slater's cotton-spinning mill began work at Pawtucket, Rhode Island. It was the start of the factory textile industry in the United States, and Slater hired children to run its machines. In 1793 Eli Whitney built the cotton gin. One small gin could clean about 50 pounds of cotton a day, work that took several hundred hours by hand. Planters then grew far more cotton, and they held far more people in slavery to grow it. About 700,000 people were enslaved in the United States in 1790. By 1860 the number was nearly 4 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The lightning rod" -->
In a letter of 1750 to Peter Collinson, Franklin described a way to protect buildings from what he called "electrical fire." In 1752 there were lightning tests in France and then Franklin's own kite test. After those tests, the first lightning rods went up on buildings in Philadelphia.

Franklin printed instructions for making one in *Poor Richard's Almanack* for 1753. He told readers to take a small iron rod. One end went three or four feet into moist ground. The other end stood six or eight feet above the top of the building. A foot of brass wire, sharpened to a fine point, went on the top end. Small staples held the rod to the house. When lightning struck, it would hit the point and pass through the metal into the ground, "without hurting any Thing," Franklin wrote. The Franklin Institute's history of his inventions states that pointed rods soon stood on buildings throughout the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
The first steam engine in America pumped water out of a mine. It was ordered from England in 1748 for Colonel John Schuyler's copper mine, in what is now North Arlington, New Jersey. One other account names the owner as Philip Schuyler. The parts arrived in 1753. An engine builder named Josiah Hornblower put it together, and it was pumping by 1755.

It was a Newcomen engine, named for Thomas Newcomen, who built the first one in England in 1712. A boiler filled a large tube, called a cylinder, with steam. Then a spray of cold water turned the steam back into water, which left the cylinder nearly empty. The weight of the air outside pushed a piston, a tight-fitting plug, down into the cylinder. The piston pulled one end of a big rocking beam, and the other end of the beam worked the pump in the mine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The cotton gin and what it did" -->
Upland cotton has sticky seeds caught in its fiber. Before 1793, enslaved people pulled the seeds out by hand. One worker needed many hours to clean a single pound of cotton. Planters could grow only as much cotton as their workers could clean.

Eli Whitney's cotton gin did the cleaning by machine. It used two cylinders that combed the cotton through small holes. The seeds could not pass through the holes and stayed behind. A history of the gin by the Eli Whitney Museum states that one small gin could clean 50 pounds of cotton a day. By hand, that much cotton took several hundred hours of work.

Planters used the gin to grow far more cotton. Across the United States they grew fewer than 9,000 bales of cotton a year around 1790. By 1800 they grew more than 200,000 bales. By 1860 they grew about 4 million. A bale is a large bundle of cotton pressed tight and tied.

With the gin cleaning cotton so fast, planters wanted far more enslaved people to grow and pick it. The 1790 census, the government's count of everyone in the country, counted 697,897 enslaved people in the United States. By 1860 there were nearly 4 million, and about 2.5 million of them lived in the cotton-growing regions from eastern Maryland to Texas. Slaveholders spread slavery west across the South.

Enslaved people picked the cotton by hand in the summer sun, about 200 pounds each a day. Solomon Northup was a free Black man from New York. Kidnappers seized him in Washington, D.C., in 1841, and he was held in slavery for twelve years. He picked cotton on Edwin Epps's plantation in Louisiana until he was rescued in 1853.

In his book *Twelve Years a Slave*, published that year, Northup described the rule: "An ordinary day's work is two hundred pounds. A slave who is accustomed to picking, is punished, if he or she brings in a less quantity than that." Each night the cotton was weighed at the gin-house. "After weighing, follow the whippings," Northup wrote.

The punishment was the lash. A lash is one stroke of a whip on a person's bare body. Northup wrote that twenty-five lashes were "deemed a mere brush," which means they were thought of as a small punishment. Fifty was "the ordinary penalty," and one hundred was "called severe." Northup described Epps himself seizing the whip and lashing the people he enslaved. When Epps had an enslaved woman named Patsey whipped, Northup wrote, her back was left "covered with long welts," raised and swollen stripes, and "the lash was wet with blood."

Enslaved people also ran the gins and the cotton presses. The machines had many moving parts that could catch and injure a worker. The tiny cotton fibers the gins threw into the air damaged the workers' lungs.

The profit went to the people who owned the plantations. With the gin, Southern plantation owners gained money and political power. Their cotton supplied cloth mills in the Northern states and in Britain. By 1830, cloth and other finished cotton goods were the largest group of factory-made goods the country sold to other countries.

Planters also wanted new land for cotton. The Eli Whitney Museum's history of the gin calls that demand a major factor in the removal of five Native nations from their homelands in the Southeast. They were the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole. On May 28, 1830, President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act. It allowed the president to trade land west of the Mississippi River for the lands of Native nations inside the states. US officials then forced the five nations west to Indian Territory, land in what is now Oklahoma that the officials set aside for the nations they removed. At least 60,000 people were forced to make the journey, and more than 8,000 died on the way.

In 1838 US Army soldiers under General Winfield Scott began forcing the Cherokee out of their homeland. Scott was sent to another post soon after the removal began. In the fall and winter of 1838 and 1839, about 4,000 Cherokee died on the march west that the soldiers forced them to make. White landowners took the land the five nations had been forced to leave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eli-whitney" name="Eli Whitney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eli Whitney

> **Who:** The builder of the cotton gin, patented in 1794. He also promoted the idea of machine-made, interchangeable gun parts, which the workers at his own factory never managed to make.
> **When and where:** Georgia and New Haven, Connecticut. Born 1765, died 1825.

The patent on the cotton gin belonged to Eli Whitney. He was born in Westborough, Massachusetts, on December 8, 1765. He graduated from Yale College in 1792 and traveled south to work as a tutor, a private teacher.

On the way, Catherine Littlefield Greene invited him to her plantation, Mulberry Grove, north of Savannah, Georgia. She was the widow of General Nathanael Greene, who had died in 1786 and left her dozens of enslaved people. Enslaved workers on the plantation grew mostly rice and some cotton. There, in 1793, Whitney built the machine that pulled cotton fiber away from its seeds. He applied for a patent in 1794, and the patent was granted on March 14, 1794.

Whitney held the patent. Some historians credit Catherine Greene with part of the design. The Eli Whitney Museum states that it "has been suggested that Catherine Greene also contributed ideas to the design." The record does not settle how much of the design was hers.

In 1798 Whitney signed a contract to make muskets for the federal government. He promoted the idea of making gun parts by machine, so that any part would fit any gun of the same model. Parts like these are called interchangeable parts. According to a famous story, Whitney showed government officials such parts in 1801. Historians treat that story as legend.

In 1960 the historian Robert Woodbury showed that Whitney did not start the idea of interchangeable parts. The credit people gave Whitney for it rests on a myth. Gunmakers at French armories, factories that made weapons, had worked on the idea before Whitney. Workers at Whitney's own factory never made parts that truly fit any gun. The best-documented first American use of interchangeable parts in a factory came at John H. Hall's rifle works at the Harpers Ferry armory. Hall's workers were putting rifles together from interchangeable parts by 1819.

Whitney died in New Haven, Connecticut, on January 8, 1825.

### Samuel Slater

> **Who:** An English mill worker who rebuilt English spinning machines from memory at Pawtucket, the start of the American factory textile industry. He hired children to run the machines.
> **When and where:** Trained in Derbyshire, England. The mill ran at Pawtucket, Rhode Island, from December 1790.

Samuel Slater rebuilt English cotton-spinning machines from memory in the United States. In England, Richard Arkwright had patented a spinning machine in 1769. It was called the water frame because a waterwheel drove it. Arkwright and his partners built a mill several floors high at Cromford, in Derbyshire, with waterwheels to drive the machines. He moved cotton spinning out of homes and into powered mills with hundreds of workers. Other mill owners copied his system.

Slater trained in a mill in Derbyshire that used Arkwright's machines. British law did not allow textile machines to leave the country. It also did not allow skilled textile workers to move abroad. In 1789 Slater left England without declaring his trade. He did not state that he was a trained mill worker.

In December 1789 Moses Brown hired him. Brown's family firm, Almy & Brown, put up the money and the building. With local mechanics, people who build and repair machines, among them the Wilkinson family, Slater rebuilt the carding and spinning machines from memory. A carding machine combs raw cotton so the fibers lie straight. A spinning machine twists the fibers into thread, called yarn. The machines at Pawtucket spun their first yarn in December 1790.

Slater had worked in a factory for part of his own childhood. At Pawtucket he hired children to run the machines. The machines were built for children to run. Most of the children at Slater's mill were about 10 years old. Some were as young as six. They were paid by the hour, and they earned much less than adults. In 1801 a Boston politician described mills that employed "children from four to Ten years old." In the mill villages that grew up later, the child workers were 8 to 14 years old. The names of the first children who worked for Slater are not recorded.
