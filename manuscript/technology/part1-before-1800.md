## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 8 of 37 · Technology · 10 eras · about 85 minutes**

**Many famous American inventors had help that their patents did not name, and the hardest work around the new machines often fell to people who had no say in it.**

The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds a strip of paper tape from May 24, 1844. It records the first public message sent from Washington to Baltimore by telegraph, a machine that sent messages over a wire as pulses of electricity. The words were "What hath God wrought" (What has God done).

Samuel Morse sent the message, and the code is named for him. He also held the patents, the government grants that gave him alone the right to make and sell the telegraph for a set number of years. His partner, Alfred Vail, who built the instruments, received the message in Baltimore. In 1911 one of Vail's sons had his father's gravestone changed to read "INVENTOR OF THE TELEGRAPHIC DOT AND DASH ALPHABET."

This chapter follows American tools and machines from fish traps under a Boston street to the AI programs of the 2020s. As you read, notice whose name goes on each invention. A patent could name one man while a partner, a team of workers or an enslaved man did part of the work. In 1858 the government's chief lawyer ruled that an enslaved person's invention could not be patented.

Notice also who did the hard work: Scottish prisoners at an ironworks, enslaved people at furnaces and in cotton fields, children at spinning machines. The last era covers smartphones and AI programs, and what happened when people trusted a machine's answer.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why did planters enslave far more people after the cotton gin?
- Why could an enslaved inventor not get a patent?
- What went wrong when people trusted a computer's answer?

**Five minutes?** Read "Who could own an invention?" in 1850 to 1900.
**Can skip:** "The Cambridge printing press", "Tractors replace horses", "What is inside a smoke detector?", "Batteries, printers and gene tools". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

People in North America made their tools from wood, stone, bone, clay and animal skin, and near Lake Superior from copper. With no metal machines, they built traps that caught fish with the tide, fields that grew three crops together and boats of birch bark. The names of the people who first worked out these methods are not recorded. Some of the methods are still in use, and an Ojibwe builder in Wisconsin still makes birchbark canoes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spear-throwers, fish traps and fields" -->
Before the bow and arrow, hunters in western North America threw spears with an atlatl, a spear-thrower stick that holds the back end of the spear during the throw.

Hunters across the West began using the bow and arrow about 1,400 years ago, around A.D. 600 to 700. A study published in 2026 in the *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* gives that date. South of an east-west line called the 55th parallel, archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study what people left behind, stop finding atlatls soon after the bow appears. North of that line, hunters used both weapons side by side for more than 1,000 years.

**Fish weirs.** People built fixed traps called fish weirs, fences of wooden stakes set across the water with bundles of brush woven between the stakes. Fish swam in over the mud flats with the rising tide, and the fence trapped them when the water went out. People then scooped them up with baskets and nets, or caught them by hand.

Lines of wooden stakes from fish weirs lie under Boylston Street in Boston. People built these weirs in channels that filled and emptied with the tide, between about 4,700 and 3,700 years ago. They used them for more than a thousand years, until mud filled the bay around 1550 B.C., about 3,500 years ago.

Native farmers planted corn, beans and squash together in the same field, three crops known as the Three Sisters. They also set controlled fires, started on purpose and kept in bounds, to shape the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crafts, and a calendar made of posts" -->
People made pottery from clay and wove baskets. They tanned animal hides, treating the skins so they stayed soft and did not rot, and they made canoes from the bark of birch trees.

At Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, people built the largest city north of Mexico before 1500. Between about A.D. 900 and 1100, builders there raised five circles of upright wooden posts, one after another.

Each circle had between 24 and 72 posts and measured about 240 to 475 feet (73 to 145 meters) across. The biggest was wider than a football field is long. The posts marked the places on the horizon where the sun rose at different times of the year, so the circles worked as a calendar.

Near Lake Superior, people worked copper for thousands of years. They worked it cold, shaping it without melting it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wayne-valliere" name="Wayne Valliere" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Wayne Valliere

> **Who:** An Ojibwe builder of birchbark canoes who has taught more than 100 students and teaches the Ojibwe language.
> **When and where:** Lac du Flambeau, Wisconsin, in the present day.

**Spruce roots are the thread that sews his canoes together.**

Wayne Valliere builds birchbark canoes, the same kind of boat people built in North America before 1500. He is a member of the Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Ojibwe, in Wisconsin. A profile of him by the National Endowment for the Arts, a US government agency that gives money and honors to artists, describes the Ojibwe way of building canoes as handed down over many generations.

Valliere began learning at 16 from elders, among them Joe Chosa, Marvin DeFoe and Ojaanimigiizhig. He also studied old objects kept in museums, along with writings and photographs.

Valliere builds each canoe from birchbark, cedar, spruce roots and pitch. The hull, the body of the boat, is thick birchbark. Ribs of straight-grained cedar hold the hull in shape, and spruce roots sew the pieces of bark together. To seal the seams against water, he mixes pitch, a sticky tree sap, with oak ash and deer tallow, the fat of a deer. Each material is gathered in a different season of the year.

More than 100 students have learned from him. At least one of them now builds canoes without his help, and a second is learning as an apprentice, working beside him. Valliere also teaches the Ojibwe language at Lac du Flambeau Public School.

Valliere's National Heritage Fellowship, which he received from the National Endowment for the Arts in 2020, is the highest honor in the United States for folk and traditional arts, the crafts and music that families and communities hand down.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 1 minute**

European metal reached Native towns on the East Coast before most Europeans did. Native smiths, people who shape metal into tools, cut up European copper and brass, a yellow metal made by melting copper and zinc together, and made tools of their own design from it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Copper kettles cut into arrow points" -->
Copper and brass from Europe reached the East Coast through trade with Europeans along the shore. Both metals had been made in European furnaces from rock dug out of the ground. Native traders passed the metal from one group to the next, so it spread along the East Coast ahead of most Europeans. In 1524 Verrazzano, a European sailing along the Northeast coast, wrote that the Native people there had sheets of worked copper.

Some of the metal came as kettles, large cooking pots. Native smiths cut the kettles apart and hammered the pieces into arrow points, knives, pipe stems, bells and ornaments. They took the new metal and made it into the tools and goods they already used.

Spanish expeditions, groups of men sent out to explore, crossed parts of what is now the United States in the 1500s, through lands where Native nations lived. The men on these expeditions carried iron weapons and tools. They left behind no lasting trade in tools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 9 minutes**

English colonists used running water to drive machines. They dammed streams to turn waterwheels, and the wheels drove grist mills, which ground grain into flour, and sawmills, which cut logs into boards. At ironworks, places where workers made iron from rock dug out of the ground, waterwheels worked the bellows and the hammers.

The first ironworks begun in the English colonies stood on Powhatan land in Virginia. The first one that ran for years stood on Pawtucket land in Massachusetts. Its workers were skilled English ironworkers, nearby farmers, at least two Native woodcutters, and Scottish prisoners of war whom the company's agents forced to work there for seven years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Falling Creek, 1619 to 1622" -->
Powhatan warriors destroyed the ironworks at Falling Creek, Virginia, in 1622, three years after Virginia Company officers sent the first ironworkers there. It was the first ironworks begun in the English colonies. Colonists built it at the falls of the James River, where the river drops over rocks, near present-day Richmond. In 1619 officers of the Virginia Company, the English company that ran the colony, hired Captain Benjamin Bluett to run the ironworks and sent workers with him.

Bluett died on the voyage or soon after he arrived. Others of his men died too, of what colonists called seasoning, the sickness that killed between 50 and 90 percent of early newcomers, according to Chesterfield County's history of the site. The men who lived could not finish the furnace, the tall oven where iron is melted out of rock.

Company officers hired John Berkeley to take over in 1621. He sailed with his son Maurice and 25 other men, according to the county, and he promised iron by June 9, 1622.

**Why the Powhatan attacked.** Like the colonists' tobacco farms, the ironworks stood on Powhatan land. Opechancanough, a Powhatan leader, planned an attack across the colony to force the English to leave Powhatan territory, as the county's history states it. From 1618 to 1620, English tobacco planters, owners of large farms called plantations, had taken Powhatan land without paying for it. Virginia Company officials had pressed to take Powhatan children for English schooling.

**The attack.** Powhatan warriors attacked the ironworks on March 22, 1622. They killed 27 English people there, John Berkeley among them. Two children at Falling Creek survived. The warriors left the works in ruins. Across the colony that day, Powhatan warriors killed about 350 English colonists.

Maurice Berkeley was away from the works and lived. He tried to start the ironworks again and failed. Another Englishman tried from 1634 to 1636 and failed too.

Whether Falling Creek ever made iron is disputed. Virginia's official list of historic places states that workers produced iron there in 1620, and it calls Falling Creek the first successful ironworks in English North America. The county's history states that Bluett's men could not finish the furnace, and that in 1621 Berkeley was still promising iron for the next year. Either way, the first ironworks in the English colonies that ran for years was the one at Saugus, in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Seven waterwheels at Saugus" -->
The first ironworks in the English colonies that ran for years stood on the Saugus River in Massachusetts. By 1643 John Winthrop Jr. had gathered about two dozen investors for it, people who put in money hoping for a profit. They formed the Company of Undertakers of the Iron Works in New England, using undertakers in an old sense, people who take on a big job. The works ran from 1646 to about 1670.

Seven waterwheels drove three kinds of machine. The first was a blast furnace. Air blown into its fire made the fire hot enough to melt iron out of rock, about 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit. The air came from bellows, giant bags that squeeze air into a fire, and these bellows were 18 feet long.

Next came a forge, where 10 to 12 men hammered the iron. The last machine was a rolling and slitting mill, which pressed iron into flat sheets and cut it into thin rods.

The ironworks burned more than 3,600 cords of wood a year, each cord a stack 8 feet long, 4 feet wide and 4 feet high. Set end to end, one year's cords would have made a stack more than 5 miles long. Workers cut the wood with axes and saws made at the works.

The Saugus works closed because of debt, high costs and competition from iron brought in from overseas. The men who had lent money sued. Some of the ironworkers, such as James Leonard, went on to run forges in other places. The works have been rebuilt on the old site, which is now the Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who did the work at Saugus?" -->
About 100 people worked at the Saugus ironworks. The company's managers brought skilled ironworkers over from the iron-making parts of England, and colonists from nearby farms did the unskilled work. Company records show that at least two Native people cut wood for the same wages as the other workers. The records do not give their names.

The workers lived in a village the company owned, called Hammersmith after the English town that many of the ironworkers came from. The works stood on the land of the Pawtucket, the Native people who lived there when the English arrived. Saugus had been a Native settlement, and the English first called it Lynn.

**The Scots.** Some of the workers were prisoners of war. In September 1650, Oliver Cromwell's English soldiers defeated a Scottish army at Dunbar, in Scotland, and captured Scottish soldiers. Members of England's Council of State, the leaders who ran England's government at the time, ordered 150 of the prisoners handed over to agents of two of the ironworks company's investors.

The agents shipped the prisoners across the ocean on the ship *Unity*, which left Gravesend, England, on November 11, 1650, and reached Boston in December. How many died on the voyage is not known. The company kept 62 of the prisoners for its ironworks, and about 36 of them went to work at Saugus, though a National Park Service history gives about 35. The agents sent others to mills in Maine and New Hampshire and sold the rest to farmers and merchants in New England.

The company held the Scots at Saugus to seven years of forced labor under indentures, written contracts that required a person to work for a master for a set number of years. The Scots cut wood, worked as colliers, who make charcoal, and did other labor. They received clothing, food and tobacco.

Some of the Scots are known by name. A list of 35 Scots at the works in 1653 includes James Adams, John Clark, Alexander Innes, Peter Grant, John Stewart and Thomas Tower. A November 1653 list of the property of the works includes Alexander Innes, with a value of 10 pounds. The Scottish Prisoners of War Society prints these lists from the company's record books.

**The danger.** Flying sparks and pieces of hot metal were a constant danger to the men at the forge. The forge hammer weighed 500 pounds, and its noise cost many workers their hearing. The surviving records of the works do not give a count of deaths.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cambridge printing press" -->
The first printing press in the English colonies, a machine that presses paper hard onto metal letters covered in ink, arrived in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1638. The Reverend Jose Glover, a minister, bought the press and hired a locksmith named Stephen Daye to run it. Glover died on the voyage across the ocean. His widow, Elizabeth Glover, owned the press, and Daye ran it.

Daye printed the *Bay Psalm Book* on the press in 1640. It was a book of psalms, the religious songs of the Bible, and the first book printed in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-jenks" name="Joseph Jenks" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Jenks

> **Who:** A London sword maker who ran the blacksmith shop at the Saugus ironworks and asked the colony to protect his machines from copiers.
> **When and where:** Saugus, then part of Lynn, Massachusetts. At the works from 1646. Died in 1683.

**The colony gave Jenks 14 years of protection from copiers.**

Joseph Jenks wanted to stop other men from copying his machines. In 1646 Massachusetts leaders gave him a patent for his water-driven mills. A National Park Service page about his shop states that US Patent Office officials credit him with the first patent for a machine in what is now the United States.

Jenks learned his trade in England. In the 1630s he worked for a sword maker whose sword mill at Hounslow ran on water power. A sword marked with Jenks's name as its maker survives in a museum in Wales.

**The petition.** In May 1646 Jenks asked the Massachusetts General Court, the leaders who made the colony's laws, for the only right to build water-driven mills for 14 years. The mills would make scythes, long curved blades for cutting grass. He also asked to build "a new Invented Saw Mill" and other machines for making tools with edges. His written request survives in the Massachusetts Archives, dated May 10, 1646.

In it he explained why he wanted protection. Without it, another man would see his machine and copy it, and Jenks would lose the profit. In his own spelling: "Another when he seeth it makes the like," and "soe I loose the benefit." The court's magistrates, the leaders who sat as its judges, granted his request.

**The shop.** The agent in charge of the ironworks let Jenks build a water-driven hammer at the tail of the blast furnace on January 20, 1647. It ran on the water that had already turned the furnace wheel. The company owned the shop, and Jenks paid rent.

There he made saw blades, felling saws for cutting down trees, and steelyards, a kind of weighing scale. He drew iron into fine wire, pulling it through small holes to make it thin. In 1652 he sent two saw blades for a water-powered sawmill that John Winthrop Jr. ran in Connecticut.

When the ironworks company went bankrupt, unable to pay its debts, Jenks bought a share of his shop in September 1656 and the rolling and slitting mill that October, with borrowed money. By October 1667 he had little money left, and he asked the court for 50 pounds to start making iron wire. He died in March 1683.

**What the ground showed.** An archaeologist dug up the site of Jenks's shop in 1952. He found waterwheels, the supports of a water-driven hammer, an anvil base and a stone forge.

**Not known:** whether Jenks made the dies, the stamps, for the first silver coins made in New England in 1652, as a marker put up in 1898 states, because no document from the time shows that he did.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 6 minutes**

Iron furnaces and forges went up across Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia, and in Maryland many of the ironworkers were enslaved people or convicts. At the Baltimore Iron Works, which opened in 1731, 42 of the first 89 workers, almost half, were enslaved. Waterwheels still drove the machines, and the furnaces still burned charcoal. In Philadelphia, the printer Benjamin Franklin designed a new kind of stove in 1741 or 1742 and refused to patent it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the Franklin stove saved heat" -->
Benjamin Franklin designed a stove that sent more of a fire's heat into the room and less of it up the chimney. He made it in 1741 or 1742. The Franklin Institute, a science museum in Philadelphia, states 1742 in its history of his inventions.

Franklin's stove was a fireplace lined with metal that stood a few inches out from the chimney. At the back was a hollow iron wall, called a baffle. Air from the room flowed into the baffle, picked up the fire's heat and came back out into the room.

Behind the baffle was a U-shaped passage called an inverted siphon. The smoke had to go up over the baffle, down behind it and only then up the chimney. On that longer path, the iron took in more of the smoke's heat. The stove made less smoke than an ordinary fireplace, too.

Franklin refused to patent the stove. He gave his reason later in his autobiography, the story of his life that he wrote himself. "As we enjoy great Advantages from the Inventions of others," he wrote, "we should be glad of an Opportunity to serve others by any Invention of ours." He added, "and this we should do freely and generously." Everyone gains from other people's inventions, he meant, so people should share their own for free. He never patented any of his inventions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved people and convicts at the Maryland furnaces" -->
Many of the workers at the Maryland ironworks were enslaved people or convicts. In 1719, investors in Britain set up the Principio Company in Cecil County, Maryland. In 1731 Dr. Charles Carroll and four other Maryland planters, two of them also named Carroll, founded the Baltimore Iron Works on the Patapsco River. Dr. Carroll put in 2,200 acres of land and served as the first manager. The five partners supplied the money and the enslaved workers.

**Whose land.** Susquehannock leaders had signed away the Patapsco valley in 1652, though the Piscataway lived in the region too. Five Susquehannock war chiefs signed that treaty, a written agreement between nations, with Maryland colonial officials. In it they gave up their claim to a long stretch of the Chesapeake Bay shore that includes the valley. Emma Bilski, a history graduate student at Johns Hopkins University, points out that the land was not really the Susquehannock's to give away.

**Convicts.** The Baltimore Iron Works opened with 89 workers, and 42 of them were enslaved. Over the years, many other workers there were convicts, people found guilty of crimes in Britain. Under a British law of 1718, English judges sentenced many such people to be shipped to the colonies. Merchants who held contracts with the British government carried them across the ocean and sold their labor to colonists, most often for seven years. Iron makers were among the buyers.

The managers of the Baltimore Iron Works preferred enslaved workers to convicts. Many of the enslaved ironworkers were Igbo people from West Africa, who brought their own skill at working metal. By 1785 an advertisement for the works listed a workforce of "more than 200 Negroes," using a word of that time for Black people.

**The work.** The workers hauled charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore to the top of the furnace. They worked 12-hour shifts, day and night, six or seven days a week.

The owners lived away from the works and often quarreled, and they refused to send the food and workers they had promised. In 1775 the manager, Clement Brooke, wrote to the partners: "I cannot do my job unless you send more slaves and more corn." The Mount Clare Museum's history of the works states that the workers and the animals were starving.

Each of the five owners took one fifth of the iron. In good years the partners earned 55 percent on their money each year. The works closed in 1799. In 1810 the 30 heirs of the original owners, the people who had inherited their shares, had the land that was left sold at auction. It came to more than 30,000 acres. The heirs had 200 enslaved people sold at the same auction.

Some workers ran away. In August 1754 the *Maryland Gazette* newspaper printed a notice for the capture of people who had escaped. Others worked slowly.

An enslaved blacksmith named Anthony and an indentured servant named Thomas Livy were jailed for robbing a store in the spring of 1771. They were caught with the stolen goods, most likely food. The Mount Clare Museum's account of the case does not say who caught them or who jailed them. The overseer, the man in charge of the workers, was William Hammond. He wrote about Anthony: "We shall feel the loss of Anthony's time very much, I cannot tell how we can carry on the Forge business without him."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-technology" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Philadelphia printer who designed the Franklin stove and, later, the lightning rod, and refused to patent either one.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston in 1706. Worked in Philadelphia. Died there in 1790.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a two-part documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about Franklin's life.

**A London ironmonger patented a copy of Franklin's stove.**

Benjamin Franklin made his living as a printer in Philadelphia. He printed a newspaper and a yearly almanac, a book of calendars, weather guesses and sayings.

Franklin gave the model of his stove to an old friend, Robert Grace, who owned an iron furnace in Pennsylvania. Grace cast the iron plates for the stoves there, and he made money on them, because more and more people wanted one. To help sales, Franklin wrote a pamphlet in 1744 that explained how the stove worked.

Governor Thomas of Pennsylvania liked the stove so much that he offered Franklin a patent, which would have let only Franklin sell the stoves for a set number of years. Franklin turned the offer down.

In London an ironmonger, a seller of iron goods, copied much of Franklin's pamphlet. He made small changes to the stove that "rather hurt its operation," took out a patent on it in England and, Franklin wrote, "made, as I was told, a little fortune by it." Other people patented Franklin's inventions too. Franklin never challenged them, he wrote, "having no desire of profiting by patents myself, and hating disputes."

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 10 minutes**

Machines began to do work that had taken many hands, and after one of them, the cotton gin, planters enslaved far more people. Eli Whitney built the gin in 1793. One small gin could clean about 50 pounds of cotton a day, work that took several hundred hours by hand.

Planters grew far more cotton, counted in bales, large bundles pressed tight and tied: fewer than 9,000 bales a year around 1790, more than 200,000 by 1800. To grow it, they held far more people in slavery, about 700,000 in 1790 and nearly 4 million by 1860.

Other machines came into use in the same years. Benjamin Franklin's lightning rods went up on Philadelphia roofs in the 1750s, and by 1755 the first steam engine in America was pumping water from a New Jersey mine. Samuel Slater's cotton-spinning mill began work at Pawtucket, Rhode Island, in 1790, the start of the factory cloth industry in the United States. Slater hired children to run its machines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Franklin's lightning rod, step by step" -->
Franklin described a way to protect buildings from what he called "electrical fire" in a letter of 1750 to Peter Collinson. After lightning tests in France in 1752, and then the kite test that Franklin described, the first lightning rods went up on buildings in Philadelphia.

Franklin printed instructions for making one in *Poor Richard's Almanack* for 1753. Readers were to take a small iron rod and push one end three or four feet into moist ground, with the other end standing six or eight feet above the top of the building. A foot of brass wire, sharpened to a fine point, went on the top end. Small staples held the rod to the house.

When lightning struck, it would hit the point and pass through the metal into the ground, "without hurting any Thing," Franklin wrote. Pointed rods soon stood on buildings throughout the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
The first steam engine in America pumped water out of a mine. It was ordered from England in 1748 for Colonel John Schuyler's copper mine, in what is now North Arlington, New Jersey. The parts arrived in 1753. An engine builder named Josiah Hornblower put it together, and it was pumping by 1755.

It was a Newcomen engine, named for Thomas Newcomen, who built the first one in England in 1712. A boiler filled a large tube, called a cylinder, with steam. Then a spray of cold water turned the steam back into water, which left the cylinder nearly empty. The weight of the air outside pushed a piston, a tight-fitting plug, down into the cylinder. The piston pulled one end of a big rocking beam, and the other end of the beam worked the pump in the mine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The cotton gin and slavery" -->
The cotton gin cleaned cotton so fast that planters grew far more of it, and they held far more people in slavery to grow and pick it. Upland cotton has sticky seeds caught in its fiber. Before 1793, enslaved people pulled the seeds out by hand, and one worker needed many hours to clean a single pound. Planters could grow only as much cotton as their workers could clean.

Eli Whitney's cotton gin did the cleaning by machine. It used two cylinders that combed the cotton through small holes. The seeds could not pass through the holes and stayed behind. A history of the gin by the Eli Whitney Museum states that one small gin could clean 50 pounds of cotton a day. By hand, that much cotton took several hundred hours of work.

Planters used the gin to grow far more cotton. Across the United States they grew fewer than 9,000 bales of cotton a year around 1790 and more than 200,000 bales by 1800. By 1860 the cotton crop was more than 400 times as large as in 1790.

**More people enslaved.** With the gin cleaning cotton so fast, planters wanted far more enslaved people to grow and pick it. The 1790 census, the government's count of everyone in the country, recorded 697,897 enslaved people in the United States. By 1860 there were nearly 4 million, more than five times as many, and about 2.5 million of them lived in the cotton-growing regions from eastern Maryland to Texas. Slaveholders spread slavery west across the South.

Enslaved people picked the cotton by hand in the summer sun, about 200 pounds each a day. Solomon Northup was a free Black man from New York. Kidnappers seized him in Washington, D.C., in 1841 and forced him into slavery. Slaveholders held him for twelve years. He picked cotton on Edwin Epps's plantation in Louisiana until he was rescued in 1853.

Northup described the rule in his book *Twelve Years a Slave*, published that year. "An ordinary day's work is two hundred pounds," he wrote. "A slave who is accustomed to picking, is punished, if he or she brings in a less quantity than that." Each night the cotton was weighed at the gin-house, the building where the gin stood. "After weighing, follow the whippings," Northup wrote.

The punishment was the lash, one stroke of a whip on a person's bare body. Northup wrote that twenty-five lashes were "deemed a mere brush," thought of as a small punishment. Fifty was "the ordinary penalty," and one hundred was "called severe." Northup described Epps himself seizing the whip and lashing the people he enslaved.

**Patsey.** Epps once forced Northup to whip an enslaved woman named Patsey, and then took the whip himself. Northup wrote that her back was "covered with long welts," raised and swollen stripes, and that "the lash was wet with blood," meaning the whip itself. Epps then had her washed with salt and water.

Enslaved people ran the gins and the cotton presses too, the machines that squeezed cotton into bales. The machines had many moving parts that could catch and injure a worker. The tiny cotton fibers the gins threw into the air damaged the workers' lungs.

The people who owned the plantations kept the profit. With the gin, Southern plantation owners gained money and political power. Their cotton supplied cloth mills in the Northern states and in Britain. By 1830, cloth and other finished cotton goods were the largest group of factory-made goods the country sold to other countries.

**Cotton land.** Planters wanted new land for cotton. The Eli Whitney Museum's history of the gin states that this demand was "a major factor" in the removal of five Native nations from their homelands in the Southeast: the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole.

President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could trade land west of the Mississippi River for the lands of Native nations inside the states. US officials then forced the five nations west to Indian Territory, land that is now part of Oklahoma. At least 60,000 people were forced to make the journey, and more than 8,000 of them died on the way.

US Army soldiers under General Winfield Scott began forcing the Cherokee out of their homeland in 1838. Scott was sent to another post soon after the removal began. The soldiers first held the Cherokee in crowded, dirty camps, where many died.

About 4,000 Cherokee died on the march west that the soldiers forced them to make in the fall and winter of 1838 and 1839. On the march they faced heavy rain, rivers blocked by ice and too little food. Many died of illness, and some died of disease after drinking dirty standing water. White landowners took the land the five nations had been forced to leave.

### Eli Whitney

> **Who:** The builder of the cotton gin, patented in 1794, and the subject of a famous myth about machine-made gun parts.
> **When and where:** Georgia and New Haven, Connecticut. Born 1765, died 1825.

**Workers at Whitney's gun factory never made parts that fit any gun.**

Eli Whitney graduated from Yale College in 1792 and traveled south to work as a tutor, a private teacher. On the way, Catherine Littlefield Greene invited him to her plantation, Mulberry Grove, north of Savannah, Georgia. She was the widow of General Nathanael Greene, who had died in 1786 and left her dozens of enslaved people. Enslaved workers on the plantation grew mostly rice and some cotton.

There, in 1793, Whitney built the machine that pulled cotton fiber away from its seeds. He applied for a patent in 1794, and the patent was granted on March 14, 1794.

**Catherine Greene's part.** Whitney held the patent. In 1870 the women's rights activist Matilda Joslyn Gage wrote that Catherine Greene invented the cotton gin. The Eli Whitney Museum states that it "has been suggested that Catherine Greene also contributed ideas to the design."

**Not known:** how much of the design was Catherine Greene's.

**The gun parts.** Whitney signed a contract in 1798 to make muskets for the federal government. He promoted the idea of interchangeable parts, gun parts made by machine so that any part would fit any gun of the same model. According to a famous story, he showed government officials such parts in 1801.

The historian Robert S. Woodbury showed in 1960 that Whitney did not start the idea, and that the credit people gave him for it rests on a myth. Gunmakers at French armories, factories that made weapons, had worked on the idea before Whitney. Workers at Whitney's own factory never made parts that truly fit any gun.

The best-documented first American use of interchangeable parts in a factory came at John H. Hall's rifle works at the Harpers Ferry armory. Hall's workers were putting rifles together from interchangeable parts by 1819.

### Samuel Slater

> **Who:** An English mill worker who rebuilt England's spinning machines from memory at Pawtucket and hired children to run them.
> **When and where:** Trained in Derbyshire, England. The mill ran at Pawtucket, Rhode Island, from December 1790.

**He crossed the ocean with England's spinning machines stored in his memory.**

Samuel Slater rebuilt English cotton-spinning machines from memory in the United States. In England, Richard Arkwright had patented a spinning machine in 1769, called the water frame because a waterwheel drove it. Arkwright and his partners built a mill several floors high at Cromford, in Derbyshire, with waterwheels to drive the machines. He moved cotton spinning out of homes and into powered mills with hundreds of workers, and other mill owners copied his system.

Slater trained in a mill in Derbyshire that used Arkwright's machines. Under British law, machines for making cloth could not leave the country, and skilled cloth-mill workers could not move abroad. In 1789 Slater left England without telling officials that he was a trained mill worker.

**Pawtucket.** Moses Brown hired him in December 1789, and Brown's family firm, Almy & Brown, put up the money and the building. With local mechanics, people who build and repair machines, among them the Wilkinson family, Slater rebuilt two kinds of machine from memory. Carding machines combed raw cotton so the fibers lay straight, and spinning machines twisted the fibers into thread, called yarn. The machines at Pawtucket spun their first yarn in December 1790.

**The children.** Slater had worked in a factory for part of his own childhood. At Pawtucket he hired children to run the machines, which were built for children to run. Most of the children at Slater's mill were about 10 years old, and some were as young as six. They were paid by the hour, and they earned much less than adults.

A Boston politician in 1801 described mills that employed "children from four to Ten years old." In the mill villages that grew up later, the child workers were 8 to 14 years old. The names of the first children who worked for Slater are not recorded.
