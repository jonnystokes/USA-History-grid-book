## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 23 of 37 · America and the World · 10 eras · about 90 minutes**

**From 1494 to today, people far away decided who would rule this land and its islands, and the people living there almost never got to choose.**

Native Hawaiians put 21,269 signatures on a petition against letting the United States take their islands. They signed at meetings on all five main islands between September 11 and October 2, 1897. The signers were more than half of the Native Hawaiians counted that year. Four men carried the petition to Washington, and the treaty to take Hawaii failed. In July 1898 members of Congress took the islands anyway, by a method that did not need the votes the Hawaiians had blocked.

This chapter follows America's dealings with other countries, from a line drawn across the Atlantic in 1494 to the five territories of today. For the first four eras there was no United States, and rulers in Europe traded this land among themselves. In 1775 the new country started out weak. Its leaders needed France's help, and for years they could not free American sailors enslaved in Algiers. Then watch American leaders become the ones deciding for others, from the land taken from Mexico to the elected governments they helped overthrow after 1950. In every era, watch who was in the room when a deal was signed, and who was left out.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Who got to choose when this land and its islands changed hands?
- How did a weak new country come to rule islands and overthrow governments?
- Are the people of the territories American citizens?

**Five minutes?** Read the Hawaii sections of 1850 to 1900, from "Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1887 to 1893" through "21,269 names against annexation, 1897".
**Can skip:** "Trade laws made in London", "A committee for secret letters, 1775 to 1789", "Perry's warships in Japan, 1853 and 1854", "Trade and the climate agreement". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 2 minutes**

Native nations on this land made alliances, promises to stand together against their enemies, long before any European arrived. Their leaders met in councils and gave gifts to seal an agreement. No Native diplomat, a person sent to speak and bargain for a nation, is known by name from the years before the Europeans came. There was no United States yet. American leaders declared independence in 1776, 276 years after 1500.

Far out in the Pacific Ocean, Polynesian voyagers, people of the many islands of the central and southern Pacific, sailed to the islands now called Hawaii and settled them. Then, in 1494, the rulers of Spain and Portugal signed a treaty, a written agreement between countries, that split the lands across the Atlantic between their two kingdoms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="When did people first reach Hawaii?" -->
Polynesian voyagers were the first people to live on the islands of Hawaii. Scientists using revised radiocarbon dating, a way of measuring the age of old wood, bone and charcoal, now put their landing at about the years 1000 to 1200.

Older estimates were much earlier. For a long time most archaeologists, scientists who study the past through the things people left behind, put the first landing between about the year 300 and the year 750. Some newer studies put it a little later, between about 1200 and 1290.

Only one early home site in the islands has been dated with confidence, the Bellows Beach site at Waimānalo, on the island of Oʻahu. The argument over the first date is still open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A line drawn at Tordesillas, 1494" -->
The rulers of Spain and Portugal divided the lands across the Atlantic between them in a treaty signed in the Spanish town of Tordesillas on June 7, 1494. King Ferdinand II of Aragon and Queen Isabella I of Castile signed for Spain. King John II signed for Portugal.

The treaty described a line running from the North Pole to the South Pole, 370 leagues, or about 1,185 miles, west of the Cape Verde Islands in the Atlantic Ocean. The king of Portugal could claim new lands east of the line. The king and queen of Spain could claim new lands west of it.

Millions of people lived in the Americas, on the land these three rulers had divided. The rulers had never seen it. None of the people who lived there was at Tordesillas, and the rulers did not ask them or tell them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 1 minute**

Kings in Spain, Portugal, France and England claimed land on this side of the ocean from their capitals in Europe. French Protestants, Christians who had broken away from the Catholic Church, built a fort in Florida, on land the king of Spain claimed. Spanish soldiers settled the matter in 1565 by killing hundreds of French soldiers and sailors on the Florida coast. Most of the dead had already surrendered or been captured.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Caroline and Matanzas, 1565" -->
Fort Caroline, the French fort, stood in the homeland of peoples who spoke the Timucua language. No French or Spanish leader asked them about it.

**The king's contract.** King Philip II of Spain made a contract in 1565 with Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, a Spanish commander. Under it, Menéndez was to settle Florida and drive the French out of their fort. Menéndez arrived in August. In his own words, he came "to plant the Gospel in these parts," which meant spreading his Catholic faith there.

**The killings.** Menéndez's soldiers took Fort Caroline on September 20, 1565. By his own count they cut the throats of 132 men there and killed 10 more the next day, and he sent about 50 women and children by ship to Havana. Timucua people told him where shipwrecked French sailors had come ashore. At that inlet, a narrow arm of the sea south of St. Augustine, his soldiers killed about 245 Frenchmen who had surrendered, among them the French commander, Jean Ribault. Chapter 22, War and the Military, tells the killings in full.

Menéndez wrote to King Philip that punishing the French this way was "serving God Our Lord, and Your Majesty," because they were Protestants. The inlet is still called Matanzas, the Spanish word for slaughters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 3 minutes**

New Netherland, the Dutch colony on Lenape land, changed hands three times in ten years. English forces took it in 1664, Dutch forces took it back in 1673, and in 1674 Dutch diplomats handed it to England again.

The colonies on this coast belonged to England, the Dutch Republic, France and Spain. Officials in London, Amsterdam, Paris and Madrid set each colony's borders, chose its wars and decided who it could trade with. Colonists had no ambassadors, officials sent to live in another country and speak for their own, and they could not sign treaties in their own names.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade laws made in London" -->
Members of Parliament, England's lawmakers in London, decided what English colonists could buy and sell. They did it through a series of laws called the Navigation Acts.

They aimed the first act, in 1651, mainly at Dutch merchants. It required all trade between England and its colonies to travel in English or colonial ships, and it was one of the causes of a war at sea that English and Dutch sailors began fighting in 1652. The act of 1660 listed goods that colonists could ship only to England or to another English colony: sugar, tobacco, cotton, wool, ginger and indigo, a plant used to make blue dye.

Under the Staple Act of 1663, European goods bound for the colonies had to land in England first. Customs officers there, who collected the taxes on goods coming in, checked them, and then merchants shipped the goods on. Under these laws, merchants in Boston could not legally buy rope from Dutch traders or sell tobacco to French ones.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dutch diplomats gave up New Netherland for a nutmeg island" -->
The Dutch colony of New Netherland stood on the homeland of the Lenape people, which they call Lenapehoking. It covered what is now New York City, parts of Long Island and the Hudson Valley, New Jersey, northeastern Delaware and eastern Pennsylvania.

**Mannahatta.** Dutch colonists said they had bought the island of Mannahatta in 1626 for trade goods worth 60 guilders, the Dutch money of the time. The Dutch most likely misread what the Lenape meant by the exchange. Diseases that the colonists brought cut the number of Lenape people by 85 percent by 1700.

An English fleet arrived at New Amsterdam, the colony's main town, in 1664, and the Dutch surrendered the colony without a fight. Accounts give different days for the surrender because the English and the Dutch used different calendars at the time.

**The trade.** English and Dutch diplomats ended their war with the Treaty of Breda, signed on July 21, 1667. No Lenape leader took part. The Dutch had done better in the fighting, but the Dutch men who bargained at Breda let England keep New Netherland. In return the Dutch took Suriname and Run, an island in what is now Indonesia where nutmeg grew. Suriname had sugar plantations, large farms where the planters forced enslaved people to do the work.

Dutch forces sailed in during a third war, in August 1673, took the colony back and renamed its main town New Orange. Under the Treaty of Westminster, signed on February 19, 1674, Dutch diplomats handed the colony back to England, and it stayed English. By then the English called it New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 4 minutes**

New England soldiers captured a French fortress in 1745, and three years later British diplomats gave it back without asking them. Colonists lived under the rule of the king of Britain, France or Spain. When their king went to war, they were at war too, and they had no say in the peace terms.

Native nations made treaties with English and French officials in their own names. In the summer of 1701 the Haudenosaunee, a league of five Native nations in what is now New York, made two agreements about two weeks apart, one with the English and one with the French.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two agreements in the summer of 1701" -->
**At Albany.** Haudenosaunee representatives met the acting English governor of New York at Albany on July 19, 1701. The English called them the Five Nations. They made a deed, a signed paper that hands over land or rights to land, to King William of England. It was about hunting lands that ran west from what is now western New York toward what is now Chicago.

People still argue over what the Haudenosaunee kept and what they gave up in that deed. Its English text states that they handed the land to the king, and that he would protect their "free hunting" there forever. Haudenosaunee groups read it as protecting their right to hunt there.

**The raccoon case.** In Canada, government lawyers argued against that reading in a court case. In 1987 Ontario officials charged Jesse Ireland and David Jamieson, two men of the Oneida, one of the five Haudenosaunee nations, with hunting raccoons without a license in southern Ontario, inside the area the deed describes.

The government lawyers said the hunting promise counted only if the English and the Haudenosaunee won the land back from the French and their Native allies, and that never happened. In 1990 an Ontario judge rejected that argument and ruled that the two men had a right under the deed to hunt there.

**At Montréal.** About two weeks after the Albany meeting, on August 4, 1701, the governor of New France, the French colony in North America, signed a peace at Montréal with about 1,300 representatives of 39 Native nations. It is called the Great Peace of Montréal, and with it the French and the Native nations ended decades of war.

French officials meant the peace to keep the Five Nations neutral, taking neither side, if the French and the English colonists went to war. The Haudenosaunee kept out of the next war between France and Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kondiaronk-america-world" name="Kondiaronk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Kondiaronk

> **Who:** A Huron-Wendat chief who argued for the Great Peace of Montréal while he was dying of a fever. The French called him "the Rat."
> **When and where:** Montréal, August 1701. Born around 1649.

**He spoke for peace on August 1 and died within hours.**

Kondiaronk was a chief of the Huron-Wendat people. The French nicknamed him "the Rat" for the tricks he used when he bargained.

A disease was spreading through Montréal while the great council met, and Kondiaronk caught it. On August 1, 1701, weak with fever, he spoke to the council and pushed the leaders toward agreement. Afterward he was carried to the Hôtel-Dieu, a hospital in the town, and he died a few hours later. Accounts give his death as August 1 or August 2.

The governor of Montréal, French officers, war chiefs and priests walked in the procession at his large funeral on August 3, and he was buried at the town's Notre-Dame church. The Great Peace of Montréal was signed the next day, August 4, 1701.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kondiaronk-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What British diplomats won at Utrecht, 1713" -->
In the Treaty of Utrecht, British diplomats won a contract to ship kidnapped African people across the ocean and sell them as slaves. The treaty was signed on April 11, 1713, at Utrecht in the Netherlands. With it the diplomats ended a war in Europe that the colonists called Queen Anne's War.

Under the contract, called the asiento, British traders had the only right for thirty years to carry enslaved Black people into Spain's colonies in the Americas and sell them there. The diplomats wrote the asiento into the peace as one of Britain's prizes, beside land.

The land came from France. French diplomats signed over three things to Britain: all the land whose rivers drain into Hudson Bay, France's claims to Newfoundland, and Acadia, a French colony on the Atlantic coast of what is now Canada. A New England force had taken Acadia's capital, Port-Royal, in 1710.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Louisbourg, taken in 1745 and given back in 1748" -->
New England soldiers under Colonel William Pepperrell sailed from Boston on March 24, 1745, to take Louisbourg, a French fortress on Cape Breton Island. With British warships offshore, they laid siege to it, surrounding it and cutting it off, and the French surrendered on June 17, 1745, after 49 days.

**The cost.** Far more of the New Englanders died holding the fortress than taking it. In June 1746 Pepperrell estimated that about 1,200 of his men had died, most of them of disease in the fortress that winter. Chapter 22, War and the Military, tells the siege and that winter in full.

**Given back.** British diplomats gave Louisbourg back to France in 1748, in the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. In exchange French diplomats gave back Madras, a city in India that French forces had taken from Britain. British officials did not ask the colonists, and New Englanders were furious.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 16 minutes**

In the middle of their war for independence, American leaders looked in secret for anyone abroad who would help them fight Britain. They started in November 1775. French and American diplomats signed an alliance in 1778, and in 1783 American diplomats in Paris signed the peace that ended the war.

**Weak after the war.** The new country had almost no navy and little money. Rulers in North Africa seized American ships, and their officials enslaved the crews. When the French and the British went to war with each other in the 1790s, American leaders tried to keep out of it. President Washington accepted a treaty that favored Britain because he judged the country too weak to fight the British. From 1798 to 1800 American sailors fought an undeclared war at sea against France.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A committee for secret letters, 1775 to 1789" -->
Members of the Second Continental Congress, the meeting of delegates each colony chose to speak for it, set up the Committee of Secret Correspondence on November 29, 1775. The committee's job was to make secret contact with people in other countries who might help. Its first five members included Benjamin Franklin and John Jay.

The delegates renamed it the Committee for Foreign Affairs on April 17, 1777. Later they set up a Department of Foreign Affairs. Accounts disagree on whether that happened on January 10, 1780, or January 10, 1781. On August 10, 1781, they chose Robert R. Livingston of New York as the first Secretary for Foreign Affairs.

Under the new Constitution, members of Congress set up the department again in a law of July 27, 1789. In a second law, on September 15, 1789, they renamed it the Department of State, because they had given it record-keeping work at home on top of foreign affairs. It still has that name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Which country recognized the United States first?" -->
Sultan Mohammed III, the ruler of Morocco, was the first ruler of any country to recognize the United States, or accept officially that it existed, in Encyclopaedia Britannica's account. On December 20, 1777, he declared that ships flying the American flag could enter Moroccan ports freely. That was about six weeks before French officials signed their treaties with the Americans.

His declaration named ships of several other nations too. It was part of his plan to trade in peace with Christian countries, and he did not aim it at the Americans alone.

A treaty with Morocco followed nine years later. The American agent Thomas Barclay signed it at Marrakesh on June 28, 1786. Thomas Jefferson and John Adams added their signatures in Paris and London, and delegates to Congress ratified it, giving it final approval, in July 1787.

It was the first treaty American officials made with an African country or a Muslim one. Officials of the two countries rewrote parts of it in 1836 (some accounts say 1837), and it is still in force. No other treaty relationship in United States history has lasted as long without a break.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What it took to win over France, 1778" -->
France's foreign minister, the Comte de Vergennes, a French nobleman, agreed to an alliance only after the Americans won a major battle. Benjamin Franklin sailed to France in late 1776. His ship reached Auray, in Brittany, on December 3 or 4, 1776, and he was in Paris by December 21. He, Silas Deane and Arthur Lee were the American commissioners, the men sent to bargain for the United States.

**Saratoga.** A British army surrendered to American forces after the Battle of Saratoga, in New York. The State Department's own historians write that Vergennes "finally decided in favor of an alliance" when that news reached him in December 1777.

**Two treaties.** American and French diplomats signed two treaties on February 6, 1778. In the Treaty of Amity and Commerce, a treaty of friendship and trade, the French formally recognized American independence. In the Treaty of Alliance, the two sides promised that neither would make peace with Britain without the other. A secret section stated that other countries in Europe could join.

Spain's leaders took their country into the war against Britain on June 21, 1779, as France's ally. They did not sign a treaty with the United States, and they did not recognize American independence during the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Franklin (2024)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** The American who spoke for his country in France for more than eight years, and one of the three Americans who made the peace with Britain.
> **When and where:** Paris, December 1776 to May 1785.
> **Movie:** *Franklin* (2024), an eight-part drama series on Apple TV+, with Michael Douglas as Franklin. It is about his time in France and is based on Stacy Schiff's book *A Great Improvisation*. It is a drama, not a documentary.

**In Paris, Franklin wore a fur cap instead of a wig.**

His biographer Walter Isaacson identifies the fur as marten. Franklin wore plain clothes too, and the French took the cap as a sign that he was a plain, rugged American. Augustin de Saint-Aubin made an engraving of Franklin in the cap in 1777, and the print is in the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

Franklin represented the United States in France from December 1776 to May 1785, first as one of the commissioners and then as minister. Delegates to Congress named him Minister Plenipotentiary to France in 1778, a title meaning he had full power to act for his country there. He presented his credentials, the letters that proved his position, to the French government the next spring.

The delegates also named him minister to Sweden, in 1782. Franklin never went to Sweden, but he worked out a treaty with it from Paris and signed it in April 1783. In the same years he was one of the three Americans who made the peace with Britain. His mission in France ended in May 1785.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What British diplomats signed away in 1783" -->
In the Treaty of Paris, British diplomats accepted that the United States was independent. They also handed the United States Britain's claim to land that Native nations lived on.

Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and John Jay bargained for the United States, and they signed the Treaty of Paris on September 3, 1783.

Under the treaty, the British accepted a western border for the United States at the Mississippi River. Americans could fish off Newfoundland, and British lenders could still collect the debts Americans had owed them since before the war. The American side promised Loyalists, colonists who had sided with Britain, payment for property that Americans had taken from them. British troops had to leave the thirteen states. Franklin asked the British to hand over Canada as well, and they refused.

The treaty took effect in 1784, after delegates to the Confederation Congress, meeting at Annapolis, Maryland, and British officials had both approved it.

**Not invited.** No Native nation was invited to Paris, though the land inside that western border was their home. Among them were the Haudenosaunee in what is now New York and Pennsylvania. The Tuscarora had joined their league in about 1722, and after that it was called the Six Nations: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and Tuscarora.

The British king had set aside much of that land for Native nations in 1763, in a royal order called the Royal Proclamation. In 1783 British diplomats handed it to the United States, and they ignored many earlier British treaties with Native nations.

At Fort Stanwix in 1784, American officials backed by a strong force of soldiers treated the Six Nations as nations they had beaten in war, and they took huge parts of Six Nations land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-adams-america-world" name="John Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Adams

> **Who:** The first American minister to Great Britain, sent to the king he had rebelled against.
> **When and where:** London. Presented to King George III on June 1, 1785.

**In 1785 Adams met the king who had called the colonies rebels.**

Adams met the king as the representative of the United States on June 1, 1785. Nearly ten years earlier, in August 1775, the same king had declared the colonies to be in open rebellion against him, and the next year Adams signed the Declaration of Independence.

Two days after the meeting, on June 3, 1785, Adams wrote to Thomas Jefferson that the king had received him with more kindness than he had expected. The Library of Congress holds that letter.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Americans enslaved in Algiers, 1785 to 1796" -->
**By 1786:** British diplomats had accepted American independence, and John Adams was the first American minister in London. The alliance with France, signed in 1778, was still in force.

Officials of the ruler of Algiers held American sailors as slaves from 1785 to 1796, and American leaders could not free them. Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli and Morocco, called the Barbary states, were states on the coast of North Africa. Many of their rulers sent armed ships to seize the ships of weaker countries, to make those countries pay them for safe passage, and they held the captured sailors for ransom.

Moroccan ships seized an American merchant ship in 1784, and a treaty with Morocco followed in 1786. In 1785 Muhammad, the Dey, or ruler, of Algiers, declared war on the United States, and his ships captured several American ships.

**A Boston ship.** An Algerian warship took the Boston ship *Maria* about three miles off Cape St. Vincent, in Portugal, on July 25, 1785. The *Maria* reached Algiers on August 4, and the Dey's officials enslaved its crew. More American crews followed.

American leaders had almost no navy and very little money. They could not free the sailors by force, and for years they could not pay what the Dey asked for them. In 1795 they sent Joel Barlow, Joseph Donaldson and Richard O'Brien to North Africa, and the three men made treaties with Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli. The treaty with Algiers was signed on September 5, 1795, and the American ratification is dated March 7, 1796.

**The price.** The treaty freed 83 American sailors, by the count of the State Department's historians. Counts of the captives differ with the year, from 83 freed in 1796 to 115 held in 1793.

In all, the treaty cost the United States about $1 million, by the Treasury's estimate. The money for the first payment reached Algiers late, and the Dey demanded more, which roughly doubled the price. Other accounts give a smaller sum paid at once, plus supplies for the Dey's ships every year.

The Dey's officials released the surviving sailors on July 8, 1796. Six captives had died shortly before. Several others carried lasting injuries from their years of captivity.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-leander-cathcart" name="James Leander Cathcart" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Leander Cathcart

> **Who:** An American sailor enslaved in Algiers for eleven years, who rose to be the Dey's chief Christian clerk and put the 1795 treaty into English.
> **When and where:** Born June 1, 1767, in County Westmeath, Ireland. Captured July 25, 1785, off Cape St. Vincent, Portugal. Held in Algiers until May 1796. Died October 6, 1843.

**Captured at 18, Cathcart spent eleven years as a slave in Algiers.**

He had come to America from Ireland as a boy. During the Revolution the British had already captured him once and held him on prison ships at New York, until he escaped.

**The capture.** Cathcart was one of six crewmen on the Boston ship *Maria* when an Algerian warship with 14 guns took it on July 25, 1785. He later explained why Algerian warships were out in the Atlantic that summer. Spain's war with Algiers had kept them out until the two made peace in 1785.

Then Charles Logie, the British consul in Algiers, spoke to the Dey's government. A consul is an official who looks after his country's trade and citizens in another country. Logie told them that American ships were no longer under the British king's protection, so the Algerians could take them.

The Algerian crew drove the six Americans into a boat and took their hats, handkerchiefs and shoes. On the voyage the captors locked all 42 of their prisoners every night in the sail room in the ship's hold. The drinking water was so foul, Cathcart wrote, that "we were, literally, obliged to strain through our teeth." The *Maria* reached Algiers on August 4. On August 12 the *Dauphin* of Philadelphia arrived with 15 more American captives, which made 21 in all.

Dey Muhammad chose some of the captives to work in his palace and garden. His officials sent others to the slave prisons and to hard labor at the harbor. A man at hard labor got one suit of clothes, worth about a dollar and a half, and two rough blankets meant to last his whole captivity.

**The beating.** About four months after he arrived, officials who ran the Dey's household ordered Cathcart beaten with the bastinado, blows with sticks on the soles of the bare feet. Four men threw him down and tied his arms and legs. In the usual method, Cathcart wrote, the man lay face down with his ankles tied to a pole that two men held up, and a guard sat on his shoulders. Cathcart took 28 blows and lost four toenails.

He wrote that all fourteen slaves in the garden got the same beating, "old men of sixty and children of ten years of age" among them.

**The clerk.** Cathcart rose step by step. He served coffee to the minister of the harbor, then became clerk of the harbor, then clerk of a slave prison, and at last chief Christian clerk to the Dey. Foreign ambassadors who could not get a meeting with the Dey went through him. He helped Joseph Donaldson, who bargained for the Americans, and he translated the Turkish text of the 1795 treaty into English.

By Cathcart's own list, 9 of the 21 Americans taken in 1785 died in Algiers, most of them of plague, a disease that spread quickly and killed many people. The other 12 went home at different times. Family or friends bought some of them free before 1796, and the rest were freed in 1796. Cathcart left Algiers on May 8, 1796, carrying dispatches, official letters for the American government.

In January 1799 he sailed from Philadelphia as the American consul for Tunis and Tripoli. His daughter, J. B. Newkirk, published his account, *The Captives: Eleven Years a Prisoner in Algiers*, in 1899.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-leander-cathcart" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Staying out of Europe's war, 1793 to 1796" -->
President George Washington decided in 1793 to keep the United States out of the new war between France and Britain.

**The French minister.** France's new minister, Edmond-Charles Genêt, landed at Charleston, South Carolina, on April 8, 1793. Two weeks later, on April 22, Washington issued the Proclamation of Neutrality, which stated that the United States would take neither side.

Genêt hired American privateers, privately owned armed ships with a government's permission to attack enemy ships, to go after British ships. When Washington would not do as Genêt wished, Genêt threatened to go over the President's head to the American people. Washington demanded that the French government replace him.

**The treaty with Britain.** John Jay signed a treaty with Britain on November 19, 1794, and senators approved it on June 24, 1795, by 20 votes to 10. Under it, British troops were to leave the forts in the Northwest that British diplomats had promised to give up in 1783. British officials agreed to give American traders terms as good as those of any other country, and they put heavy limits on trade with the British islands in the Caribbean.

The two sides sent several quarrels to arbitration, a way of settling a dispute by letting a neutral panel decide: the border between Maine and Canada, the old debts, and British seizures of American ships. British officials kept the right to seize American cargo bound for France, with payment, and to take French goods off American ships without paying.

The State Department's historians sum it up this way: the treaty "addressed few U.S. interests, and ultimately granted Britain additional rights." It was very unpopular with Americans. Washington signed it anyway, because he judged that the country was too weak to fight Britain.

**The treaty with Spain.** Thomas Pinckney signed a treaty with Spain on October 27, 1795, after bargaining with a Spanish minister, Manuel de Godoy. Senators approved it on March 7, 1796. Under it, the border with Spanish West Florida was set at the 31st parallel, an east-west line on the map, and Americans got free use of the Mississippi River and of the port of New Orleans without paying tax.

That mattered to farmers in Kentucky and Tennessee. They could not haul their crops over the mountains to sell them, so they floated the crops down the rivers to New Orleans, which Spanish officials controlled. Under Pinckney's Treaty they could sell them there.

**The Farewell Address.** Washington's Farewell Address was printed in the *American Daily Advertiser*, a Philadelphia newspaper, on September 19, 1796. Alexander Hamilton had helped him write it. In it Washington said he would not run for a third term, and he argued that American leaders should not make permanent alliances with any foreign country.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The XYZ Affair and a war at sea, 1798 to 1800" -->
American navy crews fought French ships in the Caribbean from 1798 to 1800, in a war that neither government declared. It followed a failed attempt at talks. President John Adams had sent three commissioners to France: Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and John Marshall.

French agents told the three Americans that talks could not begin until the United States met their demands. The agents asked for a low-interest loan to France, and they wanted the United States government itself to pay what American merchants said France owed them. On top of that, they wanted "a substantial bribe" for the French foreign minister, Talleyrand.

**W, X, Y and Z.** Adams made the Americans' reports public, with the four agents' names replaced by the letters W, X, Y and Z. The agents were Nicholas Hubbard, Jean Hottinguer, Pierre Bellamy and Lucien Hauteval.

The fighting at sea that followed is called the Quasi-War, from a word meaning "almost." The State Department's historians call it "a limited, undeclared war." American and French diplomats ended it with an agreement called the Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine, and in the same agreement they ended the alliance of 1778 between France and the United States. Senators did not ratify it until December 18, 1801.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1800" -->
**If you remember one thing:** For its first twenty-five years the United States was too weak to free its sailors by force or to fight Britain, so its leaders made treaties and paid ransom instead.
- In 1775: The colonies were at war with Britain, and their leaders were looking in secret for help abroad.
- In 1800: American diplomats had signed treaties with France, Britain, Spain, Morocco and Algiers, and American navy crews had just fought French ships at sea.
**Next:** In the next fifty years American leaders doubled the size of the country with one purchase and pushed its border to the Pacific.
