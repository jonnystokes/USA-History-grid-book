## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500 there was no United States, so there was no American dealing with other countries. The United States declared its independence in 1776, nearly three hundred years after this era ends. The nations already living on this land dealt with each other through councils, alliances and gifts given to seal an agreement. No Native diplomat from before the Europeans arrived is known by name. A diplomat is a person sent to speak and bargain for a nation.

Two events from this era reach into the later history of the United States. Polynesian voyagers sailed across the Pacific Ocean and settled the islands now called Hawaii. And in 1494 two kingdoms in Europe signed a paper that split the Americas between them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first people of Hawaii" -->
Polynesian voyagers were the first people to live on the islands of Hawaii. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past through the things people left behind, disagree about when they arrived.

For a long time most of them put the first landing between about the year 300 and the year 750. Newer tests use radiocarbon dating, a way of measuring the age of old wood, bone and charcoal. Those tests point to about 1000 to 1200. Some studies narrow it to between about 1200 and 1290. The oldest place in the islands where people lived that scientists can date with confidence is the Bellows Beach site at Waimānalo, on the island of Oʻahu. The argument over the first date is still open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A line drawn at Tordesillas, 1494" -->
On June 7, 1494, Spain and Portugal signed a treaty in the Spanish town of Tordesillas. A treaty is a written agreement between countries. King Ferdinand II of Aragon and Queen Isabella I of Castile signed for Spain. King John II signed for Portugal.

The treaty drew a line from the North Pole to the South Pole, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands in the Atlantic Ocean. A league was a measure of distance, and 370 leagues is about 1,185 miles. Portugal could claim new lands east of the line. Spain could claim new lands west of it.

Millions of people lived in the Americas, on land the two kingdoms had divided. None of them was at Tordesillas, and none was asked or told.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s the kings of Spain, Portugal, France and England claimed land on this side of the ocean from their capitals in Europe. Most of the men who made those claims never saw the land. France and Spain both claimed Florida. In 1565 Spanish soldiers ended that quarrel on the Florida coast by killing hundreds of French soldiers and sailors, most of them after they had surrendered or been captured.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Caroline and Matanzas, 1565" -->
French Protestants had built a fort called Fort Caroline in Florida, on land that Spain claimed. Protestants are Christians who broke away from the Catholic Church. The fort stood in the homeland of peoples who spoke the Timucua language. Neither France nor Spain asked them about it.

Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, a Spanish commander, arrived in August 1565 to destroy the fort. The French commander, Jean Ribault, was away at sea with his ships, and a storm had scattered them. On September 20, 1565, Menéndez marched about 500 men overland and took Fort Caroline. René de Laudonnière, the French officer in charge at the fort, escaped into the woods.

Menéndez reported to King Philip II of Spain in a letter dated October 15, 1565. He wrote that 132 men at the fort "had their throats cut," and that his men killed ten more the next day who were caught in the woods. The National Park Service gives no number for the fort. Other accounts give other counts.

Timucua people told Menéndez that a group of white men was on the beach south of St. Augustine. They were French sailors whose ships the storm had wrecked. In mid-September the men surrendered to Menéndez at an inlet. He wrote, "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife." That means his soldiers stabbed them to death. His men killed 111 of them. He spared sixteen, twelve sailors from Brittany and four "carpenters and caulkers" he needed. A caulker seals the seams of a ship so water cannot get in.

On October 12 more shipwrecked Frenchmen came to the same inlet, and Menéndez's men killed 134 of them. Jean Ribault was among the dead. He had surrendered with his men before they were killed. Menéndez wrote that punishing the French this way was "serving God Our Lord, and Your Majesty," because they were Protestants. The inlet is still called Matanzas, the Spanish word for slaughters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonies on this coast in the 1600s belonged to England, the Dutch Republic, France and Spain. The rulers in Europe set each colony's borders, chose its wars and decided who it could trade with. No colony had an ambassador, an official sent to live in another country and speak for his own. No colony signed a treaty in its own name. In 1664 and again in 1674, the colony of New Netherland changed hands from one European country to another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade laws made in London" -->
Members of Parliament, England's lawmaking body in London, decided what English colonists could buy and sell. They passed a series of laws called the Navigation Acts.

The Navigation Act of 1651 was aimed mainly at Dutch merchants. It required all trade between England and its colonies to travel in English or colonial ships. In 1652 England and the Dutch went to war, and this act was one of the causes. The act of 1660 listed goods that colonists could ship only to England or to another English colony. The list was sugar, tobacco, cotton, wool, indigo and ginger. Indigo is a plant used to make blue dye.

The Staple Act of 1663 covered goods coming the other way. European goods bound for the colonies had to land in England first. Customs officers there checked them, and then the goods were shipped on. Under these laws, a merchant in Boston could not legally buy rope from a Dutch trader or sell his tobacco to a French one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Netherland traded for a nutmeg island" -->
The Dutch colony of New Netherland stood on the homeland of the Lenape people. Lenape land covered what is now New York City, parts of Long Island and the Hudson Valley, New Jersey, northeastern Delaware and eastern Pennsylvania. The Lenape call it Lenapehoking. In 1626 Dutch colonists said they had bought the island of Mannahatta for 60 guilders' worth of trade goods. Historians think the Dutch misread what the Lenape meant by the exchange. Diseases that the colonists brought cut the number of Lenape people by 85 percent by 1700.

In 1664 an English fleet sailed into the harbor of New Amsterdam, the colony's main town. The Dutch surrendered the colony without a fight. Sources give different days for the surrender because England and the Dutch Republic used different calendars at the time.

On July 21, 1667, English and Dutch diplomats ended their war with the Treaty of Breda. No Lenape leader took part. The Dutch had done better in the fighting. Even so, their negotiators at Breda let England keep New Netherland. In return the Dutch took Suriname and Run, a small island in what is now Indonesia where nutmeg grew. Suriname had sugar plantations worked by enslaved people.

Dutch forces took the colony back for a short time. Under the Treaty of Westminster, signed in 1674, the colony went back to England for good. By then the English called it New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A colonist in the early 1700s was a subject of the king of Britain, France or Spain. When his king went to war, the colonist was at war too, and he had no say in the peace terms. Native nations on this continent made treaties in their own names with each of the empires. In the summer of 1701 the Haudenosaunee made one agreement with the English and another with the French, three weeks apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two agreements in the summer of 1701" -->
The Haudenosaunee were a league of five nations in what is now upstate New York. The English called them the Five Nations.

On July 19, 1701, Haudenosaunee representatives met John Nanfan, the acting English governor of New York, at Albany. They made a deed to King William of England covering hunting lands. The lands ran west from what is now western New York toward what is now Chicago. People still argue over what that deed promised. Many Haudenosaunee people today read it as protecting their right to hunt there. Other writers disagree.

On August 4, 1701, at Montréal, the governor of New France signed a peace with about 1,300 representatives of 39 Native nations. The governor was Louis-Hector de Callière. The agreement is called the Great Peace of Montréal. With it the French and the Native nations ended decades of war. Under its terms the Five Nations would stay neutral, which means taking neither side, if France and the English colonies went to war. The Haudenosaunee had made a deal with the English and a peace with the French in one summer, and they kept out of the next war between the two empires.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kondiaronk-america-world" name="Kondiaronk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Kondiaronk

> **Who:** A Huron-Wendat chief and one of the main negotiators of the Great Peace of Montréal. The French called him "the Rat."
> **When and where:** Montréal, August 1701. Born around 1649.

Kondiaronk spoke for peace at the great council in Montréal while he was dying of a fever. He was a chief of the Huron-Wendat people, born around 1649. The French nicknamed him "the Rat" for the tricks he used when he bargained.

A disease was spreading through Montréal while the council met, and Kondiaronk caught it. On August 1, 1701, weak with fever, he made a speech to the council that pushed the leaders toward agreement. Afterward he was carried to the Hôtel-Dieu, a hospital in the town. He died a few hours later. Sources give his death as August 1 or August 2.

On August 3 the people of Montréal held a large funeral for him at the town's Notre-Dame church. The governor of Montréal, French officers, war chiefs and priests walked in the procession, and he was buried at the church. The Great Peace of Montréal was signed the next day, August 4, 1701.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kondiaronk-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Treaty of Utrecht, 1713" -->
In the Treaty of Utrecht, British diplomats won a contract to ship kidnapped African people across the ocean and sell them as slaves. The treaty was signed on April 11, 1713, at Utrecht in the Netherlands. It ended a war in Europe that the colonists called Queen Anne's War.

The contract was called the asiento. Under it, for thirty years, British traders had the only right to carry enslaved Black people into Spain's colonies in the Americas and sell them there. The asiento was written into the peace as one of Britain's prizes, beside land.

The land came from France. French diplomats signed over to Britain the land around Hudson Bay, France's claims to Newfoundland, and Acadia. A New England force had taken Acadia's capital, Port-Royal, in 1710.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Louisbourg, taken in 1745 and given back in 1748" -->
In 1745 New England soldiers captured a French fortress, and three years later British diplomats handed it back to France. On April 4, 1745, almost 3,000 New England troops under Colonel William Pepperrell sailed from Boston. A squadron of Royal Navy ships under Commodore Peter Warren joined them. They laid siege to Louisbourg, a French fortress on Cape Breton Island. A siege is an attack in which an army surrounds a place and cuts it off until it gives up. After 49 days, on June 17, 1745, the French surrendered.

French fire killed about 100 New England men during the siege, and about 30 more died of illness. The troops stayed in the broken, dirty fortress through the winter. Between late November 1745 and mid-February 1746 they buried 561 of their own men, dead of disease. Pepperrell counted about 1,200 men lost in all.

In 1748 British and French diplomats signed the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. The British gave Louisbourg back to France. In exchange the French gave back Madras, a city in India that French forces had taken from Britain. The British government did not consult the colonists, and New Englanders were furious.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The United States began dealing with other countries in the middle of its war for independence from Britain. Its leaders needed other governments to accept that the new country existed, and they needed help to win the war. French and American diplomats signed an alliance in 1778. In 1783 American diplomats in Paris signed the peace that ended the war.

After the war the country had almost no navy and little money. Rulers in North Africa seized American ships, and their officials enslaved the crews. In the 1790s, when France and Britain went to war with each other, American leaders tried to keep the United States out of it. By 1800 the country had come close to war with each of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A committee for secret letters, 1775 to 1789" -->
On November 29, 1775, members of the Second Continental Congress set up the Committee of Secret Correspondence. The Congress was the meeting of delegates from the colonies that ran the war. The committee's job was to make secret contact with people in other countries who might help. Its first members were Benjamin Franklin, Benjamin Harrison, Thomas Johnson, John Dickinson and John Jay.

On April 17, 1777, the delegates renamed it the Committee for Foreign Affairs. The delegates later set up a Department of Foreign Affairs. Sources disagree on whether that happened on January 10, 1780, or January 10, 1781. On August 10, 1781, they chose Robert R. Livingston of New York as the first Secretary for Foreign Affairs.

Under the new Constitution, members of Congress set up the department again in a law of July 27, 1789. In a second law, on September 15, 1789, they renamed it the Department of State. The department had been given record-keeping work at home as well as foreign affairs. It still has that name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Morocco, 1777 and 1786" -->
Encyclopaedia Britannica calls Sultan Mohammed III of Morocco the first head of state to recognize the United States. To recognize a country is to accept officially that it exists. On December 20, 1777, he declared that ships flying the American flag could enter Moroccan ports freely. He did this about six weeks before France signed its treaties with the United States. His declaration named ships of several other nations too. It was part of his plan to trade in peace with Christian countries, and it was not aimed at the Americans alone.

A treaty with Morocco followed nine years later. The American agent Thomas Barclay reached Marrakesh on June 19, 1786. Moroccan officials approved the agreement on June 23, and Barclay signed it on June 28. Thomas Jefferson signed it in Paris on January 1, 1787, and John Adams signed it in London on January 25, 1787. Delegates to Congress ratified it on July 18, 1787. To ratify is to give final, formal approval.

It was the first treaty the United States made with an African country or a Muslim one. Officials of the two countries renegotiated it in 1836, and it is still in force. No other treaty relationship in United States history has lasted as long without a break.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The French alliance, February 6, 1778" -->
France's foreign minister, the Comte de Vergennes, agreed to an alliance only after the Americans won a major battle. Benjamin Franklin sailed to France in late 1776. His ship reached Auray, in Brittany, on December 3 or 4, 1776, and he was in Paris by December 21. He, Silas Deane and Arthur Lee were the American commissioners, the men sent to bargain for the United States.

A British army surrendered to American forces after the Battle of Saratoga, in New York. The State Department's own historians write that Vergennes "finally decided in favor of an alliance" when that news reached him in December 1777.

On February 6, 1778, the Americans and the French signed two treaties. In the Treaty of Amity and Commerce, the French formally recognized American independence. In the Treaty of Alliance, each country promised not to make a separate peace with Britain. A secret part of the treaty left room for other countries in Europe to join.

Spain went to war against Britain on June 21, 1779. It fought as France's ally. Spain did not sign a treaty with the United States, and its government did not recognize American independence during the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Franklin (2024)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** An American commissioner and then minister to France, and one of the three Americans who made the peace with Britain.
> **When and where:** Paris, December 1776 to May 1785.
> **Movie:** *Franklin* (2024), an eight-part drama series on Apple TV+, with Michael Douglas as Franklin. It is about his time in France and is based on Stacy Schiff's book *A Great Improvisation*. It is a drama, not a documentary.

Benjamin Franklin represented the United States in France for eight and a half years, from December 1776 to May 1785. In Paris he wore plain clothes and a fur cap instead of a wig. His biographer Walter Isaacson identifies the fur as marten. The French took the cap as a sign that Franklin was a plain, rugged man from the American forests. Augustin de Saint-Aubin made an engraving of Franklin in the cap in 1777. The print is in the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

Delegates to Congress named Franklin Minister Plenipotentiary to France on September 14, 1778. A minister plenipotentiary is an official with full power to act for his country in another country. He presented his credentials, the letters that proved his position, to the French government on March 23, 1779.

They also named him minister to Sweden, on September 28, 1782. He never went there. He worked out a treaty with Sweden from Paris instead and signed it on April 3, 1783. He was one of the three Americans who made the peace with Britain. His mission in France ended on May 17, 1785.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Treaty of Paris, 1783" -->
In the Treaty of Paris, British diplomats accepted that the United States was independent and signed over land that Native nations lived on. Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and John Jay negotiated for the United States. They signed the first version of the peace terms on November 30, 1782. French diplomats signed their own first version on January 20, 1783. The final Treaty of Paris was signed on September 3, 1783.

The British negotiators accepted a western border for the United States at the Mississippi River. The treaty set out fishing rights off Newfoundland. It stated that debts owed to British lenders from before the war would be paid. It also stated that Loyalists, colonists who had sided with Britain, would be paid for property that had been taken from them. It required British troops to leave the thirteen states. Franklin asked the British to hand over Canada as well, and they refused.

Members of the Confederation Congress, meeting at Annapolis, Maryland, ratified the treaty on January 14, 1784. The British ratified it on April 9, 1784. The two sides traded signed copies in Paris on May 12, 1784.

The land inside that western border was the home of Native nations. Among them were the Haudenosaunee in what is now New York and Pennsylvania. By then the Tuscarora had joined their league, and the league was called the Six Nations. The Six Nations were the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and Tuscarora. No Native nation was invited to Paris. In 1763 a British royal order called the Royal Proclamation had set aside much of that land for Native nations. In 1783 British diplomats handed it to the United States. They also ignored many earlier British treaties with Native nations. At Fort Stanwix in 1784, American negotiators backed by a strong force of soldiers treated the Six Nations as a conquered people. They took huge parts of Six Nations land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-adams-america-world" name="John Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Adams

> **Who:** The first United States minister to Great Britain.
> **When and where:** London. Presented to King George III on June 1, 1785.

On June 1, 1785, John Adams met King George III as the representative of the United States. Nine years earlier, the same king had called Adams a rebel and could have had him hanged. Now Adams stood in the king's palace as the representative of a foreign country.

Adams bought a new coat for the meeting. Two days later, on June 3, 1785, he wrote to Thomas Jefferson about it. He wrote that the king had received him with more kindness than he had expected. The Library of Congress holds that letter.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Americans enslaved in Algiers, 1785 to 1796" -->
From 1785 to 1796, officials of the ruler of Algiers held American sailors as slaves, and the United States government could not free them. Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli and Morocco were states on the coast of North Africa, called the Barbary states. Their rulers' ships seized the ships of countries that did not pay them, and they held the sailors for ransom.

In 1784 Moroccan ships seized an American merchant ship, and a treaty with Morocco followed in 1786. In 1785 Muhammad, the Dey of Algiers, declared war on the United States. The Dey was the ruler of Algiers. His ships captured several American ships. On July 25, 1785, an Algerian warship took the schooner *Maria* of Boston about three miles off Cape St. Vincent, in Portugal. A schooner is a sailing ship with two or more masts. The *Maria* reached Algiers on August 4, and its crew was enslaved. More American crews followed.

The United States government had almost no navy and very little money. It could not free the sailors by force, and for years it could not pay what the Dey asked for them. In 1795 President George Washington's government sent Joel Barlow, Joseph Donaldson and Richard O'Brien to North Africa. They made treaties with Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli. The treaty with Algiers was signed on September 5, 1795. The American ratification is dated March 7, 1796.

Sources disagree on what the treaty cost. The State Department's historians say it freed 83 American sailors, and they give no dollar figure. Treasury officials estimated the total cost at about $992,463. That total began with a first payment of about $585,000. The money reached Algiers late, and the Dey demanded more. Other accounts give $642,500 paid at once, plus $21,600 a year in supplies for ships. The counts of captives also differ, from 83 freed in 1796 to 115 held in 1793.

The Dey's officials released the sailors on July 8, 1796. Six of them had died shortly before the release. Others carried injuries from their captivity for the rest of their lives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-leander-cathcart" name="James Leander Cathcart" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Leander Cathcart

> **Who:** An Irish-born American sailor, enslaved in Algiers for eleven years, who rose to be the chief Christian clerk to the Dey and helped put the 1795 treaty into English.
> **When and where:** Born June 1, 1767, in County Westmeath, Ireland. Captured July 25, 1785, off Cape St. Vincent, Portugal. Held in Algiers until May 1796. Died October 6, 1843.

James Leander Cathcart was 18 when an Algerian warship captured his ship, and he spent eleven years as a slave in Algiers. He had come to America as a boy. During the Revolution he served as a midshipman, a young officer in training, on the frigate *Confederacy*. The British captured him and held him on prison ships at New York until he escaped.

In 1785 he was one of six crewmen on the Boston schooner *Maria*. An Algerian warship with 14 guns took the *Maria* on July 25, 1785. Cathcart later gave his own reason for the timing. Spain had just made peace with Algiers, and the British consul, Charles Logie, told the Algerians they could take any ship that lacked a certain British pass. A consul is an official who looks after his country's trade and citizens in a foreign port.

The Algerian crew drove the six Americans into a boat and took their hats, handkerchiefs and shoes. On the voyage the captors locked all 42 of their prisoners every night in the sail room in the ship's hold. The drinking water was so foul, Cathcart wrote, that "we were, literally, obliged to strain through our teeth." The *Maria* reached Algiers on August 4. On August 12 the *Dauphin* of Philadelphia arrived with 15 more American captives, which made 21 in all.

Dey Muhammad chose some of the captives to work in his palace and garden. His officials sent others to the slave prisons and to hard labor at the harbor. A man at hard labor got one suit of clothes, worth about a dollar and a half, and two rough blankets meant to last his whole captivity.

About four months after he arrived, Cathcart was beaten on the orders of the Dey's chamberlains, the officials who ran his household. Four men threw him down, tied his arms and legs, and beat the soles of his bare feet with sticks. This punishment was called the bastinado. In the usual method, the man lay face down while two men held up a pole with his ankles tied to it, and a guard sat on his shoulders. Cathcart took 28 blows and lost four toenails. He wrote that all fourteen slaves in the garden got the same beating, "old men of sixty and children of ten years of age" among them.

Cathcart rose step by step. He served coffee to the minister of the harbor, then became clerk of the harbor, then clerk of a slave prison. At last he became chief Christian clerk to the Dey. Foreign ambassadors who could not get a meeting with the Dey went through him. He helped the American negotiator Joseph Donaldson, and he translated the Turkish text of the 1795 treaty into English.

By Cathcart's own list, 9 of the 21 Americans taken in 1785 died in Algiers, most of them of plague. The other 12 went home at different times. Some were bought free by family or friends before 1796, and the rest were freed in 1796. Cathcart left Algiers on May 8, 1796, carrying the treaty papers.

He married Jane B. Woodside in Philadelphia in 1798. In January 1799 he sailed from Philadelphia as the American consul for Tunis and Tripoli. From 1807 to 1817 he was the American consul-general at Cádiz, in Spain. His daughter, J. B. Newkirk, published his account, *The Captives: Eleven Years a Prisoner in Algiers*, in 1899.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-leander-cathcart" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Staying out of Europe's war, 1793 to 1796" -->
In 1793 France and Britain went to war again, and President George Washington decided to keep the United States out of it.

On April 8, 1793, Edmond-Charles Genêt, France's new minister, landed at Charleston, South Carolina. He hired American privateers to attack British ships. A privateer was a privately owned armed ship that had permission from a government to attack enemy ships. When Washington would not do as Genêt wished, Genêt threatened to go over the President's head to the American people. Washington asked the French government to replace him. On April 22, 1793, Washington issued the Proclamation of Neutrality. It stated that the United States would take neither side.

John Jay signed a treaty with Britain on November 19, 1794. Senators approved it on June 24, 1795, by 20 votes to 10. Under it, British troops were to leave the forts in the Northwest that Britain had promised to leave in 1783. The British agreed to give American traders terms as good as those of any other country, but with heavy limits on trade with the British islands in the Caribbean.

The two sides sent several quarrels to arbitration, a way of settling a dispute by letting a neutral panel decide. These included the border between Maine and Canada, the old debts, and British seizures of American ships. Britain kept the right to seize American cargo bound for France, with payment, and to take French goods off American ships without paying. The State Department's historians sum it up this way: the treaty "addressed few U.S. interests, and ultimately granted Britain additional rights." Many Americans hated it. Washington signed it anyway, because he judged that the country was too weak to fight Britain.

Thomas Pinckney signed a treaty with Spain on October 27, 1795. He negotiated it with Manuel de Godoy, a Spanish minister. Senators approved it on March 7, 1796. Under it, the border with Spanish West Florida was set at the 31st parallel, an east-west line on the map. Americans got free use of the Mississippi River and the right to use the port of New Orleans without paying tax. A farmer in Kentucky or Tennessee could not haul his crop over the mountains to sell it. He floated it down the rivers to New Orleans, which Spain controlled. Under Pinckney's Treaty he could sell it there.

On September 19, 1796, Washington's Farewell Address was printed in the *American Daily Advertiser*, a Philadelphia newspaper. Alexander Hamilton had helped him write it. In it Washington said he would not run for a third term. He also argued that the United States should not tie itself permanently to any foreign country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The XYZ Affair and a war at sea, 1798 to 1800" -->
From 1798 to 1800 the United States Navy fought French ships in the Caribbean in a war that neither country declared. The war followed a failed attempt at talks. President John Adams sent three commissioners to France: Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and John Marshall.

French agents told the three Americans that talks could not begin until the United States met their demands. The agents wanted a loan to France at low interest, a promise that the United States would take on the claims American merchants had against France, and "a substantial bribe" for the French foreign minister, Talleyrand. A bribe is money paid to get someone to do something dishonest. Adams made the Americans' reports public, with the agents' names replaced by the letters W, X, Y and Z. The agents were Nicholas Hubbard, Jean Hottinguer, Pierre Bellamy and Lucien Hauteval.

The fighting at sea that followed is called the Quasi-War. Quasi means "almost." The State Department's historians call it "a limited, undeclared war." American and French negotiators ended it with the Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine. That agreement also ended the alliance of 1778 between France and the United States. Senators did not ratify it until December 18, 1801.
