## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1900s American officials ruled lands overseas, and judges of the Supreme Court ruled that the people living there did not get the whole Constitution. American troops finished a war in the Philippines and occupied Haiti, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua. American leaders built the Panama Canal on a strip of land they held forever by treaty. Americans fought in two world wars. After the first, senators refused to join the League of Nations that President Woodrow Wilson had proposed. After the second, American leaders helped found the United Nations and the NATO military alliance.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Finishing the war in the Philippines, 1900 to 1902" -->
American soldiers in the Philippines tortured Filipinos and killed civilians. Of the officers who ordered it, one was suspended for a month and fined, one was scolded and forced to retire, and one went free. One method of torture was called the water cure.

At Igbaras on November 27, 1900, a group of five or six American soldiers tied up Tobeniano Ealdama, the town's mayor. They held his throat so he could not stop himself from swallowing, and they poured water into him until his stomach filled. Then they forced the water out by pressing on his stomach with a foot or with their hands. They did it to make him talk. Captain Edwin F. Glenn, an Army lawyer, directed it, along with a doctor working for the Army. After a second round, Ealdama said he was a captain among the Filipino fighters. That night Glenn ordered the whole town burned, 400 to 500 houses.

On September 28, 1901, at Balangiga on the island of Samar, townspeople and Filipino fighters attacked a company of American foot soldiers at breakfast. They killed about 48 soldiers. It was the Army's worst loss in a single fight since the Little Bighorn in 1876.

General Jacob H. Smith gave the orders for the American answer on Samar. His orders survive in the record: "I want no prisoners. I wish you to kill and burn." "The more you kill and burn, the better it will please me." He said the inside of Samar "must be made a howling wilderness." When he was asked how young a person could be and still be a target, Smith said any Filipino male over ten, because in his words a boy that age could carry a weapon. On Samar, Marine Major Littleton Waller had eleven Filipino guides and carriers shot without a trial.

How many people American troops killed on Samar is disputed, and the counts are far apart. An American soldier who was there said 39. A British researcher, Bob Couttie, studied it for ten years and put it at about 2,500. Most estimates fall between 2,000 and 2,500. Some Filipino historians say 5,000. An old figure of 50,000 is now traced to misread documents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What happened to the officers" -->
The torture came out in the Senate. Senator George Frisbie Hoar asked for an investigation. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge kept it inside his own Committee on the Philippines. On April 14, 1902, Charles S. Riley, a former sergeant, told the committee what he had seen at Igbaras.

Secretary of War Elihu Root ordered Glenn court-martialed, which means tried in a military court. Army officials moved the trial from San Francisco to Catbalogan, in the Philippines, far from the witnesses. The officers on the court found Glenn guilty. They suspended him for one month and fined him fifty dollars. He retired in 1919 as a brigadier general. Ealdama was then serving a prison sentence as a "war traitor."

Waller told his court-martial that he had followed Smith's orders, and the officers on the court found him not guilty. Another court-martial found Smith guilty of "conduct to the prejudice of good order and military discipline." Its officers asked only that he be formally scolded. President Roosevelt went further and forced Smith to retire, two years before he was due to leave the Army. Smith was not sent to prison. In a private letter, President Theodore Roosevelt called the water cure "the old Filipino method of mild torture" and wrote that "nobody was seriously damaged." In the same letter he wrote, "torture is not a thing that we can tolerate."

Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902. American troops kept fighting in the Moro provinces of the southern Philippines until 1913.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Insular Cases, 1901 to 1922" -->
Between 1901 and 1922, the judges of the Supreme Court, who are called justices, decided that the islands taken in 1898 belonged to the United States but were not fully part of it. The decisions are called the Insular Cases. Insular means having to do with islands.

In *Downes v. Bidwell*, decided on May 27, 1901, the justices held that Puerto Rico "belongs to, but is not a part of, the United States." From these cases came the idea of the "unincorporated territory." That is a place the United States owns and rules, where the people do not get the whole Constitution unless Congress chooses to give it to them. In *Balzac v. Porto Rico* in 1922, the justices held that Puerto Ricans had no constitutional right to a trial by jury at home. By then Puerto Ricans were American citizens.

The justices who decided these cases believed the people of the islands were not fit for full constitutional government. In 2022 Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that the Insular Cases "have no foundation in the Constitution and rest instead on racial stereotypes." He added, "They deserve no place in our law." The justices of the Supreme Court have never overturned them. In 2026 they are still the law for the people of Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who became a citizen, and when" -->
Members of Congress decided, island by island, whether the people of each territory would be American citizens. The answers came at different times. People born in American Samoa are still not citizens at birth.

Under the Foraker Act, signed on April 12, 1900, members of Congress set up a government for Puerto Rico. The law called the island's people "citizens of Porto Rico," under the protection of the United States but not citizens of it. On March 2, 1917, President Wilson signed the Jones-Shafroth Act, and under it Puerto Ricans became American citizens. One month later the United States entered the First World War. Two months after that, Wilson signed the law for the draft, which required young men to serve in the military. Accounts disagree on how many Puerto Ricans were drafted. The figure usually given is about 18,000, and History.com gives 20,000. Their citizenship came from a law passed by Congress, not from the Constitution. It did not come with a vote for president.

Hawaii's territorial government came from the Hawaiian Organic Act, signed on April 30, 1900. In a treaty signed on August 4, 1916, Danish officials agreed to sell the Danish West Indies to the United States for $25 million in gold. The islands changed hands on March 31, 1917, and became the U.S. Virgin Islands. Members of Congress did not make their people American citizens until 1927.

Officers of the United States Navy governed Guam from 1899. Its people did not become citizens until President Harry Truman signed the Organic Act of Guam on August 1, 1950. That was fifty-two years after the Navy took the island. Under the act, Guam got a government run by civilians and stayed an unincorporated territory.

The chiefs of Tutuila, in Samoa, signed a deed, a paper handing their island to the United States, on April 17, 1900. Tui Manuʻa Elisala signed one for the Manuʻa islands on July 16, 1904. Members of Congress did not approve either deed until 1929. The Navy governed American Samoa until 1951. Under federal law its people are American nationals, not citizens, and that is still true. A national can live in the United States and carry an American passport but is not a citizen. American Samoa is the only territory with people living on it where they do not become citizens at birth.

Under laws Congress passed in 1902 and 1916, American officials ran a colonial government in the Philippines, with a promise of independence someday. Under the Tydings-McDuffie Act, signed on March 24, 1934, the Philippines would become independent on July 4, 1946, after ten years of self-rule. The same act put Filipinos in a different group under immigration law, so far fewer could move to the United States. The Philippines became independent on July 4, 1946.

Filipinos served in the American armed forces in the Second World War, at the call of the United States. In 1946 members of Congress passed the Rescission Act. It stated that their service "shall not be deemed to have been active military, naval, or air service" for most veterans' benefits. Under it, they could not get benefits that other veterans of the same war received.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panama Canal, 1903 to 1914" -->
President Theodore Roosevelt and his officials backed Panama's break from Colombia in 1903. Philippe-Jean Bunau-Varilla, a Frenchman, signed the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty for Panama on November 18, 1903. Under the treaty the United States got a strip of land 10 miles wide across Panama, forever. Panama got $10,000,000 at once and $250,000 a year, starting nine years later.

The canal opened on August 15, 1914. The official count of the dead during the American building of the canal is 5,609, out of about 56,000 workers. Most of the workers came from Barbados, Jamaica and other islands of the Caribbean. Many historians think the real number of dead was several times the official one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marines in Haiti, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua" -->
American presidents sent the Marines to occupy three nearby countries for years at a time. To occupy a country is to hold it with troops and control its government.

In December 1914 American Marines took $500,000 from Haiti's national bank and moved it to New York. In July 1915 Haitian President Jean Vilbrun Guillaume Sam was killed, and President Woodrow Wilson sent the Marines into Haiti. They stayed for nineteen years, until 1934. Under a treaty of 1915, American officials took full control of Haiti's money. They set up a Haitian police force, the Gendarmerie, commanded by Marines. American officials forced the election of Philippe Sudré Dartiguenave as Haiti's president in August 1915.

American officials wanted a new Haitian constitution that let foreigners own land in Haiti. When Haiti's lawmakers resisted it, American officials forced President Dartiguenave to break up the legislature, and it did not meet again until 1929. The new constitution, written under American direction, went to a public vote on June 12, 1918. The official count recorded it as approved by 99 percent of the votes, with fewer than 5 percent of Haitians voting. Marines and the Gendarmerie brought back the corvée, a system of forced labor. They made Haitians build roads without pay.

Haitian fighters called cacos rose against the occupation. By some estimates the Marines and the Gendarmerie killed more than 2,000 cacos. American forces lost 10 killed and 26 wounded. On December 6, 1929, Marines fired on about 1,500 protesters at Les Cayes, and at least 12 Haitians died. President Franklin D. Roosevelt pulled the Marines out in 1934.

Charlemagne Péralte led the armed resistance from 1917. Marine officers ordered their men to "get Charlemagne." Sergeant Herman H. Hanneken went into Péralte's camp in disguise and shot him dead near Sainte-Suzanne on November 1, 1919. The Marines photographed the body and passed the picture around. Accounts give his birth year as 1885 or 1886.

Marines landed in the Dominican Republic in May 1916, and American officers declared a military government that November. From 1917 to 1921 they fought guerrillas in the east, fighters in small bands called gavilleros. The Library of Congress's country study calls the Americans' methods "often brutal." Dominicans took back control when President Horacio Vásquez took office on July 13, 1924.

In Nicaragua, American officials encouraged a revolt against President José Santos Zelaya in 1909, and Marines landed at Bluefields. In 1912 Marines came to support President Adolfo Díaz. A guard of 100 Marines stayed at the American embassy until 1925. In 1927 about 2,000 more Marines arrived.

Augusto César Sandino fought them for as long as they stayed. The Marines trained a Nicaraguan National Guard and left in January 1933, with Anastasio Somoza García in command of the Guard. In 1934 Somoza and other high officers agreed to kill Sandino. On February 23, 1934, Sandino came to what looked like a peace meeting, and National Guardsmen seized him and murdered him. Somoza became president on January 1, 1937, and ruled for about twenty years. No reliable count exists of the Nicaraguans killed in the fighting with the Marines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Woodrow Wilson

> **Who:** President of the United States during the First World War. He proposed the League of Nations.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1915 to 1920.

Woodrow Wilson proposed a League of Nations, and the United States never joined it. He was president when the United States entered the First World War on April 6, 1917. On January 8, 1918, he set out his terms for peace, called the Fourteen Points. One of them was a League of Nations, a group of countries that would settle quarrels by talking instead of fighting. Senators voted against the peace treaty and the League in November 1919 and again in March 1920.

Wilson also sent the Marines into Haiti in 1915, and they stayed until 1934. In 1917 he signed both the law that made Puerto Ricans American citizens and the draft law that followed it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woodrow-wilson-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puerto Rico under an appointed governor, 1937" -->
In 1937, under Governor Blanton Winship, police in Puerto Rico shot marchers at Ponce, and officials began using public money to sterilize Puerto Rican women. Winship was an American general. The President of the United States appointed him to govern the island. Puerto Ricans did not elect him.

On Palm Sunday, March 21, 1937, members of the Nationalist Party marched in Ponce. They marked the end of slavery in Puerto Rico and protested the jailing of their leader, Pedro Albizu Campos. The Insular Police were under Winship's direct military command. The police opened fire on the marchers. Accounts give the dead as between 19 and 21 and the wounded as between about 150 and 235. A human rights commission that looked into it found that all the dead had been shot by police. The killings are known as the Ponce Massacre.

Also in 1937, under Winship, Law 116 took effect. Under it, officials set up a Eugenics Board, and public money paid for sterilizations. Eugenics was a set of beliefs, now rejected, that some groups of people should be kept from having children. Sterilization is an operation after which a woman can never become pregnant again. In Puerto Rico the usual method was tubal ligation, which Puerto Ricans called *la operación*. In it a doctor cut or tied the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb. Some women instead had a hysterectomy, in which a surgeon removed the womb.

From 1937 until the law was repealed in 1960, members of the Eugenics Board forced 97 sterilizations. Many thousands more women had the operation under pressure. Some women were told, falsely, that it could be undone, or that employers would not hire them unless they were sterilized, and accounts do not name who told them. Employers did favor hiring sterilized women. Hospital staff turned away some pregnant women unless they agreed to be sterilized after giving birth. Clarence Gamble, an heir to the Procter and Gamble fortune who believed in eugenics, set up clinics on the island. By 1968 about one Puerto Rican woman in three of childbearing age had been sterilized. A 1982 study found 41 percent of married women in Puerto Rico sterilized.

The record on consent is mixed. Some women chose the operation because it was the only birth control offered to them. The doctor Helen Rodríguez-Trías said, "Women make choices based on alternatives, and there haven't been many alternatives in Puerto Rico."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pedro-albizu-campos-america-world" name="Pedro Albizu Campos" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pedro Albizu Campos

> **Who:** A United States Army officer in the First World War who became the leader of the movement for Puerto Rican independence.
> **When and where:** Born in Ponce, Puerto Rico, on September 12, 1891.

Pedro Albizu Campos was an American citizen who could be drafted into the American army but could not vote for the president who commanded it. He was born in Ponce on September 12, 1891. He was the first Puerto Rican to graduate from Harvard Law School.

In the First World War he served in the United States Army and rose to second lieutenant. Army officers assigned him to the 375th Infantry Regiment, which was all Black. The Army kept Black and white soldiers in separate units, which is called segregation. The segregation he met there shaped his politics.

In 1930 he became president of the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party, which wanted independence from the United States. He was later convicted of sedition, the crime of urging people to rise against the government, and sent to prison. In 1937 Nationalists marched in Ponce partly to protest his imprisonment, and police shot them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pedro-albizu-campos-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The St. Louis, 1939" -->
In 1939 American officials refused to let a ship full of Jewish refugees land. Refugees are people who flee their country to escape danger. The ship *St. Louis* sailed from Hamburg, in Germany, on May 13, 1939, with 937 passengers. Almost all of them were Jews escaping Nazi Germany.

Cuban officials let 28 of them land. American and Canadian officials refused them. The ship carried the rest back to Europe. Officials in Great Britain let in 288 of them, in the Netherlands 181, in Belgium 214 and in France 224. According to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, 254 of the passengers were later killed in the Holocaust, the Nazis' mass murder of the Jews of Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Guam under Japanese occupation, 1941 to 1944" -->
The people of Guam lived through two and a half years of Japanese occupation as people of the United States who were not American citizens. Japanese forces invaded Guam on December 8, 1941, hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor. Japanese soldiers beat CHamoru people, forced them to work without pay and put some of them to death. By the spring of 1944 Japanese officers had put every able-bodied man into a labor battalion, working without pay. In the last weeks, Japanese troops force-marched the people to concentration camps, where soldiers held civilians under guard. The largest camp was at Manenggon. American forces landed on July 21, 1944. Guam marks that day as Liberation Day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The United Nations, the Marshall Plan and NATO, 1945 to 1949" -->
After the Second World War, American leaders helped build the kind of world organization that senators had refused in 1920. Delegates from many countries signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco on June 26, 1945. It took effect on October 24, 1945.

In March 1948 members of Congress passed the law for the Marshall Plan, under which American officials sent money to rebuild Western Europe after the war. The money eventually rose to more than $12 billion. On April 4, 1949, twelve countries signed the treaty that created NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a military alliance. The State Department's historians call it "the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered into outside of the Western Hemisphere."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For forty years after 1950, American leaders shaped their dealings with the world around a rivalry with the Soviet Union, called the Cold War. With that rivalry in mind, American presidents and their advisers chose where aid, weapons and troops went, and they sent troops to fight in Korea and Vietnam. American officials secretly helped overthrow elected governments in Iran, Guatemala and Chile. In these same years Alaska and Hawaii became states. Puerto Rico got a new name but stayed a territory. American officials set off nuclear bombs on Pacific islands whose people the United States had taken charge of protecting for the United Nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cold War" -->
The Cold War was a long rivalry between the United States and its allies on one side and the Soviet Union and its allies on the other. American leaders let the rivalry guide nearly everything they did abroad: which countries they allied with, whom they gave aid and weapons to, and where they sent troops. American troops fought in Korea from 1950 to 1953 and later in Vietnam, as part of this contest.

On November 9, 1989, the Berlin Wall, which had divided the city of Berlin, was opened. The Soviet Union broke apart on December 26, 1991, and the Cold War was over.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cuban Missile Crisis, October 1962" -->
In October 1962 American and Soviet leaders came closer to nuclear war than at any other time. In July 1962 Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev and Cuban leader Fidel Castro secretly agreed to put Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. Before that, a force backed by the United States had tried and failed to overthrow Castro in the Bay of Pigs invasion.

On October 14, 1962, an American U-2 spy plane took pictures of missile sites being built in Cuba. On October 22 President John F. Kennedy ordered American warships to stop ships carrying weapons to Cuba. He called it a quarantine, and he told the country about it on television. American forces went on alert for war. On October 27 a U-2 was shot down over Cuba.

Kennedy then offered to promise not to invade Cuba if the missiles were removed. His brother Robert Kennedy secretly told the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, that American missiles in Turkey would also be taken out, though not as part of any public deal. On October 28 Khrushchev announced that the Soviet missiles would leave Cuba. The American Jupiter missiles left Turkey in April 1963.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Peace Corps, 1961" -->
On March 1, 1961, President Kennedy created the Peace Corps by an order he signed. It is a program for American volunteers to serve in other countries. Sargent Shriver was its first director. The first volunteers went to Ghana, in Africa, that same year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lillian-carter-america-world" name="Lillian Carter" movie="Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother (2021)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lillian Carter

> **Who:** A nurse from Plains, Georgia, who joined the Peace Corps at 68. Mother of President Jimmy Carter.
> **When and where:** India, 1966 to 1968.
> **Movie:** *Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother* (2021), a documentary film about her directed by Vivian Winther.

Lillian Carter joined the Peace Corps at the age of 68 and worked as a nurse in India from 1966 to 1968. She told the recruiter she wanted to go "where it's warm, people have dark skins and need a nurse's service."

She was sent to the Godrej Colony, about 30 miles from Mumbai, and she turned 70 there. After she came home, her son Jimmy Carter became president. She died in 1983. In 1986 Peace Corps officials created the Lillian Carter Award for volunteers who serve at the age of 50 or older.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lillian-carter-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Governments overthrown with American help" -->
Three times in these years, American officials secretly helped overthrow an elected government. A coup is the sudden overthrow of a government by a small group, often by the army.

**Iran, 1953.** Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh had taken control of Iran's oil industry away from British companies. In June 1953 President Dwight Eisenhower approved a secret American and British plan to overthrow him. CIA Director Allen Dulles oversaw the planning. The CIA is the Central Intelligence Agency, the American government's spy service. Kermit Roosevelt of the CIA ran the plan in Iran. CIA officers paid journalists, religious leaders and politicians to attack Mosaddegh as corrupt and power-hungry.

On August 19, 1953, General Fazlollah Zahedi replaced Mosaddegh, and the Shah, Iran's king, took back power. The Shah had Mosaddegh convicted of treason. Mosaddegh served three years in prison and lived the rest of his life under house arrest. Officials of the Shah's government put to death one of his advisers and more than a dozen military officers and student leaders on his side. In 2013 CIA officials released the agency's own history, which states that the agency "planned and helped implement the coup."

**Guatemala, 1954.** On June 27, 1952, President Jacobo Árbenz began a land reform, a plan to share out farmland, with a law called Decree 900. Under it his officials took land from owners and gave about 1.5 million acres to about 100,000 families. The owners were paid more than $8 million in all. The American-owned United Fruit Company was one of the landowners. Árbenz's government offered United Fruit $2.99 an acre for its land. State Department officials demanded $75 an acre.

In August 1953 Eisenhower approved a CIA operation to overthrow Árbenz. CIA officers ran what they called "psychological warfare and political action" against Árbenz's government. Psychological warfare means using fear, rumors and false stories to turn people against their leaders. The officers also drew up lists of Guatemalan officials to be killed. One list had 58 names. On June 18, 1954, a force under Carlos Castillo Armas invaded from Honduras, after planes had harassed the capital. On June 27 Árbenz resigned. Castillo Armas became president, and his officials gave the land back to the companies and broke up the labor unions. Human rights groups estimate that between 1954 and 1990, the forces of Guatemala's military governments murdered more than 100,000 civilians.

**Chile, 1970 to 1973.** Salvador Allende was elected president of Chile in September 1970. On September 15, 1970, President Richard Nixon met CIA Director Richard Helms and ordered him to stop Allende. Helms wrote down Nixon's words in his notes: "make the economy scream" and "$10,000,000 available, more if necessary." CIA officers gave weapons to men who planned to kidnap the head of Chile's army, General René Schneider. A different group of plotters killed Schneider on October 22, 1970.

On September 11, 1973, General Augusto Pinochet led the army coup that overthrew Allende. Pinochet ruled Chile until 1990. Chile's official commissions count 3,216 people killed or disappeared by Pinochet's forces between 1973 and 1990, and 38,254 people who survived political imprisonment, torture or both. To be disappeared means to be taken by the government's forces and never seen again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alaska and Hawaii become states, 1959" -->
Alaska became the 49th state on January 3, 1959, and Hawaii became the 50th on August 21, 1959. Hawaii's voters could only say Yes or No to becoming a state right away. On June 27, 1959, the ballot asked: "Shall Hawaii immediately be admitted into the Union as a State?" The only answers were Yes and No.

Independence was not on the ballot. In 1953 members of the United Nations General Assembly had passed Resolution 742. It stated that votes like this one should offer a free choice among several futures, including independence. A large majority of Hawaii's voters chose statehood. Hawaii and Alaska were then taken off the United Nations list of territories that did not govern themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puerto Rico: a new name, and armed revolt, 1950 to 1954" -->
In 1950 Puerto Ricans got the right to write their own constitution, but the island stayed a territory under the Insular Cases. Members of Congress passed Public Law 600 that year. Puerto Rico's voters approved the law in 1951 and the constitution in 1952. On July 25, 1952, Governor Luis Muñoz Marín proclaimed the Commonwealth, the *Estado Libre Asociado*. Under that new name, the island was still a territory of the United States. The date was the anniversary of the American landing at Guánica in 1898. On November 27, 1953, members of the United Nations General Assembly voted to take Puerto Rico off the list of territories that did not govern themselves. American officials then stopped sending the United Nations reports about the island.

Some Puerto Ricans fought the new arrangement with guns. On October 30, 1950, Nationalists rose up in several towns. Soldiers and pilots of the Puerto Rico National Guard, under Major General Luis R. Esteves, attacked the towns of Jayuya and Utuado with P-47 Thunderbolt warplanes, artillery, mortars and grenades. Pilots in American military aircraft fired on and bombed towns full of American citizens. On November 1, 1950, two Nationalists, Griselio Torresola and Oscar Collazo, attacked Blair House in Washington, where President Harry Truman was living. Torresola shot a White House guard, Leslie Coffelt, who killed Torresola before he died himself. Collazo was sentenced to death, and in 1952 Truman changed the sentence to life in prison.

On March 1, 1954, four Puerto Rican Nationalists went into the visitors' gallery of the House of Representatives. They were Lolita Lebrón, Rafael Cancel Miranda, Andrés Figueroa Cordero and Irvin Flores Rodríguez. They unfurled a Puerto Rican flag and fired down onto the floor. They wounded five members of the House, and all five recovered. All four Nationalists were convicted and sent to prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pacific: a trust, nuclear tests and one vote to join" -->
After the Second World War, members of the United Nations placed more than 2,000 Pacific islands under American rule. The islands were the Marianas, the Carolines and the Marshalls. The arrangement was called the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands, and it lasted from 1947 to 1986. A trust territory is land that one country governs for the United Nations, with a duty to look after its people.

Between 1946 and 1958 American officials set off 67 nuclear tests at Bikini and Enewetak, two atolls in the Marshall Islands. An atoll is a ring of small coral islands around a lagoon. American officials moved the people off those atolls before the tests.

The largest test was Castle Bravo, on March 1, 1954. Its explosive force was 15 megatons, equal to 15 million tons of TNT. That was about two and a half times what its designers had predicted. Fallout, the radioactive dust from the blast, came down on islands where people lived. On Rongelap, about 110 miles away, the fallout looked like snow, and children played with the flakes. All 64 people on Rongelap took in radiation from it. American officials moved the people of Rongelap and Utirik away about 72 hours after the blast, and Rongelap could no longer be lived on. Radioactive iodine from fallout collects in the thyroid, a gland in the neck. In later years thyroid disease, including thyroid cancer, was the most common lasting harm among the people exposed. A screening from 1993 to 1997 found 57 Marshallese born before 1954 who had thyroid cancer or had had it removed. Twenty-three Japanese fishermen on the boat *Lucky Dragon* were also caught in the fallout, and one of them died.

The people of the Northern Mariana Islands voted to join the United States, the only place in the territories where people did. A Covenant, a formal agreement, between the islands and the United States was signed on February 15, 1975. On June 17, 1975, 78.8 percent of the people who voted approved it. The Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands began in 1978, and its people became American citizens in 1986. The Marshall Islands and the Federated States of Micronesia became independent countries in 1986, tied to the United States by agreements called Compacts of Free Association. Palau's compact took effect in 1994.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The capital, the canal and an apology" -->
Washington, D.C. is not a state, and the people living there could not vote for president at all until 1961. That year enough states ratified, or approved, the Twenty-third Amendment to the Constitution, and it took effect on March 29, 1961. In 1971 the city got back a delegate in the House, who can speak there but cannot vote on bills. Under the Home Rule Act of 1973, its people elected their own mayor from 1975. Members of Congress passed an amendment in 1978 to give the city full seats in Congress. Lawmakers in too few states approved it, and it ran out of time in 1985.

On September 7, 1977, American and Panamanian leaders signed the Torrijos-Carter Treaties. Under them, American officials handed the Panama Canal to Panama on December 31, 1999.

In 1993 members of Congress passed, and President Bill Clinton signed, the Apology Resolution, Public Law 103-150. The resolution states that the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893 was illegal. It also states that the Native Hawaiian people never directly gave up their claims to sovereignty, the right to govern themselves.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2001 American leaders have sent troops to wars in Afghanistan, Iraq and, in 2026, Iran. They have argued over trade and over the Paris agreement on climate. More than 3.6 million people live in the five American territories with people on them. They still live under the Insular Cases, and none of them can vote for president while living at home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="September 11, 2001, and the wars after it" -->
On September 11, 2001, 19 hijackers attacked the United States and killed 2,977 people. The count does not include the 19 hijackers. The next day, the leaders of NATO's member countries called on Article 5 of their treaty for the first and only time. Article 5 is the promise that an attack on one member is an attack on all of them.

American forces went into Afghanistan on October 7, 2001. The last American troops left on August 30, 2021. On March 20, 2003, American forces invaded Iraq. American leaders said Iraq had weapons of mass destruction, meaning nuclear, chemical or biological weapons. None were found.

Researchers count the dead of these wars in different ways, and their counts are far apart. The Costs of War project at Brown University counts more than 940,000 people killed directly by war violence in Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen and Pakistan from 2001 to 2023. More than 432,000 of them were civilians. The project estimates that another 3.6 to 3.8 million people died from hunger, destroyed hospitals and disease caused by the wars. That makes at least 4.5 to 4.7 million deaths in all.

For Iraq, counts built from deaths recorded one by one come out much lower than estimates built from surveys of households. Iraq Body Count, which records deaths one at a time, had counted more than 187,000 civilians killed from 2003 to 2024. A survey published in the medical journal *The Lancet* in 2006 estimated about 600,000 violent deaths in Iraq in just the first three years of the war. American and Iraqi officials disputed it at the time. A survey by the World Health Organization and Iraq's health ministry estimated about 151,000 violent deaths over the same three years.

On January 11, 2002, American officials began holding prisoners at the navy base at Guantánamo Bay, in Cuba. Most of them were never tried in court. According to the journalist Andy Worthington, who tracks the prison, 779 men had been held there by January 2025, and 15 were still held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade and the climate agreement" -->
On November 10, 2001, members of the World Trade Organization approved China's entry into the organization. The WTO is a body of countries that sets rules for trade between them. China joined at the end of that year. On July 1, 2020, according to the Office of the United States Trade Representative, a new agreement among the United States, Mexico and Canada took effect. It replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement, called NAFTA.

American presidents have joined and left the Paris climate agreement twice. In that agreement, countries promise to cut the gases that are warming the earth. The United States left it in November 2020 and rejoined in February 2021. On January 20, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an order to leave again. The Congressional Research Service, which studies issues for Congress, reports that the withdrawal took effect on January 27, 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Strikes at sea, Venezuela and Iran, 2025 and 2026" -->
From September 2, 2025, the American military struck boats in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific Ocean that President Trump's government said were carrying drugs. By September 19, 2026, the strikes had killed at least 231 people in 69 strikes, the Associated Press reported. In at least one strike, the military gave no evidence that the boat was carrying drugs. The ACLU, a group that defends civil rights in court, is suing for the families of two men from Trinidad, Chad Joseph and Rishi Samaroo. The two were killed in a strike on October 14, 2025. The ACLU says they were fishermen and farm workers.

On January 3, 2026, American special operations troops took Venezuela's president, Nicolás Maduro, and his wife out of Caracas, Venezuela's capital. According to the Brookings Institution, a research group, the raid went ahead without approval from Congress beforehand.

On February 28, 2026, a war with Iran began with American and Israeli bombing. The Human Rights Activists News Agency, an Iranian rights group, counted 3,636 people killed in Iran by April 7, 2026, and 1,701 of them were civilians. The Pentagon is the headquarters of the American military. Its public count showed 19 American service members dead on September 22, 2026, and more than 820 wounded. That same day, five American officials told the *Washington Post* that at least 22 service members had died in the Middle East since the war began, and a sixth said 23. Not all of those deaths came from the fighting.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The territories today" -->
In 2020 more than 3.6 million people lived in the five American territories with people on them, according to the Census Bureau. The 2020 census counted 3,285,874 people in Puerto Rico, 11.8 percent fewer than in 2010. It counted 153,836 in Guam, about 87,146 in the U.S. Virgin Islands, 49,710 in American Samoa and 47,329 in the Northern Mariana Islands. Every one of the five lost people between 2010 and 2020.

People born in Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands and the Northern Mariana Islands are American citizens. People born in American Samoa are American nationals. Their passports say so, and to vote they must go through naturalization, the process a person from another country uses to become a citizen. No one living in a territory can vote for president. Voters in each territory elect one delegate to the House of Representatives. The delegate can speak and work on committees but cannot vote on whether a bill passes. Puerto Rico's delegate is called the Resident Commissioner.

By 2016 Puerto Rico's government owed more than $72 billion, plus more than $55 billion it had promised in pensions. The island had no legal way to reorganize the debt. On June 30, 2016, members of Congress passed a law called PROMESA. Under it, a board of seven members, appointed in Washington, has power over Puerto Rico's budget. Puerto Ricans call it *la junta*. The people whose budget the board controls did not elect its members. By the board's own account, it has reorganized about 80 percent of the debt, cutting it from more than $70 billion to about $37 billion.

On August 5, 2025, President Trump fired several members of the board. CBS News named five of them. Under PROMESA a president may remove a member "only for cause," which means for a real reason such as wrongdoing. Three of the fired members, Arthur J. González, Andrew G. Biggs and Betty A. Rosa, went to court. On October 3, 2025, Judge María Antongiorgi-Jordán of the federal court in Puerto Rico put them back on the board. In 2026 she made that order permanent and called the firings "plainly contrary to law," according to the Democracy Defenders Fund, a group that campaigns on political causes.

Puerto Ricans have voted on their island's status several times, and Congress has not acted on any of the results. In 2020 statehood won 52.52 percent. On November 5, 2024, statehood won 58.61 percent, free association 29.57 percent and independence 11.82 percent, according to the island's certified results. Free association means a separate country tied to the United States by a formal agreement, as the Marshall Islands are. Some voters stayed away from these votes in protest. Members of Congress do not have to follow the results, and only Congress can change Puerto Rico's status.

Washington, D.C. had about 690,000 people in the 2020 census, more than the state of Wyoming, which had 576,851, or Vermont. It has one delegate in the House who cannot vote on bills, and no senators. On April 22, 2021, members of the House of Representatives voted 216 to 208 to make it a state. Senators did not take up the bill.

### John Fitisemanu

> **Who:** A man born in American Samoa who lives in Utah. He is an American national, not an American citizen.
> **When and where:** Utah. His case was decided by a federal appeals court in 2021.

John Fitisemanu lives in Utah and cannot vote there, because he was born in American Samoa. He sued the United States to be recognized as a citizen by birth. A federal judge in Utah agreed with him. In 2021 judges of a higher federal court, the Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, overturned that decision. They held that the Insular Cases were still the law. On October 17, 2022, the justices of the Supreme Court refused to take his case.

American Samoa's own elected leaders opposed his lawsuit. They argued that citizenship ordered by a court could put *faʻa Sāmoa* at legal risk. *Faʻa Sāmoa* is the Samoan way of life, which includes land held in common by families and the matai system of chiefs. Fitisemanu wants to be a citizen. The leaders of his home territory did not want citizenship granted to Samoans that way.

### José Luis Vaello Madero

> **Who:** A man the United States government sued for $28,081 after he moved to Puerto Rico.
> **When and where:** New York, then Puerto Rico. His case was decided by the Supreme Court on April 21, 2022.

José Luis Vaello Madero moved from New York to Puerto Rico, and lawyers for the United States government sued him to get back $28,081 in benefits. While he lived in New York, he lawfully received Supplemental Security Income, a federal payment for people with very low incomes who are elderly, blind or disabled. Members of Congress have not made that benefit available to people living in Puerto Rico. After he moved, the payments kept coming, and he did not know he was no longer eligible.

His case went to the Supreme Court. On April 21, 2022, the justices ruled 8 to 1 that the Constitution does not require Congress to pay that benefit to people in Puerto Rico. Justice Neil Gorsuch agreed with the result. It was in a separate opinion in this case that he called for the Insular Cases to be overturned.
