## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 17 minutes**

Puerto Rico "belongs to, but is not a part of, the United States," the judges of the Supreme Court ruled in 1901. In a set of cases between 1901 and 1922 they held the same for the other islands taken in 1898, and the people living there did not get the whole Constitution. American troops finished a war in the Philippines and occupied Haiti, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua.

Workers, most of them from islands in the Caribbean, built the Panama Canal for the United States, on a strip of land that American officials held forever under a treaty. Americans fought in two world wars. After the first, senators refused to join the League of Nations that President Woodrow Wilson had proposed, and after the second, American leaders helped found the United Nations and the NATO military alliance.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Finishing the war in the Philippines, 1900 to 1902" -->
American soldiers in the Philippines tortured Filipino prisoners and killed civilians. Chapter 22, War and the Military, tells what they did at Igbaras and on Samar in full. Here is what happened in Washington, and to the officers who gave the orders.

**Igbaras and Samar.** At Igbaras, on November 27, 1900, Captain Edwin F. Glenn, an Army lawyer, and a doctor working for the Army directed the water cure of the town's mayor, Tobeniano Ealdama, until Ealdama said he was a captain among the Filipino fighters. That night Glenn had the town burned. On Samar, townspeople and Filipino fighters at Balangiga killed about 48 American soldiers on September 28, 1901, the Army's worst loss in a single fight since the Little Bighorn. General Jacob H. Smith then ordered, "I want no prisoners. I wish you to kill and burn."

**Disputed:** Counts of the people American troops killed on Samar are far apart. Most estimates fall between 2,000 and 2,500, and a British researcher, Bob Couttie, who studied it for ten years, put it at about 2,500. An American soldier who was there said 39, and other counts reach 5,000. An old figure of 50,000 is now traced to misread documents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What happened to the officers" -->
The torture came out in the Senate. Senator George Frisbie Hoar asked for an investigation, and Senator Henry Cabot Lodge kept it inside his own Committee on the Philippines. On April 14, 1902, Charles S. Riley, a former sergeant, told the committee what he had seen at Igbaras.

**Glenn.** Secretary of War Elihu Root ordered Glenn court-martialed, tried in a military court. Army officials moved the trial from San Francisco to Catbalogan, in the Philippines, far from the witnesses. The officers on the court found Glenn guilty, suspended him for one month and fined him fifty dollars. Ealdama, the mayor he had tortured, was then serving a prison sentence as a "war traitor."

**Waller and Smith.** On Samar, Marine Major Littleton Waller had eleven Filipino guides and carriers shot without a trial. He told his court-martial that he had followed Smith's orders, and the officers on the court found him not guilty.

Officers at another court-martial found Smith guilty of "conduct to the prejudice of good order and military discipline," behaving in a way that harmed order and discipline in the armed forces. They asked only that he be formally scolded. President Theodore Roosevelt went further and forced Smith to retire, two years before he was due to leave the Army. Smith was not sent to prison.

**Roosevelt's letter.** In a private letter, Roosevelt called the water cure "the old Filipino method of mild torture" and wrote that "nobody was seriously damaged." In the same letter he also wrote, "torture is not a thing that we can tolerate."

Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902. American troops kept fighting in the Moro provinces of the southern Philippines until 1913.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Insular Cases, 1901 to 1922" -->
The justices, the judges of the Supreme Court, decided between 1901 and 1922 that the islands taken in 1898 belonged to the United States but were not fully part of it. Their decisions are called the Insular Cases, from a word meaning having to do with islands.

The justices decided *Downes v. Bidwell* on May 27, 1901, holding that Puerto Rico "belongs to, but is not a part of, the United States." From these cases came the idea of the "unincorporated territory," a place the United States owns and rules, where the people do not get the whole Constitution unless Congress chooses to give it to them.

**A trial by jury.** In *Balzac v. Porto Rico* in 1922, the justices held that Puerto Ricans had no constitutional right to a trial by jury at home. By then Puerto Ricans were American citizens.

The justices who decided these cases believed the people of the islands were not fit for full constitutional government. In 2022 Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that the Insular Cases "have no foundation in the Constitution and rest instead on racial stereotypes," and he added, "They deserve no place in our law."

The justices of the Supreme Court have never overturned them. In 2026 they are still the law for the people of Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who became a citizen, and when" -->
Members of Congress decided, island by island, whether the people of each territory would be American citizens. The answers came at different times. People born in American Samoa are still not citizens at birth.

**Puerto Rico.** Members of Congress set up a government for Puerto Rico under the Foraker Act, signed on April 12, 1900. The law identified the island's people as "citizens of Porto Rico," under the protection of the United States but not citizens of it. President Wilson signed the Jones-Shafroth Act on March 2, 1917, and under it Puerto Ricans became American citizens. Their citizenship came from a law passed by Congress, not from the Constitution, and it did not come with a vote for president.

One month later Americans entered the First World War. Two months after that, Wilson signed the law for the draft, which required young men to serve in the military. Accounts disagree on how many Puerto Ricans were drafted: the figure usually given is about 18,000, and History.com gives 20,000.

**Hawaii and the Virgin Islands.** Hawaii's territorial government was set up under the Hawaiian Organic Act, signed on April 30, 1900. Under the same law, the people who had been citizens of the Republic of Hawaii in August 1898 became American citizens. Danish officials agreed in a treaty signed on August 4, 1916, to sell the Danish West Indies to the United States for $25 million in gold. The islands changed hands on March 31, 1917, and became the U.S. Virgin Islands, but members of Congress did not make their people American citizens until 1927.

**Guam.** Officers of the United States Navy governed Guam from 1899. Its people did not become citizens until President Harry Truman signed the Organic Act of Guam on August 1, 1950, fifty-two years after Navy officers took the island. Under the act, Guam got a government run by civilians and stayed an unincorporated territory.

**American Samoa.** Members of Congress did not approve the Samoan chiefs' deeds of 1900 and 1904 until 1929, and Navy officers governed American Samoa until 1951. Under federal law its people are still American nationals, people who can live in the United States and carry an American passport but are not citizens. American Samoa is the only territory with people living on it where they do not become citizens at birth.

**The Philippines.** Under laws members of Congress passed in 1902 and 1916, American officials ran a colonial government in the Philippines, with a promise of independence someday. Under the Tydings-McDuffie Act, signed on March 24, 1934, the Philippines would become independent on July 4, 1946, after ten years of self-rule. In the same act, members of Congress put Filipinos in a different group under immigration law, so far fewer could move to the United States. The Philippines became independent on July 4, 1946.

Filipinos served in the American armed forces in the Second World War, at the call of the United States. Members of Congress then passed the Rescission Act in 1946, which stated that their service "shall not be deemed," or counted, "to have been active military, naval, or air service" for most veterans' benefits. Under it, they could not get benefits that other veterans of the same war received.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Frenchman who signed for Panama, 1903 to 1914" -->
American officials backed Panama's break from Colombia in 1903. President Theodore Roosevelt sent American warships to Panama, and Panama's leaders declared independence on November 3. They at once named Philippe-Jean Bunau-Varilla, a French engineer who had worked on France's failed attempt to dig a canal there, to bargain for them in Washington.

Bunau-Varilla signed the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty for Panama on November 18, 1903, and no Panamanian signed it. Under the treaty American officials got a strip of land 10 miles wide across Panama, forever. Panama got $10,000,000 at once and $250,000 a year, starting nine years later.

**The cost in lives.** The canal opened on August 15, 1914. The official count of the dead during the American building of the canal is 5,609, out of about 56,000 workers, about one worker in ten. Most of the workers came from Barbados, Jamaica and other islands of the Caribbean. The historian Caroline Lieffers, writing for The Conversation, says many historians think the real number was several times higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marines in Haiti, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua" -->
American presidents sent the Marines to occupy three nearby countries, holding them with troops and controlling their governments, for years at a time.

**Haiti.** American Marines took $500,000 from Haiti's national bank in December 1914 and moved it to New York. Haitian President Jean Vilbrun Guillaume Sam was killed in July 1915, and the State Department's historians do not name who killed him. President Woodrow Wilson then sent the Marines into Haiti, and they stayed for nineteen years, until 1934.

Under a treaty of 1915, American officials took full control of Haiti's money and set up a Haitian police force, the Gendarmerie, commanded by Marines. They forced the election of Philippe Sudré Dartiguenave as Haiti's president in August 1915.

American officials wanted a new Haitian constitution that let foreigners own land in Haiti. When Haiti's lawmakers resisted it, American officials forced President Dartiguenave to send the lawmakers home, and they did not meet again until 1929. The new constitution, written under American direction, went to a public vote on June 12, 1918. The official count recorded it as approved by 99 percent of the votes, with fewer than 5 percent of Haitians voting.

Marines and the Gendarmerie brought back the corvée, a system of forced labor, and made Haitians build roads without pay.

**The killing.** Haitian fighters called cacos rose against the occupation. The Marines and the Gendarmerie killed more than 2,000 cacos, by estimates in a report by the Congressional Research Service, and American forces lost 10 killed and 26 wounded. The report gives no count of other Haitians killed.

General George Barnett, a former head of the Marine Corps, spoke in October 1920 to a navy court of inquiry, a panel of officers looking into what Marines had done in Haiti. He said the Marines had recorded 2,250 Haitians killed from 1915 to 1920, 1,861 of them in 1919.

Marines fired on about 1,500 protesters at Les Cayes on December 6, 1929, and killed at least 12 Haitians. President Franklin D. Roosevelt pulled the Marines out in 1934.

**Charlemagne Péralte.** Péralte, born in 1885 or 1886 by different accounts, led the armed resistance from 1917, and Marine officers ordered their men to "get Charlemagne." Sergeant Herman H. Hanneken went into Péralte's camp in disguise and shot him dead near Sainte-Suzanne on November 1, 1919. The Marines photographed the body and passed the picture around.

**The Dominican Republic.** Marines landed in the Dominican Republic in May 1916, and American officers declared a military government that November. From 1917 to 1921 they fought guerrillas in the east, called gavilleros. The Library of Congress's country study describes the Americans' methods as "often brutal" but gives no count of the Dominicans killed. Dominicans took back control when President Horacio Vásquez took office on July 13, 1924.

**Nicaragua.** American officials encouraged a revolt against President José Santos Zelaya in 1909, and Marines landed at Bluefields. Marines came again in 1912 to support President Adolfo Díaz, and a guard of 100 Marines stayed at the American embassy until 1925. About 2,000 more Marines arrived in 1927.

Augusto César Sandino fought them for as long as they stayed. The Marines trained a Nicaraguan National Guard and left in January 1933, with Anastasio Somoza García in command of the Guard.

Somoza and other high officers agreed in 1934 to kill Sandino. On February 23, 1934, Sandino came to what looked like a peace meeting, and National Guardsmen seized him and murdered him. Somoza became president on January 1, 1937, and ruled for about twenty years.

**Not known:** No reliable count exists of the Nicaraguans killed in the fighting with the Marines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="woodrow-wilson-america-world" name="Woodrow Wilson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Woodrow Wilson

> **Who:** The president who proposed the League of Nations and sent the Marines into Haiti.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1915 to 1920.

**Senators voted twice against the League of Nations Wilson proposed.**

Wilson was president when Americans entered the First World War on April 6, 1917. He set out his terms for peace, called the Fourteen Points, on January 8, 1918. One of them was a League of Nations, a group of countries that would settle quarrels by talking instead of fighting. Senators voted against the peace treaty and the League in November 1919 and again in March 1920, and the United States never joined it.

Wilson also sent the Marines into Haiti in 1915, and they stayed until 1934. In 1917 he signed both the law that made Puerto Ricans American citizens and the draft law that followed it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woodrow-wilson-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puerto Rico under an appointed governor, 1937" -->
**By 1935:** The Marines had left Nicaragua and Haiti, and members of Congress had promised the Philippines independence on July 4, 1946. Puerto Ricans had been American citizens since 1917, but they could not vote for president.

Police in Puerto Rico shot marchers at Ponce in 1937, under Governor Blanton Winship, and the same year officials began using public money to sterilize Puerto Rican women, which means operating on them so they could never become pregnant again. Winship was an American general whom the President of the United States appointed to govern the island. Puerto Ricans did not elect him.

**Ponce.** Members of the Nationalist Party marched in Ponce on Palm Sunday, March 21, 1937. They marked the end of slavery in Puerto Rico and protested the jailing of their leader, Pedro Albizu Campos. The Insular Police, who were under Winship's direct military command, opened fire on the marchers.

Accounts give the dead as between 19 and 21 and the wounded as between about 150 and 235. A human rights commission that looked into it found that police had shot all the dead. The killings are known as the Ponce Massacre.

**Law 116.** Law 116 took effect the same year, under Winship. Under it, officials set up a Eugenics Board, and public money paid for sterilizations. The board took its name from eugenics, a set of beliefs, now rejected, that some groups of people should be kept from having children.

In Puerto Rico the usual method of sterilization was tubal ligation, which Puerto Ricans called *la operación*. In it a doctor cut or tied the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb. Some women instead had a hysterectomy, in which a surgeon removed the womb.

**Forced and pressured.** Members of the Eugenics Board forced 97 sterilizations from 1937 until the law was repealed in 1960. Many thousands more women had the operation under pressure. Some women were told, falsely, that the operation could be undone. Others were told that they would not be hired unless they were sterilized, and employers did favor hiring sterilized women. The accounts do not name who told them these things. Hospital staff turned away some pregnant women unless they agreed to be sterilized after giving birth.

Clarence Gamble, an heir to the Procter and Gamble fortune who believed in eugenics, set up clinics on the island. By 1968 about one Puerto Rican woman in three of childbearing age had been sterilized, and a 1982 study found 41 percent of married women in Puerto Rico sterilized.

The record on consent is mixed. Some women chose the operation because it was the only birth control offered to them. The doctor Helen Rodríguez-Trías said, "Women make choices based on alternatives, and there haven't been many alternatives in Puerto Rico."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pedro-albizu-campos-america-world" name="Pedro Albizu Campos" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pedro Albizu Campos

> **Who:** A United States Army officer in the First World War who became the leader of the movement for Puerto Rican independence.
> **When and where:** Born in Ponce, Puerto Rico, on September 12, 1891.

**Albizu Campos could be drafted but could not vote for president.**

Under the 1917 law he was an American citizen, so officials could draft him into the army, but no one living in Puerto Rico could vote for president. He was also the first Puerto Rican to graduate from Harvard Law School.

In the First World War he served in the United States Army and rose to second lieutenant. Army officers assigned him to the 375th Infantry Regiment, which was all Black, because Army leaders kept Black and white soldiers in separate units, a practice called segregation. The segregation he met there shaped his politics.

He became president of the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party, whose members wanted independence from the United States, in 1930. He was later convicted of sedition, the crime of urging people to rise against the government, and sent to prison. Nationalists marched in Ponce in 1937 partly to protest his imprisonment, and police shot them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pedro-albizu-campos-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ship American officials turned away, 1939" -->
American officials refused to let a ship full of Jewish refugees land in 1939. The *St. Louis* sailed from Hamburg, in Germany, on May 13, 1939, with 937 passengers, almost all of them Jews escaping Nazi Germany.

Cuban officials let 28 of them land, and American and Canadian officials refused the rest. The ship carried them back to Europe, where officials in Great Britain let in 288 of them, in the Netherlands 181, in Belgium 214 and in France 224.

Nazis later killed 254 of the passengers in the Holocaust, their mass murder of the Jews of Europe, according to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Guam under Japanese occupation, 1941 to 1944" -->
The people of Guam lived through two and a half years of Japanese occupation as people of the United States who were not American citizens. Japanese forces invaded Guam on December 8, 1941, hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor. Japanese soldiers beat CHamoru people, forced them to work without pay and put some of them to death.

Japanese officers had put every able-bodied man into a labor battalion, working without pay, by the spring of 1944. In the last weeks, Japanese troops force-marched the people to concentration camps, where soldiers held civilians under guard. The largest camp was at Manenggon. American forces landed on July 21, 1944, and people on Guam mark that day as Liberation Day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The United Nations, the Marshall Plan and NATO, 1945 to 1949" -->
After the Second World War, American leaders helped build the kind of world organization that senators had refused in 1920. Delegates from many countries signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco on June 26, 1945, and it took effect on October 24, 1945.

**The Marshall Plan and NATO.** Members of Congress passed the law for the Marshall Plan in March 1948, under which American officials sent money to rebuild Western Europe after the war. The money eventually rose to more than $12 billion. Diplomats of twelve countries signed the treaty that created NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a military alliance, on April 4, 1949. The State Department's historians call it "the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered into outside of the Western Hemisphere."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 12 minutes**

Three elected governments, in Iran, Guatemala and Chile, were overthrown with secret American help in these years. For forty years after 1950, American presidents chose where aid, weapons and troops went with one rival in mind, the Soviet Union. That rivalry is called the Cold War, and American troops fought in Korea and Vietnam as part of it.

**The territories.** Alaska and Hawaii became states, and Puerto Rico got a new name but stayed a territory. On Pacific islands that American officials governed for the United Nations, with a duty to look after the people, they set off nuclear bombs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was the Cold War?" -->
The Cold War was a long rivalry between the United States and its allies on one side and the Soviet Union and its allies on the other. The Soviet Union was a large country ruled from Moscow, in Russia, from 1922 to 1991. It was a communist country, which meant that one party ruled it and the government owned the businesses. From 1947 on, American leaders' plan, which they called containment, was to block the spread of Soviet power wherever they saw a risk of it growing.

**The end.** The Berlin Wall, which had divided the city of Berlin, was opened on November 9, 1989. The Soviet Union broke apart on December 26, 1991, and the Cold War was over.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cuban Missile Crisis, October 1962" -->
American and Soviet leaders came closer to nuclear war in October 1962 than at any other time. Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev and Cuban leader Fidel Castro had secretly agreed in July 1962 to put Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. Before that, a force backed by the United States had tried and failed to overthrow Castro in the Bay of Pigs invasion.

**The pictures.** An American U-2 spy plane took pictures of missile sites being built in Cuba on October 14, 1962. On October 22 President John F. Kennedy ordered American warships to stop ships carrying weapons to Cuba. He called it a quarantine, and he told the country about it on television. American forces went on alert for war, and on October 27 a U-2 was shot down over Cuba.

**A secret promise.** Kennedy then offered to promise not to attack Cuba if the missiles were removed. His brother Robert Kennedy secretly told the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, that American missiles in Turkey would also be taken out, though not as part of any public deal. Khrushchev announced on October 28 that the Soviet missiles would leave Cuba, and the American Jupiter missiles left Turkey in April 1963.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Peace Corps, 1961" -->
President Kennedy created the Peace Corps, a program for American volunteers to serve in other countries, by an order he signed on March 1, 1961. Sargent Shriver was its first director, and the first volunteers went to Ghana, in Africa, that same year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lillian-carter-america-world" name="Lillian Carter" movie="Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother (2021)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lillian Carter

> **Who:** A nurse from Plains, Georgia, who joined the Peace Corps at 68. Mother of President Jimmy Carter.
> **When and where:** India, 1966 to 1968.
> **Movie:** *Miss Lillian: More Than a President's Mother* (2021), a documentary film about her directed by Vivian Winther.

**She joined the Peace Corps at 68 and turned 70 in India.**

Lillian Carter, a nurse, worked in India for the Peace Corps from 1966 to 1968. She told the recruiter she wanted to go "where it's warm, people have dark skins and need a nurse's service."

She was sent to the Godrej Colony, about 30 miles from Mumbai. After she came home, her son Jimmy Carter became president. She died in 1983, and in 1986 Peace Corps officials created the Lillian Carter Award for volunteers who serve at the age of 50 or older.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lillian-carter-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Governments overthrown with American help" -->
American officials secretly helped overthrow elected governments in Iran, Guatemala and Chile, in coups, sudden takeovers of a government by a small group, often the army.

**Iran, 1953.** Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh had taken control of Iran's oil industry away from British companies. In June 1953 President Dwight Eisenhower approved a secret American and British plan to overthrow him. Officers of the Central Intelligence Agency, or CIA, the American government's spy service, carried it out. The CIA's director, Allen Dulles, oversaw the planning, and Kermit Roosevelt, a CIA officer, ran the plan in Iran. CIA officers paid journalists, religious leaders and politicians to attack Mosaddegh as corrupt and power-hungry.

General Fazlollah Zahedi replaced Mosaddegh on August 19, 1953, and the Shah, Iran's king, took back power. The Shah had Mosaddegh convicted of treason. Mosaddegh served three years in prison and lived the rest of his life under house arrest, and officials of the Shah's government put to death one of his advisers and more than a dozen military officers and student leaders on his side. In 2013 CIA officials released the agency's own history, which states that the agency "planned and helped implement the coup."

**Guatemala, 1954.** President Jacobo Árbenz began a land reform, a plan to share out farmland, with a law called Decree 900 on June 27, 1952. Under it his officials took land from owners and gave about 1.5 million acres to about 100,000 families, and the owners were paid more than $8 million in all. The United Fruit Company was one of the landowners. Árbenz's officials offered United Fruit $2.99 an acre for its land, and State Department officials demanded $75 an acre.

Eisenhower approved a CIA operation to overthrow Árbenz in August 1953. CIA officers ran what they called "psychological warfare and political action" against Árbenz's government, using fear, rumors and false stories to turn people against their leaders. The officers also drew up lists of Guatemalan officials to be killed, and one list had 58 names.

A force under Carlos Castillo Armas invaded from Honduras on June 18, 1954, after planes had harassed the capital, and on June 27 Árbenz resigned. Castillo Armas became president, and his officials gave the land back to the companies and broke up the labor unions. Human rights groups estimate that between 1954 and 1990, the forces of Guatemala's military governments murdered more than 100,000 civilians.

**Chile, 1970 to 1973.** Salvador Allende was elected president of Chile in September 1970. On September 15, President Richard Nixon met CIA Director Richard Helms and ordered him to stop Allende. Helms wrote down Nixon's words in his notes, among them "make the economy scream" and "$10,000,000 available, more if necessary."

CIA officers then ran a plan they called Track II, whose stated aim was "a firm and continuing policy that Allende be overthrown by a coup." They gave weapons to men who planned to kidnap the head of Chile's army, General René Schneider. A different group of plotters killed Schneider on October 22, 1970.

General Augusto Pinochet led the army coup that overthrew Allende on September 11, 1973. An American military attaché, an officer posted to the American embassy, called the coup "close to perfect." Pinochet ruled Chile until 1990.

Chile's official commissions count 3,216 people whom Pinochet's forces killed or "disappeared" between 1973 and 1990. The disappeared were people his forces took who were never seen again. The commissions also count 38,254 people who survived political imprisonment, torture or both.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two new states and a ballot with two answers, 1959" -->
Alaska became the 49th state on January 3, 1959, and Hawaii became the 50th on August 21, 1959. Hawaii's voters could only say Yes or No to becoming a state right away. Their ballot of June 27, 1959, asked: "Shall Hawaii immediately be admitted into the Union as a State?"

**Not on the ballot.** Independence was not one of the choices. Members of the United Nations General Assembly had passed Resolution 742 in 1953, which stated that votes like this one should offer a free choice among several futures, including independence. A large majority of Hawaii's voters chose statehood. Hawaii and Alaska were then taken off the United Nations list of territories that did not govern themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Puerto Rico: a new name, and armed revolt, 1950 to 1954" -->
Puerto Ricans got the right to write their own constitution in 1950, but the island stayed a territory under the Insular Cases. Members of Congress passed Public Law 600 that year, and Puerto Rico's voters approved the law in 1951 and the constitution in 1952.

**A new name.** Governor Luis Muñoz Marín proclaimed the Commonwealth, the *Estado Libre Asociado*, on July 25, 1952, the anniversary of the American landing at Guánica in 1898. Under that new name, the island was still a territory of the United States. Members of the United Nations General Assembly voted on November 27, 1953, to take Puerto Rico off the list of territories that did not govern themselves, and American officials then stopped sending the United Nations reports about the island.

**The revolt of 1950.** Some Puerto Ricans fought the new arrangement with guns. Nationalists rose up in several towns on October 30, 1950, and *Time* magazine reported that about 70 of them took Jayuya and killed four policemen there. Soldiers and pilots of the Puerto Rico National Guard, under Major General Luis R. Esteves, attacked the towns of Jayuya and Utuado with P-47 Thunderbolt warplanes, artillery, mortars and grenades. Pilots in American military aircraft fired on and bombed towns full of American citizens.

Governor Muñoz Marín counted 31 Puerto Ricans dead across the island in the revolt, *Time* reported. *Time*'s report does not state how many people the Guard killed in Jayuya and Utuado.

**Blair House.** Two Nationalists, Griselio Torresola and Oscar Collazo, attacked Blair House in Washington, where President Harry Truman was living, on November 1, 1950. Torresola shot a White House guard, Leslie Coffelt, three times. Coffelt was dying, but he got up and shot Torresola dead, and then Coffelt died of his wounds. Collazo was sentenced to death, and in 1952 Truman changed the sentence to life in prison.

**The House, 1954.** Four Puerto Rican Nationalists went into the visitors' gallery of the House of Representatives on March 1, 1954: Lolita Lebrón, Rafael Cancel Miranda, Andrés Figueroa Cordero and Irvin Flores Rodríguez. They unrolled a Puerto Rican flag and fired down onto the floor, wounding five members of the House, and all five recovered. All four Nationalists were convicted and sent to prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pacific: a trust, nuclear tests and one vote to join" -->
After the Second World War, members of the United Nations placed more than 2,000 Pacific islands under American rule: the Marianas, the Carolines and the Marshalls. American officials governed them for the United Nations from 1947 to 1986, with a duty to look after the people. The arrangement was called the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands.

American officials set off 67 nuclear tests between 1946 and 1958 at Bikini and Enewetak, two atolls, rings of small coral islands around a lagoon, in the Marshall Islands. They moved the people off those atolls before the tests.

**Castle Bravo.** The largest test was Castle Bravo, on March 1, 1954. It was two and a half times as powerful as its planners expected, and its fallout, the radioactive dust from the blast, came down on islands where people lived. All 64 people on Rongelap, about 110 miles away, took in radiation from it. American officials moved the people of Rongelap and Utirik away about three days after the blast, and Rongelap could no longer be lived on. Chapter 22, War and the Military, tells what the fallout did to them and to a Japanese fishing crew.

**The study.** Officials of the Atomic Energy Commission, the government agency in charge of nuclear weapons, then ran a study of the exposed people, called Project 4.1. The people in the study lived on islands that the United States governed for the United Nations, with a duty to look after them. Radioactive iodine from fallout collects in the thyroid, a gland in the neck, and in later years thyroid disease, including thyroid cancer, was the most common lasting harm among the people exposed.

**One vote to join.** The people of the Northern Mariana Islands voted to join the United States. Of today's five territories with people living in them, it is the only one whose people voted to come in. A Covenant, a formal agreement, between the islands and the United States was signed on February 15, 1975, and 78.8 percent of the people who voted approved it on June 17, 1975. The Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands began in 1978, and its people became American citizens in 1986.

The Marshall Islands and the Federated States of Micronesia became independent countries in 1986, tied to the United States by agreements called Compacts of Free Association. Palau's compact took effect in 1994.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Washington, D.C., and the vote" -->
Washington, D.C. is not a state either. Until 1961 the people living there could not vote for president at all, just as the people of the territories still cannot. That year lawmakers in enough states ratified, or approved, the Twenty-third Amendment to the Constitution, and it took effect on March 29, 1961.

The city got back a delegate in the House in 1971, one who can speak there but cannot vote on bills. Under the Home Rule Act of 1973, its people elected their own mayor from 1975. Members of Congress passed an amendment in 1978 to give the city full seats in Congress, but lawmakers in too few states approved it, and it ran out of time in 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panama Canal and an apology to Native Hawaiians" -->
American and Panamanian leaders signed the Torrijos-Carter Treaties on September 7, 1977. Under them, American officials handed the Panama Canal to Panama on December 31, 1999.

**An apology.** Members of Congress passed, and President Bill Clinton signed, the Apology Resolution, Public Law 103-150, in 1993. It is about the overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii a hundred years earlier. On January 17, 1893, a group of mostly American businessmen had overthrown Queen Liliʻuokalani, and John L. Stevens, the top American diplomat in Hawaii, had landed 162 American sailors and Marines to back them.

The resolution states that Stevens "conspired," or plotted in secret, with "a small group of non-Hawaiian residents of the Kingdom of Hawaii, including citizens of the United States." It names the men who took power as a Committee of Safety that "represented the American and European sugar planters, descendents of missionaries, and financiers."

The resolution states that the overthrow was illegal. In it, members of Congress apologized to Native Hawaiians "on behalf of the people of the United States" for the overthrow, carried out "with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States." It also states that the Native Hawaiian people never directly gave up their claims to sovereignty, the right to govern themselves. Its last section states that it does not settle any claims against the United States.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 8 minutes**

More than 3.6 million people live in the five American territories with people on them, and none of them can vote for president while living at home. They still live under the Insular Cases. Since 2001 American leaders have sent troops to wars in Afghanistan, Iraq and, in 2026, Iran, and they have argued over trade and over the Paris agreement on climate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="September 11, 2001, and the wars after it" -->
Nineteen hijackers attacked the United States on September 11, 2001, and killed 2,977 people, not counting the hijackers. The next day, representatives of NATO's member countries called on Article 5 of their treaty, the promise that an attack on one member is an attack on all of them, for the first and only time.

American forces went into Afghanistan on October 7, 2001, and the last American troops left on August 30, 2021. American forces invaded Iraq on March 20, 2003. American leaders said Iraq had weapons of mass destruction, meaning nuclear, chemical or biological weapons. None were found.

**The dead.** Chapter 22, War and the Military, gives the counts of the people killed in these wars.

**Disputed:** For Iraq, counts built from deaths recorded one by one come out much lower than estimates built from surveys of households. Workers at Iraq Body Count, a group that records deaths one at a time, had counted more than 187,000 civilians killed from 2003 to 2024.

A survey published in the medical journal *The Lancet* in 2006 estimated about 600,000 violent deaths in Iraq in just the first three years of the war, and American and Iraqi officials disputed it at the time. A survey by the World Health Organization and Iraq's health ministry estimated about 151,000 violent deaths over the same three years.

**Guantánamo.** American officials began holding prisoners at the navy base at Guantánamo Bay, in Cuba, on January 11, 2002. Most of them were never tried in court. According to the journalist Andy Worthington, who tracks the prison, 779 men had been held there by January 2025, and 15 were still held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade and the climate agreement" -->
Members of the World Trade Organization, a group of countries that agree on rules for trade between them, approved China's entry on November 10, 2001, and China joined at the end of that year. A new agreement among the United States, Mexico and Canada took effect on July 1, 2020, according to the Office of the United States Trade Representative. It replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement, called NAFTA.

The United States has joined and left the Paris climate agreement twice. The governments that join it promise to cut the gases that are warming the earth. American presidents took the country out of it in November 2020 and brought it back in February 2021. President Donald Trump signed an order to leave again on January 20, 2025, and researchers at the Congressional Research Service, which studies issues for Congress, report that the United States was out of the agreement as of January 27, 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Strikes at sea, Venezuela and Iran, 2025 and 2026" -->
**Strikes at sea.** From September 2, 2025, American forces fired on boats in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific Ocean that President Trump's officials said were carrying drugs. By September 19, 2026, they had killed at least 231 people, the Associated Press reported. Chapter 22, War and the Military, tells the strikes in full, with the families who went to court over two of the dead.

**Venezuela.** American troops captured Venezuela's president, Nicolás Maduro, and his wife in Caracas, Venezuela's capital, on January 3, 2026, and took them to New York to face charges tied to drugs. According to the Brookings Institution, a research group, the raid went ahead without approval from Congress beforehand.

**Iran.** American and Israeli forces began a war with Iran by bombing it on February 28, 2026. Workers at the Human Rights Activists News Agency, a rights group that tracks Iran, counted 3,636 people killed in Iran by April 7, 2026, and 1,701 of them were civilians. Chapter 22 gives the count of American service members killed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The territories today" -->
The 2020 census, the government's count of everyone who lives in the country and its territories, recorded more than 3.6 million people in the five American territories with people on them. Census Bureau workers counted 3,285,874 people in Puerto Rico, 11.8 percent fewer than in 2010.

They counted 153,836 in Guam, about 87,146 in the U.S. Virgin Islands, 49,710 in American Samoa and 47,329 in the Northern Mariana Islands. Every one of the five lost people between 2010 and 2020.

**Citizens and nationals.** People born in Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands and the Northern Mariana Islands are American citizens. People born in American Samoa are American nationals. Their passports say so, and to vote they must go through naturalization, the process a person from another country uses to become a citizen.

No one living in a territory can vote for president. Voters in each territory elect one delegate to the House of Representatives, who can speak and work on committees but cannot vote on whether a bill passes. Puerto Rico's delegate is called the Resident Commissioner.

**La junta.** Puerto Rico's government owed more than $72 billion by 2016, and more than $55 billion in pensions, the money its officials had promised to pay its workers after they retired. Puerto Rico's leaders had no legal way to change the terms of the debt. Members of Congress passed a law called PROMESA on June 30, 2016. Under it, a board of seven members, appointed in Washington, has power over Puerto Rico's budget, and Puerto Ricans call it *la junta*.

The people whose budget the board controls did not elect its members. By the board's own account, its members have changed the terms of about 80 percent of the debt, cutting it from more than $70 billion to about $37 billion.

President Trump fired several members of the board on August 5, 2025, and CBS News named five of them. Under PROMESA a president may remove a member "only for cause," which means for a real reason such as wrongdoing.

Three of the fired members, Arthur J. González, Andrew G. Biggs and Betty A. Rosa, went to court. Judge María Antongiorgi-Jordán of the federal court in Puerto Rico put them back on the board on October 3, 2025. In 2026 she made that order permanent and called the firings "plainly contrary to law," according to the Democracy Defenders Fund, a group that campaigns on political causes.

**The status votes.** Puerto Ricans have voted on their island's status several times, and members of Congress have not acted on any of the results. Statehood won 52.52 percent in 2020. On November 5, 2024, statehood won 58.61 percent, free association 29.57 percent and independence 11.82 percent, according to the island's certified results. Free association would make the island a separate country tied to the United States by a formal agreement, as the Marshall Islands are.

Some voters stayed away from these votes in protest. Members of Congress do not have to follow the results, and only Congress can change Puerto Rico's status.

**Washington, D.C.** The capital had about 690,000 people in the 2020 census, more than the state of Wyoming, which had 576,851, or Vermont. It has one delegate in the House who cannot vote on bills, and no senators. Members of the House of Representatives voted 216 to 208 to make it a state on April 22, 2021, but senators did not take up the bill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### John Fitisemanu

> **Who:** A man born in American Samoa and living in Utah who sued to be counted an American citizen from birth.
> **When and where:** Utah. His case was decided by a federal appeals court in 2021.

**Fitisemanu sued for citizenship, and his home territory's leaders opposed him.**

Because he was born in American Samoa, Fitisemanu is an American national, not a citizen, and a national must become a citizen before he can vote. He sued the United States to be recognized as a citizen by birth, and a federal judge in Utah agreed with him.

**The appeal.** In 2021 judges of a higher federal court, the Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, overturned that decision, holding that the Insular Cases were still the law. On October 17, 2022, the justices of the Supreme Court refused to take his case.

American Samoa's own elected leaders opposed his lawsuit. They argued that citizenship ordered by a court could put at legal risk *faʻa Sāmoa*, the Samoan way of life, which includes land held in common by families and the matai system of chiefs. Fitisemanu wants to be a citizen. The leaders of his home territory did not want citizenship granted to Samoans that way.

### José Luis Vaello Madero

> **Who:** A man the United States government sued for $28,081 after he moved to Puerto Rico.
> **When and where:** New York, then Puerto Rico. His case was decided by the Supreme Court on April 21, 2022.

**Moving to Puerto Rico ended his right to a federal payment.**

While Vaello Madero lived in New York, he lawfully received Supplemental Security Income, a federal payment for people with very low incomes who are elderly, blind or disabled. Members of Congress have not made that benefit available to people living in Puerto Rico. After he moved, the payments kept coming, and he did not know he was no longer allowed to get them. Lawyers for the United States government sued him to get back $28,081.

**At the Supreme Court.** His case reached the justices, and on April 21, 2022, they ruled 8 to 1 that the Constitution does not require Congress to pay that benefit to people in Puerto Rico. Justice Neil Gorsuch agreed with the result, but he also wrote a separate statement of his own, and in it he called for the Insular Cases to be overturned.

**The three questions, answered**
- Who got to choose when this land and its islands changed hands? Almost never the people who lived there: no Native nation was at Paris in 1783, and no one from Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines was at Paris in 1898. Of today's five territories, only the Northern Mariana Islands voted to join, and Hawaii's voters chose statehood in 1959 from a ballot that left out independence.
- How did a weak new country come to rule islands and overthrow governments? First it grew: by 1848 it reached the Pacific, and by 1900 its mills made more steel than Britain's and Germany's together. Then its leaders used force. Marines helped overthrow Hawaii's queen in 1893, American leaders took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines after a war with Spain in 1898, and after 1950 CIA officers secretly helped overthrow governments in Iran, Guatemala and Chile.
- Are the people of the territories American citizens? Most are, but people born in American Samoa are nationals, not citizens, and no one living in a territory can vote for president.

**What changed:** The chapter begins with rulers dividing land they had never seen and ends with a country that began as colonies holding five island territories whose people cannot vote for president.

**Five dates to keep**
- June 7, 1494: The rulers of Spain and Portugal split the lands across the Atlantic.
- September 3, 1783: British diplomats accept American independence in Paris.
- February 2, 1848: Mexican officials sign over the Southwest.
- December 10, 1898: Spanish diplomats sign over Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines.
- May 27, 1901: The justices rule that Puerto Rico belongs to the United States without being part of it.

**Not known:** No one has worked out how many people died at Kake after the crew of the USS *Saginaw* burned the villages in 1869.

**Where to go:** ʻIolani Palace, in Honolulu, where Queen Liliʻuokalani was tried and held prisoner in 1895.
