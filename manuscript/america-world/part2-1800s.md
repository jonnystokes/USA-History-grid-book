## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850 American leaders pushed the country's borders from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Ocean. American leaders bought land from France, took Florida from Spain, split the Oregon country with Britain and took the Southwest from Mexico in a war. Native nations lived across all that land, and none of them signed the sales and treaties. In the same years American sailors fought wars against rulers on the coast of North Africa. In 1823 President James Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in the Americas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tribute and the Barbary wars, 1801 to 1815" -->
American leaders had been paying tribute to the rulers of the Barbary states, four states on the coast of North Africa: Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli and Morocco. Tribute here means protection money. American officials paid the rulers so that the rulers' ships would not seize American ships. The crews of seized ships could be held for ransom, a price paid to free a prisoner.

When American officials would not raise the payments, the pasha of Tripoli declared war on the United States on May 14, 1801. Pasha was the title of Tripoli's ruler. The fighting ended with a treaty signed on June 10, 1805. A treaty is a written agreement between countries. Under this one, American officials paid no more yearly tribute to Tripoli. They did pay a ransom to free the crew of the *Philadelphia*, an American warship that Tripoli's forces had captured. A second Barbary war followed in 1815.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
On April 30, 1803, American envoys signed a treaty to buy about 828,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi from France. An envoy is a person sent to bargain for a government. The price was $15 million, and the purchase roughly doubled the size of the United States.

The envoys had been sent to buy only the port of New Orleans. French officials offered them all of Louisiana instead. The people enslaved in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, now called Haiti, had risen against French rule. Napoleon, the ruler of France, had sent an army to put them down. The attempt failed, and he lost the army he sent. With a war against Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to raise money and to keep the British from taking it.

Article III of the treaty states that the people living in the territory would be "incorporated in the Union of the United States." It also states that they would be admitted to "all the rights, advantages and immunities of citizens of the United States." To be incorporated in the Union meant to be made part of the country. Immunities here are the protections the law gives every citizen. So in 1803, land the United States bought came with a written promise that its people would become full citizens. A century later, judges on the Supreme Court ruled that no such promise covered Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines.

Native nations lived across those 828,000 square miles, but they were not part of the sale and no one asked them. The promise of citizenship in Article III did not include them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Florida, 1818 and 1819" -->
In 1818 General Andrew Jackson led American troops into Spanish Florida. They attacked the Seminole people and people who had escaped slavery. American officials who approved the raid saw these people as a threat to Georgia. Jackson's troops seized the Spanish forts at Pensacola and St. Marks. Jackson had two British citizens put to death, on charges of stirring up the Seminoles and the escaped slaves.

President James Monroe and his advisers seriously thought about speaking out against Jackson. The Secretary of State, John Quincy Adams, defended him. The Secretary of State is the official who runs the country's dealings with other countries. Adams then used Jackson's raid to put a choice to Spain: control the people of East Florida, or hand it over to the United States.

In 1819 Adams and the Spanish minister, a diplomat named Luis de Onís, signed the Adams-Onís Treaty. Under it, Spain's leaders handed East Florida to the United States and gave up their claim to West Florida. Spanish officials received no payment. Instead, American officials agreed to take on the bill for $5 million in damage that American citizens had done when they rebelled against Spanish rule. The treaty also fixed the western edge of the Louisiana Purchase. Spain's leaders gave up their claims to the Pacific Northwest, and American officials accepted Spanish rule over Texas. Leaders in both countries ratified the treaty in 1821. To ratify is to give a treaty final approval.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" name="John Quincy Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Quincy Adams

> **Who:** Secretary of State under President James Monroe, 1817 to 1825.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C.

John Quincy Adams got Florida from Spain for the United States, and the State Department's historians credit him with writing the statement now called the Monroe Doctrine. He was Secretary of State from 1817 to 1825. The Secretary of State is the official who runs the country's dealings with other countries.

Adams negotiated the 1819 treaty with Spain's minister, Luis de Onís, and it carries both their names. Before that, he used Jackson's raid to press Spain to give up Florida.

In that statement, President Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in North or South America. Monroe had thought of making the statement jointly with Britain. Adams talked him out of it, so the United States made the statement alone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Monroe Doctrine, 1823" -->
On December 2, 1823, President James Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in North or South America. He said it in his yearly message to Congress. His words were that "the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers." Henceforth means from now on. Colonization means starting colonies.

The State Department's historians credit John Quincy Adams with writing it. In 1823 the United States Navy was too small to enforce the warning. British leaders also wanted other European powers kept out of Latin America. Under Spain's colonial trade rules, Spanish officials had blocked British merchants from trading there, and British leaders wanted the new Latin American countries open to British trade. British warships kept those powers' fleets away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Texas, Oregon and the land taken from Mexico, 1845 to 1848" -->
In the 1840s American leaders took Texas and the Southwest, and they split the Oregon country with Britain. On March 1, 1845, members of Congress passed a joint resolution, a measure voted on by both the House and the Senate, to annex Texas, and Texas joined the United States on December 29. To annex a place is to take it and make it part of a country. The Oregon country stretched from the Pacific coast to the Rocky Mountains. It covered what is now Oregon, Washington and most of the Canadian province of British Columbia. On June 15, 1846, American and British negotiators set the border there at the 49th parallel. Negotiators are officials sent to bargain. The 49th parallel is an east-west line on the map. The United States got the part of the Oregon country south of that line. The two sides settled it by treaty, without a war.

The land in the Southwest came from a war with Mexico, fought from 1846 to 1848. On February 2, 1848, American and Mexican negotiators signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. They signed it at Guadalupe Hidalgo, a city north of Mexico City where Mexico's leaders had fled. Mexican officials, whose army had lost the war, signed over more than 525,000 square miles. American officials agreed to pay $15,000,000. The land became all or part of California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming.

Between about 80,000 and 100,000 Mexican citizens lived on that land, depending on the count. Without moving, they found themselves living in the United States. As signed, Article IX of the treaty said that Mexicans who became American citizens would keep "the free enjoyment of their liberty and property." They would also keep the free practice of their religion. Senators then rewrote Article IX and cut its last two paragraphs.

When senators voted to approve the treaty on March 10, 1848, they struck out Article X completely. Article X was the part that kept Spanish and Mexican land grants valid under American law. A land grant was a legal title to land given by Spanish or Mexican officials. American negotiators then wrote a side agreement, the Protocol of Querétaro. In it they told Mexican officials that deleting Article X had not canceled those land titles. American judges did not treat the Protocol as equal to the treaty.

In 1851 a new law made Mexican landowners in California prove their deeds before officials of the United States government. A deed is the paper showing that someone owns land. The cases took an average of 17 years. Many families sold most of their land during those years to pay English-speaking lawyers and land taxes. The Peralta family of San José and the East Bay kept only 700 of their 49,000 acres.

English-speaking American squatters, newcomers who settled on land that was not theirs, took more. In some cases, as with the Suñol and Berryessa families, squatters lynched the Mexican landowners, killing them without any trial, and stayed on the land. Many Mexican American families lost their land or fell into debt.

In 1850 California's new lawmakers passed the Foreign Miners' Tax, $20 a month for each miner born outside the country. The lawmakers aimed it at Chinese and Latino miners, and officials collected it mostly from them. Thousands of foreign-born miners left the mining towns. Lawmakers ended the tax the next year. The historians William Carrigan and Clive Webb counted at least 597 Mexicans whom mobs in the United States lynched between 1848 and 1928.

In northern New Mexico and southern Colorado, some land grants belonged to whole villages together. The families of a village shared its common land, including forest on the nearby mountains. In the late 1800s American officials and judges broke up these shared grants, and the villages lost most of their common land. From 1891 a special Court of Private Land Claims judged these grants. Its judges, and United States land officials before them, often recognized a grant only up to the foot of the mountains. They left out the forests. In 1897 the judges of the Supreme Court decided a case called *United States v. Sandoval*. They agreed with government lawyers that a village's common lands belonged to the United States government. Seven grants lost between 95 and 99 percent of their land under that ruling. Other villages lost their common land in lawsuits that split it into private pieces, or because they could not pay the taxes on it.

Article XI of the treaty describes the Native nations who lived on the land as "savage tribes," an insulting name. It states that "a great part" of the land was "now occupied" by them. Its words are that they "will hereafter be under the exclusive control of the Government of the United States." The article also states that the United States government would "forcibly restrain" their raids into Mexico. Among the nations on that land were the Tohono O'odham. No Native nation signed the treaty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-trist-america-world" name="Nicholas Trist" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Trist

> **Who:** The American diplomat who made peace with Mexico after President James K. Polk ordered him home.
> **When and where:** Mexico, 1847 and 1848.

Nicholas Trist signed the treaty that ended the war with Mexico after the president had already ordered him to come home. President James K. Polk sent Trist to Mexico to bargain for an end to the war. Then Polk recalled him in October 1847.

Trist ignored the order. He kept bargaining with no authority to do so. In February 1848 he signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

Polk sent the treaty to the Senate anyway, because it gave him what he wanted, including California. Then he fired Trist. Polk refused to pay Trist's expenses or his salary for his last months of work.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the second half of the 1800s, American leaders began taking lands across the oceans and keeping them. Americans claimed small islands for their fertilizer, and American officials bought Alaska from Russia. In Hawaii, a group of mostly American businessmen overthrew the queen with the help of American Marines. More than half of the Native Hawaiian people signed a petition against annexation. A petition is a paper that many people sign to ask leaders for something or to object to something. In 1898 members of Congress annexed the islands. That same year, after a war with Spain, American leaders took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. No one asked the people who lived in these places.

These places became territories of the United States. A territory is land that belongs to the United States but is not a state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Perry's warships in Japan, 1853 and 1854" -->
Commodore Matthew Perry opened two Japanese ports to American ships by bringing warships to Japan. A commodore is a navy officer in charge of a group of warships. In July 1853 Perry sailed a group of American warships into Edo Bay, the bay beside the city of Edo, now called Tokyo. He came back in 1854. On March 31, 1854, Japanese and American officials signed the Convention of Kanagawa, a written agreement that opened the two ports to American ships. The Japanese officials signed it under the threat of Perry's warships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Islands claimed for bird droppings, 1856" -->
Americans first claimed land overseas for the United States in 1856, forty-two years before the war with Spain. The reason was fertilizer. Guano, the droppings of seabirds, makes crops grow.

On August 18, 1856, members of Congress passed the Guano Islands Act. Under it, an American citizen who found an island with guano on it could take the island for the United States, unless another country already claimed it. Americans claimed about a hundred islands under this law. In 1890, in a case called *Jones v. United States*, the judges of the Supreme Court ruled that the law was valid. Lawmakers have never ended it, and it is still part of United States law today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alaska, 1867" -->
In 1867 American officials bought Alaska from Russia. The diplomats who wrote the treaty left Alaska's Native peoples out of American citizenship by name. American and Russian diplomats signed the treaty on March 30, 1867. The price was $7,200,000. On October 18, 1867, at a ceremony on Castle Hill in Sitka, Russian officials formally handed over the territory. Then the United States flag went up.

Russian officials sold land that they did not really control. Alaska Native nations lived on it, and they had no part in the sale. Castle Hill itself was Tlingit land. In 1804 Alexander Baranov, the first Russian governor, had attacked the hill to take it from the Kiksadi clan of the Tlingit people. After a six-day battle, the Russians seized the Kiksadi fort and burned it. In 1867 a Tlingit village still stood beside the Russian town of Sitka.

Article III of the treaty states who would become an American citizen. People living in Alaska who stayed would be admitted to "the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages, and immunities of citizens of the United States." The article then leaves out "uncivilized native tribes," an insulting name for Alaska's Native peoples. They would instead be "subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt."

Alaska had no civil government, one run by elected or appointed civilians, until 1884. For 17 years American officers ruled it, first from the Army, then from the Treasury Department, then from the Navy. The historian Rebecca Poulson, writing for the Alaska Historical Society, says that American officials considered Native people "savages," an insulting word, and did not allow them to be citizens.

Under Russian rule, officials of the Russian company that ran Alaska had paid compensation when a Tlingit person was killed. Compensation is a payment to the dead person's family. Under Tlingit law, a death was settled with a payment, not with punishment. American officers refused to pay. In early 1869 an army guard at Sitka killed two men from the village of Kake. Poulson calls the deaths accidental. Army officers at Sitka refused to pay compensation for their deaths. Relatives of the dead men then killed two white men.

General Jefferson C. Davis ordered the American warship USS *Saginaw* to attack Kake. It was winter. The crew fired exploding shells from the ship's guns at the Kake villages. They burned 28 of the villages' 29 clan houses, the houses of the Kake clans, which are large family groups. They also burned the villagers' canoes, totem poles and stored food. In 2024 reporters at KTOO, a public radio station in Juneau, wrote that the number of deaths that followed has not been worked out. Kake oral history, the accounts the Tlingit of Kake passed down by word of mouth, records many, especially among elders and children.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1887 to 1893" -->
In January 1893 a group of mostly American businessmen overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani of Hawaii, and American Marines and sailors helped them. Two of their leaders had already used armed men to force a Hawaiian king to do what they wanted. On June 30, 1887, a group of haole lawyers and sugar planters forced King Kalākaua to sign a new constitution. Haole is the Hawaiian word for white people who are not Native Hawaiian. An armed militia, a force of armed citizens, backed them. Their leaders were two lawyers, Sanford B. Dole and Lorrin Thurston. The document became known as the Bayonet Constitution. A bayonet is a blade fixed to the end of a rifle.

That month members of the Annexation Club, Thurston among them, contacted John L. Stevens. He was the United States Minister to Hawaii, the top American diplomat there. The club's men wanted to know whether the Americans would back them. On January 15, 1893, the group, calling itself the Committee of Safety, began to move against the queen. On January 17 Stevens had 162 American sailors and Marines landed from the warship USS *Boston*. The queen's government had several hundred soldiers and police, and they did not fight. Dole became president of the new provisional government, a government set up for a short time until a lasting one is formed.

Queen Liliʻuokalani gave up her throne under protest. She addressed her protest to the United States, not to the men who had taken power. She wrote that she yielded "to the superior force of the United States of America, whose Minister Plenipotentiary, His Excellency John L. Stevens, has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu." She meant that Stevens, the top American diplomat in Hawaii, had brought American troops ashore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two American reports that disagree, 1893 and 1894" -->
Two American investigations of the overthrow reached opposite findings. President Grover Cleveland sent James H. Blount to Hawaii to find out what had happened. Blount reported that Stevens had landed the Marines on a false excuse to help the plotters. On December 18, 1893, Cleveland sent Congress a message about it. He said the "military demonstration on the soil of Honolulu was of itself an act of war." A military demonstration is a show of armed force. He also said that "the provisional government owes its existence to an armed invasion by the United States." Albert S. Willis, the new American minister in Hawaii, apologized for "the reprehensible conduct of the American minister and the unauthorized presence on land of a military force." He meant Stevens. Reprehensible means deserving blame.

Cleveland could not get the queen put back on her throne. In 1894 members of a Senate committee made their own investigation, called the Morgan Report, and they cleared everyone involved. On July 4, 1894, the men who had taken power declared the Republic of Hawaii.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" name="Queen Liliʻuokalani" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Queen Liliʻuokalani

> **Who:** The last queen of the Kingdom of Hawaii, and the writer of the song "Aloha ʻOe."
> **When and where:** Honolulu. Overthrown January 17, 1893. Held prisoner in ʻIolani Palace in 1895.

Queen Liliʻuokalani lost her throne to an armed takeover backed by American troops. Two years later officials of the Republic of Hawaii put her on trial in her own palace. On January 17, 1893, she yielded under protest. She asked American leaders to undo what their own minister had done. President Cleveland agreed that Stevens had acted wrongly, but he could not get her put back on the throne.

In January 1895 Hawaiians loyal to the queen, among them Robert Wilcox and Prince Kūhiō, tried to restore her by force. Within 36 hours officials of the Republic of Hawaii had caught the rebels and jailed them. The Republic's officials then arrested the queen. On January 24, 1895, they forced her to sign a paper giving up her throne for good.

They tried her before a military court in the throne room of ʻIolani Palace, where she had ruled. The charge was misprision of treason, which means knowing about a plot against the government and not reporting it. The officers who sat on the court found her guilty, fined her $5,000, and sentenced her to five years in prison at hard labor, which means forced heavy work. The Republic's officials later reduced the sentence. For nearly eight months they held her prisoner in an upstairs bedroom of the palace. They allowed her no visitors except one woman companion. She spent her days praying, reading, writing music, crocheting and quilting.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="21,269 names against annexation, 1897" -->
Native Hawaiians defeated a treaty to annex their islands by signing their names against it. Between September 11 and October 2, 1897, two Hawaiian groups held meetings on the five main islands. The groups were Hui Aloha ʻĀina, which had separate branches for men and for women, and Hui Kālaiʻāina. They gathered 21,269 signatures of Native Hawaiians against annexation. That year about 39,000 Native Hawaiian and part-Hawaiian people were counted, so more than half of them signed.

Four men carried the petition to Washington: James Kaulia, David Kalauokalani, John Richardson and William Auld. They arrived on December 6 and presented it to the Senate on December 9, 1897. A treaty needs the votes of two-thirds of the senators. When the four men left Washington on February 27, 1898, only 46 senators were still willing to vote for the annexation treaty, short of the two-thirds it needed. The treaty failed.

Supporters of annexation in Congress then used a method that did not need the Senate votes the Hawaiians had blocked. A joint resolution passes when more than half of the House and more than half of the Senate vote for it. Their joint resolution was called the Newlands Resolution, and under it the United States took Hawaii. Members of the House voted for it on June 15, 1898, and senators on July 6. President William McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898: Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines" -->
After a war with Spain in 1898, American officials ruled Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. On February 15, 1898, the American battleship USS *Maine* blew up in the harbor of Havana, in Cuba. In April members of Congress declared war on Spain. On June 21, 1898, Juan Marina, the Spanish governor of Guam, surrendered the island to officers of the American warship USS *Charleston*. American troops landed in Puerto Rico at Guánica on July 25.

Spanish and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Paris on December 10, 1898. Spanish diplomats gave up Cuba and signed Puerto Rico and Guam over to the United States. They sold the Philippines to the United States for $20 million.

No one from Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines took part in the talks in Paris. Several million people in those places got new rulers without being asked. Among them on Guam were the CHamoru, the island's Native people. In the Philippines, Filipinos had already declared their own independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Philippine-American War begins, 1899" -->
War between American troops and the Philippine Republic began on February 4, 1899, because American officials had decided to keep the islands. Filipinos had been fighting Spain before the Americans arrived. On June 12, 1898, they declared their independence. American officials kept the islands instead of recognizing the new republic, and the two sides went to war.

The war lasted until 1902. About 4,200 Americans and about 20,000 Filipino soldiers were killed. Counts of Filipino civilians, the people who were not soldiers, who died do not agree, and they are far apart. The State Department's historians say as many as 200,000 died "from violence, famine, and disease." Encyclopaedia Britannica gives more than 200,000. Other estimates run from 250,000 to 1,000,000, and the Philippine-American War Centennial Initiative gives 510,000. Much of the civilian toll came from hunger and from a cholera epidemic in 1902, which killed more than 200,000 people by itself. Cholera is a disease of the gut spread by dirty water, and an epidemic is an outbreak that spreads to many people at once. The epidemic was worse because the fighting had broken up daily life in the islands.

The State Department's historians also record what American troops did. In their words, "U.S. forces at times burned villages, implemented civilian reconcentration policies, and employed torture on suspected guerrillas." Reconcentration meant forcing civilians out of their homes into camps or zones that soldiers guarded. Guerrillas are fighters in small bands who hide among the people and attack by surprise. One kind of torture American soldiers used was called the water cure. To make a prisoner talk, they held his throat so that he had to swallow water until it filled his stomach. Then they forced the water out by pressing on his stomach.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" name="Emilio Aguinaldo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Aguinaldo

> **Who:** The Filipino leader who declared the Philippines independent from Spain and then led the war against the United States.
> **When and where:** Born March 22 or 23, 1869, near Cavite, on the island of Luzon. President of the Philippine Republic from January 1899. Captured at Palanan on March 23, 1901. Died February 6, 1964.

Emilio Aguinaldo led Filipino fighters against Spain, then led the Philippine Republic against the United States until American officers captured him by a trick in 1901. In 1896 he was the mayor of his town, Cavite Viejo, and a local leader of the Katipunan. The Katipunan was a secret society fighting Spanish rule.

In December 1897 Aguinaldo made a deal with the Spanish governor. He agreed to leave the islands in return for money and a promise of reforms. From Hong Kong and Singapore he made plans with American consuls and with officers of Commodore George Dewey's fleet. A consul is an official who looks after his country's trade and citizens in a foreign port. He would come back and help the Americans fight Spain. He landed in the Philippines again on May 19, 1898.

On June 12, 1898, Filipinos declared independence with Aguinaldo at the head of their government. Six months later Spanish diplomats sold the islands to the United States in the Treaty of Paris. On January 23, 1899, Filipinos proclaimed a constitution and elected Aguinaldo president of the Philippine Republic. The fighting with American troops began on February 4, 1899. From November 1899 his forces fought as guerrillas.

On March 23, 1901, General Frederick Funston captured him at Palanan, in northern Luzon. Macabebe scouts, Filipinos who served the United States Army, dressed as rebel soldiers. Funston and a few other American officers went along, pretending to be their prisoners. Fake letters sent ahead to Aguinaldo said that more of his own soldiers were coming and were bringing American prisoners to him. At Palanan the scouts opened fire on his guards, killed two and seized him.

Aguinaldo swore loyalty to the United States. On April 19, 1901, he called on his fighters to lay down their weapons and accept American rule. He lived until 1964 and saw the Philippines become independent in 1946.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samoa divided, 1899" -->
On December 2, 1899, in an agreement called the Tripartite Convention, diplomats divided the Samoan islands between the United States and Germany. Tripartite means having three sides. The three sides that signed it were the United States, Germany and Great Britain. The chiefs of the island of Tutuila signed a paper handing their island to the United States on April 17, 1900. Tui Manuʻa Elisala, the ruler of the Manuʻa islands, signed his islands over on July 16, 1904.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->
