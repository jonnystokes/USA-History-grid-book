## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 9 minutes**

With one purchase in 1803, American diplomats roughly doubled the size of the United States, and by 1848 the country's border reached the Pacific Ocean. American leaders bought land from France, took Florida from Spain, split the Oregon country with Britain and took the Southwest from Mexico in a war. Native nations lived across all that land, and none of them signed the sales and treaties.

American sailors fought two wars against rulers on the coast of North Africa in the same years. In 1823 President James Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in the Americas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tribute and the Barbary wars, 1801 to 1815" -->
American leaders had been paying tribute, protection money, to the rulers of the Barbary states, the four states on the coast of North Africa: Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli and Morocco. American officials paid the rulers so that their ships would not seize American ships and hold the crews for ransom.

When American officials would not raise the payments, the pasha, or ruler, of Tripoli declared war on the United States on May 14, 1801. The fighting ended with a treaty signed on June 10, 1805. Under it, American officials paid no more yearly tribute to Tripoli, but they did pay a ransom to free the crew of the *Philadelphia*, an American warship that Tripoli's forces had captured. A second Barbary war followed in 1815.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
American envoys, men sent to bargain for their government, signed a treaty on April 30, 1803, to buy about 828,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi from France. The price was $15 million, and the purchase roughly doubled the size of the United States.

The envoys had been sent to buy only the port of New Orleans, and French officials offered them all of Louisiana instead.

**Why Napoleon sold.** The people enslaved in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, now called Haiti, had risen against French rule. Napoleon, the ruler of France, had sent an army to put them down. The attempt failed, and he lost the army he sent. With a war against Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to raise money and to keep the British from taking it.

**A written promise.** Article III of the treaty states that the people living in the territory would be "incorporated in the Union of the United States," made part of the country. It also states that they would be admitted to "all the rights, advantages and immunities of citizens of the United States" (immunities are the protections the law gives every citizen).

So in 1803, the land the United States bought came with a written promise that its people would become full citizens. A century later, judges on the Supreme Court ruled that no such promise covered Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines.

Native nations lived across those 828,000 square miles, but they were not part of the sale and no one asked them. The promise of citizenship in Article III did not include them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How Florida changed hands, 1818 and 1819" -->
General Andrew Jackson led American troops into Spanish Florida in 1818. They attacked the Seminole people and people who had escaped slavery, whom the American officials who approved the raid saw as a threat to Georgia. Jackson's troops seized the Spanish forts at Pensacola and St. Marks, and Jackson had two British citizens put to death, on charges of stirring up the Seminoles and the escaped slaves.

President James Monroe and his advisers seriously thought about speaking out against Jackson. John Quincy Adams defended him. As Secretary of State, Adams ran the country's dealings with other countries, and he used Jackson's raid to put a choice to Spain: control the people of East Florida, or hand it over to the United States.

**The treaty.** Adams and the Spanish minister, Luis de Onís, signed the Adams-Onís Treaty in 1819. Under it, Spain's leaders handed East Florida to the United States and gave up their claim to West Florida. Spanish officials received no payment. Instead, American officials agreed to take on the bill for $5 million in damage that American citizens had done when they rebelled against Spanish rule.

The treaty also fixed the western edge of the Louisiana Purchase. Spain's leaders gave up their claims to the Pacific Northwest, and American officials accepted Spanish rule over Texas. Leaders in both countries ratified the treaty in 1821.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" name="John Quincy Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Quincy Adams

> **Who:** The Secretary of State who got Florida from Spain and wrote the warning now called the Monroe Doctrine.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1817 to 1825.

**Adams talked President Monroe out of sharing his warning with Britain.**

Monroe had thought of making his 1823 warning to Europe's rulers jointly with Britain. Adams talked him out of it, so Monroe made it alone. The State Department's historians credit Adams with writing the statement.

Adams was Secretary of State from 1817 to 1825, under President James Monroe. He had already used Jackson's raid to press Spain to give up Florida, and he negotiated the 1819 treaty with Spain's minister, Luis de Onís, which carries both their names.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Monroe Doctrine, 1823" -->
President James Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in North or South America in his yearly message to Congress, on December 2, 1823. In his words, the American continents "are henceforth," meaning from now on, "not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers."

**Who enforced it.** The United States Navy was too small to enforce the warning in 1823. British leaders wanted other European powers kept out of Latin America too. Spanish officials had blocked British merchants from trading there under Spain's colonial trade rules, and British leaders wanted the new Latin American countries open to British trade. British warships kept those powers' fleets away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Texas, Oregon and the land taken from Mexico, 1845 to 1848" -->
American leaders took Texas and the Southwest in the 1840s, and they split the Oregon country with Britain.

**Texas.** Members of the House and the Senate passed a joint resolution on March 1, 1845, to annex Texas, which means to take it and make it part of the country. Texas joined the United States on December 29.

**Oregon.** The Oregon country stretched from the Pacific coast to the Rocky Mountains, covering what is now Oregon, Washington and most of the Canadian province of British Columbia. American and British officials set the border there at the 49th parallel, an east-west line on the map, on June 15, 1846. The United States got the part south of that line, and the two sides settled it by treaty, without a war.

**Mexico.** The land in the Southwest came from a war with Mexico, fought from 1846 to 1848. American and Mexican officials signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo on February 2, 1848, at Guadalupe Hidalgo, a city north of Mexico City where Mexico's leaders had fled. Mexican officials, whose army had lost the war, signed over more than 525,000 square miles, and American officials agreed to pay $15,000,000. The land became all or part of California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming.

Between about 80,000 and 100,000 Mexican citizens lived on that land, depending on the count, and without moving they found themselves living in the United States. As signed, Article IX of the treaty said that Mexicans who became American citizens would keep "the free enjoyment of their liberty and property" and the free practice of their religion. Senators then rewrote Article IX and cut its last two paragraphs.

When senators voted to approve the treaty on March 10, 1848, they struck out Article X completely. That was the part that kept Spanish and Mexican land grants, legal titles to land given by Spanish or Mexican officials, valid under American law. American officials then wrote a side agreement, the Protocol of Querétaro, telling Mexican officials that deleting Article X had not canceled those land titles. American judges did not treat the Protocol as equal to the treaty.

Under a new law of 1851, Mexican landowners in California had to prove their deeds, the papers showing they owned their land, before officials of the United States government. The cases took an average of 17 years. Many families sold most of their land during those years to pay English-speaking lawyers and land taxes. The Peralta family of San José and the East Bay kept only 700 of their 49,000 acres.

English-speaking American squatters, newcomers who settled on land that was not theirs, took more. In some cases, as with the Suñol and Berryessa families, squatters lynched the Mexican landowners, killing them without any trial, and stayed on the land. Many Mexican American families lost their land or fell into debt. The historians William Carrigan and Clive Webb counted at least 597 Mexicans whom mobs in the United States lynched between 1848 and 1928.

**The miners' tax.** California's new lawmakers passed the Foreign Miners' Tax in 1850, $20 a month for each miner born outside the country. They aimed it at Chinese and Latino miners, and officials collected it mostly from them. Thousands of foreign-born miners left the mining towns, and lawmakers ended the tax the next year.

**Village land.** In northern New Mexico and southern Colorado, some land grants belonged to whole villages together. The families of a village shared its common land, including forest on the nearby mountains. In the late 1800s American officials and judges broke up these shared grants, and the villages lost most of their common land.

A special Court of Private Land Claims judged these grants from 1891. Its judges, and United States land officials before them, often recognized a grant only up to the foot of the mountains, leaving out the forests.

The judges of the Supreme Court decided a case called *United States v. Sandoval* in 1897. They agreed with government lawyers that a village's common lands belonged to the United States government, and seven grants lost between 95 and 99 percent of their land under that ruling. Other villages lost their common land in lawsuits that split it into private pieces, or because they could not pay the taxes on it.

Article XI of the treaty describes the Native nations who lived on the land as "savage tribes," an insulting name. It states that "a great part" of the land was "now occupied" by them, and that from then on they would "be under the exclusive control of the Government of the United States." The article also states that the United States government would "forcibly restrain" their raids into Mexico. Among the nations on that land were the Tohono O'odham. No Native nation signed the treaty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-trist-america-world" name="Nicholas Trist" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Trist

> **Who:** The American diplomat who made peace with Mexico after President James K. Polk ordered him home.
> **When and where:** Mexico, 1847 and 1848.

**Polk fired the diplomat who made his peace with Mexico.**

President James K. Polk sent Trist to Mexico to bargain for an end to the war, then recalled him in October 1847. Trist ignored the order and kept bargaining with no authority to do so. In February 1848 he signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

Polk sent the treaty to the Senate anyway, because under it he got what he wanted, including California. Then he fired Trist, and he refused to pay Trist's expenses or his salary for his last months of work.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 13 minutes**

Americans claimed their first land overseas in 1856, small islands covered in seabird droppings, which make crops grow. American officials bought Alaska from Russia in 1867, and in 1898 they took Hawaii and, after a war with Spain, Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. No one asked the people who lived in these places. They became territories, lands that belonged to the United States but were not states.

**Hawaii.** A group of mostly American businessmen overthrew Hawaii's queen in 1893 with the help of American Marines. More than half of the Native Hawaiian people signed a petition against annexation, and members of Congress annexed the islands anyway.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Perry's warships in Japan, 1853 and 1854" -->
Commodore Matthew Perry opened two Japanese ports to American ships by bringing warships to Japan. Perry, the navy officer in command of the group, sailed into Edo Bay, beside the city of Edo, now called Tokyo, in July 1853, and he came back in 1854. Japanese and American officials signed the Convention of Kanagawa, a written agreement that opened the two ports, on March 31, 1854. The Japanese officials signed it under the threat of Perry's warships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Islands claimed for bird droppings, 1856" -->
Americans first claimed land overseas for the United States in 1856, forty-two years before the war with Spain. They wanted guano, the droppings of seabirds, because it makes crops grow.

Members of Congress passed the Guano Islands Act on August 18, 1856. Under it, an American citizen who found an island with guano on it could take the island for the United States, unless another country already claimed it. Americans claimed about a hundred islands under this law.

The judges of the Supreme Court ruled that the law was valid in 1890, in a case called *Jones v. United States*. Lawmakers have never ended it, and it is still part of United States law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was left out of the Alaska sale?" -->
American officials bought Alaska from Russia in 1867, and the diplomats who wrote the treaty left Alaska's Native peoples out of American citizenship by name. American and Russian diplomats signed the treaty on March 30, 1867, for a price of $7,200,000. Russian officials formally handed over the territory at a ceremony on Castle Hill in Sitka on October 18, 1867, and then the United States flag went up.

**Tlingit land.** Russian officials sold land that they did not really control. Alaska Native nations lived on it, and they had no part in the sale. Castle Hill itself was Tlingit land. Alexander Baranov, the first Russian governor, had attacked the hill in 1804 to take it from the Kiksadi clan of the Tlingit people, and after a six-day battle the Russians seized the Kiksadi fort and burned it. In 1867 a Tlingit village still stood beside the Russian town of Sitka.

Article III of the treaty states who would become an American citizen. People living in Alaska who stayed would be admitted to "the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages, and immunities of citizens of the United States." The article then leaves out "uncivilized native tribes," an insulting name for Alaska's Native peoples. They would instead be "subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt."

Alaska had no civil government, one run by elected or appointed officials outside the armed forces, until 1884. For 17 years American officers ruled it, first from the Army, then from the Treasury Department, then from the Navy. The historian Rebecca Poulson, writing for the Alaska Historical Society, says that American officials considered Native people "savages," an insulting word, and did not allow them to be citizens.

**Kake.** Under Russian rule, officials of the Russian company that ran Alaska had paid compensation, a payment to the dead person's family, when a Tlingit person was killed. Under Tlingit law, a death was settled with a payment, not with punishment. American officers refused to pay. In early 1869 an army guard at Sitka killed two men from the village of Kake, deaths Poulson calls accidental. Army officers at Sitka refused to pay compensation for them, and relatives of the dead men then killed two white men.

General Jefferson C. Davis ordered the American warship USS *Saginaw* to attack Kake. It was winter. The crew fired exploding shells from the ship's guns at the Kake villages and burned 28 of the villages' 29 clan houses, the houses of the Kake clans, which are large family groups. They also burned the villagers' canoes, totem poles and stored food.

**Not known:** No one has worked out how many people died after the attack, reporters at KTOO, a public radio station in Juneau, wrote in 2024. Kake oral history, the accounts the Tlingit of Kake passed down by word of mouth, records many deaths, especially among elders and children.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1887 to 1893" -->
A group of mostly American businessmen overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani of Hawaii in January 1893, and American Marines and sailors helped them. Two of their leaders had already used armed men to force a Hawaiian king to do what they wanted.

**The Bayonet Constitution.** A group of lawyers and sugar planters who were haole, the Hawaiian word for white people who are not Native Hawaiian, forced King Kalākaua to sign a new constitution on June 30, 1887. An armed militia, a force of armed citizens, backed them, and their leaders were two lawyers, Sanford B. Dole and Lorrin Thurston. The document became known as the Bayonet Constitution, after the blade fixed to the end of a rifle.

**The overthrow.** Early in 1893 members of the Annexation Club, Thurston among them, contacted John L. Stevens, the United States Minister to Hawaii and the top American diplomat there. They wanted to know whether the Americans would back them. The group, calling itself the Committee of Safety, began to move against the queen on January 15, 1893.

Stevens had 162 American sailors and Marines landed from the warship USS *Boston* on January 16. The queen's government had several hundred soldiers and police, and they did not fight. Dole became president of the new provisional government, a government set up for a short time until a lasting one is formed.

Queen Liliʻuokalani gave up her throne under protest. She addressed her protest to the United States, not to the men who had taken power. She wrote that she yielded "to the superior force of the United States of America," whose minister, John L. Stevens, "has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two American reports that disagree, 1893 and 1894" -->
Two American investigations of the overthrow reached opposite findings. President Grover Cleveland sent James H. Blount to Hawaii to find out what had happened, and Blount reported that Stevens had landed the Marines on a false excuse to help the plotters.

**What Cleveland said.** Cleveland sent Congress a message about it on December 18, 1893. He said that the show of armed force, the "military demonstration on the soil of Honolulu," was "of itself an act of war." He also said that "the provisional government owes its existence to an armed invasion by the United States."

Albert S. Willis, the new American minister in Hawaii, apologized for what Stevens had done, which he called "the reprehensible conduct of the American minister," reprehensible meaning deserving blame. He also apologized for "the unauthorized presence on land of a military force."

**What the Senate said.** Cleveland could not get the queen put back on her throne. Members of a Senate committee made their own investigation in 1894, called the Morgan Report, and they cleared everyone involved. On July 4, 1894, the men who had taken power declared the Republic of Hawaii.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" name="Queen Liliʻuokalani" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Queen Liliʻuokalani

> **Who:** The last queen of Hawaii, overthrown with American help and then tried in her own palace. She wrote the song "Aloha ʻOe."
> **When and where:** Honolulu. Overthrown January 17, 1893. Held prisoner in ʻIolani Palace in 1895.

**The queen was put on trial in her own throne room.**

Liliʻuokalani yielded under protest on January 17, 1893, and asked American leaders to undo what their own minister had done. President Cleveland agreed that Stevens had acted wrongly, but he could not get her put back on the throne.

**The rising of 1895.** Hawaiians loyal to the queen, among them Robert Wilcox and Prince Kūhiō, tried to restore her by force in January 1895. Within 36 hours officials of the Republic of Hawaii had caught the rebels and jailed them. Then they arrested the queen, and on January 24, 1895, they forced her to sign a paper giving up her throne for good.

**The trial.** They tried her before a military court in the throne room of ʻIolani Palace, where she had ruled. The charge was misprision of treason, knowing about a plot against the government and not reporting it. The officers who sat on the court found her guilty, fined her $5,000, and sentenced her to five years in prison at hard labor, forced heavy work.

The Republic's officials later reduced the sentence. For nearly eight months they held her prisoner in an upstairs bedroom of the palace, and they allowed her no visitors except one woman companion. She spent her days praying, reading, writing music, crocheting and quilting.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="21,269 names against annexation, 1897" -->
Native Hawaiians defeated a treaty to annex their islands by signing their names against it. Two Hawaiian groups held meetings on the five main islands between September 11 and October 2, 1897: Hui Aloha ʻĀina, which had separate branches for men and for women, and Hui Kālaiʻāina. They gathered 21,269 signatures of Native Hawaiians against annexation. That year about 39,000 Native Hawaiian and part-Hawaiian people were counted, so more than half of them signed.

Four men carried the petition to Washington: James Kaulia, David Kalauokalani, John Richardson and William Auld. They arrived on December 6 and presented it to the Senate on December 9, 1897. A treaty needs the votes of two-thirds of the senators. When the four men left Washington on February 27, 1898, only 46 senators were still willing to vote for the annexation treaty, short of the two-thirds it needed. The treaty failed.

**Taken anyway.** Supporters of annexation in Congress then used a method that did not need the Senate votes the Hawaiians had blocked: a joint resolution, which passes when more than half of the House and more than half of the Senate vote for it. Members of the House voted for theirs, the Newlands Resolution, on June 15, 1898, and senators on July 6. President William McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898, and under it American officials took over Hawaii.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898: Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines" -->
After a war with Spain in 1898, American officials ruled Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. The American battleship USS *Maine* blew up in the harbor of Havana, in Cuba, on February 15, 1898, and in April members of Congress declared war on Spain.

**Guam and Puerto Rico.** Juan Marina, the Spanish governor of Guam, surrendered the island to officers of the American warship USS *Charleston* on June 21, 1898. American troops landed in Puerto Rico at Guánica on July 25.

Spanish and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Paris on December 10, 1898. Spanish diplomats gave up Cuba and signed Puerto Rico and Guam over to the United States. They sold the Philippines to the United States for $20 million.

No one from Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines took part in the talks in Paris. Several million people in those places got new rulers without being asked. Among them on Guam were the CHamoru, the island's Native people. In the Philippines, Filipinos had already declared their own independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Americans and Filipinos went to war, 1899" -->
War between American troops and the Philippine Republic began on February 4, 1899, because American officials had decided to keep the islands. Filipinos had been fighting Spain before the Americans arrived. On June 12, 1898, they declared their independence. American officials kept the islands instead of recognizing the new republic, and the two sides went to war.

**The dead.** The war lasted until 1902. About 4,200 Americans and about 20,000 Filipino soldiers were killed.

**Disputed:** Counts of the Filipino civilians, people who were not soldiers, who died are far apart. The State Department's historians say as many as 200,000 died "from violence, famine, and disease," and Encyclopaedia Britannica gives more than 200,000. Other estimates run from 250,000 to 1,000,000, and the Philippine-American War Centennial Initiative gives 510,000.

Much of the civilian toll came from hunger and from a cholera epidemic in 1902, an outbreak of a gut disease spread by dirty water, which killed more than 200,000 people by itself. The epidemic was worse because the fighting had broken up daily life in the islands.

**What American troops did.** The State Department's historians also record what American troops did. In their words, "U.S. forces at times burned villages, implemented civilian reconcentration policies, and employed torture on suspected guerrillas." Reconcentration meant forcing civilians out of their homes into camps or zones that soldiers guarded, and the guerrillas were fighters in small bands who hid among the people and attacked by surprise.

One kind of torture American soldiers used was called the water cure. To make a prisoner talk, they held his throat so that he had to swallow water until it filled his stomach. Then they forced the water out by pressing on his stomach.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" name="Emilio Aguinaldo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Aguinaldo

> **Who:** The Filipino leader who declared the Philippines independent from Spain and then led the war against the United States.
> **When and where:** Born March 22 or 23, 1869, near Cavite, on the island of Luzon. President of the Philippine Republic from January 1899. Captured at Palanan on March 23, 1901. Died February 6, 1964.

**American officers captured him by pretending to be prisoners.**

Aguinaldo was the mayor of his town, Cavite Viejo, in 1896, and a local leader of the Katipunan, a secret society fighting Spanish rule.

**Two deals.** Aguinaldo made a deal with the Spanish governor in December 1897: he would leave the islands in return for money and a promise of reforms. From Hong Kong and Singapore he then made plans with American consuls, officials who looked after American trade and citizens there, and with officers of Commodore George Dewey's fleet. He would come back and help the Americans fight Spain. He landed in the Philippines again on May 19, 1898.

Filipinos declared independence on June 12, 1898, with Aguinaldo at the head of their government. Six months later Spanish diplomats sold the islands to the United States in the Treaty of Paris. Filipinos proclaimed a constitution on January 23, 1899, and elected Aguinaldo president of the Philippine Republic. The fighting with American troops began on February 4, and from November 1899 his forces fought as guerrillas.

**The trick.** General Frederick Funston captured him at Palanan, in northern Luzon, on March 23, 1901. Macabebe scouts, Filipinos who served the United States Army, dressed as rebel soldiers, and Funston and a few other American officers went along, pretending to be their prisoners. Fake letters sent ahead to Aguinaldo said that more of his own soldiers were coming and were bringing American prisoners to him. At Palanan the scouts opened fire on his guards, killed two and seized him.

Aguinaldo swore loyalty to the United States. On April 19, 1901, he called on his fighters to lay down their weapons and accept American rule. He lived until 1964 and saw the Philippines become independent in 1946.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samoa divided, 1899" -->
Diplomats divided the Samoan islands between the United States and Germany on December 2, 1899, in an agreement called the Tripartite Convention, from a word meaning three-sided. The three sides that signed it were the United States, Germany and Great Britain.

By signing it, the German and British ambassadors gave up their countries' claims to Tutuila and every other Samoan island east of a north-south map line, 171 degrees west. John Hay, the American secretary of state, gave up American claims to Upolu, Savaiʻi and the other islands west of that line, leaving them to Germany.

**The chiefs' papers.** The chiefs of the island of Tutuila signed a paper handing their island to the United States on April 17, 1900. Tui Manuʻa Elisala, the ruler of the Manuʻa islands, signed his islands over on July 16, 1904.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

**If you remember one thing:** In the second half of the 1800s American leaders took lands across two oceans, and most of the people who lived in them were never asked.

**One question this era leaves open:** Were the people of Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines now American citizens, with all the rights in the Constitution? The next era answers it.

**Next:** After 1900 American officials ruled the new islands, and the judges of the Supreme Court decided how much of the Constitution reached the people there.
