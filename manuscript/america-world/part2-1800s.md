## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850 the United States spread from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Ocean. American leaders bought land from France, took Florida from Spain, split the Oregon country with Britain and took the Southwest from Mexico in a war. People already lived on every part of that land, and none of them agreed to the sales and treaties. In the same years American ships fought their first war across an ocean, and President James Monroe told the empires of Europe to stop starting colonies in the Americas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tribute and the Barbary wars, 1801 to 1815" -->
American presidents after the Revolution paid tribute to the rulers of the Barbary states. Tribute here means protection money. The rulers were paid so that their ships would not seize American ships and hold American sailors for ransom.

When American officials would not raise the payments, the pasha of Tripoli declared war on the United States on May 14, 1801. Pasha was the title of Tripoli's ruler. The fighting ended with a treaty signed on June 10, 1805. Under it the United States paid no more yearly tribute to Tripoli. American officials did pay a ransom to free the crew of the *Philadelphia*, an American warship that Tripoli's forces had captured. A second Barbary war followed in 1815.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
On April 30, 1803, American envoys signed a treaty to buy about 828,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi from France. An envoy is a person sent to bargain for a government. The price was $15 million, and the purchase roughly doubled the size of the United States.

The envoys had been sent to Paris to buy only the port of New Orleans. French officials offered them all of Louisiana instead. Napoleon, the ruler of France, had sent an army to retake the colony of Saint-Domingue, now called Haiti. The people enslaved there had risen against French rule. Napoleon's attempt to put them down failed, and he lost the army he sent. With a war against Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to raise money and to keep the British from taking it.

Article III of the treaty states that the people living in the territory would be "incorporated in the Union of the United States." It also states that they would be admitted to "all the rights, advantages and immunities of citizens of the United States." So in 1803, land the United States bought came with a written promise that its people would become full citizens. A century later, justices of the Supreme Court ruled that no such promise covered Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines.

Native nations lived across those 828,000 square miles. They were not part of the sale. No one asked them, and the promise of citizenship in Article III did not include them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Florida, 1818 and 1819" -->
In 1818 General Andrew Jackson led American troops into Spanish Florida against the Seminole people and against people who had escaped slavery and lived among them. American officials called them a threat to Georgia. Jackson's troops seized the Spanish forts at Pensacola and St. Marks. Jackson had two British citizens put to death, on charges of stirring up the Seminoles and the escaped slaves.

President James Monroe and his advisers thought about condemning Jackson. Secretary of State John Quincy Adams defended him. Adams then used Jackson's raid to put a choice to Spain: control the people of East Florida, or hand it over to the United States.

In 1819 Adams and the Spanish minister, Luis de Onís, signed the Adams-Onís Treaty. Spain handed East Florida to the United States and gave up its claim to West Florida. Spain received no payment. The United States agreed instead to cover $5 million in damage claims. The treaty also defines the western edge of the Louisiana Purchase. Spain gave up its claims to the Pacific Northwest, and the United States accepted Spanish rule over Texas. It was ratified in 1821.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" name="John Quincy Adams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Quincy Adams

> **Who:** Secretary of State under President James Monroe, 1817 to 1825.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C.

John Quincy Adams got Florida from Spain for the United States, and he wrote the statement now called the Monroe Doctrine. He was Secretary of State from 1817 to 1825. The Secretary of State is the official who runs the country's dealings with other countries.

Adams negotiated the 1819 treaty with Spain's minister, Luis de Onís, and it carries both their names. Before that, he used Jackson's raid to press Spain to give up Florida.

Adams also wrote the message that became the Monroe Doctrine. Monroe had thought of making the statement jointly with Britain. Adams argued that the United States should make it alone, and he talked Monroe out of the joint statement.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-quincy-adams-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Monroe Doctrine, 1823" -->
On December 2, 1823, President James Monroe told Europe's rulers to start no new colonies in North or South America. He said it in his yearly message to Congress. His words were that "the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers." Henceforth means from now on. Colonization means starting colonies.

Most historians, including those at the State Department, credit John Quincy Adams with writing it. In 1823 the United States Navy was too small to enforce the warning. British leaders also wanted other European powers kept out of Latin America, for their own reasons. British warships kept those powers' fleets away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Texas, Oregon and the land taken from Mexico, 1845 to 1848" -->
In the 1840s the United States gained Texas, the southern half of the Oregon country and the Southwest. The United States annexed Texas in 1845. To annex a place is to take it and make it part of a country. On June 15, 1846, American and British negotiators set the border between the United States and British land in Oregon at the 49th parallel. They settled it by treaty, without a war.

The land in the Southwest came from a war with Mexico, fought from 1846 to 1848. On February 2, 1848, American and Mexican negotiators signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. They signed it at Guadalupe Hidalgo, a town north of Mexico City where the Mexican government had fled. Mexico gave up more than 525,000 square miles, and the United States paid $15,000,000. The land became all or part of California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming.

Between about 80,000 and 100,000 Mexican citizens lived on that land, depending on the count. Without moving, they became residents of the United States. Article IX of the treaty states that they would keep "the free enjoyment of their liberty and property" and the free practice of their religion. Senators rewrote Article IX and cut its last two paragraphs.

When senators ratified the treaty on March 10, 1848, they struck out Article X completely. Article X was the part that kept Spanish and Mexican land grants valid under American law. A land grant was a legal title to land given by the Spanish or Mexican government. American negotiators then wrote a side agreement, the Protocol of Querétaro. It told the Mexican government that deleting Article X had not canceled those land titles. American courts did not treat the Protocol as equal to the treaty.

A law of 1851 made Mexican landowners in California prove their deeds before officials of the United States government. The court cases dragged on for years. Through those cases and through illegal seizures of land, many Mexican American families lost their land or fell into debt. Many also faced violence and discrimination. In northern New Mexico and southern Colorado, some land grants belonged to whole villages together. In the late 1800s those shared grants were broken up, and the villages lost the land.

Article XI of the treaty describes the Native nations who lived on the land only as "savage tribes." It states that "a great part" of the land was "now occupied" by them, and that they "will hereafter be under the exclusive control of the Government of the United States." Among the nations on that land were the Tohono O'odham. No Native nation signed the treaty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-trist-america-world" name="Nicholas Trist" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Trist

> **Who:** The American diplomat who made peace with Mexico after President James K. Polk ordered him home.
> **When and where:** Mexico, 1847 and 1848.

Nicholas Trist signed the treaty that ended the war with Mexico after the president had already ordered him to stop. President James K. Polk sent Trist to Mexico to bargain for an end to the war. Then Polk lost patience and recalled him in October 1847.

Trist ignored the order. He kept bargaining with no authority to do so. In February 1848 he signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

Polk sent the treaty to the Senate anyway, because it gave him what he wanted, including California. Then he fired Trist. Polk refused to pay Trist's expenses or his salary for his last months of work.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the second half of the 1800s, American leaders began taking lands across the oceans and keeping them. Americans claimed small islands for their fertilizer, and the government bought Alaska from Russia. In Hawaii, American businessmen overthrew the queen with the help of American Marines, and members of Congress then annexed the islands. In 1898, after a war with Spain, the United States took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. The people who lived in these places were not asked, and none of them got a vote on it.

These places became territories of the United States. A territory is land that belongs to the United States but is not a state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Perry's warships in Japan, 1853 and 1854" -->
Commodore Matthew Perry opened two Japanese ports to American ships by bringing warships to Japan. A commodore is a navy officer in charge of a group of warships. In July 1853 Perry sailed a squadron of American warships into Edo Bay. He came back in 1854. On March 31, 1854, Japanese and American officials signed the Convention of Kanagawa, which opened the two ports to American ships. The Japanese officials signed it with Perry's warships in their waters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Islands claimed for bird droppings, 1856" -->
The United States first took land overseas in 1856, forty-two years before the war with Spain. The reason was fertilizer. Guano, the droppings of seabirds, makes crops grow.

On August 18, 1856, members of Congress passed the Guano Islands Act. Under it, any American citizen who found an island with guano on it, if no other country claimed it, could take the island for the United States. Americans claimed about a hundred islands under this law. In 1890, in a case called *Jones v. United States*, the justices of the Supreme Court upheld it. The law is still in force today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alaska, 1867" -->
In 1867 the United States bought Alaska from Russia, and the diplomats who wrote the treaty left Alaska's Native peoples out of American citizenship by name. American and Russian diplomats signed the treaty on March 30, 1867. The price was $7,200,000. On October 18, 1867, American soldiers raised the United States flag at Castle Hill in Sitka, and Russian officials handed over the territory.

Russia sold land that it did not really control. Alaska Native nations lived on it, and they had no part in the sale. Castle Hill itself was Tlingit land. In 1804 Alexander Baranov, the first Russian governor, had attacked the hill to take it from the Kiksadi clan of the Tlingit people. After a six-day battle, the Russians seized the Kiksadi fort and burned it. In 1867 a Tlingit village still stood beside the Russian town of Sitka.

Article III of the treaty sets out who would become an American citizen. People living in Alaska who stayed would be admitted to "the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages, and immunities of citizens of the United States." The article then makes an exception for "uncivilized native tribes." They would instead be "subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt."

Alaska had no civil government, one run by elected or appointed civilians, until 1884. For 17 years American officers ruled it, first from the Army, then from the Treasury Department, then from the Navy. American officials treated Native people as "savages" and did not allow them to be citizens.

Under Russian rule, officials of the Russian company that ran Alaska had paid compensation when a Tlingit person was killed, even by accident. That followed Tlingit law, which settles a death with payment rather than punishment. American officers refused to pay. In early 1869 Army officers at Sitka refused to pay compensation for the accidental deaths of two men from the village of Kake. Relatives of the dead men then killed two white men. The crew of the American warship USS *Saginaw* shelled the Kake villages and burned 28 of their 29 clan houses. Accounts of the attack give no count of the people killed and do not name the officer in command.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii: a kingdom overthrown, 1887 to 1893" -->
In January 1893 a group of mostly American businessmen overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani of Hawaii, and American Marines and sailors helped them. Some of the same men had already taken power from the Hawaiian crown once. On June 30, 1887, a group of haole lawyers and sugar planters forced King Kalākaua to sign a new constitution. Haole is the Hawaiian word for white people who are not Native Hawaiian. An armed militia, a force of armed citizens, backed them. Their leaders were two lawyers, Sanford B. Dole and Lorrin Thurston. The document became known as the Bayonet Constitution. A bayonet is a blade fixed to the end of a rifle.

In January 1893 members of the Annexation Club, Thurston among them, contacted John L. Stevens, the United States Minister to Hawaii. They wanted to know whether the Americans would back them. On January 15, 1893, they began to move against the queen. They called themselves the Committee of Safety. On January 17 Stevens had 162 American sailors and Marines landed from the warship USS *Boston*. The queen's government had several hundred soldiers and police, and they did not fight. Dole became president of the new provisional government, a government set up for a short time until a lasting one is formed.

Queen Liliʻuokalani gave up her throne under protest. She addressed her protest to the United States, not to the men who had taken power. She wrote that she yielded "to the superior force of the United States of America, whose Minister Plenipotentiary, His Excellency John L. Stevens, has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two American reports that disagree, 1893 and 1894" -->
Two American investigations of the overthrow reached opposite findings. President Grover Cleveland sent James H. Blount to Hawaii to find out what had happened. Blount reported that Stevens had landed the Marines on a false excuse to help the plotters. On December 18, 1893, Cleveland sent Congress a message about it. He said the "military demonstration on the soil of Honolulu was of itself an act of war." He also said that "the provisional government owes its existence to an armed invasion by the United States." Albert S. Willis, the new American minister in Hawaii, apologized for "the reprehensible conduct of the American minister." Reprehensible means deserving blame.

Cleveland could not get the queen put back on her throne. In 1894 members of a Senate committee made their own inquiry, called the Morgan Report, and they cleared everyone involved. Both reports are part of the record. On July 4, 1894, the men who had taken power declared the Republic of Hawaii.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" name="Queen Liliʻuokalani" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Queen Liliʻuokalani

> **Who:** The last monarch of the Kingdom of Hawaii.
> **When and where:** Honolulu. Overthrown January 17, 1893. Held prisoner in ʻIolani Palace in 1895.

Queen Liliʻuokalani lost her throne to an armed takeover backed by American troops, and two years later her enemies put her on trial in her own palace. On January 17, 1893, she yielded under protest. She asked the United States government to undo what its own minister had done. President Cleveland agreed that Stevens had acted wrongly, but he did not restore her.

In January 1895 Hawaiians loyal to the queen, among them Robert Wilcox and Prince Kūhiō, tried to restore her by force. Within 36 hours officials of the Republic of Hawaii had caught the rebels and jailed them. The Republic's officials then arrested the queen.

They tried her before a military court in the throne room of ʻIolani Palace, where she had ruled. The court found her guilty, fined her, and sentenced her to five years in prison at hard labor. The Republic's officials later changed the sentence. For nearly eight months they kept her locked in an upstairs bedroom of the palace. They allowed her no visitors except one woman companion. She spent her days praying, reading, writing music, crocheting and quilting. Among the many songs Liliʻuokalani wrote is "Aloha ʻOe."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="queen-liliuokalani-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="21,269 names against annexation, 1897" -->
Native Hawaiians defeated a treaty to annex their islands by signing their names against it. Between September 11 and October 2, 1897, two Hawaiian groups held meetings on the five main islands. The groups were Hui Aloha ʻĀina, which had separate branches for men and for women, and Hui Kālaiʻāina. They gathered 21,269 signatures of Native Hawaiians against annexation. That year about 39,000 Native Hawaiian and part-Hawaiian people were counted, so more than half of them signed.

Four men carried the petition to Washington: James Kaulia, David Kalauokalani, John Richardson and William Auld. They arrived on December 6 and presented it to the Senate on December 9, 1897. A treaty needs the votes of two-thirds of the senators. When the four men left Washington on February 27, 1898, only 46 senators were still willing to vote for the annexation treaty. The treaty failed.

Supporters of annexation in Congress then used a joint resolution, which needs only a simple majority in each house. It was called the Newlands Resolution. The House passed it on June 15, 1898, and the Senate on July 6. President William McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898. Members of Congress annexed Hawaii by a method that did not need the Senate votes the Hawaiians had blocked.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898: Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines" -->
A short war with Spain in 1898 left the United States ruling Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. On February 15, 1898, the American battleship USS *Maine* blew up in the harbor of Havana, in Cuba. In April members of Congress declared war on Spain. Spanish officials on Guam surrendered to the United States Navy on June 21, 1898. American troops landed in Puerto Rico at Guánica on July 25.

Spanish and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Paris on December 10, 1898. Spain gave up Cuba. It handed Puerto Rico and Guam to the United States, and it sold the Philippines to the United States for $20 million.

No one from Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines took part in the talks in Paris. Diplomats of two empires signed a paper in France, and several million people had a new government. On Guam they were the CHamoru people, the island's Native people. In the Philippines, Filipinos had already declared their own independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Philippine-American War begins, 1899" -->
The United States went to war with the Philippine Republic on February 4, 1899, to keep the islands. Filipinos had been fighting Spain before the Americans arrived. On June 12, 1898, they declared their independence. American officials kept the islands instead of recognizing the new republic, and the two sides went to war.

The war lasted until 1902. About 4,200 Americans and about 20,000 Filipino soldiers were killed. Counts of Filipino civilians who died do not agree, and they are far apart. The State Department's historians say as many as 200,000 died "from violence, famine, and disease." Encyclopaedia Britannica gives more than 200,000. Other estimates run from 250,000 to 1,000,000, and the Philippine-American War Centennial Initiative gives 510,000. Much of the civilian toll came from hunger and from a cholera epidemic in 1902, which killed more than 200,000 people by itself. Cholera is a disease of the gut spread by dirty water. The war made that epidemic worse. There is no single agreed number.

The State Department's historians also record what American troops did. In their words, "U.S. forces at times burned villages, implemented civilian reconcentration policies, and employed torture on suspected guerrillas." Reconcentration meant forcing civilians out of their homes into camps or zones that soldiers guarded. Guerrillas are fighters in small bands who hide among the people and attack by surprise.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" name="Emilio Aguinaldo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Aguinaldo

> **Who:** The Filipino leader who declared the Philippines independent from Spain and then led the war against the United States.
> **When and where:** Born March 22 or 23, 1869, near Cavite, on the island of Luzon. President of the Philippine Republic from January 1899. Captured at Palanan on March 23, 1901. Died February 6, 1964.

Emilio Aguinaldo led the Philippine Republic against Spain and then against the United States, until American officers captured him by a trick in 1901. In 1896 he was the mayor of his town, Cavite Viejo, and a local leader of the Katipunan. The Katipunan was a secret society fighting Spanish rule.

In December 1897 Aguinaldo made a deal with the Spanish governor. He agreed to leave the islands in return for money and a promise of reforms. From Hong Kong and Singapore he made plans with American consuls and with officers of Commodore George Dewey's fleet. He would come back and help the Americans fight Spain. He landed in the Philippines again on May 19, 1898.

On June 12, 1898, Filipinos declared independence with Aguinaldo at the head of their government. Six months later Spain handed the islands to the United States in the Treaty of Paris. On January 23, 1899, Filipinos proclaimed a constitution and elected Aguinaldo president of the Philippine Republic. The fighting with American troops began on February 4, 1899. From November 1899 his forces fought as guerrillas.

On March 23, 1901, General Frederick Funston captured him at Palanan, in northern Luzon. Macabebe scouts, Filipinos who served the United States Army, dressed as rebel soldiers. Funston and a few other American officers went along, pretending to be their prisoners. Forged letters said that his own reinforcements were bringing American prisoners to him. At Palanan the scouts opened fire on his guards, killed two and seized him.

Aguinaldo swore loyalty to the United States. On April 19, 1901, he called on his fighters to lay down their weapons and accept American rule. He lived until 1964 and saw the Philippines become independent in 1946.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilio-aguinaldo-america-world" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samoa divided, 1899" -->
In 1899, in an agreement called the Tripartite Convention, diplomats divided the Samoan islands between the United States and Germany. Samoan chiefs signed papers handing their islands to the United States in 1900 and 1904.
