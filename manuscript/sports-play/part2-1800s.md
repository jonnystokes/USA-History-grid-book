## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, crowds of many thousands began to gather to watch sport. The biggest crowds came to see horses race. By 1850 trotting races and prize fights drew crowds just as large. Clubs in Philadelphia and New York wrote down rules for their ball games. In 1845 members of the Knickerbocker club of New York wrote the rules that grew into baseball.

Enslaved people played ball, wrestled and ran races on their few days off, and enslaved jockeys rode horses that other men owned. Most children still made their own toys and played outdoors. In 1838 a Philadelphia firm began to make toys to sell in shops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native ball games and the loss of Choctaw land" -->
About 1800 the Choctaw and the Creek both claimed the land between the Tombigbee and Black Warrior rivers, in present-day Alabama. They agreed to settle the claim with a ball game about a mile below present-day Tuscaloosa. The Creek chief was Tuskeegee and the Choctaw chief was Luee. Both sides said they had won. A battle followed "in which many were killed and wounded on both sides," and more battles followed that fall. The account, which H. B. Cushman printed in 1899, gives no count of the dead.

On 27 September 1830 Choctaw leaders signed the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek. Under it, the Choctaw gave up about 11 million acres of their land, most of it in Mississippi. US officials moved the Choctaw west to Indian Territory, in present-day Oklahoma, in three groups between 1831 and 1833. The Choctaw Nation records that more than a quarter of the Choctaw people moved in those years died.

In 1832 a settler named Charles Dobbs took the ground in Mississippi where the Creek and the Choctaw had played for the beaver pond around 1790. The players killed in the fight after that game were buried there with money and silver ornaments. Dobbs dug up their graves. He took about $500 in silver coins and about $250 in silver ornaments.

The Choctaw kept playing in Indian Territory. In 1834 the painter George Catlin watched a Choctaw ball game there. Several thousand people camped beside the field. Two leaders had chosen the players three or four months before and sent runners to each one with a decorated ball stick. The women placed the bets. They bet knives, dresses, blankets, pots and kettles, dogs, horses and guns.

Through the night before the game, the players danced by torchlight every half hour while the women sang. At 9 the next morning, at the firing of a gun, four old men who served as judges threw up the ball. Six or seven hundred players ran for it. Each player held a stick in each hand, with a small net at the end, and could not touch the ball with his hands.

Catlin painted a Choctaw player named Tul-lock-chísh-ko, which means "Drinks the Juice of the Stone." Catlin called him "the most distinguished ball-player of the Choctaw nation." By the rules of the game he played barefoot, in a breechcloth, a strip of cloth worn around the hips, with a tail of white horsehair.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Surfing in Hawaii, 1847" -->
Hawaii was still an independent kingdom. Hiram Bingham led the first American Protestant missionaries there, who arrived in 1820. In 1847 he wrote that surfing was "the favorite amusement of all classes." Surfers used boards from 3 feet to 14 feet long and paddled out as far as half a mile. People used to bet on it, he wrote.

Bingham wrote that fewer Hawaiians surfed than before. He gave his reasons. Hawaiians now wore cloth clothing in the style the missionaries wore, and the people who made that clothing had less free time. He also named "modesty, industry or religion." He wrote that the missionaries had not caused any law against surfing. Peter T. Young, a historian of Hawaii, writes that only the king and the chiefs could make such a law, and that Hawaiians kept surfing through these years. No law against surfing is on record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Games on the days off from slavery" -->
Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. In his book of 1845 he wrote that the enslaved people he knew had the days from Christmas to New Year's Day off. Some of them made brooms, mats and baskets or hunted opossums, hares and raccoons. Most played ball, wrestled, ran footraces, fiddled, danced and drank whiskey. Douglass wrote that the drinking was what "our masters" liked best.

For a time Douglass taught other enslaved men to read on Sundays in St. Michael's, Maryland. Two white church class leaders, Wright Fairbanks and Garrison West, came in with other men carrying sticks and stones and broke up the school. Douglass wrote that the enslavers "had much rather see us engaged in those degrading sports," by which he meant wrestling, boxing and drinking whiskey.

In the South, planters set up boxing matches between enslaved men and bet on them. The history that records this names no planter and no fighter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Tom Molineaux

> **Who:** A boxer from Virginia who fought twice for the championship of England.
> **When and where:** Virginia, New York, England and Ireland, 1784 to 1818.

Tom Molineaux was born in Virginia on 23 March 1784. The Virginia Museum of History and Culture says he was born enslaved and fought other enslaved men while plantation owners bet on the fights. It says he won his freedom and $500 in one of those fights. Other accounts say the story comes from boxing writers of the 1800s and cannot be proved.

By 1809 he had gone to New York and then to England. His trainer there was Bill Richmond, a boxer who had been born into slavery on Staten Island, New York, in 1763. The Virginia museum says Richmond was born in Virginia.

On 18 December 1810 Molineaux fought the English champion, Tom Cribb, in Oxfordshire. In the 19th round the crowd pushed into the ring, and Molineaux hurt his left hand. Later in the fight, people argued over whether Cribb had taken longer than the 30 seconds allowed to come back to the mark. Cribb won. Accounts give the length of the fight as 35 rounds or about 39. The English boxing writer Pierce Egan wrote that Molineaux surprised everyone with his power and his skill.

About 15,000 people watched the second fight, on 28 September 1811. Cribb broke Molineaux's jaw and knocked him out in the 11th round.

Molineaux died in Ireland on 4 August 1818, at 34, with no money. Most accounts say he died in Galway, and the Virginia museum says Dublin.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tom-molineaux" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved jockeys and trainers" -->
By 1830 every state east of the Mississippi River had at least one racetrack, and Virginia had more than twelve. Many of the men who rode, trained and cared for the horses were enslaved.

From 1810 to 1815 an enslaved jockey named Simon raced against horses owned by Andrew Jackson, who later became president. The National Museum of Racing records nine races between them. Jackson's side lost eight and gave up the ninth without running.

In 1834 an enslaver named John Henry Hammond bought an enslaved trainer and groom named George. Hammond and other enslavers talked about splitting the ownership of George three ways.

On 30 September 1839 an enslaved jockey named Cato rode the horse Wagner to victory at Louisville, Kentucky, for a prize of $14,000. About 10,000 people watched. Some of them made bets in dollars and some bet enslaved people. Wagner's owner, John Campbell, also held Cato in slavery. The Notable Kentucky African Americans Database says Campbell freed Cato for the win and that Cato kept riding for him. Other accounts say Cato stayed enslaved.

In 1846 a race card for the Washington Race Course at Charleston, South Carolina, named the owner of each horse, including Wade Hampton. It named no rider. Each jockey appears only by the colors he wore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crowds for horses, runners and fighters" -->
On 27 May 1823 the northern horse American Eclipse beat the southern horse Sir Henry at the Union Course on Long Island, New York. One history of the period puts the crowd at as many as 100,000 people. Horses from the North and the South raced each other in more matches of this kind until 1845.

People also came to watch men run. In 1835 a New Yorker named John Cox Stevens offered $1,000 to any man who could run ten miles in under an hour. Henry Stannard did it in 59 minutes and 49 seconds. About 1845, trotting races, in which a horse pulled a driver in a light cart, began to draw bigger crowds than races with riders on horseback.

A prize fight was a boxing match with bare fists, fought for a money prize. Prize fighting was against the law in most states, so organizers named the place of a fight at the last minute. By 1850 its crowds were as large as the crowds at trotting races. In the North, many fights set Irish Catholic immigrants against Protestants born in America.

On 13 September 1842 Christopher Lilly and Thomas McCoy fought at Hastings, New York. McCoy was 21. A coroner's jury, a group of citizens who look into a sudden death, found that he died "by blows and injuries received in a fight with Christopher Lilly." The jury named eight men who had helped run the fight. James "Yankee" Sullivan, who arranged it, was sentenced to two years in prison and let out after two months. The records do not say what happened to Lilly.

In 1849 Tom Hyer beat Sullivan in 17 minutes and 18 seconds in Maryland and won a purse of $10,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Written rules for ball games" -->
Boys and men in many towns played bat-and-ball games that went by local names, such as town ball. On 4 July 1833 two groups of town ball players from Philadelphia met in Camden, New Jersey, and joined to form one club, the Olympic. In 1838 the club printed its rules and its list of members.

On 23 September 1845 the Knickerbocker Base Ball Club of New York adopted twenty written rules. Two members, William Wheaton and William Tucker, wrote them down. A game ended when one side scored 21 runs. Three outs ended a side's turn at bat. A fielder put a runner out by throwing the ball to a baseman, not by hitting the runner with it. The members were merchants, lawyers and doctors. They played at the Elysian Fields in Hoboken, New Jersey.

On 19 June 1846 the Knickerbockers played the New York club under the new rules in one of the first recorded games between two clubs. One account says the New York club won 23 to 1. Another says the Knickerbockers won.

Baseball's beginnings were later made into a story that was not true. In 1905 the sporting-goods maker Albert Spalding called for an inquiry into how baseball began. Abner Graves, a 71-year-old mining engineer, wrote to an Ohio newspaper that Abner Doubleday had invented baseball in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839. Graves had been 5 years old in 1839. Doubleday was then a cadet at West Point, the army's college, and his 67 surviving diaries never mention baseball. Spalding's committee never met Graves or wrote to him. In a report of 30 December 1907 the committee's chairman, Abraham Mills, wrote that Doubleday had invented the game. Spalding wanted to show that baseball owed nothing to any English game. Baseball historians today call the Doubleday story a myth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children, toys and the circus" -->
Children in towns and on farms played outside with what they could find or make. There were no public playgrounds.

Toys began to be made to sell. In 1838 Francis, Field and Francis opened on North Second Street in Philadelphia. It was Philadelphia's first toy maker and one of the first in the country. The owners sold toys brought from France and Germany and made tin toys of their own. Their toy fire engine pulled by horses is thought to be the first toy made in a factory in the United States.

The travelling circus began in these years. In 1825 the city leaders of Wilmington, Delaware, banned public shows. Joshua Purdy Brown put up a canvas tent for his circus just outside the city line. After that, circuses travelled from town to town and brought their own tents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lucy-larcom" name="Lucy Larcom" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lucy Larcom

> **Who:** A girl who went to work in a cotton mill at 11 and later wrote about it.
> **When and where:** Beverly and Lowell, Massachusetts, 1824 to the 1840s.

Lucy Larcom was born in Beverly, Massachusetts, on 5 March 1824. She later wrote about her childhood there. She and her friends shaped burrs from burdock plants into chairs, cradles and sofas for their dolls. They played school on the doorsteps and climbed over the wall onto the hill. She wrote that the neighborhood children, from large families, enjoyed "a little wholesome neglect."

Her father, a retired sea captain, died in 1832. In 1835 her mother, Lois Larcom, moved the family to Lowell, a mill town. There she ran a boarding house for women who worked in the cotton mills, cooking and keeping house for up to forty of them. The boarding house did not earn enough. Lucy heard someone say, "The children will have to leave school and go into the mill." She was 11.

Her job was to take full bobbins off the spinning machines and put on empty ones, about every 45 minutes. A bobbin is a spool that holds thread. At first she told her family, "It is just like play." Between changes, she and the other little girls played games in a corner.

In winter the working day ran from first daylight to 7:30 at night. She earned one dollar a week plus her board, and her family needed the money, so she could not go on to high school. She wrote that mill work "was not, and could not be, the right sort of life for a child." Larcom later became a teacher and a poet. Her book about her childhood, *A New England Girlhood*, came out in 1889.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1869 Harry Wright put together the Cincinnati Red Stockings, the first baseball team whose players were all openly paid. That same year, students from Rutgers and Princeton played the first college football game. In 1891 a teacher named James Naismith invented basketball. Black jockeys won most of the early Kentucky Derbies. Isaac Murphy, who was born enslaved, won it three times. In baseball, the men who ran the leagues shut Black players out, first in 1867 and again in 1887.

Adults also began to organize children's play. In 1886 a Boston health group put piles of sand in chapel yards for small children, and people in other cities opened playgrounds in the 1890s. Many children had no time to play. The census of 1900 counted more than 1.75 million children under 15 at work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first paid team and new games" -->
Harry Wright found ten men to pay for the Cincinnati Red Stockings in 1869, including himself and his brother George. They won all 57 of their games against clubs in the national association of that time. That September they travelled to San Francisco, and they became the first club to play on both coasts. In 1870 the Brooklyn Atlantics beat them in 11 innings while 20,000 people watched.

On 6 November 1869 Rutgers beat Princeton 6 to 4 at New Brunswick, New Jersey, in the first college football game. Each side had 25 players. They could kick the ball or hit it with their fists or hands, but they could not hold it or trip anyone. About 100 people watched. The game looked more like soccer than like football today.

In the winter of 1891 James Naismith was a young teacher from Canada at a YMCA training school in Springfield, Massachusetts. His boss, Luther Gulick, gave him a class that needed a game to play indoors in winter. Naismith wrote 13 rules. He asked the janitor for two boxes to use as goals, and the janitor brought two peach baskets instead. Nine players played on each side. Accounts date the first game to 21 or 29 December 1891.

On 6 April 1896 James Connolly, a 27-year-old Harvard student from Boston, won the hop, skip and jump at Athens, Greece. That event is now called the triple jump. It was the first final of the first modern Olympic Games, so Connolly was the first modern Olympic champion. A dean at Harvard had warned him that he might not be let back into the college if he left to compete, and he went anyway.

Families bought new games to play at home. In 1860 Milton Bradley sold a board game called *The Checkered Game of Life*. Buyers took more than 45,000 copies by 1861. In 1866 Bradley printed rules for croquet, a lawn game played by hitting wooden balls with mallets.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The color line in baseball" -->
A color line was a rule, written or unwritten, that kept Black people out. In 1867 the members of the National Association of Base Ball Players, the group that ran club baseball, voted to keep Black players and Black clubs out of the association.

Black men still played for pay. Bud Fowler played for pay in 1878. He is the first Black professional baseball player on record. In 1884 Moses Fleetwood Walker and his brother Weldy played for Toledo in the American Association, which was then a major league. They were the first Black major-league players known to the public as Black men. William Edward White, who had been born enslaved, played one game for Providence in 1879. He lived as a white man and gave his race as white in the census.

In 1887 Cap Anson, the captain of the Chicago club, said his team would not play Newark if Newark's two Black players took the field. They were Walker and the pitcher George Stovey. On 14 July 1887 Newark played Chicago without them.

That same day, at a meeting in Buffalo, New York, the owners of the International League voted 6 to 4 to tell the league's secretary, C. D. White, to approve no more contracts with Black players. The owners of teams with only white players voted for the ban. The owners of teams with a Black player voted against it. The surviving accounts do not give the owners' names.

The owners in the major leagues never wrote down a rule against Black players. They kept Black players out by an unwritten agreement among themselves. No Black player played in the International League again until Jackie Robinson in 1946.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Moses Fleetwood Walker

> **Who:** A catcher, the first major-league player known to the public as a Black man.
> **When and where:** Ohio, Toledo, Newark and Syracuse, 1856 to 1924.

Moses Fleetwood Walker was born in Mount Pleasant, Ohio, on 7 October 1856. He studied at Oberlin College and then studied law at the University of Michigan.

He caught for Toledo in 1883. In an exhibition game that year, Cap Anson of Chicago used a racist slur about Walker and said his team would never again play a team with Walker on it.

On 1 May 1884 Walker played his first major-league game for Toledo, at Louisville. He played 42 games and batted .263. He often caught the ball barehanded, with little padding. Toledo's pitcher Tony Mullane later said Walker "was the best catcher I ever worked with, but I disliked a Negro." Mullane said he threw his pitches without looking at the signals Walker gave him.

In September 1884 Toledo's manager got a letter warning that a mob would attack if Walker played in Richmond, Virginia. The letter did not name its writers. Toledo's managers let Walker go before the trip.

Walker played for Newark in 1887 and for Syracuse in 1888 and 1889. His contracts had been signed before the International League owners' vote, so he could keep playing.

On 9 April 1891 a group of white men attacked Walker outside a saloon in Syracuse. One of them, Patrick Murray, hit him in the head with a stone. Walker stabbed Murray with a pocketknife, and Murray died. On 3 June 1891 an all-white jury found Walker not guilty of murder.

In 1898 Walker was convicted of robbing the mail and spent a year in jail. He later ran an opera house, a theater, in Cadiz, Ohio. In 1908 he and his brother Weldy published *Our Home Colony*, a book arguing that Black Americans should move to Africa. He died of pneumonia in Cleveland on 11 May 1924, at 67.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="moses-fleetwood-walker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black jockeys" -->
Oliver Lewis rode the horse Aristides to win the first Kentucky Derby on 17 May 1875. The horse's trainer, Ansel Williamson, had been born enslaved in 1806. Black jockeys won 15 of the first 28 Kentucky Derbies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-murphy" name="Isaac Murphy" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Murphy

> **Who:** A jockey born into slavery who won the Kentucky Derby three times.
> **When and where:** Kentucky and the Eastern racetracks, 1861 to 1896.

Isaac Burns Murphy was born into slavery in Clark County, Kentucky, on 6 January 1861. His father, Jerry Burns, joined the Union Army in 1864 and died that fall in the army hospital at Camp Nelson, Kentucky.

Murphy won the Kentucky Derby in 1884, 1890 and 1891. He was the first jockey to win it three times and the first to win it two years in a row. By his own count he won 628 of his 1,412 races.

At his best he earned $15,000 to $20,000 a year, at a time when a family of four lived on about $1,200. In 1887 he and his wife, Lucy, bought a 10-room house in Lexington for $10,000.

In 1890 he rode the favorite, Firenze, in a race at Monmouth Park, New Jersey. He swayed in the saddle and fell off after the finish. The race officials, called stewards, said he was drunk and suspended him. Murphy said he had been drugged. Men had bet heavily against his horse. Who bet against it is not recorded.

Murphy died in Lexington in February 1896, at 35. Accounts give the cause as pneumonia or heart failure. The records do not show how much money he left. After he died, Lucy Murphy could not pay back a $5,500 loan and lost the house in a court case. It was sold at auction in 1900.

His grave lost its marker. In 1955 he became the first jockey named to the Racing Hall of Fame. In 1967 his body was moved to a site near the grave of the racehorse Man o' War, and later to the Kentucky Horse Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-murphy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Football deaths" -->
Football players in the 1890s wore no helmets, no mouthpieces and no padding. A New York newspaper, the *World*, counted at least eight deaths and more than 200 serious injuries in the 1897 season. Its list included broken bones, fractured skulls, twisted necks and crushed chests.

On 30 October 1897 Richard Von Gammon, a 17-year-old University of Georgia player, dove into a pile of players to make a tackle against the University of Virginia in Atlanta. He lay still. He died early the next morning of a concussion, which is an injury to the brain from a hard blow to the head.

Georgia's lawmakers voted to ban football at the state's colleges. Von Gammon's mother, Rosalind Burns Gammon, wrote to her representative. She asked that her son's death not be used as an argument against the sport. Governor William Atkinson vetoed the ban on 7 December 1897, which means he refused to sign it and it did not become law.

On 30 October 1898 a Tennessee newspaper, the *Knoxville Tribune*, printed a full page calling for a ban. It told of three deaths, without names. Two players' heads struck together and one of them died. A player at the bottom of a pile had his neck broken. A player kicked in the head by a heavy shoe died three days later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women on bicycles" -->
Frances Willard learned to ride a bicycle at 53. In 1895 she published a book about it. As a girl in the country she had almost no toys except the ones she made herself, such as a wooden plow. She practiced on the bicycle for three months. She wrote that women who rode would have to dress more sensibly than in long, tight dresses.

In February 1896 Susan B. Anthony told the reporter Nellie Bly that the bicycle "has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world." To emancipate means to set free. Anthony said that bloomers, which were loose pants gathered at the ankle, or very short skirts were the right clothes for riding.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first playgrounds" -->
In 1886 Dr. Marie Zakrzewska wrote to a Boston health group, the Massachusetts Emergency and Hygiene Association. She told its leaders that in Berlin's public parks, children rich and poor dug and played in heaps of sand. That year the association's members put three piles of yellow sand in the yards of chapels in Boston for small children. By 1887 there were ten, and about 400 children came each day. The children's favorite games in the sand were "house" and "funeral." By 1900 the association ran 21 playgrounds, 20 of them in school yards.

Other cities followed in the 1890s. Donors in Philadelphia started two summer playgrounds in 1893. In 1894 the staff of Hull-House, a center that reformers ran in a poor neighborhood of Chicago, opened a playground with swings, a sand pile and benches. A policeman worked there and usually captained one of the baseball teams. In 1897 a New York City committee reported that New York "has as yet not a single municipal playground," meaning none run by the city. The reformer Jacob Riis was the committee's secretary.

Amusement rides came in these years too. In 1884 LaMarcus Thompson opened a roller coaster, the Switchback Railway, at Coney Island in Brooklyn. A ride cost 5 cents, and the car went just over 6 miles an hour.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The children who had no time to play" -->
The census of 1870 was the first to count working children. It found more than 750,000 workers under 15. That count left out children who worked on their own family's farm or in its shop. By 1900 more than 1.75 million children under 15 had jobs. After 1880 most working children aged 10 to 14 worked somewhere other than a farm.

In 1870 one child in eight had a job. By 1900 more than one in five did. In 1900 about 25,000 of the nearly 100,000 workers in the cloth mills of the South were children under 16. Children in the mills worked 12-hour days, six days a week.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cherokee ball play, 1890" -->
In 1890 the researcher James Mooney watched the Eastern Cherokee ball game in North Carolina. In the fall a game took place somewhere on the reservation at least every other week. Each player carried two sticks just under 3 feet long, each with a net of twisted squirrel skin or hemp. For 7 to 28 days before a game, a player did not eat rabbit or frog or hot or salted food, and did not touch a woman.

Before a game an old man named Standing Water scratched each player with a comb made of seven sharp splinters of turkey leg bone. He pressed the teeth into the skin from shoulder to elbow and from elbow to wrist, and down each leg. He also cut an X on each player's chest and back. The scratches added up to nearly three hundred cuts. Mooney wrote that the cuts were shallow but painful, and that the players took them willingly as part of the ceremony that would help them win.

The games drew large crowds, including white people from nearby towns. No order banning the Cherokee or the Choctaw ball game in these years is on record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
