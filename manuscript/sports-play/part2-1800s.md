## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 15 minutes**

Crowds of many thousands began to gather to watch sport in the first half of the 1800s, and the biggest crowds came to see horses race. By 1850 trotting races, in which a horse pulled a driver in a light cart, had begun to replace races with riders as the sport Americans most liked to watch. Prize fights, boxing matches with bare fists fought for money, had become about as popular to watch as trotting.

Members of clubs in Philadelphia and New York wrote down rules for their ball games, and in 1845 members of the Knickerbocker club of New York wrote the rules that grew into baseball.

Enslaved people played ball, wrestled and ran races on their few days off, and enslaved jockeys rode horses that other men owned. Children like Lucy Larcom in Massachusetts made their own toys from burrs and played outdoors. A Philadelphia firm began to make toys to sell in 1838.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native ball games and the loss of Choctaw land" -->
The Choctaw and the Creek both claimed the land between the Tombigbee and Black Warrior rivers, in present-day Alabama, about 1800. They agreed to settle the claim with a ball game about a mile below present-day Tuscaloosa. The Creek chief was Tuskeegee and the Choctaw chief was Luee. Both sides said they had won. A battle followed "in which many were killed and wounded on both sides," and more battles followed that fall. The account, which H. B. Cushman printed in 1899, gives no count of the dead.

**The treaty.** Choctaw leaders signed the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, a written agreement with the United States, on September 27, 1830. The men who bargained for the United States, Secretary of War John Eaton and John Coffee, gave the Choctaw two choices: move west of the Mississippi River, or stay and live under US and state law.

The chief Choctaw men at the talks were Greenwood LeFlore, Mosholatubbee and Nittucachee. Under the treaty, the Choctaw had to hand over about 11 million acres of their land to the United States, most of it in Mississippi.

US officials forced the Choctaw to leave their homeland and go west to Indian Territory, in present-day Oklahoma, in three groups between 1831 and 1833. About 15,000 people made the trip. A winter blizzard and an outbreak of cholera, a disease that kills by draining the body of water, struck them on the way. Writers for the Choctaw Nation say that more than a quarter of the Choctaw people forced out in those years died. Another count gives about 2,500 dead. About 5,000 to 6,000 Choctaw stayed in Mississippi.

**The graves.** The ball players killed after the beaver-pond game of about 1790 had been buried at the ball ground with money and silver ornaments. A settler named Charles Dobbs took that ground in 1832, part of the land the Choctaw had to hand over under the 1830 treaty. Dobbs dug up their graves. He took about $500 in silver and about $250 in silver ornaments.

**The game in Indian Territory.** The Choctaw kept playing in their new land. The painter George Catlin watched a Choctaw ball game there in 1834. Several thousand people camped beside the field. Two leaders had chosen the players three or four months before and sent runners to each one with a decorated ball stick. The women placed the bets. They bet knives, dresses, blankets, pots and kettles, dogs, horses and guns.

Through the night before the game, the players danced by torchlight every half hour while the women sang. At 9 the next morning, at the firing of a gun, four old men who served as judges threw up the ball. Six or seven hundred players ran for it. Each player held a stick in each hand and could not touch the ball with his hands.

Catlin painted a Choctaw player named Tul-lock-chísh-ko, which means "Drinks the Juice of the Stone." Catlin called him "the most distinguished ball-player of the Choctaw nation." By the rules of the game he played barefoot, in a breechcloth, a strip of cloth worn around the hips, with a tail of white horsehair.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Surfing in Hawaii, 1847" -->
Hawaii was still an independent kingdom. Hiram Bingham led the first American missionaries there from the Protestant churches, the Christian churches that broke away from the Catholic Church, and they arrived in 1820. He wrote in 1847 that surfing was "the favorite amusement of all classes." Surfers used boards from 3 feet to 14 feet long and paddled out as far as half a mile. People used to bet on it, he wrote.

Fewer Hawaiians surfed than before, Bingham wrote, because Hawaiians now wore cloth clothing in the missionaries' style and the people who made that clothing had less free time. He also gave the cause as more "modesty, industry or religion." In his time, modesty meant keeping the body covered, and industry meant hard work.

**Was surfing banned?** No law against surfing is on record. In the same passage, Bingham denied that the missionaries had caused any law against it. Peter T. Young, a historian of Hawaii, writes that only the king and the chiefs could make such a law, and that Hawaiians kept surfing through these years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Games on the days off from slavery" -->
Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. In his book of 1845 he wrote that the enslaved people he knew had the days from Christmas to New Year's Day off. Some of them made brooms, mats and baskets or hunted opossums, hares and raccoons. Most played ball, wrestled, ran footraces, fiddled, danced and drank whiskey. Douglass wrote that the drinking was what "our masters" liked best.

For a time Douglass taught other enslaved men to read on Sundays in St. Michael's, Maryland. Two class leaders, men who led small religious groups in the church, Wright Fairbanks and Garrison West, came in with other men carrying sticks and stones and broke up the school. Douglass wrote that the enslavers "had much rather see us engaged in those degrading sports," by which he meant wrestling, boxing and drinking whiskey.

In the South, planters set up boxing matches between enslaved men and bet on them. The reference book *American Eras*, which records this, gives the name of no fighter and no match.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tom-molineaux" name="Tom Molineaux" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tom Molineaux

> **Who:** A boxer from Virginia who fought twice for the championship of England.
> **When and where:** Virginia, New York, England and Ireland, 1784 to 1818.

**About 15,000 people watched the English champion break his jaw.**

Tom Molineaux fought the English champion, Tom Cribb, in Oxfordshire on December 18, 1810. In the 19th round the crowd pushed into the ring, and Molineaux hurt his left hand. Later in the fight, people argued over whether Cribb had taken longer than the 30 seconds allowed to come back to the mark. Cribb won. Accounts give the length of the fight as 35 rounds or about 39.

The English boxing writer Pierce Egan wrote that Molineaux surprised everyone with his power and his skill. About 15,000 people watched the second fight, on September 28, 1811. Cribb broke Molineaux's jaw and knocked him out in the 11th round.

**Where he came from.** Molineaux was born in Virginia on March 23, 1784. Writers at the Virginia Museum of History and Culture say he was born enslaved and fought other enslaved men while plantation owners bet on the fights. They say he won his freedom and $500 in one of those fights. Other accounts say the story comes from boxing writers of the 1800s and cannot be proved.

He had gone to New York and then to England by 1809. His trainer there was Bill Richmond, who had been born into slavery on Staten Island, New York, in 1763. The Virginia museum's writers say Richmond was born in Virginia.

Molineaux died in Ireland on August 4, 1818, at 34, with no money. He died in the band room of the 77th Regiment, a unit of soldiers, of tuberculosis, a lung disease, and of heavy drinking. The Galway City Museum in Ireland gives the place as an army barracks in Galway, and the Virginia museum's writers give Dublin.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tom-molineaux" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved jockeys and trainers" -->
Every state east of the Mississippi River had at least one racetrack by 1830, and Virginia had more than twelve. Enslaved men rode, trained and cared for horses at these tracks, and the records name a few of them.

An enslaved jockey named Simon raced against horses owned by Andrew Jackson, who later became president, from 1810 to 1815. The National Museum of Racing records nine races between them. Jackson's side lost eight times and gave up once without running.

An enslaver named John Henry Hammond bought an enslaved trainer and groom, a worker who cares for horses, named George in 1834. Hammond and other enslavers talked about splitting the ownership of George three ways.

**Cato's win.** An enslaved jockey named Cato rode the horse Wagner to victory at Louisville, Kentucky, on September 30, 1839, for a prize of $14,000. About 10,000 people watched. Some of them made bets in dollars and some bet enslaved people. Wagner's owner, John Campbell, also held Cato in slavery.

The Notable Kentucky African Americans Database states that Campbell freed Cato for the win and that Cato kept riding for him. Jessica Dallow, who has written about the painter Edward Troye's pictures of racehorses, writes that his freedom was only rumored at the time.

She points to the writer Edward Hotaling. He noted that each win made an enslaved jockey worth more to his owner, so freedom would have been an unusual reward. Enslaved jockeys could earn some money by riding, and Dallow writes that Cato may have been freed or may have bought his own freedom.

A race card, a printed list of the horses in a race, for the Washington Race Course at Charleston, South Carolina, in 1846 listed the owner of each horse, including Wade Hampton. It listed no rider. Each jockey appears only by the colors he wore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crowds for horses, runners and fighters" -->
**North against South.** The northern horse American Eclipse beat the southern horse Sir Henry at the Union Course on Long Island, New York, on May 27, 1823. One history of the period puts the crowd at as many as 100,000 people. Horses from the North and the South raced each other in more matches of this kind until 1845.

People also came to watch men run. John Cox Stevens offered $1,000 in 1835 to any man who could run ten miles in under an hour. Henry Stannard did it in 59 minutes and 49 seconds. Trotting races began to take the place of races with riders on horseback about 1845.

**Fights against the law.** Prize fighting was against the law in most states, so organizers set up fights at the last minute. By 1850 prize fighting was about as popular to watch as trotting races. In the North, many fights set Irish Catholic immigrants against Protestants born in America.

Thomas McCoy, who was 21, died after a prize fight with Christopher Lilly at Hastings, New York, on September 13, 1842. A coroner's jury, a group of citizens who look into a sudden death, found that he died "by blows and injuries received in a fight with Christopher Lilly." The jury named eight men as the main people in the fight. None of them had been arrested when a Michigan newspaper printed the jury's finding on September 26.

A judge sentenced James "Yankee" Sullivan, who arranged the fight, to two years in prison. Sullivan served two months. He then paid a $200 bond, money a court keeps if a person breaks a promise, and promised not to fight for two years, and he was let out. The records do not say what happened to Lilly. Tom Hyer beat Sullivan in 17 minutes and 18 seconds in Maryland in 1849 and won a purse of $10,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who wrote the rules of baseball?" -->
No one person wrote them. Players in many towns played bat-and-ball games that went by local names, such as town ball, and clubs wrote down their own rules. Two groups of town ball players from Philadelphia met in Camden, New Jersey, on July 4, 1833, and joined to form one club, the Olympic. The club printed its rules and its list of members in 1838.

**Twenty rules.** The members of the Knickerbocker Base Ball Club of New York adopted twenty written rules on September 23, 1845. Two members, William Wheaton and William Tucker, wrote them down. A game ended when one side scored 21 runs. Three outs ended a side's turn at bat. A fielder put a runner out by throwing the ball to a baseman, not by hitting the runner with it. The members were merchants, lawyers and doctors. They played at the Elysian Fields in Hoboken, New Jersey.

The Knickerbockers played the New York club under the new rules on June 19, 1846, in one of the first recorded games between two clubs. One account says the New York club won 23 to 1. Another says the Knickerbockers won.

**The Doubleday story.** Albert Spalding and the members of a committee he asked for later spread a story about how baseball began that was not true. Spalding, a maker of sporting goods, asked for a study of how baseball began in 1905. Abner Graves, a 71-year-old mining engineer, wrote to an Ohio newspaper that Abner Doubleday had invented baseball in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839. Graves had been 5 years old in 1839. Doubleday was then a cadet, a student training to be an army officer, at West Point.

The historian Victor Salvatore found that baseball does not appear in any of Doubleday's 67 surviving diaries. Other writers say he mentioned it once in his letters or diaries. No member of Spalding's committee met Graves or wrote to him. Even so, the committee's chairman, Abraham Mills, wrote in a report of December 30, 1907, that Doubleday had invented the game.

Spalding wanted to show that baseball owed nothing to any English game. Salvatore titled his article about Doubleday "The Man Who Didn't Invent Baseball."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children, toys and the circus" -->
The toy firm Francis, Field and Francis opened on North Second Street in Philadelphia in 1838. It was Philadelphia's first toy maker and one of the first in the country. The owners sold toys brought from France and Germany and made toys of their own. According to the Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, their toy fire engine pulled by horses is believed to be the first toy made in a factory in the United States.

The city leaders of Wilmington, Delaware, banned public shows in 1825. Joshua Purdy Brown put up a canvas tent for his circus just outside the city line. With his own tent, Brown could set up his circus in any town, and the circus became a traveling show.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lucy-larcom" name="Lucy Larcom" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lucy Larcom

> **Who:** A girl who went to work in a mill at 11 and later wrote about it.
> **When and where:** Beverly and Lowell, Massachusetts, 1824 to the 1840s.

**"The children will have to leave school and go into the mill."**

Lucy Larcom grew up in Beverly, Massachusetts, in the 1820s and 1830s. She and her friends shaped burrs from burdock plants into chairs, cradles and sofas for their dolls. They played school on the doorsteps and climbed over the wall onto the hill. She wrote that the neighborhood children, from large families, enjoyed "a little wholesome neglect." She meant that the grown-ups mostly left them alone, and that this was good for them.

**The move to Lowell.** Her father, a retired sea captain, died in 1832. Three years later her mother, Lois Larcom, moved the family to Lowell, a mill town. There she ran a boarding house for women who worked in the mills, cooking and keeping house for up to forty of them. Each boarder paid her $1.25 a week. In her book about her childhood, Lucy wrote down the sentence that sent her to work: "The children will have to leave school and go into the mill." She was 11.

She went to work at the Lawrence Manufacturing Company as a doffer. A doffer took full bobbins, the spools that held thread, off the spinning machines and put on empty ones, about every 45 minutes. At first she told her family, "It is just like play." Between changes, she and the other little girls played games in a corner.

In winter the working day ran from first daylight to 7:30 at night. She earned one dollar a week plus her board, and her family needed the money, so she could not go on to high school. She wrote that mill work "was not, and could not be, the right sort of life for a child."

The mill rules banned books, so she pasted poems cut from newspapers all over the side of her window seat in the mill. She and her sister Emeline published poems in two magazines, the *Operatives' Magazine* and the *Offering*. Lucy Larcom grew up to teach school and to write poems. Her book about her childhood, *A New England Girlhood*, came out in 1889.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 14 minutes**

Sport turned into a business between 1850 and 1900, and the men who ran it decided who could play. Harry Wright put together the Cincinnati Red Stockings in 1869, the first known baseball team whose players were all openly paid. Students from Rutgers and Princeton played the first college football game that same year, and a teacher named James Naismith invented basketball in 1891. Black jockeys won most of the early Kentucky Derbies, and Isaac Murphy, who was born enslaved, won it three times.

The men who ran baseball's leagues shut Black players out, first in 1867 and again in 1887.

Adults also began to organize children's play. Members of a Boston health group put piles of sand in chapel yards for small children in 1885 or 1886, and people in other cities opened playgrounds in the 1890s. Many children had no time to play. Census takers in 1900, who counted everyone in the country, found more than 1.75 million children under 15 at work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first paid team and new games" -->
Harry Wright found ten men to pay for the Cincinnati Red Stockings in 1869, including himself and his brother George. They won all 57 of their games against clubs in the national association of that time. That September they traveled to San Francisco, and they became the first club to play on both coasts. The Brooklyn Atlantics beat them in 1870, in 11 innings, while 20,000 people watched.

**Football.** Rutgers beat Princeton 6 to 4 at New Brunswick, New Jersey, on November 6, 1869, in the first college football game. Each side had 25 players. They could kick the ball or hit it with their fists or hands, but they could not hold it or trip anyone. About 100 people watched. The game looked more like soccer than like football today.

**Basketball.** James Naismith was a young teacher from Canada at a YMCA training school in Springfield, Massachusetts, in the winter of 1891. Luther Gulick told him to take a class that needed a game to play indoors in winter: "Naismith, I want you to take that class and see what you can do with it."

Naismith wrote 13 rules. He asked the janitor for two boxes to use as goals, and the janitor brought two peach baskets instead. Nine players played on each side. Accounts date the first game to December 21 or 29, 1891.

James Connolly, a 27-year-old Harvard student from Boston, won the hop, skip and jump at Athens, Greece, on April 6, 1896. That event is now called the triple jump. It was the first final of the first modern Olympic Games, so Connolly was the first modern Olympic champion. A dean at Harvard had warned him that he might not be let back into the college if he left to compete, and he went anyway.

**Games at home.** Milton Bradley sold a board game called *The Checkered Game of Life* in 1860. Buyers took more than 45,000 copies by 1861. Six years later Bradley printed rules for croquet, a lawn game played by hitting wooden balls with mallets.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The color line in baseball" -->
A color line was a rule, written or unwritten, that kept Black people out. The members of the National Association of Base Ball Players, the group that ran club baseball, put in place a formal ban on Black players and Black clubs in 1867.

Some Black men still played baseball for pay after that. Bud Fowler did in 1878. The Society for American Baseball Research calls him the first acknowledged Black professional baseball player. William Edward White, who had been born enslaved, played one game for Providence in 1879. He lived as a white man and gave his race as white in the census.

Moses Fleetwood Walker and his brother Weldy played for Toledo in the American Association, then a major league, in 1884. They were the first Black major-league players known to the public as Black men.

**The vote in Buffalo.** Cap Anson, the captain of the Chicago club, said in 1887 that his team would not play Newark if Newark's two Black players took the field. They were Walker and the pitcher George Stovey. On July 14, 1887, Newark played Chicago without them.

That same day, at a meeting in Buffalo, New York, the owners of the International League, whom the surviving accounts do not name, voted 6 to 4 to tell the league's secretary, C. D. White, to approve no more contracts with Black players. The owners of teams with only white players voted for the ban, and those with a Black player voted against it.

Two Black players stayed in the league because their contracts had been signed before the vote: Frank Grant of Buffalo and Walker, who moved to Syracuse. Walker played in the league until 1889. After him, no Black player played in the International League until Jackie Robinson joined the Montreal Royals in 1946. The owners in the major leagues never wrote down a rule against Black players. They kept Black players out by an unwritten agreement among themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="moses-fleetwood-walker" name="Moses Fleetwood Walker" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Moses Fleetwood Walker

> **Who:** A catcher, the first major-league player known to the public as a Black man.
> **When and where:** Ohio, Toledo, Newark and Syracuse, 1856 to 1924.

**A letter warned that a mob would attack if Walker played.**

Moses Fleetwood Walker, born in Mount Pleasant, Ohio, in 1856, studied at Oberlin College and then studied law at the University of Michigan. He caught for Toledo in 1883. In a game that year that did not count in the standings, Cap Anson of Chicago used a racist slur about Walker and said his team would never again play a team with Walker on it.

Walker played his first major-league game for Toledo, at Louisville, on May 1, 1884. He played 42 games and batted .263, which means he got a hit in about 26 of every 100 times at bat. He often caught the ball barehanded, with little padding. Toledo's pitcher Tony Mullane later said Walker "was the best catcher I ever worked with, but I disliked a Negro." "Negro" was a word of the time for a Black person. Mullane said he threw his pitches without looking at the signals Walker gave him.

**The letter.** Toledo's manager got a letter in September 1884 warning that a mob would attack if Walker played in Richmond, Virginia. David Zang's account of Walker's life does not name the men who wrote it. The men who ran the Toledo team released Walker before the trip. Walker played for Newark in 1887 and for Syracuse in 1888 and 1889. His contracts had been signed before the International League owners' vote, so he could keep playing.

**Syracuse, 1891.** A group of white men attacked Walker outside a saloon in Syracuse on April 9, 1891. One of them, Patrick Murray, threw a stone that hit Walker in the head. Walker stabbed Murray with a pocketknife and killed him. On June 3, 1891, an all-white jury found Walker not guilty of second-degree murder, which means killing someone on purpose but without planning it ahead.

Walker was found guilty of robbing the mail in September 1898, and he spent a year in jail. He later ran an opera house, a theater, in Cadiz, Ohio. In 1908 he and his brother Weldy published *Our Home Colony*, a book arguing that Black Americans should move to Africa. He died of pneumonia, an infection of the lungs, in Cleveland on May 11, 1924, at 67.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="moses-fleetwood-walker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black jockeys" -->
Oliver Lewis rode the horse Aristides to win the first Kentucky Derby on May 17, 1875. The horse's trainer, Ansel Williamson, had been born enslaved in 1806. Black jockeys won 15 of the first 28 Kentucky Derbies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-murphy" name="Isaac Murphy" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Murphy

> **Who:** A jockey born into slavery who won the Kentucky Derby three times.
> **When and where:** Kentucky and the Eastern racetracks, 1861 to 1896.

**By his own count he won 628 of his 1,412 races.**

Isaac Burns Murphy was born into slavery in Clark County, Kentucky, on January 6, 1861. His father, Jerry Burns, joined the Union Army in 1864 and died that fall in the hospital at Camp Nelson.

Murphy won the Kentucky Derby in 1884, 1890 and 1891. He was the first jockey to win it three times and the first to win it two years in a row. The racing writer Terry Conway writes that Murphy was said to earn $15,000 to $20,000 a year, at a time when the average family of four earned about $1,200. He and his wife, Lucy, bought a 10-room house in Lexington for $10,000 in 1887.

**The fall at Monmouth.** Murphy rode the favorite, Firenze, in a race called the Monmouth Handicap in 1890. He swayed in the saddle and fell off after the finish. The race officials, called stewards, said he was drunk and barred him from riding for a time.

Murphy said he had been drugged. One account says he blamed a sudden loss of weight. Someone had bet heavily against his horse, and that betting made some people suspect a crime. The accounts of the race do not say who placed those bets.

Murphy died in Lexington in February 1896, at 35. Accounts give the cause as pneumonia or heart failure. How much money he left is not known. After he died, Lucy Murphy could not pay back a $5,500 loan from Gus Luigart, and she lost the house in a lawsuit. It was sold at auction in 1900.

He was buried in African Cemetery No. 2 in Lexington. In later years the grave had no marker. He became the first jockey named to the Racing Hall of Fame in 1955. His body was moved in 1967 to a site near the grave of the racehorse Man o' War, and later to the Kentucky Horse Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-murphy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Football deaths" -->
Football players in the 1890s wore no helmets, no mouthpieces and no padding. Reporters at a New York newspaper, the *World*, counted at least eight deaths and more than 200 serious injuries in the 1897 season. Their list included broken bones, fractured skulls, twisted necks and crushed chests.

Richard Von Gammon, a 17-year-old University of Georgia player, dove into a pile of players to make a tackle against the University of Virginia in Atlanta on October 30, 1897. He lay still on the field and died early the next morning of a concussion, which is an injury to the brain from a hard blow to the head.

**A mother's letter.** Georgia's lawmakers voted to ban football at the state's colleges. Von Gammon's mother, Rosalind Burns Gammon, wrote to her representative. She asked that her son's death not be used as an argument against the sport. Governor William Atkinson vetoed the ban on December 7, 1897, which means he refused to sign it and it did not become law.

A Tennessee newspaper, the *Knoxville Tribune*, printed a full-page editorial, an article giving the paper's own opinion, calling for a ban on October 30, 1898. The editorial listed three deaths and gave no names. Two players' heads struck together and one of them died. A player at the bottom of a pile had his neck broken. A player kicked in the head by a heavy shoe died three days later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women on bicycles" -->
Frances Willard learned to ride a bicycle at 53. In 1895 she published a book about it. As a girl in the country she had almost no toys except the ones she made herself, such as a wooden plow. She practiced on the bicycle for three months. She wrote that women who rode "must, when riding, dress more rationally," which meant in clothes that made sense for the job.

Susan B. Anthony told the reporter Nellie Bly, in an article printed on February 2, 1896, that the bicycle "has done more to emancipate women," or set them free, "than anything else in the world." Anthony said that bloomers, which were loose pants gathered at the ankle, or very short skirts were the right clothes for riding.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first playgrounds" -->
Dr. Marie Zakrzewska wrote to a Boston health group, the Massachusetts Emergency and Hygiene Association. She told its leaders that in Berlin's public parks, children rich and poor dug and played in heaps of sand.

**Which year?** Accounts give two different years for the first sand in Boston. Joseph Lee, who wrote a book about helping the poor in 1902, gives 1886. That year, he wrote, members of the association put three piles of yellow sand in the yards of a children's mission, a church charity for children, and of two chapels, which are small churches.

A 1925 history by the Playground and Recreation Association of America says that a large sand pile was put in the yard of the Children's Mission on Parmenter Street in 1885.

The association had ten sand piles by 1887, and about 400 children came each day. The children's favorite games in the sand were "house" and "funeral." By 1900 members of the association ran 21 playgrounds, 20 of them in school yards.

**Other cities.** People in other cities opened playgrounds in the 1890s. Donors in Philadelphia started two summer playgrounds in 1893. A playground opened at Hull-House in Chicago in 1894, with swings, a may-pole, a tall pole that children danced around holding ribbons, a sand pile and benches. A kindergarten teacher and a policeman ran it, and the policeman usually captained one of the baseball teams.

The members of a New York City committee wrote in an 1897 report that New York "has as yet not a single municipal playground," meaning none run by the city. Jacob Riis was the committee's secretary.

People began to build amusement rides in these years too. LaMarcus Thompson opened a roller coaster, the Switchback Railway, at Coney Island in Brooklyn in 1884. A ride cost 5 cents, and the car went just over 6 miles an hour.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The children who had no time to play" -->
Census takers counted working children for the first time in 1870. They found more than 750,000 workers under 15. That count left out children who worked on their own family's farm or in its shop. The number more than doubled by 1900, to more than 1.75 million children under 15 with jobs. After 1880 most working children aged 10 to 14 worked somewhere other than a farm.

One child in eight had a job in 1870, and more than one in five did by 1900. The census counts do not name the employers. About 25,000 of the nearly 100,000 workers in the cloth mills of the South in 1900 were children under 16. Children in the mills worked 12-hour days, six days a week.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cherokee ball play, 1890" -->
The researcher James Mooney described the Eastern Cherokee ball game in North Carolina in 1890. In the fall a game took place somewhere on the reservation, the land where the Eastern Cherokee lived, at least every other week. Each player carried two sticks just under 3 feet long, each with a net of twisted squirrel skin or hemp. For 7 to 28 days before a game, a player did not eat rabbit or frog or hot or salted food, and did not touch a woman.

**The scratching.** Before a game an old man named Standing Water scratched each player with a comb made of seven sharp splinters of turkey leg bone. He pressed the teeth into the skin from shoulder to elbow and from elbow to wrist, and down each leg. He also cut an X on each player's chest and back. The scratches added up to nearly three hundred cuts.

Mooney wrote that the cuts were shallow but painful, and that the players took them willingly as part of the ritual they believed would help them win. No order banning the Cherokee or the Choctaw ball game in these years is on record. The games drew large crowds, including local white people.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Paid teams, leagues and new games turned play into a business in these fifty years, and the men who ran baseball used that power to shut Black players out.
In 1850: no baseball team paid all its players, and nobody had played basketball or a college football game.
In 1900: paid baseball teams played in leagues closed to Black players, Isaac Murphy had won the Kentucky Derby three times, and more than 1.75 million children under 15 were at work.
**Next:** After 1900 adults filled children's free time with clubs, troops and leagues, and football deaths brought college coaches to the White House.
