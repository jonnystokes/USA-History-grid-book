## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 36 of 37 · Sports and Play · 10 eras · about 125 minutes**

**From chunkey at Cahokia to Fortnite, American games moved out of open fields and into leagues, arenas and screens run by adults and companies, and at almost every step someone in charge decided who was allowed to play.**

On September 10, 1674, the judges of the York County court in Virginia punished a tailor named James Bullocke. He had bet 2,000 pounds of tobacco on a race between his mare and a gentleman's horse. Racing, the judges ruled, was "a sport only for Gentlemen," and they fined him. Then they found that the gentleman had fixed the race so that the tailor's mare would win. They sent the gentleman to sit in the stocks for an hour.

This chapter follows play through ten eras, from Native ball games on fields a mile long to games played on phones with strangers. Watch who runs the game. At first the players ran it themselves. Later club members wrote the rules, owners paid the players, adults ran leagues for children, and companies sold games played on a screen. And watch who gets kept out: a tailor in 1674, enslaved riders whose names the race cards left off, Black players after 1887, girls after 1951, Black children at city pools.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Who invented baseball?
- Who was kept out of American games, and how did they get in?
- How has children's free time changed?

**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 6 minutes**

Native nations played games of skill and games of chance long before 1500, and their children made toys out of clay. Nobody wrote down the rules at the time. What survives are objects in the ground, found by archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study the things people left behind. Stone disks for a game called chunkey, and little clay animals made by children, date from these centuries.

Archaeologists have also found split reeds used as dice and wooden pieces kicked along a race course, but no one has dated those. How the games were played is known from the nations' own traditions and from Europeans who watched them in later centuries.

**Two kinds of games.** Stewart Culin sorted Native games into two kinds in a long study printed in 1907 by the Bureau of American Ethnology, a government office in Washington that studied Native peoples.

In games of chance, players threw marked pieces and counted how they landed. In games of skill, players won by how well they used their hands and bodies. Culin found no game like chess, which a player wins by planning alone. Native children played many games with no objects at all, such as tag, he wrote, and he left those out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chunkey at Cahokia" -->
Chunkey was a game played with a stone disk and long poles. One player rolled the stone along the ground. The players ran after it and threw their poles. The aim was to land a pole closest to the spot where the stone came to rest. The stones had rounded edges and hollowed sides, and some had a hole through the middle.

The game began about the year 600, in the area around present-day St. Louis. People built the city of Cahokia in that same area around 1050. By hand, they filled in and leveled a plaza, a huge open square, of about 50 acres. They used the plaza for ceremonies and for chunkey.

**The stones got bigger.** A chunkey stone made before Cahokia existed measures about 2.5 inches across. A stone from the 1100s measures about 3.5 inches. It is made of quartzite, a hard rock, from a single place on the Mississippi River. A shell collar found 90 miles south of Cahokia shows a chunkey player holding a pole and a stone.

Timothy Pauketat, an archaeologist at the University of Illinois, thinks messengers from Cahokia carried a chunkey stone in one hand and a war club in the other. In his account, they went out to the lands around the city to make peace or to make agreements with other peoples. He also writes that chunkey played a large part in how Cahokia's people lived together and how they were governed.

Chunkey stones from about 1300 have turned up as far away as Georgia, and in Maryland at sites in Frederick and Montgomery counties. Most of Cahokia's people had left the city by about 1350, and it was empty by about 1400. Staff at the Jefferson Patterson Park and Museum in Maryland say that people across North America went on playing chunkey after that.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ball games with sticks" -->
Nations in the East, among them the Choctaw, the Huron and the Haudenosaunee, played a ball game with sticks. The Choctaw call their game kapucha toli, which means "stick play." The Huron and the Haudenosaunee, also called the Iroquois, played the game known today as lacrosse. Writers for the Choctaw Nation, on the nation's website, say that stickball "has been the Choctaw national sport for unknown centuries."

No object or written record dates a stick-and-ball game before 1500. Jean de Brebeuf, a French missionary, a man who traveled to other peoples to spread the Christian religion, described Huron lacrosse in 1636. He belonged to the Jesuits, a group of Catholic priests. Another Jesuit missionary described a Choctaw match in 1729, the oldest written account of a Choctaw game.

**How big a game could be.** Fields ran from about 100 yards long to, perhaps, several miles, according to the same Choctaw writers. Some games had several hundred players on each side, and others had twenty or fewer. Among the Choctaw, women played alongside men. Betting was a large part of the game, especially for the women. The Choctaw writers also say that deaths were common in the old games.

People used the game to teach and to heal. The Choctaw have used it to show their young people how Choctaw families were arranged and what men and women each did, the Choctaw writers say. Among the Huron, Brebeuf wrote, a sick person or a healer could call for a lacrosse game to make the sick person well. Whole villages played against each other and bet beaver robes and what Brebeuf called "porcelain collars," his word for collars of wampum, beads made from shells.

Choctaw tradition says that towns sometimes played ball to settle an argument. The two games of this kind that someone wrote down took place long after 1500, around 1790 and 1800.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Games of chance" -->
Pieces for games of chance survive in the Southwest. The archaeologist J. Walter Fewkes dug up split reeds used for a dice game at the Chevlon ruin, about 15 miles east of present-day Winslow, Arizona. Culin took the reeds as a sign that the Hopi and Zuni cane game was old. No one has dated them.

Pieces from a racing game survive too. Runners kicked a small wooden piece ahead of them along a race course. Pieces like these were found in cliff dwellings in Mancos Canyon, Colorado. One is 4 3/4 inches long and marked with lines cut into the wood.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What did children play with?" -->
Most of the children's things that survive from before 1500 are made of clay. Toys made of wood, hide and grass rot away, so few of them last.

The archaeologist Kathryn Kamp studied clay objects about 800 years old from Sinagua village sites in northern Arizona. She measured the fingerprints left in the clay. The ridges of a child's fingerprint are narrower than an adult's. Kamp found that very young Sinagua children made little clay animals, and probably small dishes, to play with. While making them, the children learned how clay behaves.

People in the Southwest made animal figures out of split twigs about 2,000 years ago. Archaeologists had thought the figures were used in religious ceremonies for hunting. Then three of them, two deer and a duck, turned up in a child's grave. Archaeologists now think that some of the figures were toys.

Bacqueville de la Potherie, who wrote about the Huron in the early 1700s, said that Huron children played lacrosse. He wrote that they "never or rarely" played the dice games that Huron women played.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

Nearly everything written about games here in the 1500s describes Native games. The writers were Spanish and French soldiers, sailors and missionaries, and they set down what they saw Native people play. The Spanish soldiers at the forts of St. Augustine and Santa Elena on the Atlantic coast left guns, tools and pottery behind. No record from the 1500s describes what those soldiers played.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Timucua games in Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
René de Laudonnière led a French colony at Fort Caroline, near present-day Jacksonville, Florida, in 1564 and 1565. The Timucua lived there. Laudonnière wrote that they trained their young men to run. In one of their races, the runner who could keep going longest won. The young men also practiced often with bows and arrows.

Laudonnière described a Timucua ball game too. The players set up a tree trunk about 50 feet high in an open space. At the top they fixed a square mat woven from reeds. Whoever hit the mat with the ball won the game.

Jacques Le Moyne, the colony's artist, painted Timucua life. Theodor de Bry, an engraver in Europe, a man who cut pictures into metal plates so they could be printed many times, made prints from the paintings. He never went to Florida. One print shows young men running, shooting arrows and throwing a ball at a target on a tall pole.

**Which record to trust.** Most sources date the print to 1591, and a National Park Service web page gives 1587. The same page states that de Bry mixed in details from other parts of the Americas to sell more copies. Laudonnière's written account is the firmer record of the game.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish games on horseback, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north to the Rio Grande, in what is now New Mexico, in 1598. Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá wrote about that year in a book printed in 1610. Writers for the Museum of New Mexico's magazine, *El Palacio*, state that the colonists' celebrations in 1598 included "tilts with cane spears, running bulls and tilts at the ring."

In a tilt with cane spears, two lines of riders threw light spears made of cane at each other and blocked them with shields. In a tilt at the ring, a rider galloped at full speed and tried to push the point of a lance, a long spear, through a ring and carry it off.

Spanish friars, Catholic religious men who lived by strict rules, began building missions in Florida and Georgia in the 1590s. A mission was a church settlement where friars worked to make Native people Christians. About eighty years later, a friar in Florida set out to end a Native ball game.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 10 minutes**

The men who governed the English and Dutch colonies in the 1600s wrote laws about when and where people could play. In Massachusetts and Plymouth, lawmakers and judges fined people for cards, dice and tavern games. In New Netherland, the Dutch director banned ball games, cards and bowling on Sunday before, between and during church services. Virginia planters, men who owned big farms, raced horses for thousands of pounds of tobacco, and one county court punished a tailor for racing, saying racing was "a sport only for Gentlemen."

Native nations kept playing their own games, and English and Spanish writers described some of them. In Spanish Florida, a friar and the governor worked to end the Apalachee ball game. Almost nothing written in these years describes what children did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bowling at Jamestown, 1611" -->
Sir Thomas Dale arrived at Jamestown in May 1611 with about 300 soldiers, to govern the colony. The colonist Ralph Hamor later wrote that on his first day Dale found the colonists at "their daily and usuall works, bowling in the streetes."

The historians William Kelso and Karen Ordahl Kupperman argue that the colonists were sick and badly fed. In their view, hunger and disease made the men act in ways that could look like laziness.

**Dale's code.** Dale then put the colony under a code of military rules called the Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall. Chapter 21 describes the punishments under those rules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusett football, 1634" -->
William Wood, an Englishman, described the games of the Massachusett people on the coast in a book printed in 1634. He listed football, shooting, running and swimming. When one area played another, Wood wrote, "their goals be a mile long," set on sand as flat as a board. Players kicked a ball about the size of a handball with their bare feet. A game could last two days. The players marked the ground they had won and started again from that spot the next day.

The winners took the prizes hung on the goals: wampum, beaver skins and black otter skins. Players painted themselves before a game, as they did for war. Wood wrote that they did it so that no one could tell who had hurt him and take revenge later. While the men played, the boys played pipes and the women danced and sang. Wood also claimed that one Englishman could beat ten Native players at football.

**Hubbub.** Wood described a dice game called hubbub. A player put five small bones, black on one side and white on the other, in a small smooth tray. He thumped the tray on the ground so that the bones jumped, and scored by the colors that landed face up. The players shouted "Hub, Hub, Hub," and Wood wrote that they could be heard a quarter of a mile away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fines for games in New England" -->
Lawmakers and judges in Massachusetts Bay and Plymouth fined people for playing games, although betting on the games was rare. The historian T. H. Breen found only a little betting in Massachusetts in the 1600s, most of it among poor people and servants, and none among the colony's leaders.

**Tavern games.** The lawmakers of Massachusetts Bay banned shuffleboard, a game of sliding pieces down a long board, in taverns in 1647. Three years later they banned bowling and every other game in taverns as well. They wrote that the games wasted "much precious time" and much wine and beer.

A tavern keeper who allowed the games paid 20 shillings, which made one pound in English money, and each player paid 5 shillings. Anyone who bet money had to pay three times the amount of the bet. A law of 1659 fined anyone caught playing cards or dice anywhere in the colony 5 shillings.

**Fined by name.** The judges of Plymouth Colony's court wrote down the name of each person they fined, wives among them. In June 1679 they fined John Holbrooke one pound for playing cards once in the woods, and "His man, Patricke," a servant who had played with him, 10 shillings. Mathew Gannet paid two pounds for playing cards several times and letting others play cards in his house.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Netherland: Sunday bans, the goose and golf" -->
New Netherland was the Dutch colony that is now New York. Peter Stuyvesant was its director, the man who governed it. He and the men of his council, who helped him govern, banned work on Sunday on October 26, 1656. They also banned "playing Ball, Cards, Tricktrack, Tennis, Cricket or Ninepins" (tricktrack was a board game like backgammon, and ninepins was bowling at nine wooden pins) before, between or during church services, along with boat and wagon trips. Anyone caught paid a double fine.

**Pulling the goose.** Dutch farmers brought a game from home called pulling the goose. They hung a goose by a rope between two poles and greased its neck and head with oil or soap. Riders galloped past and grabbed at the goose to pull it down. The winner was called king of the festival. The Dutch records printed in 1868 do not say whether the goose was alive.

Stuyvesant called the game "heathenish and Popish," which meant un-Christian and Catholic, and he banned it. Some farmers held it anyway. They were fined, and some were put in jail, even though the leaders of the town of New Amsterdam protested. Those printed Dutch records do not name the farmers or the officials who punished them.

The game first came to New Netherland in about 1654, and the records do not make clear in which year the farmers were fined. Farmers on Manhattan asked to hold the game in February 1658, and Stuyvesant refused. One later book dates his ban to that same month.

**Golf in the streets.** People in Fort Orange, now Albany, complained that men playing golf in the streets broke windows and put passers-by in danger of being hurt. The town's officers banned golf in the streets on December 10, 1659. The fine was 25 guilders, the Dutch unit of money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horse racing for tobacco" -->
The English took New Netherland in 1664 and renamed it New York. A year later Colonel Richard Nicolls, its first English governor, laid out a two-mile racecourse on the Hempstead Plains of Long Island. He named it Newmarket, after a racecourse in England. According to the Encyclopaedia Britannica, he offered a silver cup to the best horses. Another account gives the prize as a silver crown or a bushel of wheat.

Native people had lived on that land. English settlers from Stamford, Connecticut, bought the land of the present towns of Hempstead and North Hempstead in 1643. They bought it from Tackapausha, a Marsapeague leader, and six other Native leaders. The town's history does not say what the leaders were paid or how they understood the sale.

**Quarter races.** Virginia planters ran quarter races, in which two horses sprinted a quarter mile along a dirt track. Men from leading families such as the Randolphs, Jeffersons and Washingtons appear in county court records of races. The largest bet in those records was made in Northumberland County in 1693. John Baker and John Haynie bet 4,000 pounds of tobacco and 40 shillings on their horses, Prince and Smoaker.

In early races a rider could shove, whip or knee the other rider and try to knock him off his horse. Later, riders agreed to "fair Rideing." They also chose a starter, judges and a stakeholder, the man who held the bets, before the race.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-bullocke" name="James Bullocke" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Bullocke

> **Who:** A tailor in York County, Virginia, punished for racing his horse against a gentleman's.
> **When and where:** September 10, 1674, the York County court, Virginia.

**The bet was 2,000 pounds of tobacco on one horse race.**

James Bullocke bet a gentleman, Mathew Slader, that his mare would beat Slader's horse. On September 10, 1674, the justices of the York County court, the county's judges, punished Bullocke for it.

The justices ruled that it was "contrary to Law for a Labourer," a man who worked with his hands, "to make a race, being a sport only for Gentlemen." The court order, as it was printed in 1894, fined Bullocke 100 pounds of tobacco. The historian T. H. Breen read the same court book and gives the fine as 200 pounds.

**The fixed race.** The justices also found that the race had been fixed. Slader had signed a promise that his horse "should runn out of the way," which meant it would leave the track, so that Bullocke's mare would win. The justices called it "an apparent cheate." They ordered Slader, the gentleman, to sit for one hour in the stocks, a wooden frame that locked a person's ankles while he sat in a public place.

W. G. Stanard, a historian writing in 1894, called the court order the earliest notice of racing in Virginia's records.

**Not known:** which law the justices meant. The order names none, and no such law has been found in the colony's printed laws.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-bullocke" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Apalachee ball game banned, 1676" -->
The Apalachee and Yustaga nations of northern Florida played a ball game between towns. A town sent a messenger to challenge another town, naming the day and the number of players, about forty or fifty. Players kicked a small ball, a little bigger than a musket ball, at a single tall pole. Eleven hits won.

Fray Juan de Paiva was the Spanish friar at the mission of San Luis, where Tallahassee is now. He wrote a long paper against the game, dated September 23, 1676, with the royal interpreter Diego Salvador and Juan Mendoza, an Apalachee leader who interpreted for the church.

**What the players did.** Paiva described what players did to each other. They stepped on faces, heads and bellies. They pulled at arms and legs without caring whether the joints came apart. When a player swallowed the ball, others made him throw it up by squeezing his windpipe or kicking him in the stomach.

Paiva listed broken legs and ribs, people who had lost the use of one or both hands, and people blinded in one eye. He wrote of two players he knew who had been killed at San Luis. Five games in a row in 1676 ended in open fighting, he wrote, and soldiers were present at the games.

Paiva wanted the game banned. He also charged that people left their fields unplanted and their houses open to thieves while they watched. Governor Pablo de Hita Salazar backed him. So did Fray Francisco Perete, the head in Florida of the Franciscans, one group of Catholic friars.

**The poles came down.** Paiva wrote that the Apalachee chiefs pulled down the ball poles and put "the holy cross" in their place, and that Captain Juan Fernández de Florencia thanked the chiefs for it on the governor's behalf. Some people, he wrote, asked the governor in writing not to end the game. The historian Amy Bushnell dates the Spanish campaign against the game from 1675 to 1684. The Talimali Band of the Apalachee lives in Louisiana today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved people and children" -->
**A law against gathering.** Members of the Virginia General Assembly, the colony's lawmakers, passed a law against gatherings of enslaved people on June 8, 1680. They named it "An act for preventing Negroes Insurrections." "Negroes" was a word of the time for Black people. An insurrection is an uprising, so the lawmakers wrote the law to stop enslaved people from rising up against the men who held them.

The law's opening words stated that meetings of enslaved people "under pretence of feasts and burialls" were dangerous. The lawmakers meant that the feasts and funerals were only an excuse to meet.

An enslaved person who left the owner's land without a written pass was to get "twenty lashes on his bare back well layd on." That meant twenty hard blows with a whip on the bare skin. An enslaved person who raised a hand against "any Christian" was to get thirty lashes.

If an enslaved person hid out and fought back against the men sent by law to catch him, the same law said those men could kill him. Two years later the lawmakers barred enslaved people from gathering at another owner's plantation for more than four hours at a time.

**Children.** No one in the 1600s wrote down how a child spent the hours of a day. The only dated records of enslaved people's free time in these years are the laws of 1680 and 1682 that punished them for gathering.

The oldest marble that can be dated at Jamestown is made of brown stoneware, a hard kind of pottery, and dates from the middle of the 1600s. It may have been made in Germany, the Netherlands or England. Historic Jamestowne's description of the marble does not say whether a child or a grown person played with it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 6 minutes**

Horse races and cockfights, and the bets men made on them, fill most of the written record of play from 1700 to 1750. Virginia planters raced horses on straight quarter-mile paths. By the late 1730s they also held race meetings that lasted several days, with prizes for fiddlers, wrestlers and runners. Men in South Carolina and Massachusetts held cockfights, in which two roosters fought until one was dead.

No record shows adults organizing children's play into teams or clubs, and almost no one wrote down what children played. The few dated records are a boys' footrace at a Virginia race meeting in 1737 and an English children's book of 1744 with a rhyme called "Base-Ball."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses in Virginia" -->
Robert Beverley wrote in 1705 that young Virginians loved to hunt wild horses. The horses were born in the woods of the upland country and had never been handled. An unmarked horse belonged to whoever caught it first.

The minister Hugh Jones wrote in 1724 that "almost every ordinary Person keeps a Horse." He had seen men spend a morning catching a horse in the woods, only to ride two or three miles to church, to court, "or to a Horse-Race."

**Who could race?** Historians disagree. In a quarter race, two horses sprinted along a straight, narrow path about a quarter mile long. Historians at Mount Vernon, George Washington's home in Virginia, write that owners rode their own farm horses, so ordinary farmers could race. The historian T. H. Breen found that gentlemen tried to keep racing to themselves, as the York County justices did in 1674.

A court record of 1678 shows a "boy" riding another man's horse, so not every rider owned his horse. The records of these races name the owners of the horses and seldom say who rode.

**Horses from England.** Planters began to bring racehorses from England. In 1730 a man named James Patton brought over a stallion, a male horse kept for breeding, named Bulle Rock. The horse was then 21 years old. Bulle Rock is thought to be the first English Thoroughbred, a breed raised in England for racing, brought to the colonies. Between 1740 and 1775 planters brought at least fifty stallions and thirty mares from England to Virginia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Race meetings with prizes, 1736 to 1739" -->
**Prizes for fiddlers.** A group of gentlemen in Hanover County, Virginia, offered prizes at Page's Warehouse on St. Andrew's Day, a church holiday, November 30, 1736. Their notice in the newspaper offered a hunting saddle to the fastest horse over a quarter mile. It offered a violin made in Cremona, Italy, to the best country fiddler. There were other prizes for dancing, singing, football, jumping and wrestling. A pair of silk stockings was to go to the young woman whom the crowd judged prettiest.

A later notice asked everyone who came to behave "with Decency and Sobriety," which meant politely and without getting drunk. Breen writes that poorer people apparently were welcome to come and watch.

A notice in the *Virginia Gazette* of July 1, 1737, stated that there would be horse races every Saturday until October near Williamsburg. That November, John Pinkerton held three days of races at the Old Field track in Virginia, with a three-mile race each day. Mount Vernon's historians call it the first documented long-distance race in Virginia.

Pinkerton's meeting also had a fiddling contest, a running race for boys, a contest for the prettiest unmarried woman, and a dinner with singing and dancing. Different sources spell the host's name Pinkerton or Brickerton, and the right spelling is not known.

**A one-mile course.** A Williamsburg fair in December 1739 had three days of races. On the first day, eight horses raced around a one-mile course for a saddle worth 40 shillings. Colonel Chiswell's horse Edgcomb won. The second horse won a bridle and the third won a whip.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cricket" -->
Colonists also played cricket, the English bat-and-ball game. William Byrd II owned the Westover plantation in Virginia. His diary for April 25, 1709, records that he played cricket and then "went to cricket again till dark." In Georgia in 1737, William Stephens wrote that townsmen, landowners and servants gathered in the main square to play cricket.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cockfighting and gander pulling" -->
In a cockfight, two roosters fought in a ring. Their owners strapped sharp spurs made of silver, steel or bone to the birds' legs, and the birds cut each other with the spurs. The fight went on until one bird was dead. Men bet on which bird would win. William Byrd II, Robert Carter and George Washington were among the men who followed cockfighting.

An advertisement in the *South Carolina Gazette* listed a cockfight at the house of a Mrs. Eldridge in 1732. Men in Salem, Massachusetts, held a cockfight in the Town House in 1744. Both Black and white people came to cockfights and bet on them. Some critics complained that "genteel people," meaning the well-off, mixed at the fights with people they called "the vulgar and debased," meaning common and low.

**Gander pulling.** Gander pulling was another game played on live birds. Men tied a live gander, a male goose, upside down by its feet to a tree branch and greased its neck. Riders galloped past and grabbed at the neck until one of them pulled the bird's head off.

Riders fell from their horses, and some lost fingers. In Virginia the game was often held on the Monday after Easter, and the crowd bet on the riders. Accounts describe gander pulling through the colonial years, but none gives a dated event before 1750.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children" -->
The London bookseller John Newbery printed *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book*, a book of rhymes and pictures for children, in 1744. He sold it with a ball for a boy or a pincushion for a girl. Its rhyme "Base-Ball" is the first known use of that word in print. It reads: "The Ball once struck off, / Away flies the Boy / To the next destin'd Post, / And then Home with Joy."

John Thorn, the historian of Major League Baseball, suggests that the game in the book may have been played without a bat. No copy of the 1744 printing survives. The rhyme is known only from later printings, such as one made in Worcester, Massachusetts, in 1787, and the words of the 1744 rhyme are taken from those later copies.

The records of these years do not show how much free time children had. Nor do they show how that time differed for free children, enslaved children and indentured children, who were bound to work for a master for a set number of years.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 10 minutes**

The records of these fifty years start to say who owned the horses and who rode them. In Virginia the owners of racehorses came more and more from the top ranks of society, and enslaved men and boys often rode. The enslaved people at one Virginia plantation had a holiday on Easter Monday 1774 and went to cockfights. That June the planter's son Bob Carter pestered his tutor, his private teacher, for leave to go to a cockfight too.

The delegates to the First Continental Congress, men chosen in each colony to meet together, agreed in 1774 to stop buying British goods. They also promised to discourage horse racing, cockfighting and games.

People still played ball games. Soldiers at Valley Forge played a running game called base, and the voters of Pittsfield, Massachusetts, banned "baseball" near their new meeting house in 1791. No record from these years shows leagues, paid teams, or towns sharing written rules.

Native nations kept playing their own ball games. In Hawaii, British sailors wrote descriptions of surfing in 1779. Chapter 23 tells how those islands later became part of the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native games written down" -->
James Adair described chunkey as the Choctaw played it, in a book printed in 1775. Only one or two players played on each side. The field was a square of ground, cleared and spread with fine sand, near the town house, the building where the town's leaders met.

Players ran after the rolling stone with 8-foot poles "most part of the day." They bet their silver ornaments, their nose, finger and ear rings, and even their clothes. Each town kept its chunkey stones from one generation to the next. Nobody buried a stone with the dead.

**The beaver pond.** The Creek and the Choctaw both claimed a large beaver pond on the Noxubee River, in present-day Mississippi, around 1790. Beaver furs sold well at the trading towns of Mobile and Pensacola. The two nations agreed to decide the claim with a game of stickball.

The account of that game comes from Stonie Hadjo, a Creek-born man whom the Choctaw adopted. He lived in Noxubee County, Mississippi, and often told the story to the white settlers there before he died in 1836. H. S. Halbert later wrote the story down, and H. B. Cushman printed it in 1899. Stonie Hadjo said that ten thousand people camped around the field. The Creek team won.

A fight broke out after the game, and the tellings differ on who started it. In Stonie Hadjo's telling, a Choctaw player insulted a Creek player, and the two began to fight. Choctaw accounts say the Creek player threw a petticoat, a woman's underskirt, on the Choctaw player first.

The fight spread to the warriors on both sides, who used sticks, knives, guns, tomahawks and bows and arrows. It lasted through the night until the chiefs arrived the next morning and stopped it. Stonie Hadjo said that the ball players "almost to a man perished," which means that nearly every ball player was killed. His account gives no count of the dead. The Choctaw kept the pond, even though the Creek team had won the game, and the account does not say why.

Writers for the Choctaw Nation describe two games around the 1790s played to settle land claims with the Creek, and both ended in battles. H. B. Cushman dates the second game, near the Black Warrior River, to about 1800.

Far to the north, the Mohawk and the Seneca played lacrosse at Grand River, in present-day Ontario, in 1797, with about 600 players on each side.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Surfing in Hawaii, 1779" -->
No outside nation ruled Hawaii in 1779. Chiefs governed the islands, and Kalaniʻōpuʻu ruled the island of Hawaii. The islands were joined into one Hawaiian Kingdom starting in 1795.

British sailors with Captain James Cook watched Hawaiians surfing at Kealakekua Bay, on the island of Hawaii, in January 1779. The ship's surgeon's mate, David Samwell, the assistant to the ship's doctor, described surf riding there on January 22, 1779.

Lieutenant James King also wrote an account, which was printed in 1784. When storms made the waves highest, twenty or thirty people each took "a long narrow board, rounded at the ends," and swam out together. They dove under the breaking waves until they were past them. Then they lay on their boards and rode the largest wave back to shore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owned the horses and who rode them" -->
Almost every town in Virginia held yearly races by 1772, according to the English traveler J. F. D. Smyth. At Williamsburg the races ran for a week each spring and fall. The winner on the first day took a purse, a prize in money, of 100 pounds.

**Jockey clubs.** Horse owners in Fredericksburg, Petersburg, Richmond and other Virginia towns formed jockey clubs by the late 1700s. A jockey is a rider in horse races, but these clubs were groups of horse owners, and the members held races for one another. George Washington helped manage a race at Alexandria in May 1761.

The historian Gabrielle McCoy writes that the owners of racehorses came more and more from the top ranks of society. She writes that the riders "often came from the bottom of the social hierarchy," the ranking of people from the most powerful to the least. They were "enslaved men and boys."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="austin-curtis" name="Austin Curtis" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Austin Curtis

> **Who:** An enslaved jockey and horse trainer who became a free man.
> **When and where:** Halifax, North Carolina, and the racecourses of Virginia and North Carolina, from the 1770s to 1807.

**He kept his enslaver's racehorses safe from British raiders.**

Willie Jones of Halifax, North Carolina, held Austin Curtis in slavery. In the 1770s Curtis rode Jones's horses in races in Virginia and North Carolina. During the Revolutionary War, British troops raided the Carolinas for horses to breed, and Curtis kept Jones's horses safe from them.

**The petition.** On December 5, 1791, Jones asked the lawmakers of North Carolina to free Curtis. He wrote that Curtis had shown "attachment to his Country during the War" and "fidelity," or loyalty, "to his Master," and so "has demonstrated that he deserves to be free." The lawmakers granted the request, and Curtis became free. His name was legally changed to Austin Curtis Jones.

Curtis kept working with horses as a trainer. He came to own more than 300 acres of land, and he bought the freedom of his son William. He and his wife, Nancy, had eleven children.

Curtis died at Halifax on December 10, 1807, at about 50 years old. A Raleigh newspaper, the *Minerva*, printed a notice of his death under the name Austin Jones on January 5, 1808. It called him "well known for many years past, as keeper of race horses," and said that he "particularly excelled" at managing them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="austin-curtis" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A plantation tutor's journal, 1773 and 1774" -->
Philip Fithian taught the children of the planter Robert Carter at Nomini Hall, Virginia, from 1773 to 1774. He kept a journal. It records how the Carter children and the people Carter enslaved spent their free hours.

Easter Monday fell on April 4, 1774. Fithian wrote that the enslaved people had a holiday until Wednesday morning and were "at Cock Fights through the County." The next day his pupils complained that they had to study while the enslaved workers had the day off.

**Sunday.** Fithian saw a ring of enslaved people at the stable fighting cocks before breakfast on Sunday, April 10. Other enslaved people were digging the small garden plots that Carter allowed them to grow food on. Fithian explained that they did all their own work on Sundays because they worked for the Carters every other day.

On two winter evenings in 1774, enslaved people gathered in the schoolroom to play the fiddle and the banjo and to dance. Two of the Carter boys, Ben and Harry, joined them. The first time, Fithian sent them all away.

Another son, Bob, "teaz'd" Fithian, meaning he pestered him, on June 18 to let him go to a cockfight and a horse race two miles away. Fithian let him go after Bob promised to be home by sunset. Pupils at another school had "barred out" their teacher, Mr. Goodlet, in December 1773. They locked him out of the schoolhouse until he agreed to give them a Christmas holiday until January 6.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fist fights with no rules" -->
Fithian wrote on August 29, 1774, that four young men had arranged two fist fights near Mr. Lane's place. He listed what such fighters did, including kicking, scratching, biting, choking, "Gouging" and "Dismembring," which meant tearing or biting off parts of the other man's body. A crowd came to watch.

**Gouging.** Gouging was a kind of fighting in which only weapons were banned. A fighter tried to push his thumbs into the other man's eye socket and force the eye out. Jim L. Sumner, writing for NCpedia, says most historians think fighters seldom lost an eye. He writes that the fights still risked broken bones, scars, crippling injuries and death.

Gabriel Johnston, the governor of North Carolina, complained about this kind of fighting in 1746. North Carolina's lawmakers made it a crime to cut out tongues, pull out eyes, or bite off noses or fingers. Sumner's article does not give the year of that law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution: a pause in racing and a game of base" -->
**A pause in racing.** The delegates to the First Continental Congress agreed to the Continental Association, a pact among the colonies, on October 20, 1774. In it they agreed to stop buying goods from Britain, starting on December 1, 1774. They also promised to discourage "all horse-racing, and all kinds of games, cock fighting," plays and "other expensive diversions," meaning ways to have fun.

Members of the jockey clubs of Annapolis and New York called off their races that year. Four years later, on October 12, 1778, the members of Congress asked the lawmakers of each state to pass laws to stop "horse racing, gaming" and plays.

**Base at Valley Forge.** Soldiers in the Continental Army still played games. George Ewing was an ensign, a young officer, in the 3rd New Jersey Regiment, a unit of soldiers. At Valley Forge in April 1778 he wrote that the men drilled in the afternoon and "playd at base this evening."

General Washington dined with General Henry Knox on May 4, 1778. Afterward he played wicket, a bat-and-ball game like cricket, with Ewing and other officers. After the war, in 1793, Virginia's lawmakers banned bets of more than 7 dollars on horse races or "any other sport or pastime."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Toys and ball games" -->
George Washington married Martha Custis in January 1759. Her children, Jacky and Patsy, were about six and four. In his first order of goods from London he asked for 10 shillings' worth of toys. He also ordered six little books for children learning to read, and a doll dressed in the latest fashion. When that doll was lost or broken, he ordered another that cost a guinea, which was 21 shillings. He also ordered a box of gingerbread toys and sugar figures.

Archaeologists at Mount Vernon found a small limestone marble at the House for Families. Most of the people George Washington enslaved at his Mansion House Farm lived in that building. Mount Vernon's historians think the marble may have belonged to one of the enslaved children who lived and worked there.

A reference book on leisure in America states that at fairs and on court days, the days when the county court met, poorer people ran footraces, wrestled, jumped, bowled and played football. They played for prizes such as a purse of money or a bottle of liquor, while wealthy people watched.

The printer Isaiah Thomas published *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book* in Worcester, Massachusetts, in 1787, with the English rhyme and a picture of "Base-Ball."

**Baseball in Pittsfield.** Voters at a town meeting in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, passed a rule on September 5, 1791, to protect the windows of their new meeting house, the town's main public building. They banned "any game of wicket, cricket, baseball, batball, football, cats, fives, or any other game played with ball" within 80 yards of the building. The rule is the oldest known record of people in an American town playing a game called baseball.
