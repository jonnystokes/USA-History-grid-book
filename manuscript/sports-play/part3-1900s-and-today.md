## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 22 minutes**

Clubs, troops and leagues run by adults filled more and more of children's free time in the first half of the 1900s. Adults set up 4-H clubs in 1902, the Boy Scouts in 1910, the Girl Scouts in 1912 and Little League baseball in 1939. Children also spent many free hours watching and listening, at Saturday movies, beside the radio and over comic books. In Muncie, Indiana, in the 1930s, at least half of the children at Saturday movies stayed five or six hours.

At least 11 people died of football injuries in 1905, and President Theodore Roosevelt called coaches to the White House. The white owners of the major leagues, the top professional baseball teams, kept Black players out until Jackie Robinson played for Brooklyn in 1947. Black players had their own leagues from 1920. In the 1920s and 1930s, white officials and white swimmers in many Northern cities separated the public pools by race and kept Black children out of the pools where other children learned to swim.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Clubs and troops for children" -->
A. B. Graham started a youth program in Clark County, Ohio, in 1902. Its first clubs were a Tomato Club and a Corn Growing Club, and they are counted among the beginnings of 4-H. William D. Boyce, a Chicago publisher, filed the legal papers that made the Boy Scouts of America an official organization on February 8, 1910.

Juliette Gordon Low signed up 18 girls in Savannah, Georgia, on March 12, 1912, as the first troop of what she called Girl Guides. The name changed to Girl Scouts in 1913. The Savannah girls started a basketball league and went camping.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Choctaw and Chickasaw ball play, 1903" -->
Choctaw and Chickasaw people met near Allen, in Indian Territory, each spring and fall to race horses and play the stick-and-ball game. Allen is now in Oklahoma. In the spring of 1903 about 300 people came and bet on a game. A player hit a Choctaw player with a ball stick, and a fight spread through the crowd. The Choctaw Nation's mounted police, called Lighthorsemen, and US marshals, who were federal police officers, stopped it. After that fight the twice-yearly games between the two nations ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many died playing football in 1905?" -->
At least 11 people died of football injuries in the 1905 season, and counts of all the deaths tied to football that year run from 18 to 25. The firmest count comes from the writer Aaron Gordon, who later checked the newspaper reports. He found about 20 deaths tied to football, 11 of them directly from football injuries, and the cause of the others was not clear.

The NCAA's published history of its own beginnings states 18 deaths and 149 serious injuries. Reporters at the *Chicago Tribune* counted 19 deaths, and reporters at another newspaper, the *Cincinnati Commercial Tribune*, counted 25.

The dead included college players and high-school boys. Harold Moore, 19, a Union College player, was knocked out in a game against New York University. He died six hours later of bleeding inside his brain. In Rockville, Indiana, a broken rib pierced the heart of Carl Osborne, a high-school player, and killed him at once. Robert Brown, 16, of Sedalia, Missouri, was paralyzed by a neck injury and died without waking.

**The White House meeting.** President Theodore Roosevelt met coaches and graduates from Harvard, Yale and Princeton at the White House on October 9, 1905. They signed a statement against rough play. That December, Henry MacCracken, the head of New York University, called a meeting of college leaders for December 9.

At a second meeting, on December 28, the men from 62 colleges formed a group. Its members renamed it the National Collegiate Athletic Association, the NCAA, in 1910. The rules of the game changed for 1906. A team now had to gain 10 yards, not 5, to keep the ball. Jumping over the line was banned, and the forward pass was allowed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-johnson" name="Jack Johnson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jack Johnson

> **Who:** A Black heavyweight boxing champion. After he beat a white former champion in 1910, white crowds attacked Black people across the country.
> **When and where:** Galveston, Texas, Sydney, Reno, Chicago and Leavenworth, 1878 to 1946.

**His opponent said he fought to prove "a white man is better."**

Jack Johnson, born in Galveston, Texas, in 1878, beat Tommy Burns in Sydney, Australia, on December 26, 1908. That made him the heavyweight boxing champion of the world, the champion of the boxers in the heaviest weight class.

He fought James Jeffries, a white former champion, in Reno, Nevada, on July 4, 1910. Before the fight Jeffries said he was fighting "for the sole purpose of proving that a white man is better than a Negro." "Negro" was a word of the time for a Black person. Jeffries's own helpers stopped the fight in the 15th round, and Johnson won.

**That night.** White crowds attacked Black people in more than 25 states. Between 11 and 26 people were killed, and hundreds were hurt. In Uvalda, Georgia, white gunmen fired into a work camp and killed three Black workers. In New York City a crowd clubbed a Black man to death. In Norfolk, Virginia, more than 300 white Navy sailors hunted Black people in the streets. The records do not name the people who were killed or the people who killed them.

Officials in many states and cities banned showing the film of the fight. The members of Congress banned carrying boxing films from one state to another in 1912. The ban lasted until 1940.

**The arrest.** Johnson opened a nightclub in Chicago, the Café de Champion, in July 1912, and Black and white customers mixed there. He was arrested on October 18, 1912, under the Mann Act, a federal law of 1910. It made it a crime to take "any woman or girl" across state lines "for the purpose of prostitution or debauchery, or for any other immoral purpose." Prostitution means selling sex, and debauchery meant wild, immoral living.

Federal prosecutors, the lawyers who bring charges for the government, used the law against Johnson because he traveled with white women. An all-white jury, a group of citizens who decide whether a person is guilty, found him guilty in June 1913. Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis ordered him to spend a year and a day in prison. Johnson fled to Canada and Europe and stayed away for seven years. He gave himself up on July 20, 1920, and served his sentence at Leavenworth prison in Kansas.

He died in a car crash near Franklinton, North Carolina, on June 10, 1946, after the staff of a diner that served only white people refused to serve him. President Donald Trump pardoned him on May 24, 2018. A pardon is an official act by a president or governor that forgives a crime and ends its punishment.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black jockeys forced out" -->
Jimmy Winkfield of Kentucky won the Kentucky Derby, a horse race held each spring in Louisville, in 1901 and 1902. No Black jockey has won it since. He left to ride in Russia in 1904, and later he rode in France. A Louisville newspaper, the *Courier-Journal*, printed the headline "Negro Jockeys Now Extinct" on January 21, 1912.

**How it happened.** Two economists, scholars who study money and business, Michael Leeds and Hugh Rockoff, studied why Black jockeys were driven out of American racing. They found that white jockeys attacked Black jockeys during races. The white riders boxed them in, ran them into the rail and hit them with their whips. Owners then stopped hiring Black jockeys, so that their horses would not be hurt. Henry King rode in the 1921 Kentucky Derby. No Black jockey rode in it again until 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jim Thorpe and his medals" -->
Jim Thorpe was a Sac and Fox athlete, born in 1887 near Prague, in what is now Oklahoma. He played football at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School for the coach Glenn "Pop" Warner. At the 1912 Olympic Games in Stockholm, Sweden, he won the pentathlon, a contest of five track and field events, and the decathlon, a contest of ten. In the decathlon he beat the second-place man by 688 points.

**The newspaper report.** Roy Johnson, a reporter for the *Worcester Telegram* in Massachusetts, reported in January 1913 that Thorpe had been paid to play minor-league baseball, in the lower leagues below the major leagues, in North Carolina in 1909 and 1910. Accounts of his pay run from $2 a game to $35 a week. Olympic athletes then had to be amateurs, people who were never paid to play a sport.

Thorpe wrote to James E. Sullivan of the Amateur Athletic Union that he "was simply an Indian schoolboy and did not know all about such things." James Ring Adams wrote in the magazine of the National Museum of the American Indian that Warner and Moses Friedman, the head of the Carlisle school, may have written the letter for him.

Sullivan and the other officials of the Amateur Athletic Union took away Thorpe's amateur standing. The members of the International Olympic Committee struck out his wins. Their own rules allowed a complaint only within 30 days of the Games, and the newspaper report came about six months later. Warner took the medals from Thorpe's house and sent them back to Stockholm. Hugo Wieslander, who had come second in the decathlon, refused the gold medal.

Thorpe played major-league baseball for the New York Giants and other teams from 1913 to 1915 and from 1917 to 1919. He played pro football for the Canton Bulldogs for $250 a game. In 1920 he became the first president of the league that later took the name National Football League. In his last years he worked as a film extra, a laborer and a doorman. He died of heart failure in 1953, at 65.

**The medals restored.** A researcher, Florence Ridlon, found the 30-day rule in the 1912 rule book in 1982. On October 13 of that year, the leaders of the International Olympic Committee named Thorpe a co-champion, sharing each title with the second-place man: Hugo Wieslander in the decathlon and Ferdinand Bie in the pentathlon. On July 15, 2022, they named him the only champion of both events.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Baseball and the Negro Leagues" -->
The Boston Americans beat the Pittsburgh Pirates 5 games to 3 in the first modern World Series, in October 1903. The owners of the two teams, Henry Killilea of Boston and Barney Dreyfuss of Pittsburgh, arranged it. About 100,000 people watched the eight games. There was no series in 1904, because the owners of the New York Giants refused to play.

**By 1920:** Football had new rules after the deaths of 1905, Black jockeys were nearly gone from American racing, and the owners of Black baseball teams were about to start a league of their own.

**A league of their own.** Rube Foster, the owner and manager of the Chicago American Giants, met other owners of Black teams at a YMCA in Kansas City on February 13, 1920. Together they started the Negro National League. It had eight teams in its first year. The league closed in 1931, during the Great Depression, when millions of people lost their jobs. Teams in other Negro Leagues kept playing until 1948.

Major League Baseball's leaders began counting seven Negro Leagues of 1920 to 1948 as major leagues in December 2020. That added about 3,400 players to the major-league record. On May 28, 2024, they added the Negro League players' numbers to the official record books.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Babe Ruth

> **Who:** A pitcher and home-run hitter for Boston and New York.
> **When and where:** Baltimore, Boston and New York, 1895 to 1948.

**His school's records called him "incorrigible," a child no one can control.**

George Herman Ruth, born in Baltimore in 1895, was seven when he entered St. Mary's Industrial School for Boys on June 13, 1902. It was a reform school, a school meant to correct boys' behavior, and an orphanage, a home for children without parents. He spent most of the next 12 years there. A teacher there, Brother Matthias, taught him baseball.

Jacques Kelly, a writer for the *Baltimore Sun*, wrote in 1995 that Ruth's parents decided to place him at the school. The school's records listed the boy as "incorrigible," Kelly wrote. Writers have argued about the parents' reasons for many years.

**The sale.** Ruth pitched for the Boston Red Sox from 1914 to 1919. The owner of the Boston team, Harry Frazee, sold him to the New York Yankees for $100,000. The sale was made in December 1919 and announced in January 1920.

Ruth hit 54 home runs in 1920 and 60 in 1927, and he finished his career with 714. In 1936 he was one of the first five players chosen for the Baseball Hall of Fame. Cancer in the back of his nose and throat killed him on August 16, 1948, at 53.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="babe-ruth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Movies, radio and comic books" -->
Children in the 1920s and 1930s spent many of their free hours at the movies. Walt Disney started Mickey Mouse Clubs that met at Saturday matinees, the afternoon showings, in 1929. Richard Butsch gathered studies of movie audiences from the 1930s in a study printed in 2001. In Muncie, Indiana, at least half of the children at Saturday matinees stayed five or six hours. In a working-class part of New Haven, Connecticut, more than half the children went twice a week or more.

Researchers of the time found that children were choosing movies over outdoor games. A child in the Bronx, in New York City, said that when the film broke, the children booed, stamped their feet and often got into fist fights.

**Worried adults.** The mayor of Chelsea, Massachusetts, closed a theater in 1930 because of how children in the audience behaved. Researchers paid by the Payne Fund, a private group that gave money to good causes, carried out 13 studies of how watching movies changed children's behavior from 1929 to 1933.

*Little Orphan Annie*, a radio show, began in Chicago in 1930 and was broadcast across the country from April 6, 1931. It had about 6 million listeners of all ages before it went off the air in 1942.

**Comic books.** Superman first appeared in a comic book, *Action Comics* number 1, on April 18, 1938. A comic book cost 10 cents. A survey for the publishers in 1944 found that 95 of every 100 boys and 91 of every 100 girls aged 6 to 11 read comic books, about 12 a month.

Sterling North, a newspaper book editor in Chicago, called comic books "badly drawn, badly written and badly printed" in 1940. He wrote that they strained children's eyes and nerves. Comic books were burned in the 1940s. The Comic Book Legal Defense Fund's history of these burnings does not name who burned them.

Lizzie Magie patented a board game called *The Landlord's Game* in 1904. Monopoly grew out of it, and Parker Brothers began selling Monopoly on November 5, 1935.

**By 1940:** Children spent hours each week at the movies and with comic books, Babe Didrikson and Jesse Owens had won Olympic gold, and the owners of the major leagues still kept every Black player out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="babe-didrikson-zaharias" name="Babe Didrikson Zaharias" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Babe Didrikson Zaharias

> **Who:** A track star, basketball player and golfer who won Olympic gold and helped start women's professional golf.
> **When and where:** Texas, Los Angeles and golf courses in the United States and Britain, 1911 to 1956.

**In three hours she won five events and tied for a sixth.**

Mildred Didrikson was born in Port Arthur, Texas, in 1911, the sixth of seven children of Norwegian immigrants. She became known as Babe. Managers at the Employers Casualty Insurance Company in Dallas hired her in 1930 to do office work and to play on the company's women's basketball team, the Golden Cyclones.

At the national track championships of 1932 she was the company's whole team. In three hours she won five events and tied for first in a sixth. She scored more points than any other team.

**The Olympics.** At the 1932 Olympic Games in Los Angeles she won the javelin and the 80-meter hurdles. In the high jump she won second place, because the officials ruled that going over the bar head first was a foul.

Later that year the Amateur Athletic Union's officials banned her from amateur sport, the contests for unpaid athletes, because her name appeared in an advertisement for a car. Her family needed money, so she played for pay. She toured with a men's traveling baseball team, the House of David.

**Golf.** She took up golf. Officials of the US Golf Association barred her from amateur golf in 1935, after complaints from members of the Texas Women's Golf Association. She married the wrestler George Zaharias in 1938. She helped found the Ladies Professional Golf Association, in 1948 or 1950 by different accounts. She won the US Women's Open in 1948, 1950 and 1954. From 1950 she lived with the golfer Betty Dodd, and the two hid that they were a couple.

She had cancer of the bowel in April 1953. Surgeons removed the cancer and gave her a colostomy. In that operation, surgeons bring the end of the large bowel out through an opening cut in the belly, so that body waste collects in a bag outside the body. Fourteen weeks later she was playing golf again, and in 1954 she won the US Women's Open. The cancer came back in 1955, and she died of it on September 27, 1956, at 45.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="babe-didrikson-zaharias" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jesse-owens" name="Jesse Owens" movie="Race (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jesse Owens

> **Who:** A runner and jumper who won four gold medals at the Olympic Games in Nazi Germany.
> **When and where:** Alabama, Cleveland and Berlin, 1913 to 1980.
> **Movie:** *Race* (2016), a drama film about Owens.

**"I had four gold medals, but you can't eat four gold medals."**

James Cleveland Owens was born in Oakville, Alabama, in 1913, the son of a sharecropper, a farmer who paid his rent with part of his crop. His family moved to Cleveland, Ohio, when he was nine. A teacher heard his initials, "J.C.," as "Jesse."

At a college track meet in Ann Arbor, Michigan, on May 25, 1935, he set five world records and tied a sixth in about 45 minutes.

**Berlin.** Owens won the 100 meters, the long jump, the 200 meters and the relay at the Olympic Games in Berlin in August 1936. Adolf Hitler's Nazi government ruled Germany then. The Nazis taught that white people they called "Aryans" were a superior race.

Back home, Owens had to ride the freight elevator, the one used for goods, to reach a party in his honor at the Waldorf Astoria hotel in New York. Accounts do not name who sent him there. President Franklin Roosevelt never invited him to the White House. Owens said later, "Hitler didn't snub me. It was our president who snubbed me. The president didn't even send me a telegram."

When Owens refused to finish a tour of Europe, Avery Brundage and other officials of the Amateur Athletic Union barred him from amateur sport. He earned money by racing against horses. Later he went bankrupt, which means he could not pay his debts. He said, "I had four gold medals, but you can't eat four gold medals." He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1976. Lung cancer killed him in Tucson, Arizona, on March 31, 1980.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jesse-owens" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was kept out of the pools" -->
Jobless workers paid by a federal program, the Works Progress Administration, built about 6,000 athletic fields and playgrounds and 770 new swimming pools from 1935 to 1943. Each year tens of millions of Americans swam in city pools.

Jeff Wiltse, who wrote a book about public pools called *Contested Waters*, studied who was allowed in. Many cities in the North began letting men and women swim in the same public pools in the 1920s and 1930s. Wiltse found that white officials and white swimmers in those cities then separated the pools by race.

He wrote that "most northern whites did not want black men to have the opportunity to interact with white women" at the pools. Black children in those cities could not use the pools where other children learned to swim.

**Fairground Park, 1949.** John O'Toole, the head of public welfare in St. Louis, opened the Fairground Park pool to Black swimmers on June 21, 1949. He said, "They are taxpayers and citizens, too." About 30 to 40 Black people came.

Crowds of white young men gathered with baseball bats and bricks and beat Black people as they left the pool. The crowd grew to thousands. Twelve people were hurt, and police arrested seven, three white and four Black. The published accounts of the attack do not name the attackers. Afterward, so few people came to the pool that it lost money, and it was closed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-stotz" name="Carl Stotz and the first Little League boys" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Carl Stotz and the first Little League boys

> **Who:** A Williamsport man who organized baseball for his nephews and the neighborhood boys, and two of the boys who played.
> **When and where:** Williamsport, Pennsylvania, 1938 to 1955.

**Three local businesses paid $30 each for the first uniforms.**

Carl Stotz, 28, tried out rules and field sizes in the summer of 1938 with his nephews, Jimmy and Major Gehron, and other boys from his neighborhood in Williamsport, Pennsylvania. He had no children of his own.

**The first season.** Stotz, George Bebble and Bert Bebble each managed a team in 1939. Three local businesses paid $30 each for uniforms, and the teams took their names: Lycoming Dairy, Lundy Lumber and Jumbo Pretzel. Thirty boys played. The bases were 60 feet apart, and the pitcher threw from 40 feet. On June 6, 1939, Lundy Lumber beat Lycoming Dairy 23 to 8 on a vacant lot. It was the first Little League game.

Two boys on the Lycoming Dairy team were Bill Bair and Al "Sonny" Yearick. Yearick later became the first Little Leaguer to play baseball for pay, in the minor leagues. Both men died in January 2020. A volunteer remembered that they talked about Stotz and about how Little League had helped them stay out of trouble.

Only boys could play. Stotz said in 1973, "When we started the Little League, the idea of a girl playing baseball, even with other girls, was simply unthinkable." He left Little League's national company in November 1955 over "commercialization and central control," which meant that he objected to the league being run as a business from one head office.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-stotz" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women's professional baseball, 1943 to 1954" -->
More than 600 women played in the All-American Girls Professional Baseball League from 1943 to 1954. Philip Wrigley, the owner of the Chicago Cubs, started it. The league's rules required players to wear skirts and lipstick and banned pants. During spring training they went to evening classes at a charm school, a school that taught manners and how to look and dress. No Black women were recruited or hired. The accounts of the league do not name who made that choice.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josh-gibson" name="Josh Gibson" movie="Soul of the Game (1996, a drama about several players)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Josh Gibson

> **Who:** A catcher and home-run hitter whom the white major-league owners never signed.
> **When and where:** Georgia and Pittsburgh, 1911 to 1947.
> **Movie:** *Soul of the Game* (1996), a television drama about several Negro Leagues players, with Mykelti Williamson as Gibson.

**His Hall of Fame plaque says he hit "almost 800" home runs.**

The catcher for the Homestead Grays, a Black team near Pittsburgh, was hurt in a game on July 25, 1930. The Grays' owner, Cum Posey, called Josh Gibson, 18, out of the stands to catch. Gibson had been born in Buena Vista, Georgia, in 1911, and his family had moved to Pittsburgh in 1926. There he worked in an air-brake factory and at a steel company.

Gibson played for the Grays and for the Pittsburgh Crawfords. The Crawfords' owner, Gus Greenlee, sold him back to the Grays in 1937 for $2,500 and two players. People called him "the Black Babe Ruth."

**Never in the majors.** The white owners of the major-league teams kept Black players out, so Gibson never played in their leagues. Doctors told him in 1943 that he had a brain tumor. He died in Pittsburgh on January 20, 1947, at 35, less than three months before Jackie Robinson played his first game for Brooklyn.

Since 2024 the major-league record books have included the Negro Leagues. A batting average shows how often a player got a hit: .466 means about 47 hits in every 100 times at bat. Gibson's .466 for 1943 is the highest for a single season in those records, and his career average of .372 is also the highest.

**The plaque.** Gibson was named to the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1972. His plaque there says he hit "almost 800" home runs.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josh-gibson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jackie-robinson" name="Jackie Robinson" movie="42 (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jackie Robinson

> **Who:** The first Black player in the white major leagues since the 1880s.
> **When and where:** Georgia, California, Kansas City and Brooklyn, 1919 to 1972.
> **Movie:** *42* (2013), a drama film with Chadwick Boseman as Robinson. An earlier film, *The Jackie Robinson Story* (1950), starred Robinson as himself.

**"I need a Negro player with guts enough not to fight back."**

Jackie Robinson was born in Cairo, Georgia, in 1919, the youngest of five children of sharecroppers. His father left, and in 1920 the family moved to Pasadena, California. At the University of California, Los Angeles, he was the first athlete to earn varsity letters, awards for playing on the college's top teams, in four sports.

As a soldier at Fort Hood, Texas, on July 6, 1944, he refused to move to the back of an Army bus. Officers charged him with disobeying orders. A panel of nine white officers found him not guilty.

**The meeting with Rickey.** Robinson played for the Kansas City Monarchs of the Negro Leagues in 1945. Branch Rickey, the head of the Brooklyn Dodgers, met him on August 28, 1945. Robinson asked, "Are you looking for a Negro who is afraid to fight back?" Rickey answered, "I need a Negro player with guts enough not to fight back." Rickey signed him for $600 a month. Robinson played the 1946 season for the Montreal Royals, a minor-league team.

He played his first game for Brooklyn on April 15, 1947, before 26,623 people. More than 14,000 of them were Black. During that first season, Ben Chapman, the manager of the Philadelphia team, told his players to "call Robinson everything and anything they wanted to." Chapman and his players shouted racial slurs, insulting names for Black people, at him. Chapman told him to "go back to the cotton fields." They kept it up until officials in the office of Happy Chandler, the commissioner who ran baseball, ordered them to stop.

**Threats.** Robinson got letters, signed by no one, telling him to leave baseball. Arthur Mann, who worked for Rickey, gave two of them to the police. A sportswriter, Stanley Woodward, reported that St. Louis players had threatened to strike rather than play against him. The St. Louis owners and managers denied it. The National League's president, Ford Frick, said he would suspend any player who went on strike. A St. Louis player, Enos Slaughter, cut Robinson's leg with the metal spikes on his shoe and left a seven-inch gash.

Robinson was named the first Rookie of the Year, the best first-year player, in 1947 and Most Valuable Player in 1949. He died on October 24, 1972, at 53. Officials of Major League Baseball retired his number, 42, for every team in 1997, so that new players could no longer be given it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jackie-robinson" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 17 minutes**

Girls and Black children pushed their way into games that had kept them out in the second half of the 1900s, while more of children's afternoons went to lessons and organized sports. Fewer children walked or biked to school. Diaries of how children spent their days in 1981 and 1997 showed more time in organized sports and lessons, and less time in free play and watching television. Children could play video games for a quarter in arcades from 1972, and on game machines at home from 1975.

Little League officials kept girls out from 1951 until 1974. After Title IX became law in 1972, the number of girls playing high-school sports rose from about 294,000 to about 2.7 million by 1999.

Boxing officials took Muhammad Ali's title after he refused to be drafted in 1967. US Olympic officials suspended Tommie Smith and John Carlos and sent them out of the Olympic Village, where the athletes lived, after they protested on the medal stand in 1968. Two television networks, CBS and NBC, showed the first Super Bowl in 1967 to about 51 million viewers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kathryn-johnston" name="Kathryn Johnston" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kathryn "Tubby" Johnston

> **Who:** A girl who played Little League baseball under a boy's name. Little League officials then banned girls.
> **When and where:** Corning, New York, 1950.

**"I ran into my brother's room, got a pair of his slacks."**

Kathryn Johnston played baseball with boys on a sandlot near her home in Corning, New York, in the spring of 1950. She wanted to play on a Little League team, and only boys could play on Little League teams.

She described what she did next: "I ran into my brother's room, got a pair of his slacks, put on a baseball cap and signed up as Tubby Johnston." She took the name Tubby from a comic strip, *Little Lulu*. She tucked her hair under her cap and went to the tryouts with her brother.

She made the King's Dairy team and played first base. Then she told the coach she was a girl. He said she was good enough to make the team, so she could stay. More people came to the games once word got out.

**The Tubby Rule.** Little League officials made a rule in 1951 that no girls could play. Johnston said later, "It's known as the Tubby Rule because I was the reason why they put that rule in." She wrote to Little League's headquarters in 1974, after the ban ended. The officials there checked her story and wrote back that she was the first girl to play in Little League.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kathryn-johnston" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who could play Little League" -->
Little League officials also stopped a team of Black boys from playing on for a title. The Cannon Street YMCA in Charleston, South Carolina, entered its all-star team of 14 Black boys in the city Little League tournament in 1955. The adults who ran the 61 other teams, all of them white, pulled every team out rather than play them. Accounts do not name those adults. Cannon Street won the city and state titles because no one would play them.

At the regional tournament in Rome, Georgia, officials ruled that Cannon Street could not go on. The team had won without playing, and the officials said a team that had not won its games on the field could not advance. Little League's president agreed with the ruling. The managers of the white leagues left Little League and started their own league for white teams only.

Little League officials invited the Cannon Street boys to watch the Little League World Series in Williamsport. The crowd there chanted "Let them play." In 2023 one of the players, John Rivers, looked back on what had happened to the team: "So we didn't talk about it. So it tells me how painful it was."

**Maria Pepe.** Maria Pepe, 12, pitched three games for a Little League team in Hoboken, New Jersey, in 1972. Little League officials told the Hoboken league to drop her or lose its right to be part of Little League, and the Hoboken league's officials dropped her. Robert Stirrat, a Little League vice president, said, "as far as we're concerned, the incident is closed."

Members of the National Organization for Women, a women's rights group, took Pepe's case to New Jersey's civil rights office. Sylvia Pressler, the officer hearing the case, ruled in 1973 that keeping girls out broke the state's law. She called Little League "as American as the hot dog and apple pie," and wrote, "There is no reason why that part of Americana should be withheld from girls."

Little League's officials asked a higher court to overturn the ruling, and they lost in 1974. Little League held a charter from Congress, a paper that gave it official standing. President Gerald Ford signed a law in December 1974 that changed the charter to let girls play. That year nearly 30,000 girls signed up for Little League's new softball program.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who could swim" -->
Some city officials closed their public pools rather than let Black residents swim in them. Officials in Jackson, Mississippi, closed four public pools in 1963. They gave up a fifth pool, and the YMCA then ran it for white people only. Five Supreme Court justices, the judges of the nation's highest court, ruled on June 14, 1971, in *Palmer v. Thompson*, that closing the pools to everyone was lawful. Four justices disagreed.

**The Monson Motor Lodge.** Black and white protesters jumped into the pool of the Monson Motor Lodge in St. Augustine, Florida, on June 18, 1964, in a protest against keeping Black and white swimmers apart. The motel's manager, James Brock, poured muriatic acid, a strong acid used to clean pools, into the water. A photograph of him doing it ran in newspapers across the country.

Jeff Wiltse, the historian of public pools, found that when Black swimmers won equal use of city pools, white swimmers "generally abandoned them for private pools," which means most of them left. He wrote that city officials then treated public pools as less important.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Television and the Super Bowl" -->
The Green Bay Packers beat the Kansas City Chiefs 35 to 10 in Los Angeles on January 15, 1967. It was the first championship game between the two professional football leagues of the time. Tickets cost $6 to $12. Two television networks, CBS and NBC, both showed the game, to about 51 million viewers. A 30-second advertisement cost $42,000.

From the third game, in 1969, the official name was the Super Bowl. Lamar Hunt, who founded one of the two leagues, took the name from his children's toy, a Super Ball.

**The 1993 game.** NFL officials took the 1993 Super Bowl away from Arizona because its voters had said no to a holiday for Martin Luther King Jr. in November 1990. The league's officials moved the game to Pasadena, California. *PHOENIX* magazine says the game was to be played in Tempe, next to Phoenix, and the Pima County Public Library says Phoenix. People there had expected about $200 million from the game. Arizona hosted the Super Bowl in 1996. Chapter 37 tells the fight over the King holiday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="muhammad-ali" name="Muhammad Ali" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Muhammad Ali

> **Who:** The heavyweight boxing champion whose title was taken from him when he refused to be drafted into the Army.
> **When and where:** Louisville, Rome, Houston, Atlanta and Zaire, 1942 to 2016.

**"I ain't got no quarrel with them Viet Cong."**

Cassius Clay was born in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1942. When he was 12, someone stole his bicycle. A police officer, Joe Martin, taught boxing, and he got Clay started in the sport. Clay won an Olympic gold medal in Rome in 1960. On February 25, 1964, at 22, he beat Sonny Liston for the heavyweight title. He then joined the Nation of Islam, a Black Muslim religious group, and took the name Muhammad Ali.

**Houston, 1967.** Ali went to Houston to be drafted, which means ordered by law to join the Army, on April 28, 1967, during the Vietnam War. He had already said, "I ain't got no quarrel with them Viet Cong," the fighters the US Army was fighting in Vietnam. He refused three times to step forward when his name was called.

That same day the members of the New York State Athletic Commission suspended his boxing license, and officials of the World Boxing Association took away his title. Officials of other boxing commissions did the same. In a federal court in Houston on June 20, 1967, a jury of six white men and six white women found him guilty of draft evasion, which means refusing to serve when drafted. The judge sentenced him to five years in prison and a $10,000 fine.

**Back in the ring.** He could not box for more than three years. He fought again on October 26, 1970, in Atlanta. On June 28, 1971, all eight Supreme Court justices who heard his appeal overturned his conviction. On October 30, 1974, he beat George Foreman in Zaire, in Africa, and won the title back.

Ali retired in 1981. In 1984 he said he had Parkinson's syndrome, a disease of the brain that makes the body shake and move slowly. He died on June 3, 2016.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1968 Olympic protest" -->
Tommie Smith won the 200 meters at the Olympic Games in Mexico City on October 16, 1968, in a world-record 19.83 seconds. John Carlos came third. Peter Norman of Australia came second. During the US national anthem, Smith and Carlos each raised a fist in a black glove. They stood in black socks without shoes to stand for poor people. All three men wore the badge of a protest group, the Olympic Project for Human Rights.

Smith said they were protesting "the lack of black assistant coaches," the taking of Muhammad Ali's title, and the "lack of access to good housing."

**Sent home.** Avery Brundage, the head of the International Olympic Committee, called the protest "a domestic political statement unfit for the apolitical, international forum." He meant that the protest was about politics inside the United States, and that the Olympic Games were a world meeting place that should be kept free of politics.

The US Olympic Committee's officials refused at first to suspend the two men. Brundage then threatened to ban the whole US track team. The US officials suspended Smith and Carlos and sent them out of the Olympic Village. The sports commentator Brent Musburger called them "black-skinned storm troopers." Storm troopers were the street fighters of Hitler's Nazi party. Smith, Carlos and their families got death threats.

Both men later played pro football. Smith coached track at Oberlin College and taught at Santa Monica College. Norman was left off Australia's 1972 Olympic team, although his times qualified him, and accounts differ on why. Lawmakers in Australia's parliament apologized to him in 2012, six years after he died. Smith and Carlos carried his coffin at his funeral.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Players and owners" -->
A team's owner could renew a player's contract year after year without his permission until 1975. The owner could also trade him to another team, and the player could not leave to sign with a team of his choice.

Curt Flood, a St. Louis Cardinals outfielder, refused to be traded to another team in December 1969. A television reporter, Howard Cosell, said to him, "You're a man who makes $90,000 a year, which isn't exactly slave wages." Flood answered, "A well-paid slave is nonetheless a slave." He sued baseball's commissioner. On June 19, 1972, five Supreme Court justices ruled against him and three sided with him.

About three years later, in 1975, an arbitrator named Peter Seitz ruled in a case brought by two pitchers, Andy Messersmith and Dave McNally. An arbitrator is a person both sides agree will settle a dispute. Seitz ruled that a team could renew a player's contract without his permission for only one season. After that the player was a free agent, free to sign with any team.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Girls' sports after Title IX" -->
President Richard Nixon signed Title IX into law on June 23, 1972. Under it, no school or college program that gets money from the federal government may shut a person out because of sex.

**The count.** About nine times as many girls played high-school sports in 1999-2000 as in 1971-72, according to counts by the National Federation of State High School Associations. In the 1971-72 school year about 294,000 girls played, and girls were about 7 of every 100 players. By 1999-2000 about 2.7 million girls played, and girls were about 41 of every 100.

Yale University had 13 women's varsity teams, the college's top teams, in 1979. Writers for Yale's library later stated in an online exhibit that those teams got "only a grudging fraction of the resources" given to the men's teams.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chris-ernst" name="Chris Ernst" movie="A Hero for Daisy (1999, documentary about her)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Chris Ernst

> **Who:** The captain of Yale's women's rowing team, who led a protest that won the team a locker room.
> **When and where:** Yale University, New Haven, Connecticut, 1972 to 1977.
> **Movie:** *A Hero for Daisy* (1999), a documentary about Ernst by Mary Mazzio.

**Nineteen women wrote "Title IX" on their bodies and walked in.**

Chris Ernst grew up in Scituate, Massachusetts. In high school she beat boys at arm wrestling to win time on the gymnastics equipment.

She began rowing at Yale in her second year and was captain of the women's team by her last year. The showers at the boathouse were for the men's team only. After practice, the women waited on a bus in wet clothes while the men showered. Then they rode back to campus to wash.

**The protest.** The 19 women on the team wrote "Title IX" on their bodies in early 1976 and went into the office of Joni Barnett, Yale's director of women's sports. They took off their clothes, and Ernst read a statement that began, "These are the bodies Yale is exploiting." To exploit someone means to use them for your own gain.

A *New York Times* reporter called the university that day. Yale officials set up a trailer with running water and planned a $250,000 addition to the boathouse. By 1977 the women had a locker room.

Ernst was a backup rower for the 1976 Olympic team, the first Olympic Games with women's rowing. She became the first woman in the plumbers' union in New Haven in 1980, and she won a world rowing title in 1986.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billie-jean-king" name="Billie Jean King" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Billie Jean King

> **Who:** A tennis champion who started the Women's Tennis Association and beat Bobby Riggs in a match called the "Battle of the Sexes."
> **When and where:** Long Beach, California, and Houston, Texas, 1943 to 1973 and after.

**She saved $8 to buy her first racket.**

Billie Jean King was born in Long Beach, California, in 1943. At 11 she switched from softball to tennis after her parents suggested a more "ladylike" sport. She saved the $8 and took free lessons on the city's public courts. As a young player she was kept out of a group photograph because she wore shorts her mother had sewn instead of a tennis dress. The account does not name who left her out.

**The Battle of the Sexes.** King founded the Women's Tennis Association, a group that represents women players, in June 1973. Three months later she played Bobby Riggs in a match called the "Battle of the Sexes," because it set a man against a woman. Riggs was 55, and King was 29. She beat him 6-4, 6-3, 6-3 at the Houston Astrodome on September 20, 1973.

About 30,000 people watched in the stadium, about 50 million on television in the United States, and about 90 million more in 37 other countries. She won $100,000.

King won 39 Grand Slam titles, the four biggest tennis championships. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2009.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Arcades and game machines at home" -->
Some computer games were made for school. Three student teachers from Carleton College, Don Rawitsch, Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger, made *The Oregon Trail* for a junior high class in Minneapolis in 1971. It was first played on December 3, 1971. A Minnesota computer group for schools put out new versions in 1975 and 1985, and Oregon Trail games have sold more than 65 million copies.

**Pong.** Nolan Bushnell and Allan Alcorn of the company Atari put a test machine of their game *Pong* in a bar called Andy Capp's Tavern in Sunnyvale, California, in August 1972. *Pong* was a simple tennis game played on a screen. The bar's owner soon called them because the machine had stopped working. Its coin box was jammed full of quarters.

Atari sold *Pong* from November 29, 1972, at 25 cents a game, and sold more than 8,000 machines by the end of 1974. Bushnell said players sat "shoulder to shoulder" and could talk, laugh and challenge each other.

Sears sold a home version of *Pong* for $98.95 at Christmas 1975. Atari's own home game machine came out in 1977 for $199. By 1992 Atari had sold about 30 million of that one model.

The company Midway began selling the arcade game *Pac-Man* in North America in October 1980. Its designer, Toru Iwatani, wanted "a nonviolent, cheerful game" that women would play. By 1981 *Pac-Man* machines had taken in more than $1 billion in quarters.

**The crash.** About 30 million game machines of all makes sat in American homes by 1982, and about 35 million households had children aged 6 to 16. Then sales fell, from about $3.2 billion in 1983 to about $100 million in 1985. Workers for Atari buried about 728,000 unsold game cartridges in a landfill at Alamogordo, New Mexico, in September 1983.

Sales rose again after Nintendo began selling its home machine in the United States in 1985. Nintendo's Game Boy, a game machine small enough to hold in the hands, came out in 1989 for $89.95.

Senators Joe Lieberman and Herb Kohl led public hearings, meetings where senators question witnesses, on violent video games such as *Mortal Kombat* in December 1993 and March 1994. Leaders of the game companies then set up the Entertainment Software Rating Board. From September 13, 1994, its staff put age ratings on games.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Miracle on Ice" -->
The US Olympic hockey team beat the Soviet Union 4 to 3 at Lake Placid, New York, on February 22, 1980. The American players were college players with an average age of 21, coached by Herb Brooks. Two days later they beat Finland and won the gold medal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Iroquois Nationals" -->
Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) people formed their own national lacrosse team in 1983, and the Grand Council of the Haudenosaunee approved it. Officials of the International Lacrosse Federation turned the team down for 1986 and accepted it as a member nation in 1988. In 1990 the team played in the world championship in Perth, Australia, and finished fifth of five.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did a child's afternoon change?" -->
Children spent less of it in free play and more of it in school, lessons and organized sports.

**Walking or riding.** Nearly half of children in kindergarten through eighth grade usually walked or biked to school in 1969, and about 12 of every 100 were driven, according to national travel surveys studied by Noreen McDonald and other researchers. A 1995 survey asked a slightly different question: how children got to school on the survey day. That day nearly half went by car, and about 14 of every 100 walked or biked.

**Time diaries.** The researchers Sandra Hofferth and John Sandberg compared diaries of how children spent their time in 1981 and in 1997. Children spent more time in school, day care, sports and art lessons. They spent less time in free play, housework and watching television. The drop in free play showed up among children whose mothers stayed home as well as those whose mothers had jobs.

More mothers did work for pay. In 1975, 47 of every 100 mothers with children under 18 were working or looking for work. By 2000 it was 73 of every 100, according to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics.

**On their own.** About 18 of every 100 children aged 5 to 14, some 6.9 million, regularly looked after themselves for part of the week in the fall of 1995. On average they did so for about six hours a week. More than twice as many children that age, about 15 million, took part in sports, lessons or clubs, according to the US Census Bureau.

**Private teams.** Organized sport also became more private. In a survey of adults published in 2025, about 4 of every 100 who were born in the 1950s said they had played on a private club or travel team as children. For those born in the 1990s it was 13 of every 100. The researchers, Chris Knoester and Chris Bjork, found that children whose parents had more schooling were more likely to play on these teams.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 22 minutes**

Much of children's free time moved onto phones and into games played online, often with strangers, after 2000. Researchers at the Pew Research Center, which surveys American teenagers, found that 73 of every 100 teenagers aged 13 to 17 had or could use a smartphone in 2014-15, and 95 of every 100 by 2022. Fewer children walked to school or played outdoors. The average sports family spent $883 on one child's main sport in 2022. Researchers disagree about whether screens harm children's attention and mood.

Doctors found a brain disease in football players who had died. A team doctor who had sexually abused more than 500 girls and women pleaded guilty, and judges gave him prison sentences so long that he will die in prison. College athletes won the right to be paid, betting on games became legal in most states, and the men's soccer World Cup was played in the United States, Canada and Mexico in 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Phones and social media" -->
Pew researchers ask American teenagers aged 13 to 17 how they use phones and the internet. In 2014-15, 24 of every 100 said they were online "almost constantly." In 2022 it was 46 of every 100, and in 2025 it was 40. In Pew's 2025 survey, about 90 of every 100 teenagers used YouTube, and about 60 used TikTok. That year 64 of every 100 teenagers had used an AI chatbot, a computer program that writes answers in plain language.

**Hours a day.** Common Sense Media, a group that studies children and media, surveyed children in 2021. Children aged 8 to 12 spent an average of 5 hours 33 minutes a day on screens for fun, not counting schoolwork. Teenagers aged 13 to 18 spent 8 hours 39 minutes. In 2019 the figures had been 4 hours 44 minutes and 7 hours 22 minutes.

When a child used two screens at once, the researchers counted the time on both, so two hours of screen time can fit into one hour of the day. The survey's authors wrote that they could not tell how much of the rise came from the COVID-19 pandemic.

**The warning.** The US Surgeon General, Vivek Murthy, the country's top public health doctor, issued an advisory, a public warning, about social media on May 23, 2023. The advisory states that "we do not yet have enough evidence to determine if social media is sufficiently safe for children and adolescents."

It cites a study of 6,595 American children aged 12 to 15. Those who spent more than 3 hours a day on social media had double the risk of symptoms of depression and anxiety. In the advisory, Murthy asked lawmakers to set safety rules and limits for children's use.

State lawmakers began to keep phones out of classrooms. Florida's lawmakers passed a law banning students' phone use during class time in 2023.

By January 2026, lawmakers in 26 states had banned phones for the whole school day, from the first bell to the last, according to a count by the group Ballotpedia. By July 2026, lawmakers in at least 39 states and Washington, DC, had passed rules requiring schools to ban or limit phones, according to a tally by *Education Week*. The two counts differ because they count different kinds of laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Games played online, and with strangers" -->
*Minecraft* is a game of building and digging in a world of blocks, played alone or with others. It came out in 2011. By 2023 it had sold more than 300 million copies, more than any other video game.

*Roblox* opened in 2006. Its players build games and play each other's games. According to the company, half of all American children under 16 play it each month. *Fortnite* came out in 2017. In its best-known version, up to 100 players land on an island and fight until one player or team is left.

*Fortnite* is free to play. The companies behind *Fortnite* and *Roblox* sell game money, called V-Bucks in *Fortnite* and Robux in *Roblox*, which players buy with real money and spend on costumes and other items inside the game.

**The Fortnite case.** Officials of the Federal Trade Commission, the government agency that enforces laws protecting buyers, accused Epic Games, the maker of *Fortnite*, of breaking the law on December 19, 2022. They said the company had collected information on children under 13 without their parents' permission. They said the game's settings turned on live voice and text chat for children, and that the company matched "children and teens with strangers to play Fortnite together."

Children had been "bullied, threatened, harassed," some of them sexually, and "exposed to dangerous and psychologically traumatizing issues such as suicide," the officials said. Epic employees had urged the company to change those settings as early as 2017. In a settlement, an agreement that ended the case, Epic Games agreed to pay $520 million.

**Roblox.** People who brought four lawsuits in June 2025 accused the company that runs *Roblox* of letting adults use the game for the sexual exploitation of children, which means using children for sex acts or sexual pictures. Every *Roblox* player had to prove their age, with an ID or a video scan of the face, before chatting from January 2026. Chat was also limited to players of similar ages.

Esports are video game contests played for prize money. The first *Fortnite* World Cup, held from July 26 to 28, 2019, in New York City, paid $30 million in prizes. About 40 million players tried to qualify.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Kyle "Bugha" Giersdorf

> **Who:** A 16-year-old who won the first Fortnite World Cup and $3 million.
> **When and where:** Arthur Ashe Stadium, New York City, July 28, 2019.

**He scored 59 points. The second-place player scored 33.**

Kyle Giersdorf was born in 2002. His father, who also played video games, got him started on *Fortnite*. Kyle played under the name "Bugha." He joined an esports team called Sentinels on March 25, 2019. That summer he qualified for the first *Fortnite* World Cup, out of about 40 million players who tried.

**The final.** At 16, he played six games against 99 other finalists in Arthur Ashe Stadium in New York City on July 28, 2019. He scored 59 points, and the second-place player scored 33. He won $3 million, more than any other *Fortnite* player has won in total.

Hours after he won, someone broke into his Twitter account and his account on Twitch, a website where players show their games live to viewers. Later he became a streamer on Twitch, playing his games live for viewers there.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What is measured" -->
Researchers who surveyed children after 2000 found that they walked less and had less free time than children in earlier years. Team sports cost families hundreds of dollars a year, children from richer homes played more, and more children had obesity.

Fewer children walk or bike to school. Nearly half did in 1969. By 2009 it was about 13 of every 100, according to the national travel surveys studied by Noreen McDonald and other researchers. In 2017 about 11 of every 100 students aged 5 to 17 usually walked or biked, according to a later government travel survey.

**Less free time.** Sandra Hofferth's later time diaries of children aged 6 to 12 showed their free time falling from about 57 hours a week in 1981 to about 48 hours in 2003, the year her diaries end.

The share who played a sport on the diary days fell from 76 to 60 of every 100 between 1997 and 2003. The share who spent time in outdoor activities fell from 16 to 10 of every 100.

The National Survey of Children's Health is a government survey of parents. It found in 2023 that 55 of every 100 children aged 6 to 17 played on a sports team or took sports lessons. Children from richer homes played more.

The gap was already plain in 2012. About 35 of every 100 children in homes earning under $25,000 a year played a sport regularly, against about 49 of every 100 in homes earning $100,000 or more. By 2024 the difference between those two groups had grown to about 20 of every 100 children, according to the Sports and Fitness Industry Association.

**What it costs.** The average parent spent $883 in a year on one child's main sport, in a 2022 survey by the Aspen Institute and Utah State University. Travel to games and tournaments cost more than any other part. The richest families spent about four times as much as the poorest.

Fifteen of the 50 states had rules in 2012 that recommended or required a daily recess, according to a 2013 report by the research group Bridging the Gap.

Doctors measure obesity by comparing a child's weight with the child's height. From 1971 to 1974, about 5 of every 100 children aged 2 to 19 had obesity. From 2017 to March 2020 it was about 20 of every 100, nearly four times as many, or about 14.7 million children, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. In the poorest homes it was about 26 of every 100, and in the richest about 12.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What researchers disagree about" -->
ADHD stands for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Doctors diagnose it when a child has trouble paying attention, or is overactive and acts without thinking, badly enough to cause problems in more than one place, such as at home and at school.

**The count.** In national surveys of parents, 9.5 of every 100 children aged 4 to 17 had been diagnosed with ADHD in 2007, and 11 of every 100 in 2011. The researchers who reported those counts wrote that the rise "could also reflect better detection" and more awareness, not only more children with ADHD.

The American Psychiatric Association changed its rules for diagnosing ADHD in 2013. Its manual of mental disorders now allowed a diagnosis when symptoms began by age 12 instead of age 7. That change came after the 2011 count, so it could explain only a rise that came later.

In 2022, 11.4 of every 100 children aged 3 to 17 had been diagnosed, about 7.1 million children, according to a 2024 study by M. L. Danielson and other researchers. They wrote that the survey changed its methods in 2022, which makes it harder to compare 2022 with earlier years.

**A study of screens and attention.** Researchers have studied whether screens are linked to attention problems. A team at the University of Southern California, led by Chaelin Ra and Adam Leventhal, followed 2,587 high-school students in Los Angeles County for two years, in a study printed in 2018. None had serious ADHD symptoms at the start. Students who used more kinds of digital media many times a day later reported more ADHD symptoms.

Among students who used no kind of media that often, 4.6 of every 100 reported the symptoms. Among students who used seven kinds, 9.5 of every 100 did. The researchers called the link "modest." They wrote that more research was needed "to determine whether this association is causal," which means whether the media use caused the symptoms. The students rated their own symptoms, and doctors did not diagnose them.

Other researchers studied something different: how well teenagers felt. The Oxford researchers Orben and Przybylski studied surveys of 355,358 teenagers in the United States and Britain in 2019. They found that technology use explained "at most 0.4%" of the differences in how well teenagers felt. That is less than half of one percent. They called the link "too small to warrant policy change," meaning too small to be a reason for new laws or rules.

**By 2022:** Ninety-five of every 100 teenagers had or could use a smartphone, 46 of every 100 said they were online "almost constantly," and researchers still disagreed about what screens did to children.

**The argument over a book.** The psychologist Jonathan Haidt of New York University published a book called *The Anxious Generation* in 2024. He wrote that phone and social media companies had "rewired childhood" by taking the place of play and time with friends.

The psychologist Candice Odgers of the University of California, Irvine, answered in the science journal *Nature* on March 29, 2024. She wrote that the book's "repeated suggestion that digital technologies are rewiring our children's brains and causing an epidemic of mental illness is not supported by science." She wrote that studies had found "a mix of no, small and mixed associations." She agreed that the social media companies needed to make large changes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Football and the brain" -->
Mike Webster was a center for the Pittsburgh Steelers who played 17 seasons in the NFL, from 1974 to 1990, and won four Super Bowls. After he retired he lost his memory, suffered from depression and pain, and at times lived in his pickup truck or in train stations. He died of a heart attack on September 24, 2002, at 50.

**What Omalu found.** Bennet Omalu, a doctor in Pittsburgh who examines bodies to find out why people died, studied Webster's brain. He found clumps of protein unlike those of Alzheimer's disease. Omalu and other doctors described the disease in a medical journal in July 2005. It is called chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE.

Doctors link CTE to repeated blows to the head. Its signs can include problems with behavior, mood and thinking. It often gets worse over time, and it can lead to dementia, the loss of the ability to think and remember. Doctors can find it only by examining the brain after death.

**What the league said.** Paul Tagliabue, the league's commissioner, had set up the NFL's own brain injury committee in 1994. He named Elliot Pellman to lead it, a doctor who treated joints and muscles, not the brain.

The committee's papers stated from 2003 on that concussions, brain injuries from a hard blow to the head, caused no lasting harm in NFL players. In May 2006 committee members wrote to the journal asking it to take back Omalu's paper. Years later Jeff Miller, a league official, told members of Congress that "there is a link between football-related head trauma and chronic traumatic encephalopathy."

More than 4,500 former players sued the NFL, saying the league had hidden the dangers. Judge Anita Brody approved a settlement in April 2015 to pay retired players with brain illness for 65 years, expected to cost more than $1 billion. Families of players who died before 2006 were not covered, so Webster's family got nothing from it. The NFL's own Retirement Board had already paid his family $1.8 million in 2005, after finding that football caused his brain injuries.

**The scoring rule.** Until 2021 the dementia tests used to judge claims were scored as if Black players started with lower thinking skills than other players. That made it harder for Black players to prove dementia and be paid. The news accounts of the lawsuit do not name who set that scoring rule. After a lawsuit, NFL officials said in June 2021 that they would stop.

Children were hurt in football too. About 23,000 football brain injuries a year that did not kill the player sent people to emergency rooms, and 90 of every 100 of those hurt were aged 5 to 18. Pop Warner, a youth football program, had 9.5 percent fewer players in 2012 than in 2010, according to Robert Boland of New York University.

Researchers at Boston University had found CTE in the brains of 345 of the 376 former NFL players they studied by 2023. They warned that families who suspected the disease were the ones who gave the brains. So the figure does not mean that 92 of every 100 NFL players have CTE.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The team doctor who abused gymnasts" -->
Larry Nassar worked with the USA Gymnastics national team from 1986. From 1996 to 2014 he was its national medical coordinator, the doctor in charge of the gymnasts' care. From 1997 to 2016 he was also a doctor for athletes at Michigan State University.

**What he did.** During appointments he said were treatment for pain, he put his ungloved finger into girls' vaginas. Sometimes a parent was in the room and could not see what he was doing. More than 500 girls and women were abused. Some were as young as 6.

**Who was told.** Many adults were told and did not stop him. Athletes said they told Michigan State employees about him as early as 1997. Court papers say the Michigan State gymnastics coach, Kathie Klages, was told in 1997, and they accuse her of pressuring gymnasts to stay quiet. Michigan State officials looked into a complaint in 2014 and let him keep treating patients.

A coach, Sarah Jantzi, reported him to USA Gymnastics on June 17, 2015. Its president, Steve Penny, told agents of the FBI, the federal police, in Indianapolis. The agents did not open a formal investigation or tell officials in Michigan. Reporters at *USA Today* wrote that for more than a year Nassar went on "treating, and raping, gymnasts" in Michigan. Rape means forcing a person into a sex act.

Michael Horowitz, the Justice Department's inspector general, the official who checks for wrongdoing inside the department, reported in July 2021 that the FBI agent W. Jay Abbott had failed to act and later lied about it. At least 70 more girls and women were abused after the FBI was told. The victims put the number at 120.

**The sentences.** Reporters at the *Indianapolis Star* published the story in September 2016. Nassar pleaded guilty to federal crimes of having sexual pictures of children, and to ten counts of first-degree criminal sexual conduct with girls in two Michigan counties. Under Michigan law, first-degree criminal sexual conduct means sexual penetration of a person in certain cases, such as when the victim is under 13, and it can be punished with life in prison.

Judges sentenced him to 60 years in federal prison, and to 40 to 175 years and 40 to 125 years in Michigan, so he will die in prison. Before he was sentenced, more than 200 women and girls spoke about what he did to them, over nine days in court.

**What happened to the others.** The heads of USA Gymnastics, Michigan State and the US Olympic Committee resigned. Nassar's dean at Michigan State, William Strampel, was convicted of neglect of duty and spent a year in jail. Klages was convicted in 2020 of lying to police, and that conviction was overturned in 2021.

John Geddert, an Olympic coach who had employed Nassar at his gym, was charged in 2021 with human trafficking, forced labor, sexual assault and other crimes. Human trafficking means using force, tricks or threats to make people work or perform sex acts. He killed himself shortly after the charges were announced.

Michigan State paid $500 million to 332 victims in 2018. USA Gymnastics and the US Olympic and Paralympic Committee together agreed to pay $380 million in 2021. Three years later the Justice Department paid $138.7 million to victims who sued over the FBI's failure.

The Olympic gymnasts Simone Biles, McKayla Maroney, Maggie Nichols and Aly Raisman told senators on September 15, 2021, how FBI agents had handled their reports. Raisman said it "was like serving innocent children up to a pedophile on a silver platter." A pedophile is an adult who is sexually drawn to children. The FBI's director, Christopher Wray, said he was "deeply and profoundly sorry."

### Rachael Denhollander

> **Who:** A former gymnast who was the first woman to accuse Larry Nassar in public.
> **When and where:** Kalamazoo and Lansing, Michigan, 2000 to 2018.
> **Movie:** *Athlete A* (2020), a Netflix documentary by Bonni Cohen and Jon Shenk about the *Indianapolis Star* reporters and the scandal.

**"How much is a little girl worth?" she asked the judge.**

Rachael Denhollander was a club gymnast from Kalamazoo, Michigan. She was 15 in 2000 when she saw Larry Nassar for pain in her lower back. He sexually abused her on five visits.

At least seven other girls and women had told someone about Nassar over twenty years before she spoke. By 2016 she was a lawyer. She reported Nassar to the Michigan State University police and filed a complaint with the university in August 2016. The *Indianapolis Star* printed her accusation on September 20, 2016. Michigan State officials fired Nassar that day. By January 2018 more than 265 women and girls had been identified as his victims, and the count later passed 500.

**In court.** She spoke last at both of his sentencing hearings, in January and February 2018. She asked the judge, "How much is a little girl worth?" She answered that "everything is what these survivors are worth." Judge Rosemarie Aquilina called her "the bravest person I have ever had in my courtroom."

*Time* magazine named her one of its 100 most influential people of 2018. In 2019 she published a book about the case, *What Is a Girl Worth?*

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="College athletes paid" -->
NCAA rules let college athletes receive only scholarships and school costs until 2021. California's governor, Gavin Newsom, signed the first state law letting college athletes be paid for their name, image and likeness, meaning for ads and appearances that use who they are, in September 2019.

All nine Supreme Court justices ruled against NCAA limits on education benefits on June 21, 2021, in *NCAA v. Alston*. Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that antitrust law, which forbids businesses from agreeing to hold down pay, "should not be a cover for exploitation of the student athletes." From July 1, 2021, NCAA officials let athletes be paid for their name, image and likeness.

**The settlement.** Grant House, a swimmer at Arizona State, and Sedona Prince, a basketball player, sued the NCAA in 2020. Judge Claudia Wilken approved a settlement on June 6, 2025. Athletes who played from 2016 to 2024 will share about $2.8 billion over ten years.

Each college may pay its athletes directly from the 2025-26 school year, up to about $20.5 million that year, a limit that rises 4 percent a year. Eight women athletes asked a higher court to change the settlement. They argued that most of the money would go to men who played football and basketball.

**By 2025:** Doctors had tied CTE to football, Larry Nassar was serving prison sentences so long that he will die in prison, and a judge had approved a settlement under which colleges may pay their athletes directly.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Betting on games" -->
A federal law of 1992 barred states from allowing betting on sports, except in Nevada and three other states. On May 14, 2018, in *Murphy v. NCAA*, a case New Jersey brought, the Supreme Court justices struck the law down. By September 2023, 38 states and Washington, DC, allowed betting on sports. Americans bet $121 billion with licensed sports betting companies in 2023, according to the American Gaming Association, the casino industry's trade group.

**Jontay Porter.** Adam Silver, the NBA's commissioner, banned Jontay Porter, a Toronto Raptors player, for life on April 17, 2024. League investigators found that Porter told a bettor about his own health before a game in March 2024. He then left that game after three minutes, saying he felt ill. A person he knew had bet $80,000 that Porter would play poorly. Porter had also placed at least 13 bets on NBA games.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Transgender athletes" -->
A transgender girl is a girl who was counted as a boy at birth. Idaho's governor, Brad Little, signed the first state law keeping transgender girls and women off girls' and women's teams in March 2020. By June 2026, 27 states had passed such laws, according to the Supreme Court.

Lia Thomas, a transgender swimmer at the University of Pennsylvania, won a women's college title in 2022. On February 5, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an order titled "Keeping Men Out of Women's Sports." The next day NCAA officials limited women's college events to athletes counted as female at birth. In July 2025 officials of the University of Pennsylvania agreed with the federal government to change Thomas's records.

**The Supreme Court.** Six Supreme Court justices ruled on June 30, 2026, in *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* and *Little v. Hecox*, that Title IX allows schools to keep separate teams "defined by biological sex." Three justices disagreed in part. B.P.J., who is named in court only by her initials, had wanted to run on the girls' cross-country and track teams at her school in West Virginia. She was 11 when she first asked to play, according to Justice Sotomayor's opinion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Protest and equal pay" -->
Colin Kaepernick, the quarterback for the San Francisco 49ers, knelt during the national anthem before games in 2016. He was protesting police brutality, which means violence by police officers, and unfair treatment of Black Americans. He chose to end his contract with the 49ers in March 2017, and after that no team signed him.

Kaepernick accused the NFL's owners in October 2017 of secretly agreeing among themselves to keep him out. He settled with the league in February 2019, and the terms were kept secret. The NFL's commissioner, Roger Goodell, apologized in June 2020 for not listening to Black players' concerns.

**Equal pay.** Five players on the US women's soccer team, among them Hope Solo, Carli Lloyd and Megan Rapinoe, filed a complaint in April 2016 that they were paid less than the men's team. The women won the World Cup in 2019. US Soccer officials settled for $24 million in February 2022. In May 2022 they signed contracts that pay the women and the men equally.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Haudenosaunee Nationals" -->
The Haudenosaunee national lacrosse team, formed in 1983, is the same team as the Iroquois Nationals. The Haudenosaunee have issued their own passports since 1927. British officials refused to accept those passports for the world lacrosse championship in England in 2010. They told the 23 players to travel on US or Canadian passports. The players refused, and they did not play.

The team won third place at the world championships of 2014, 2018 and 2023. Olympic officials added lacrosse to the 2028 Los Angeles Games in October 2023, and they said that only countries with a national Olympic committee could enter. President Joe Biden and Canada's prime minister, Justin Trudeau, asked them on January 17, 2025, to let the Haudenosaunee play under their own flag. No decision on that request is on record as of September 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 2026 World Cup" -->
FIFA, the group that runs world soccer, held the men's World Cup in the United States, Canada and Mexico from June 11 to July 19, 2026. It was the first with 48 teams. Of the 104 games, 78 were played in 11 American cities. About 6.8 million people went to the games, more than at any World Cup before.

Spain beat Argentina 1 to 0 after extra time in the final at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey. The US team lost to Belgium 4 to 1 in the round of 16.

Tickets for the final started at $6,730. For the first time, FIFA officials raised ticket prices as more people wanted them.

**The three questions, answered**
- Who invented baseball? No one person did. Clubs wrote the rules, and the Knickerbocker club of New York adopted twenty of them in 1845. The story that Abner Doubleday invented it in 1839 rests on one letter from a man who was 5 years old that year.
- Who was kept out of American games, and how did they get in? The people who ran the games kept out laborers, Black players, girls and Black swimmers, and left enslaved riders' names off the race cards. Some got in through courts and a new law, like Maria Pepe and the girls of Title IX. Jackie Robinson got in when Branch Rickey signed him, and Kathryn Johnston played anyway, under a boy's name.
- How has children's free time changed? In the 1830s Lucy Larcom made doll furniture from burrs while the grown-ups left the children alone. Since then more of children's time has gone to lessons and organized sports, and by 2022, 95 of every 100 teenagers had or could use a smartphone.

**What changed:** Play moved from fields the players ran themselves to leagues, arenas and screens run by adults and companies, and the fight over who could play moved from a county court in Virginia to the Supreme Court.

**Five dates to keep**
- September 10, 1674: a Virginia court calls racing "a sport only for Gentlemen."
- September 23, 1845: the Knickerbocker club adopts twenty rules for baseball.
- April 15, 1947: Jackie Robinson plays his first game for Brooklyn.
- June 23, 1972: President Nixon signs Title IX.
- July 28, 2019: Kyle Giersdorf, 16, wins the first *Fortnite* World Cup.

**Not known:** how old the Native ball games played with sticks are, because no object or written record dates one before 1500.

**Where to go:** The National Baseball Hall of Fame in Cooperstown, New York, holds Josh Gibson's plaque, which says he hit "almost 800" home runs.
