## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, hundreds of separate nations lived on this land, and each nation had its own religious life. So there were hundreds of Native religions, and there still are many today. The nations shared no holy book and no single founder. People held each ceremony in a set place, at a set season, with their own community.

The written record of this land begins with Europeans in the 1500s, so no written account of these religions survives from before then. What is known comes from two places. One is what people built and buried. The other is the traditions that the nations kept and still keep.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What archaeologists can learn from the ground" -->
Archaeologists are scientists who dig up and study old buildings, graves and objects. They can measure which way a doorway faces and check whether the sun rises in line with it on a certain day. A wall lined up with the midwinter sunrise shows that the builders lined it up on purpose. It does not show what the builders believed.

The nations themselves hold spoken histories that go back before 1500. The people of each nation decide how much of that to make public. Written descriptions of Native religions begin after 1500. Europeans wrote them from the outside, and most of those writers were hostile to the religions they described. Many were missionaries. A missionary is a person sent to turn other people to his own religion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chaco Canyon" -->
Chaco Canyon is in what is now northwestern New Mexico. In the middle and late 800s, people there began building large stone buildings of many stories, which are called great houses today. Three of the first were Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Peñasco Blanco. Building went on in the canyon for more than 300 years.

The builders lined many great houses up with the sun, the moon, and north, south, east and west. They also built roads out from the canyon. The roads joined the canyon to more than 150 other great houses across the region.

Pueblo people are the descendants of the people of Chaco. They say that Chaco was a gathering place where many peoples and clans came together to share their ceremonies and knowledge. A clan is a group of families who count themselves as related. Many Native people of the Southwest also say that Chaco is an important stop on their clans' sacred migration paths. A migration path is a route a people traveled as they moved from one home to another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia and its circle of posts" -->
Cahokia stood in what is now Illinois, a few miles east of the Mississippi River and of what is now St. Louis, Missouri. People lived there mainly between about 800 and 1400. It was the largest town in North America north of Mexico before Europeans came. National Park Service figures put the number of people living in Cahokia itself at 10,000 to 20,000.

The people of Cahokia built about 120 mounds of earth. Some held public buildings on top, and others held graves. The biggest is now called Monks Mound. No earth structure that Native people built north of Mexico is larger. It went up in fourteen stages. It rises in four steps, called terraces, to about 100 feet.

Near Monks Mound stood a circle of upright wooden posts. It is called Woodhenge today. Posts in the circle marked where the sun came up on the longest day of the year, on the shortest day, and on the two equinoxes. An equinox is one of the two days each year when day and night are the same length. The post for the equinox sunrise lines up with the front of Monks Mound.

Bill Iseminger, an archaeologist at the Cahokia site, gives these figures for the circle. The people rebuilt it about five times. It measured from about 240 to 480 feet across. The first circle had 24 posts, and each new circle had twelve more than the one before. No record says what the people said or believed as they watched the sun rise over the posts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The earthworks of Ohio" -->
Between about the years 1 and 400, people in the woodlands of eastern North America built long walls of earth. They laid the walls out in geometric shapes and built mounds of many shapes beside them. Many of these earthworks are in Ohio. In 2023 they were added to UNESCO's list of World Heritage Sites under the name Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks. UNESCO is the part of the United Nations that keeps that list of important places. A ceremonial place is one built for religious gatherings and ceremonies.

Objects found at the earthworks came from far away. There is mica, a shiny rock that splits into thin sheets. There are also shark's teeth, sea shells, copper, and obsidian, a black glass made by volcanoes. The people who built the earthworks traded for these things with people from the Atlantic coast to the Rocky Mountains.

Archaeologists call the builders the Hopewell culture, and that name is not the name of any Native nation. Mordecai C. Hopewell owned a farm near Chillicothe, Ohio, where some of the mounds stood. Warren K. Moorehead dug into twenty-eight mounds on that farm. The objects he took out went on show at the world's fair in Chicago in 1893. After that, archaeologists used the farmer's name for the builders. The National Park Service's account of the earthworks states that different Native groups built them.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish priests brought the Catholic religion to this land in the 1500s, and Spanish soldiers came with them. Catholics are Christians who follow the pope, the head of the church in Rome. The priests belonged to orders, groups of Catholic men who promised to live by one set of rules, such as the Franciscans and the Jesuits. Franciscan priests and brothers are called friars. They built missions in the towns of the Timucua and Guale peoples in what is now Florida and Georgia. From 1598 they built missions among the Pueblo nations of what is now New Mexico.

Friars ran each mission as a church, and Spanish officials used the missions to rule the Native towns. Native people in a mission town were baptized. Baptism is the Christian ceremony of washing a person with water to make the person a member of the church. Friars taught them Catholic beliefs. Spanish officials made them obey the Spanish government and ordered each mission town to send men to work for the Spanish.

Nearly all of the written record of the missions in this century was written by the missionaries themselves, so it tells their side.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The missions in Florida and Georgia" -->
Luis Cáncer de Barbastro was a Dominican friar from Spain. He came from Spain to the Caribbean in 1518, and he argued that friars should bring Native people to Christianity without soldiers. King Charles V of Spain gave him a written order to try it in Florida. He sailed from Havana, Cuba, in early June 1549. On June 26, 1549, he went ashore near Tampa Bay to greet a group of Tocobaga people, and they killed him. The Tocobaga left no account of why they killed him.

Missionary work in Florida began in 1568. The Jesuits came first. They won very few people to their faith and left Florida in 1571. The first Franciscan friars reached St. Augustine in 1573. A new group of friars arrived from Spain in 1587, and then the friars began building missions up the coast into what is now Georgia.

A short try among the Guale in 1574 and 1575 had failed. San Pedro de Mocama, on the coast of what is now Georgia, was the first successful mission in Georgia. By the end of 1595 the friars had missions in at least five main towns of the Guale people. The Guale spoke a Muskogee language.

A mission town with a friar living in it was called a *doctrina*. There the friar baptized people and led Mass, the main Catholic church service. Friars also taught the catechism, a set of questions and answers about Christian beliefs that people learn by heart. In 1612 the friar Francisco Pareja printed a catechism in Spanish and in the Timucua language.

Spanish officials used the mission towns to get workers as well. Under a system called the *repartimiento*, each mission town had to send a set number of unmarried men to St. Augustine every year. The men worked there for the Spanish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Killed for refusing to change faith, 1565" -->
In 1565 Spanish soldiers killed French Protestants in Florida because of their religion. Protestants are Christians whose churches broke away from the pope. It was the first killing over religion between Europeans in what became the United States. That year a hurricane wrecked the French ships, and about 127 French Protestants came ashore with no food and no weapons. They surrendered to Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, the Spanish commander. Menéndez told them to give up their Protestant faith, and they refused. His soldiers killed 111 of them. Menéndez spared 16: a few who said they were Catholic, some sailors who had been forced onto the ships, and four craftsmen the Spanish town of St. Augustine needed. On October 15, 1565, Menéndez wrote to the king of Spain about the others: "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife." That meant his men killed them with knives.

About two weeks after the first killing, a second group of Frenchmen under Jean Ribault surrendered. They refused the same demand, and Menéndez's soldiers killed 134 of them. The two killings are dated September 29 and October 11 or 12, 1565.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Guale, 1597" -->
In the autumn of 1597, Guale people killed five Franciscans and destroyed their missions. The five were Pedro de Corpa at Tolomato, Blas Rodríguez at Tupiqui, Miguel de Auñón and Antonio de Badajoz at the town of Guale, and Francisco de Beráscola at Asao. Badajoz was a lay brother, a member of the order who was not a priest. A sixth friar, Francisco de Ávila, was held captive and freed in 1598.

Who killed the friars, and why, is not known. The historians J. Michael Francis and Kathleen Kole translated the earliest Spanish records of the uprising and published them in 2011. They wrote that those two questions cannot "be answered with any certainty." Older retellings name a young leader and give a reason. In the earliest records, only two of the seven witnesses named that young man at all, and neither said he alone ordered the killings.

Gonzalo Méndez de Canzo, the governor of Florida, led soldiers into Guale country himself. On October 27 some people at Ospo shot arrows at his party, and he burned the town and its storehouses. On November 2 he burned Sapala. On November 4 he ordered every house at Tolomato burned. On November 6 his officer Alonso Díaz burned the houses and storehouses at Asao and Talaje. At Asao, Díaz found the storehouses full of corn and other goods. Méndez could not capture anyone, so he ordered his men to destroy the corn fields and other crops as well. The records describe the burning and give no count of Guale people killed.

In July 1598 Méndez took seven young Guale captives. Francis and Kole describe them as boys. One was Lucas, the son of don Felipe, the chief of Tupiqui. On July 27 Spanish officials questioned Lucas a second time, now under the threat of torture. He confessed that he had been there when Blas Rodríguez was killed. Spanish officials sentenced him to death on July 28, and he was hanged at St. Augustine on July 29, 1598. The records do not name the man who hanged him. Lucas was the only Guale person the Spanish officially charged and punished for the uprising. Spanish officials divided the other six boys among themselves as personal servants. The boys were captives, forced to work for the men who held them.

The Spanish left the northern missions empty until 1604.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
In early May 1598, at a crossing of the Rio Grande near what is now El Paso, Juan de Oñate claimed for Spain the land the Spanish called New Mexico. It was the homeland of the Pueblo nations. Franciscan friars traveled with him. That autumn Oñate went to every pueblo, as the Spanish called each Pueblo town. Through translators he demanded that the people of each town promise loyalty to the Spanish government. At those meetings he gave each town a missionary. The Franciscan missions in New Mexico began that way.

The friars also gave their approval for a war on Acoma Pueblo. In December 1598 Oñate's nephew, Juan de Zaldívar, went up to Acoma Pueblo with soldiers to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, the soldiers raped an Acoma woman. Rape means forcing a person into sex. The Acoma killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men. Oñate then asked the Franciscan friars whether a war against Acoma would be just. The friars answered that he "possessed both the authority and sufficient cause." In January 1599 his soldiers killed Acoma men, women and children and burned much of the town. No one counted the dead, and estimates run from about 300 to about 1,500. Oñate sentenced the men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off, and he sentenced most of the captives to twenty years of forced work. Whether the feet were really cut off is disputed. On Oñate's orders, Acoma children under twelve were taken from their parents and given to the friars. About seventy of the girls were sent south to convents in Mexico, and the records do not name the men who took them. A convent is a house of nuns, women who have promised their lives to the Catholic church. Almost certainly none of the girls ever came home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Doña María Meléndez

> **Who:** The Timucua chief of Nombre de Dios, the town beside St. Augustine, and a Catholic.
> **When and where:** Spanish Florida, the late 1500s and early 1600s.

Doña María Meléndez ruled Nombre de Dios, a Timucua town just outside St. Augustine. The Spanish called her the *cacica*, their word for a woman chief.

Her mother ruled the town before her. Her mother was one of the first Timucua people to become Christian. Doña María was a Christian too. She married a Spanish soldier named Clemente de Vernal. He lived with her and their children at Nombre de Dios.

After 1587 the Franciscans set up their first *doctrina* at Nombre de Dios. Staff at the Florida Museum of Natural History write that the Franciscans' success there "was largely owing to" her.

By 1606 she ruled the Timucua towns along the coast from St. Augustine north to about Cumberland Island, in what is now Georgia. The National Park Service's account says she may have gained that rule with Spanish help. The records do not settle it.

Spaniards wrote down everything known about her, and nothing survives in her own words. No record says why she became a Christian.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English groups founded several colonies in the 1600s so that they could worship in their own way, and in some of those colonies the lawmakers and judges punished people who worshipped differently. The Puritan leaders of Massachusetts had the ears of Quakers cut off and hanged four Quakers on Boston Common. Maryland's lawmakers passed the first law here that protected freedom of worship, and the same law ordered death for anyone who denied the Trinity. Roger Williams in Rhode Island let people of every faith live as neighbors with equal rights. William Penn wrote the same freedom into Pennsylvania's Frame of Government.

In New Mexico, Spanish friars and governors punished Pueblo people for holding their own ceremonies. They whipped religious leaders and hanged some of them. In 1680 the Pueblo nations rose up and drove the Spanish out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Puritans" -->
The Puritans were English Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England. They wanted to remove what they saw as leftovers of the Roman Catholic Church. In the 1620s the men who ran the English church and government ordered them to follow church practices they hated. Those leaders put Puritan ministers out of their jobs and threatened them with "extirpation from the earth," which means being wiped out.

Bishops are the high officials who ran the Church of England, and English judges punished the bodies of Protestants who attacked them. In June 1630 the judges of a London court called the Star Chamber sentenced a Scottish minister named Alexander Leighton for writing a book against the bishops. The judges fined him 10,000 pounds and ordered him whipped. He was also to stand in the pillory, a wooden frame that locked a person's head and hands in a public place. The judges ordered one of his ears cut off and one side of his nose slit open. They also ordered his face branded with the letters S.S. Branding means pressing red-hot metal onto the skin to burn in a mark that never goes away. The letters stood for "sower of sedition," a person who stirs people up against the government.

Leighton escaped the night before the punishment, but he was caught again in Bedfordshire. Officers carried out the first part of the sentence on November 26, 1630. In 1640 the members of Parliament set him free, canceled his fine and voted to pay him 6,000 pounds.

From 1630, as many as 20,000 Puritans left England for America. They wanted the freedom to worship as they believed they should. John Winthrop's company of about 1,000 arrived in 1630 and settled on the Shawmut Peninsula, where Boston stands now. It was the land of the Massachusett people, who called it Mushauwomuk, "the boat landing place." Between 1616 and 1618 an epidemic had killed all but about 25,000 of the roughly 100,000 people of that region. In 1633 the Massachusett leader Chickataubut signed a treaty with Winthrop that gave up his rights to the peninsula.

The Pilgrims who landed at Plymouth in 1620 were a different group. They had left the Church of England completely and formed churches of their own. The Puritans believed the Church of England was a true church that needed to be fixed.

In Massachusetts, a person could not simply join a church. At first, only men and women who had gone through a conversion experience could become members. A conversion experience is a moment when a person feels that God has changed him or her. Each new member also had to convince the other members that it was real. The colonists pointed to the Bible as the authority for many of their criminal laws.

In 1640 Richard Mather and two other ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into verses that church members could sing. Stephen Daye printed their work at Cambridge, Massachusetts. It is called the *Bay Psalm Book*, and it was the first book printed in the English colonies of North America. Eleven copies still exist.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading for God" -->
In New England, the reason for teaching children to read was religious. In 1642 the men of the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmakers, ordered parents and masters to teach their children and apprentices to read. An apprentice was a young person who worked for a master to learn a trade. The law required them to learn enough "to read the English tongue" and to know the colony's "Capital Lawes." The capital laws were the laws that set death as the punishment for a crime.

In 1647 the General Court passed a second law. It ordered towns of 50 households to hire someone to teach reading and writing. Towns of 100 families had to set up a grammar school, a school that prepared young people for "the university," which meant Harvard College. The law opens by saying that it is "one of the chief projects of that old deluder Satan to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures." A deluder is someone who tricks people. People have called it the Old Deluder Satan Act ever since. The colonists wanted every child to be able to read the Bible without a minister in between.

Harvard College was founded in 1636 to train ministers. In 1643 a pamphlet by the founders, called *New Englands First Fruits*, was printed in England. It gives the college's purpose. The founders were "dreading to leave an illiterate Ministery to the Churches, when our present Ministers shall lie in the Dust." They feared that when their ministers died, no trained ministers would be left to replace them.

New England children learned to read from *The New England Primer*, which the printer Benjamin Harris made. No one knows the exact year it was first printed. Staff at the American Antiquarian Society, a research library, date it between 1687 and 1690, because the only evidence left is booksellers' advertisements. The first advertisement Harris placed for it appeared in 1690. No copy printed before 1727 survives.

One page of the Primer showed a picture of John Rogers being burned alive at Smithfield, in England, on February 4, 1555. He was the first Protestant put to death under Mary, the Catholic queen of England. The picture showed his wife and ten children beside the fire. A long poem next to it was written as his last advice to his children. It told them to "Abhor the arrant Whore of Rome," which meant to hate the Catholic Church. According to the Library of Congress, generations of New England children read these pages, and the pages helped feed the prejudice against Catholics that stayed strong in New England well into the 1800s. Prejudice means disliking a whole group of people without knowing them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Praying towns and Deer Island" -->
New England ministers also tried to bring the Native nations around them to Christianity. John Eliot, the minister at Roxbury, translated the whole Bible into the Algonquian language of the Massachusett people. It was printed at Cambridge in 1663, and it was the first Bible printed in any language in North America. At one time Eliot ministered to about 1,100 Christian Native people, whom the English called Praying Indians. They lived in fourteen towns laid out in the English style.

In 1675, during the war the English called King Philip's War, the members of the Massachusetts General Court ordered the Christian Native people of Natick taken from their homes and put on Deer Island in Boston Harbor. Daniel Gookin was the Massachusetts official in charge of dealings with the Praying Indians. He wrote that the order came from "false informations" given to the court to stir its members up against the people of Natick. Samuel Shrimpton of Boston held the island, and he agreed to the plan as long as they did not cut down any trees. Captain Thomas Prentiss and a party of horsemen brought the Natick people to the Charles River.

About midnight on October 30, 1675, under the court's order, about 200 people from Natick were put on three boats and carried to Deer Island. Gookin does not name the men who took them. John Eliot met them at the landing and prayed with them. They were afraid they would never come home and would be shipped out of the country. Massachusetts officials then sent the Punkapog people to the island too, "upon as little cause as the Naticks were," Gookin wrote. By late December about 500 people were held there. Gookin wrote that they lived mostly on clams and shellfish dug out of the sand at low tide. The island was cold, their wigwams, or houses, were poor, and they had few clothes, and thin ones.

Gookin wrote that the island kept them safe "from the fury of the people." His account of that winter gives no count of the dead. In 2025 the Episcopal bishops of Massachusetts and Western Massachusetts wrote that hundreds of Native people from the praying towns died on Deer Island from the cold and from starvation in the winter of 1675 to 1676.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quakers punished in Massachusetts" -->
The Quakers were a Protestant group that formed in England around a preacher named George Fox in 1652. They call themselves the Religious Society of Friends. They taught that the Spirit, or "Light of Christ," lives inside every person. Quaker women spoke and preached at Quaker meetings for worship, and other colonists found this offensive. Other Protestants called Quaker teaching heresy, a belief that goes against the teaching of the church. By 1680, English officials had put 10,000 Quakers in prison. According to the Library of Congress, 243 of them died in the king's jails "of torture and mistreatment."

The Puritan lawmakers of Massachusetts passed law after law against Quakers. The first came on October 14, 1656. The lawmakers called Quakers "the cursed sect of heretics." They fined ship captains who brought Quakers into the colony and people who owned Quaker books.

A law of 1657 set the punishment for a Quaker who came back after being thrown out of the colony. A man would have one ear cut off the first time, and the other ear the second time. A woman would be whipped. A third return was to be punished by boring a hole through the person's tongue with a hot iron.

Christopher Holder, John Copeland and John Rous were Quakers who came back. The judges sentenced each of them to have his right ear cut off by the hangman. On September 16, 1658, the deputy of the colony's marshal, a law officer, came to the prison with a group of men and shut the door. Then he had the right ear of each of the three men cut off. Afterward the three men said: "They that do it Ignorantly, We desire the Lord from our hearts to forgive them."

Massachusetts officials also punished the children of Quakers. Daniel and Provided Southwick were the son and daughter of Lawrence and Cassandra Southwick, Quakers of Salem. Judges at Salem and Ipswich fined them ten pounds for not going to the town's church meetings. They had no money to pay it. The members of the General Court then gave the county treasurers the power to sell the two children to any English buyer, in Virginia or Barbados, to pay the fine. The county treasurer, Edmond Batter, looked for a ship's captain to carry them to Barbados. The captain he found refused. The captain asked, "will ye offer to make Slaves of so harmless Creatures?" Batter sent them home. The order to sell them was never carried out.

On October 19, 1658, the lawmakers set the punishment of death for any Quaker who came back after being banished. To be banished means to be ordered out of a place and forbidden to return. Between 1659 and 1661 the colony's officials hanged four Quakers on Boston Common under that law. The governor, John Endecott, sentenced three of them.

William Robinson was a merchant from London, and Marmaduke Stephenson was a ploughman, a farm worker, from Yorkshire. Officials hanged them together on October 27, 1659. They hanged Mary Dyer on June 1, 1660, and William Leddra on March 14, 1661.

On September 9, 1661, after Quakers in England pressed him, King Charles II ordered the Massachusetts lawmakers to stop punishing Quakers. By 1677 Quakers could hold their meetings openly in Massachusetts.

Virginia's lawmakers passed their own laws against Quakers from 1659, including death for Quakers who kept returning. No one was put to death under them. Thomas Jefferson later wrote that the reason "was not owing to the moderation of the church, or the spirit of the legislature." Moderation means holding back, and the legislature was the colony's lawmakers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-dyer" name="Mary Dyer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mary Dyer

> **Who:** A Quaker woman hanged on Boston Common for returning to Massachusetts after she had been banished.
> **When and where:** Massachusetts and Rhode Island. Hanged in Boston on June 1, 1660.

Mary Dyer was hanged on Boston Common on June 1, 1660, for being a Quaker who came back to Massachusetts after she had been ordered out.

Her first trouble with the Massachusetts leaders came when she supported Anne Hutchinson, a Boston woman who was banished for her religious teaching. In 1638 the Massachusetts leaders forced Dyer and her family out, and they moved to Rhode Island. In the 1650s, in England, she became a Quaker.

She came back to New England to spread Quaker beliefs. The Massachusetts officials arrested her and banished her three times. In the spring of 1657 her husband, William Dyer, got her out of the Boston jail by confronting Governor John Endecott. Endecott let her go on the condition that she never return.

She returned. In October 1659 Endecott sentenced her to death along with two Quaker men, William Robinson and Marmaduke Stephenson. He told them they would "be hanged until you are dead." On October 27, 1659, the officials led all three to the gallows and made Dyer watch the two men hanged. She stood on the ladder with the rope around her neck. Then the officials announced a reprieve, which Endecott had agreed to the day before. A reprieve meant her hanging was called off. The officials sent her out of the colony.

She came back again. In May 1660 Endecott sentenced her to death a second time. Massachusetts officials hanged her on Boston Common on June 1, 1660.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-dyer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maryland, 1649" -->
In England all through the 1600s, most people hated the Catholic Church, and Catholics were harassed and punished. In 1632 George Calvert got a charter from King Charles I for the land that became Maryland. According to the Library of Congress, he wanted a safe place for his fellow Catholics. Two ships, the *Ark* and the *Dove*, carried about 200 settlers there. Two Catholic priests had to sneak on board to get away from England's laws against Catholics.

Maryland's colonists first landed on March 25, 1634. They later bought land from the Yaocomaco people and built St. Mary's City there. The records do not say what the Yaocomaco were paid or how they understood the sale. By 1649 the colony belonged to Cecil Calvert, the second Lord Baltimore.

In 1649 Calvert sent the Maryland assembly a set of proposals about religion. The assembly's members met at St. Mary's City from April 2, 1649. They rewrote part of what he sent, and on April 21 they voted it into law as An Act Concerning Religion. It has been called the Toleration Act ever since. Toleration means allowing people to hold beliefs you do not share.

The act protected only Christians. It said no person "professing to believe in Jesus Christ" should be "in any ways troubled, molested, or discountenanced" for his or her religion. Molested here meant bothered or attacked, and discountenanced meant treated with disapproval.

The same act ordered death for other beliefs. Anyone who cursed God, or denied that Jesus was the Son of God, or denied the Trinity, "shall be punished with death." The Trinity is the Christian belief that God is three persons in one: the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost. The person's land and goods would also be taken and given to Lord Baltimore. Under this law anyone who denied the Trinity, such as a Jew, a Muslim, or a Christian who did not believe in it, could be put to death.

The act also punished insults about religion. Calling someone a heretic, a Puritan, a Presbyterian, a "Popish Priest" or a similar name as an insult cost a fine of ten shillings. A shilling was an English coin. Half of the fine went to the person insulted. A person who could not pay was "publickly whipt" and kept in jail until he or she asked forgiveness in public.

In 1654 Puritans took control of Maryland and threw out the act. They took legal rights away from Catholics, Jews, Quakers and others. In 1661 Maryland's colonists canceled the Puritan laws, and the act was in force again. After 1689 the Church of England became Maryland's official church by law. Officials there enforced English laws that kept Catholics from voting, holding office or worshipping in public.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two colonies that left worship alone" -->
Roger Williams founded Providence in 1636, on land of the Narragansett people. He wanted it to be "a shelter for persons distressed for conscience." In Rhode Island, Anglicans, Puritans, Quakers, Catholics, Jews and people with no religion lived as neighbors with equal rights. Anglicans were members of the Church of England.

William Penn was a Quaker. In 1681 King Charles II owed a debt to Penn's father, and Penn turned it into a charter for Pennsylvania. A charter is a written grant of land and the right to govern it. The Lenape people, also called the Delaware, lived on both sides of the Delaware River. Beginning in 1682, the Lenape gave up land on the west bank of the river to Penn in exchange for cloth, guns, gunpowder, alcohol and other goods. By 1685 as many as 8,000 Quakers had moved to Pennsylvania.

In 1682 Penn wrote a Frame of Government, a plan for how the colony would be run. It stated that everyone who believed in "One Almighty and eternal God" would "in no wayes be molested," meaning bothered, for their religion. It also stated that no one would "be compelled at any time to frequent or maintain any Religious Worship, Place or Ministry whatever." That meant no one in Pennsylvania had to attend a church, and no one had to pay for one.

The first group of Germans reached Philadelphia in 1683, from the town of Krefeld. They were Mennonites and possibly some Dutch Quakers. Mennonites, Dunkers, Schwenkfelders, Moravians and German Baptists followed. These were small Protestant groups from Germany and nearby lands, and their members had been punished for their religion in Europe. People described Pennsylvania as "an asylum for banished sects," a safe place for religious groups that had been driven out.

In 1654 twenty-three Jewish refugees from Dutch Brazil arrived in New Amsterdam, the Dutch town that became New York. Within a year they were holding religious services there. By 1658 Jews had also settled at Newport, Rhode Island, where they could worship freely.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="roger-williams" name="Roger Williams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Roger Williams

> **Who:** A minister thrown out of Massachusetts who founded Rhode Island, where the government stayed out of religion.
> **When and where:** Born in London about 1603. Massachusetts from 1631. Founded Providence in 1636. Died in Providence in 1683.

Roger Williams taught that a government has no right to force religion on anyone. He called the freedom to follow one's own conscience "soul liberty." He built a colony on that idea.

He was born in London about 1603, the son of a merchant. He studied at Cambridge University and became a minister in the Church of England. He disliked the church's ranks of officials, its wealth, its power in government and its decorations. He reached Massachusetts Bay in 1631, one year after the Puritans settled it.

In Massachusetts, Puritan religious rules governed daily life, and the government enforced them. Williams preached that the government should not enforce anyone's religion. He said a person's spiritual life should be kept separate from public life. The judges of the colony's General Court ordered him out of Massachusetts for preaching these ideas. He fled in the winter, because he might have been put to death if he had been sent back to England.

Wampanoag people took him in and kept him alive through that winter. He had traded with them and learned their language. With help from Wampanoag and Narragansett people, he traveled by canoe to a freshwater spring at a place called Moshassuck. In 1636 he settled there with the permission of the Narragansett. He named the place Providence. In 1638 the Narragansett leaders Canonicus and Miantonomi signed a deed, a written paper that hands over land, and it set up the settlement. The deed kept trading rights inside the settlement.

In Rhode Island, people of every faith lived as neighbors with the same rights. Williams thought many of them were wrong about religion. He believed that correcting them was not the government's job.

In 1643 he published *A Key into the Language of America*, a book about the Native languages of New England, mostly the Narragansett language, and about Narragansett daily life. In 1644 he published *The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution*, an argument against forced worship. In it he wrote: "God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any civill state." Uniformity of religion means everyone being made to follow the same religion. He also wrote that "forced worship stinks in God's nostrils."

In 1644 the members of England's Parliament granted Rhode Island a patent, a paper that recognized the colony. In 1663 Williams went back to England, and King Charles II signed a charter for the colony. Williams died in Providence in 1683. In 1965 the members of Congress voted to set up the Roger Williams National Memorial in Providence.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roger-williams" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-religion" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** A Boston woman who held religious meetings in her home, taught her own reading of the Bible, and was banished and put out of her church for it.
> **When and where:** Born in Alford, England, about 1591. Boston from 1634. Banished in November 1637. Killed in 1643.

The leaders of Massachusetts put Anne Hutchinson out of the colony for what she taught about God, and then the members of her church put her out of the church. She was born about 1591 in Alford, England. In the autumn of 1634 she sailed to Boston with her husband, William, and their children. They followed their minister, John Cotton.

From 1636 she held a meeting in her home once a week to talk over the Sunday sermon. At first only women came. Then men came too, including important men of the colony. In Massachusetts the minister's sermon in the meeting house was the official teaching. A second meeting in a private house, led by a woman, was a rival to it.

She taught that God's grace alone decides who is saved. Grace means God's free gift of love and forgiveness. She said good behavior played no part. She also said she could tell which ministers and which neighbors had received God's grace and which had not. That second claim put her in judgment over the colony's ministers. Her opponents called her teaching antinomian, a word that means "against the law." They meant that her teaching let a saved person ignore the rules.

At her trial she said that God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation." She said God would curse the Puritans if they harmed her. The judges called that blasphemy, which means speaking against God.

She faced two trials, five months apart. In November 1637 the members of the General Court tried her at Newtown, now Cambridge, and banished her. In March 1638 the members of the church at Boston tried her separately and excommunicated her. To be excommunicated is to be put out of the church. The church's charges said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer." The same charge went on: "and a Magistrate than a Subject." A magistrate is an official who governs or judges, and a subject is a person who is governed.

In the spring of 1638 she moved to Aquidneck Island in what became Rhode Island and founded the town of Portsmouth. After her husband died, she moved in 1642 to land on Long Island Sound that the Dutch claimed. In February 1643 soldiers of the Dutch governor, Willem Kieft, killed at least eighty, and perhaps as many as 120, Munsee people in one night. War followed. In August or September 1643, during that war, Siwanoy people attacked her home. They killed her and several of her children. One daughter survived.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-religion" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Baptism and slavery in Virginia, 1667" -->
Some colonists argued that a Christian could not be held as a slave. In September 1667 the members of Virginia's lawmaking assembly settled the question in favor of slavery. Their law said that being baptized "doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome." Bondage means slavery. The law's own words give the lawmakers' reason: once enslavers were sure that baptism would not free the people they held, they would be more willing to let them be baptized.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico: punished for their ceremonies" -->
The Pueblo nations of New Mexico hold their religious ceremonies in kivas, rooms set aside for worship. Some of their ceremonies are masked dances. For about eighty years the Franciscan friars demanded that the kivas be destroyed and the masked dances ended. The friars called the dances idolatry, which means worshipping false gods.

From 1656 to 1665, Alonso de Posada, the head of the Franciscan missionaries, forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered the missionaries to go into the kivas, burn the masks, and take or destroy the sacred objects. In 1661 Spanish soldiers as well as friars raided kivas and burned sacred masks. The Newberry Library is a research library in Chicago. Historians there write that missionaries almost certainly whipped Native people who did not take part in Christian services. Spanish investigations in New Mexico between 1620 and 1680 recorded many cases of missionaries punishing Native people severely. According to the Architect of the Capitol's account of Po'pay, Pueblo people "found practicing their religion were tortured and sometimes executed."

In 1675 Juan Francisco Treviño, the Spanish governor, ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo medicine men for sorcery. Sorcery means using magic to cause harm. Spanish officials sentenced four of them to be hanged. Three were hanged, and the fourth killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the others in public and put them in prison. The records do not name the men who did the hanging and the whipping.

One of the men whipped was Po'pay, of Ohkay Owingeh, also called San Juan Pueblo. He was born about 1630. His name means "ripe squash" in the Tewa language. He was a farmer and a member of a medicine society, and his work was healing and the spiritual life of his people. Spanish officials had him flogged in public. Flogging is beating a person on the back with a whip. The whip leaves wounds that heal into scars.

When Pueblo leaders heard about the arrests, they sent armed men to Santa Fe, where the prisoners were held. Many Spanish soldiers were away at the time, so Treviño gave in and released the prisoners. Po'pay was one of them.

On August 10, 1680, the Pueblo nations rose against the Spanish. Po'pay led the rising, and he planned it from Taos Pueblo. The leaders had set it for August 13. On August 9 the Spanish captured two of the runners who were carrying knotted cords to the pueblos, Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua of Tesuque Pueblo. Spanish officials tortured them to make them explain the cords, and later hanged them. The records do not name the men who did it. Po'pay ordered the rising to begin early. The Pueblo fighters killed about 400 people, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in New Mexico, and burned mission churches across the province. The Pueblo nations kept the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years.

The Pueblo nations run their own museum, the Indian Pueblo Cultural Center. Its account of those eighty years of Spanish rule states that "the Pueblo people suffered violence, cruelty, and hunger." It lists "exploitation of labor and resources." It also lists "harassment and persecution for practicing long-held religious traditions that are interwoven in daily life." That means the Spanish forced Pueblo people to work for them, took what they had, and bothered and punished them again and again for keeping their own religion.

In 2005 a statue of Po'pay, given by the state of New Mexico, was placed in the National Statuary Hall Collection in the United States Capitol. Po'pay was the first person in that collection who was born on land that became the United States. No picture or written description of Po'pay survives, so the face is the sculptor's own work. The sculptor, Cliff Fragua of Jemez Pueblo, wrote: "On his back are the scars that remain from the whipping he received for his participation and faith in the Pueblo ceremonies and religion."

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1730s and 1740s, traveling preachers held revivals across the colonies. A revival is a series of meetings meant to stir people to a stronger faith. People in many colonies took part in the same religious movement at the same time. It is called the Great Awakening.

Earlier, in 1704, English colonists from Carolina and Creek fighters under James Moore destroyed the Spanish missions among the Apalachee of Florida. In 1741 New York officials hanged a man they called a Catholic priest.

Most colonists already went to church. By one estimate, 75 to 80 percent of colonists attended church between 1700 and 1740, and colonists were building new churches fast. The revival preachers wanted each person to go through the new birth: a conversion that a person could feel happening inside. Churches split over whether the new birth was real. Critics said many of the conversions were false, and even the work of the devil.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Apalachee missions destroyed, 1704" -->
In 1704 English colonists from Carolina and their Creek allies destroyed the Spanish missions among the Apalachee people. The Apalachee lived around what is now Tallahassee, Florida. Many of them were baptized Catholics who lived in fourteen Franciscan mission towns.

In September 1703 the members of the Carolina assembly approved the attack. They asked James Moore, a former governor of Carolina, to lead it. Moore promised that the attack would cost the colony nothing, because he would pay for it "by the taking of loot and slaves." That meant stealing goods and capturing people to sell as slaves. He led fifty Englishmen and about one thousand Creek men, also called Muscogee.

Moore's men destroyed nearly all of the fourteen mission towns. At the church of Ayubale, on January 25, 1704, a friar named Angel de Miranda and 26 men held off the attackers for about nine hours. They surrendered when they ran out of arrows. A magazine account based on the work of the historian John Hann tells what the attackers did at Ayubale. They burned one friar alive and cut off his head. They tortured two Native people and burned them at the stake. In a later raid that year, at the Patale mission on June 23, 1704, the attackers killed the friar Manuel de Mendoza and burned the church. They tied prisoners to the Stations of the Cross, a set of markers used in Catholic prayer. They cut the prisoners with knives, pushed burning sticks into the wounds, and burned them to death. The records name James Moore as the commander of the January raid. They do not name the men who tortured and burned the prisoners.

The counts of people taken vary widely. Moore reported that his army killed more than 1,100 men, women and children and captured more than 4,300 people as slaves, most of them women and children. Moore had promised to pay for the raid with captives, and his count is the highest. Other counts run from hundreds of people to about 1,400. The historian John Hann counts about 2,000 Apalachee who went into exile after the raids, and he writes that an unknown number were enslaved. In 1680 the fourteen mission towns had held about 8,000 people.

Many Apalachee were unhappy with the forced labor the Spanish demanded, which included carrying goods about 100 miles to St. Augustine. The Spanish had also forbidden mission Apalachee to own guns. Many Apalachee left with Moore. In his report, Moore said that most of the people of seven villages chose to join him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Lights and Old Lights" -->
The revival split churches. Congregationalists were members of the churches the Puritans had founded in New England. Among them, the people who supported the revival were called New Lights. Among the Presbyterians they were called the New Side. The people who opposed the revival were the Old Lights and the Old Side.

By the middle of the 1740s the revival had ended in New England. Preachers carried it south, where it started revivals that went on into the 1800s. The Presbyterians, Baptists and Methodists had backed the revival. By the early 1800s they were the largest Protestant churches in the country. According to the Library of Congress, the Anglicans, the Quakers and the Congregationalists "were left behind."

A small number of colonists went a different way. They were deists. Deists stressed living a good life, and they rejected the Christian teaching that Jesus was God. Thomas Jefferson and John Adams were among them. Deists were always a small group.

Drawings of New York City from 1690, 1730 and 1771 show the churches multiplying. The 1690 view shows no steeples, the tall towers on top of churches. The 1771 view shows eighteen.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A college built by the revival" -->
In these years, only graduates of Harvard or Yale could become Presbyterian ministers in the colonies. Under that rule, the young men taught by William Tennent could not become Presbyterian ministers.

Tennent was a Presbyterian minister, born in Ireland and educated in Edinburgh, Scotland. From 1726 until he died in 1745 he ran a school at Neshaminy, in Bucks County, Pennsylvania. People called it the Log College, because it was a log house about twenty feet long and almost as wide. He taught his students ancient languages and the Bible. Several of them became revival preachers, including his four sons.

In early 1746 a group of New Lights planned a college in New Jersey to train young men for the ministry. On October 22, 1746, John Hamilton, the acting governor of New Jersey, granted a charter for the College of New Jersey. It is now Princeton University. Princeton's own historians call its founding "a direct outgrowth" of the Great Awakening. Six months after the charter, three Log College graduates became trustees of the college: Samuel Blair, Gilbert Tennent and William Tennent Jr.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Preaching to enslaved people" -->
Revival preachers took their message to enslaved people, and enslavers objected. In 1740 George Whitefield printed an open letter in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* to the enslavers of Maryland, Virginia and the Carolinas. He wrote down their reason for keeping enslaved people from Christianity: that "teaching them Christianity would make them proud, and consequently unwilling to submit to Slavery." He called that "a dreadful Reflection" on their own religion. He did not say in the letter that slavery was wrong. Whitefield himself later held enslaved people.

One name survives from Northampton, Massachusetts. Leah, who was enslaved in the household of the minister Jonathan Edwards, became a full member of the Northampton church during the revival.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-whitefield" name="George Whitefield" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Whitefield

> **Who:** An English preacher whose tours of the colonies were the high point of the Great Awakening, and who worked to make slavery legal in Georgia.
> **When and where:** Born in Gloucester, England, December 16, 1714. Seven visits to America from 1738. Died in Newburyport, Massachusetts, September 30, 1770.

George Whitefield drew crowds of up to 30,000 people in the Great Awakening, and he also pushed to make slavery legal in Georgia. He was born in Gloucester, England, on December 16, 1714. He became a minister in the Church of England and was often in conflict with its leaders.

He first reached Georgia in May 1738. He came back in November 1739, landing at Philadelphia, and reached Savannah in January 1740. His preaching tour of the colonies from 1739 to 1741 was the high point of the Great Awakening in America. A sermon he gave in Boston drew as many as 30,000 people.

Many churches would not let him preach inside, so he preached outdoors from a folding pulpit that he carried with him. The first recorded use of it was at Moorfields, in England, on April 9, 1742, in front of a crowd estimated at twenty or thirty thousand. People in the crowd threw stones, rotten eggs and pieces of dead cat at him. He is estimated to have preached about two thousand sermons from that pulpit.

He preached the new birth. In a printed sermon of 1739 he admitted that "how this glorious Change is wrought in the Soul cannot easily be explained." The Library of Congress lists the reasons crowds came to hear him: a strong voice, a way of speaking like an actor on a stage, the strong feelings he stirred up, simple messages, and clever use of the new ways of advertising. In 1763 a London engraving called him "Dr. Squintum," mocking his eye disease. It also accused him of sexual wrongdoing and of getting rich from his preaching.

In 1740 he founded an orphanage called Bethesda near Savannah. At that time slavery was against the law in Georgia. In 1741 he wrote that farming more land than his hired servants and older boys could manage was "impracticable without a few negroes." In 1747 he wrote to a donor that friends in South Carolina were giving money toward buying "a plantation and slaves" to support Bethesda. He added: "One negroe has been given me." He wrote that he planned to buy more that week.

From 1747 he pressed the trustees who ran Georgia to lift the ban on slavery. He wrote that "Georgia never can be a flourishing province, unless negroes are employed." He told them that unless the ban ended, he could not "cultivate the plantation in any manner." The trustees gave way. Slavery became legal in Georgia in the early 1750s. Some sources give 1751. Others give 1752, when Georgia became a royal colony, run directly by the king's officials instead of the trustees.

Whitefield died at Newburyport, Massachusetts, on September 30, 1770. In his will he left 4,000 acres of land in Georgia and fifty enslaved people to the Countess of Huntingdon. Phillis Wheatley, a girl of about seventeen who was enslaved in Boston, wrote a poem about his death that was printed that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-whitefield" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jonathan-edwards" name="Jonathan Edwards" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jonathan Edwards

> **Who:** The minister of the Northampton revival, whom the historian Perry Miller called "the first and greatest homegrown American philosopher." He bought and held enslaved people from 1731 until at least 1756.
> **When and where:** Born in East Windsor, Connecticut, October 5, 1703. Northampton, Massachusetts, from 1726. Died in Princeton, New Jersey, March 22, 1758.

Jonathan Edwards led a revival in his own church at Northampton, Massachusetts, and wrote the books that other revival preachers learned from. He was born on October 5, 1703, in East Windsor, Connecticut. He was the fifth of eleven children of a minister. He went to Yale in 1716 and finished first in the class of 1720.

In 1726 he joined his grandfather, Solomon Stoddard, as a minister of the church at Northampton. It was the largest church in Massachusetts outside Boston. He later took his grandfather's place. In 1727 he married Sarah Pierpont, and they had ten children.

In 1734 and 1735 a revival took place in his congregation. Edwards wrote a book about it, *A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God*. Revival preachers in America and Britain used it as a model, and it made him famous.

On July 8, 1741, he preached a sermon at Enfield, Connecticut, called "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God." He told the people that their own care and goodness had "no more influence to uphold you and keep you out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock." He also said: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked." Abhors means hates. He preached that God controls everything, that people are sinful by nature, that hell is real, and that a person has to be born again.

Critics said many of the revival's conversions were false, or even the work of the devil. Edwards answered them in four books between 1741 and 1749, among them *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* in 1746. In them he tried to set out how a person could tell a real change of heart from a false one.

Edwards bought and held enslaved people from 1731 until at least 1756. Kenneth Minkema, of the Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale, lays out the record. The first was a teenage girl named Venus, whom Edwards bought at Newport in 1731. The last was a child named Titus, in 1756. Others whose names are known were Leah, Rose, and a married couple, Joseph and Sue. His wife, Sarah, actively tried to get enslaved people from family members and from her husband's fellow ministers. Edwards defended slavery as allowed by the Bible. He argued that it was a way to bring enslaved people to Christianity.

Late in his life he came to oppose the slave trade. He wrote that it was unjust to take away the freedom of people born free. After his death his son, Jonathan Edwards Jr., and his student Samuel Hopkins called for ending slavery completely.

In 1750 the members of his congregation dismissed him, after he tried to make the rules stricter for who could take communion. Communion is the Christian ceremony of sharing bread and wine in memory of Jesus. From 1751 to 1757 he worked at Stockbridge, on the western edge of Massachusetts. He preached to a small English congregation and was a missionary to about 150 Mohican and Mohawk families. Stockbridge was a Mohican town. In 1749, 218 Mohican people lived there. By 1774 the Mohican land at Stockbridge had shrunk from 23,000 acres to 1,200 acres. In 1783 the Mohicans were forced to leave the rest. Their own history does not name the people who took the land or forced them out. The Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale states that Edwards was "an agent in depriving Native Americans of their lands and culture."

In late 1757 Edwards agreed to become president of the College of New Jersey. A few months after he arrived, he was inoculated against smallpox. Inoculation then meant giving a healthy person a small dose of live smallpox on purpose, so that the person would have a mild case and be safe afterward. A few of the people inoculated died from it. Edwards was one of them. He died on March 22, 1758.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jonathan-edwards" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A man hanged as a Catholic priest, New York, 1741" -->
In 1700 the members of the New York assembly, the colony's lawmakers, passed a law that threatened any Catholic priest who came to New York with prison for life.

In 1741, after a string of fires, New York City officials claimed that enslaved people and poor white people had plotted to burn the city. By the end of August, the judges and officials who ran the trials had put 30 to 34 people to death and sentenced 70 to be sold as slaves in the Caribbean. By the count of the historian Jill Lepore, they burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 Black men, two white men and two white women. While those trials were going on, James Oglethorpe, the governor of Georgia, wrote to New York's lieutenant governor, the deputy governor. He warned that Spain had sent agents to burn English towns in North America. He wrote that "many priests were employed" in disguise, pretending to be doctors or dancing masters. New York officials began hunting for hidden priests. They arrested John Ury, a schoolteacher who knew Latin. Ury said he was a minister of the Church of England.

Mary Burton, a young servant, had been the main witness in the earlier trials. A judge had promised her money and an end to her years of service. She had never mentioned Ury before. Now she said he was the leader of the plot. She said he had performed Catholic ceremonies for the plotters. Two more witnesses backed her. One was William Kane, a soldier Burton had accused, who went along to save himself. The other was Sarah Hughson, who was brought from the cell where she waited to be executed.

New York's attorney general, the colony's top lawyer, was a man named Bradley. At the trial he attacked the Catholic religion as "murderous" and read Oglethorpe's letter to the jury. Ury had no lawyer. His own witnesses said he was not a Catholic priest and that he had been elsewhere on the nights in question. The jury took less than fifteen minutes to find him guilty. New York officials hanged John Ury on August 29, 1741. Before he died he said he was innocent and forgave the men who had prosecuted him. He was the last person executed for the supposed plot.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="religion" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1776, several colonies had an established church. An established church is one that the government supports with tax money. In Massachusetts it was the Congregational church. In Virginia it was the Church of England, also called the Anglican church. People had to pay taxes for its ministers, and in Virginia they also had to attend its services. After the Revolution, the lawmakers of each state decided for themselves whether to keep a church tax. Massachusetts was the last state to end one, in 1833. The First Amendment barred Congress from setting up a national church.

Baptists and other people outside the tax-supported churches had paid for ministers they never listened to. They organized against the church taxes. They won in Virginia in 1786 and lost in Massachusetts, where the tax lasted until 1833. In the same years, Black Americans who had been born into slavery founded churches of their own. On the Pacific coast, Spanish Franciscans built missions from 1769 on the lands of many Native nations. Missionaries and soldiers whipped people, locked them in the stocks and hunted down those who ran away, to keep them in the missions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three more colleges with church backing" -->
In these years three more colleges were founded with church backing. Rhode Island College was founded in 1764 with the support of the Philadelphia Baptist Association, a group of Baptist churches. It was renamed Brown University in 1804. Queen's College, tied to the Dutch Reformed Church, received its charter on November 10, 1766. It is now Rutgers University. Dartmouth College received its charter on December 13, 1769. Its founder was Eleazar Wheelock, a Congregational minister.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fight over the church tax in Virginia" -->
Virginia's established church was the Church of England. Colonists had to attend its services and pay taxes for its ministers. A parish was the area served by one church. The men who ran each parish also cared for poor people and orphans, so they did the work of a local government as well as running a church.

In 1779 the members of the Virginia assembly stopped the tax that paid Church of England ministers. On December 24, 1784, Patrick Henry put forward a bill for a new religious tax. Under his plan, each taxpayer could send his share to the church of his choice.

Some leading Virginians backed a religious tax. Richard Henry Lee wrote that "the experience of all times shows religion to be the guardian of morals." John Marshall, who later became Chief Justice of the United States, voted to move Henry's bill forward rather than put it off. George Washington wrote to George Mason that he did not object to "making people pay towards the support of that which they profess." He thought the bill would stir up trouble, though.

James Madison led the other side. He got the vote put off until the autumn of 1785. On June 20, 1785, he published a long argument against religious taxes called the *Memorial and Remonstrance*, without his name on it. A remonstrance is a formal protest. Baptists and most Presbyterians were on his side. They argued that tax money spoils a church, and that a government has no right to control a person's religion. By the autumn of 1785 Madison had the votes, and Henry's bill was defeated.

In its place Madison brought back a bill that Thomas Jefferson had written in 1777. The members of the assembly passed it on January 16, 1786, and it became law three days later. It is called the Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom. It says: "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place, or ministry whatsoever." Jefferson wrote that it was meant to protect "the Jew and the Gentile," the "Mahometan," the "Hindoo," and "Infidel of every denomination." A Gentile is a person who is not Jewish. Mahometan was an old word for a Muslim, and Hindoo was an old spelling of Hindu. An infidel is a person who does not believe in a religion.

In most states the lawmakers did not follow Virginia in these years. The lawmakers of Massachusetts, Connecticut and New Hampshire set up religious taxes that people had to pay. Maryland's and Georgia's lawmakers passed religious taxes but never collected them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="No religious test, and the First Amendment" -->
The Constitution written in Philadelphia in 1787 says almost nothing about religion. Its one rule about religion is in Article VI. It says that "no religious Test shall ever be required" for a job in the national government. A religious test was an oath about religious beliefs that a person had to swear before taking office. Charles Pinckney of South Carolina proposed the rule on August 20, 1787. The delegates at the convention adopted it on August 30.

On June 28, 1787, Benjamin Franklin, who was eighty-one, asked the convention to open each day with prayers. He told the delegates: "the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this Truth." He went on: "that God governs in the Affairs of Men." The delegates did not approve his motion. The convention had no money to pay local ministers to lead the prayers, according to reports of the time.

Some people complained that the Constitution contained "no recognition of his mercies to us . . . or even of his existence," meaning God's. Many of the voters in Madison's part of Virginia were Baptists, and they wanted freedom of religion protected in writing. On June 8, 1789, Madison introduced a set of amendments, or changes, to the Constitution. In his notes for that speech he wrote against a "national" religion.

On September 28, 1789, the members of both houses of Congress voted to send twelve amendments to the states. By December 1791 the lawmakers of three fourths of the states, the number needed, had approved ten of them. The first of the ten begins: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." Those words mean that Congress may not set up an official church, and may not stop people from practicing their religion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last church tax" -->
Massachusetts lawmakers kept their state's church tax for forty-two years after the First Amendment was approved. Article III of the state's 1780 constitution required every town and parish to pay for "public Protestant teachers of piety, religion and morality." It also allowed the lawmakers to order people to go and listen to those teachers. A man's church tax went to the minister of his own church if his town had one. If it did not, his tax went to the town's parish minister.

Article III was the part of that constitution people argued about most. In a sermon on May 27, 1778, Phillips Payson, a Congregational minister at Chelsea, argued for it. He said: "let the restraints of religion once be broken down . . . and one might well defy all human wisdom and power to support and preserve order and government in the state." He meant that without religion, no one could keep order. Isaac Backus, the leader of the Baptists of New England, answered him that year in a pamphlet. He argued that government money spoils churches, and that the best thing a government can do for religion is to leave it alone.

The Baptists lost. There was strong doubt that Article III had won the two thirds of the vote that it needed. In 1780 the state's officials declared it adopted anyway.

Article III stood for fifty-three years. In 1833 a new amendment to the state constitution replaced it. Churches could still choose their ministers and agree to pay them, but they had to raise the money themselves. Any member could leave a church by handing its clerk a written notice. Massachusetts was the last state to end its church tax.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Baptists in Savannah" -->
George Liele was born into slavery in Virginia in 1750. In 1775 he was ordained a Baptist minister, and he preached at plantations along the Savannah River. In 1778 he gathered a congregation of Black Baptists in Savannah, Georgia. During the Revolution he told his followers to side with the British, who promised to treat African Americans as free people. When the British left their headquarters on Tybee Island, near Savannah, in 1782, many of Liele's followers went with them. They founded settlements and churches in Jamaica, Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone. Liele went to Jamaica.

Andrew Bryan kept the Savannah congregation going. He was born at Goose Creek, South Carolina, and was enslaved by a man named Jonathan Bryan. On January 20, 1788, in a barn at Brampton plantation, a white minister named Abraham Marshall and a Black minister named Jesse Peter baptized more than forty of Bryan's followers. They made the group a church and ordained Bryan as its minister. The church was named the Ethiopian Church of Jesus Christ.

Many white people in Savannah feared that any gathering of enslaved people could be the start of a revolt. A letter written from Savannah in 1790 by Jonathan Clarke tells what they did. White people of Savannah broke up the worship and took many of the worshippers before magistrates, who were local judges. The worshippers were jailed twice. About fifty were severely whipped. Andrew Bryan was cut and "bled abundantly," which means he bled heavily. The letter does not say who used the whip or who gave the order.

Clarke wrote that Bryan said he "rejoiced not only to be whipped, but would freely suffer death for the cause of Jesus Christ." The chief justice, Henry Osborne, questioned the worshippers along with James Habersham and David Montague, and they released them. George Walton said the treatment of the worshippers "would be condemned even among barbarians." Osborne then allowed them to worship between sunrise and sunset.

In 1790 Bryan counted about 575 followers. Of those, 225 were full members. About 350 others were converts, and many of them did not have their enslavers' permission to be baptized. Bryan later bought freedom for himself and his family, and he bought land for the church. He died in 1812.

### Richard Allen

> **Who:** A preacher born into slavery who bought his freedom, walked out of a white-run church in Philadelphia, and founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church.
> **When and where:** Born into slavery on February 14, 1760, held by a Philadelphia lawyer. Delaware, then Philadelphia. Died in Philadelphia, March 26, 1831.

Richard Allen founded Bethel Church in Philadelphia after a white trustee pulled a Black member up off his knees during prayer. He was born into slavery on February 14, 1760. He was enslaved by Benjamin Chew, a Philadelphia lawyer, who sold Allen and his family to Stokely Sturgis, a farmer near Dover, Delaware. Allen began preaching as a teenager, while he was still enslaved.

In 1780 he bought his freedom for $2,000. He earned the money by working in his own time for about five years. The dollars were Continental money, paper money printed during the Revolution, which by 1780 had lost most of its value.

He came to Philadelphia in February 1786. He wrote: "I frequently preached twice a day, at 5 o'clock in the morning and in the evening, and it was not uncommon for me to preach from four to five times a day." That year he gathered a group of forty-two members. Absalom Jones, William White and Dorus Ginnings joined him in wanting a church for Black Philadelphians. The elder in charge at St. George's Methodist Church, where they worshipped, forbade their prayer meetings. Allen wrote that the Black members "were considered as a nuisance."

At St. George's, Allen wrote, the Black members were moved out of their usual seats and placed along the wall. He does not say who moved them. Then one Sunday the sexton, the church's caretaker, sent them up to the gallery, a balcony above the main floor. As they knelt there to pray, a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones and pulled him up off his knees. Allen wrote down the words. The trustee said, "You must get up," and then, "you must not kneel here." Jones answered, "Wait until prayer is over, and I will get up and trouble you no more." The trustee said, "No, you must get up now, or I will call for aid and force you away." A second trustee went to pull up William White.

Allen wrote: "By this time, prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church." He added that the Black members had helped pay for the church, "in building the gallery and laying new floors."

The date of the walkout is disputed. Allen's own account gives no date. The traditional date is 1787. The historian Richard S. Newman argues for 1792 or 1793, which would make it a planned protest rather than a sudden one. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia places it "in the early 1790s."

The group rented a storeroom for worship. Allen wrote that they were threatened with being "read publicly out of meeting," which meant expelled in front of the whole church, if they kept worshipping there. Benjamin Rush and Robert Ralston gave money for a new church. On their first day collecting money, Allen and Jones raised $360. Allen called it "the greatest day's collection that we met with."

Allen bought a lot at Sixth and Lombard Streets in 1791. The first building was an old blacksmith's shop. He opened Bethel Church there in July 1794. In 1799 he became the first Black man ordained a Methodist minister. In 1816 he brought African Methodist churches from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland together in Philadelphia. They formed a new church body, the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and elected Allen its first bishop, a leader over many churches. He died at his home on March 26, 1831, and was buried in the basement of Mother Bethel, as his church came to be called.

### Absalom Jones

> **Who:** A man born into slavery who taught himself to read and became the first priest of African descent in the Episcopal Church in the United States.
> **When and where:** Born in Delaware, November 6, 1746. Philadelphia from 1762. Died 1818.

Absalom Jones was the first priest of African descent in the United States. He was born into slavery in Delaware on November 6, 1746. He taught himself to read, and the New Testament was one of the books he learned from. When he was sixteen, his mother, his sister and his five brothers were sold. The records do not name who sold them. The man who enslaved Jones took him to Philadelphia in 1762. There he went to a Quaker night school for Black people.

He bought his freedom in 1784. In 1787 he and Richard Allen started the Free African Society, which worked to end slavery and helped widows, orphans and poor people. In 1792, with help from local Quakers and Episcopalians, Jones and Allen set up the First African Church in Philadelphia.

After the walkout from St. George's, Jones led the congregation into the Episcopal Church. On October 17, 1794, the congregation was formally received into the Episcopal Diocese of Pennsylvania as the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. A diocese is a group of churches under one bishop. Jones was ordained a deacon, a minister one rank below a priest, in 1795. Bishop William White ordained him a priest when he was fifty-six. The Episcopal Church's own archives give the year as 1802. Delaware's state historical marker gives 1804.

Jones also started a day school, a Female Benevolent Society and an African Friendly Society. In 1800 he sent a petition to Congress about slavery. He died in 1818.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christian Lenape killed at Gnadenhutten, 1782" -->
On March 8, 1782, American militiamen, part-time soldiers from the local area, killed about 96 Christian Lenape and Mohican people at Gnadenhutten, in the Tuscarawas Valley of Ohio. The victims were converts of the Moravian church and lived in Moravian mission villages. They had refused to fight for either side in the Revolution. Colonel David Williamson led between 100 and 200 of them.

The militiamen falsely accused the Christian converts of taking their goods in raids. They held them in houses and voted to kill them. Williamson asked the militiamen who wanted to spare the prisoners to stand apart. Only sixteen or eighteen did. The prisoners, knowing what was coming, prayed and sang hymns. The militiamen tied them up and killed them by smashing their heads with a mallet, one after another, taking turns as each man got tired. Then they cut off their scalps, the skin and hair of the top of the head. They killed 62 adults and 34 children. Two boys escaped. One of them, named Thomas, lived after he was scalped.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The California missions" -->
In 1769 Spanish soldiers and Franciscan friars began building missions in Alta California, which is now the state of California. Spain's officials wanted to hold the Pacific coast against Russia and England, and they had little money. Sending missionaries to live on Native homelands was a cheap way for Spain to claim them. Soldiers and missionaries came together. The missionaries' job was to make Native people Catholics and to change their whole way of life.

Father Junípero Serra led the Franciscans. He was born in 1713 at Petra, on the island of Mallorca, and he had worked as a missionary in Mexico for seventeen years. He founded Mission San Carlos Borromeo at Monterey in 1770 and lived there until he died in 1784. By the end of 1800 the Franciscans had founded eighteen missions. By 1823 there were twenty-one.

About 310,000 Native people lived in California in 1769. Each mission was built in the homeland of a people with its own government and language. Mission San Diego stood on Kumeyaay land and San Luis Rey on Payómkawichum land. San Juan Capistrano was on Acjachemen land, San Gabriel on Tongva land, and San Fernando on Tataviam land. Santa Bárbara, San Buenaventura, La Purísima and San Luis Obispo were on Chumash land. San Antonio and San Miguel were on Salinan land, and Soledad was on Esselen land. Six missions around Monterey Bay and San Francisco Bay were built on the lands of different Ohlone peoples.

Serra's writings show a plan to baptize young children first. He expected the children to learn Spanish and translate for their parents. Edward Ketchum, a tribal historian, has said that once a child was baptized, the church treated the child as its "property." A National Register report on the California missions, drawing on the scholars Cook and Madley, describes persuasion in the early years, with force growing after about 1790 to 1800. Native accounts make clear that force was used in many baptisms. When Native people kept resisting, the missionaries called in soldiers, who captured runaways and people who had not been baptized.

Missionaries punished baptized Native people to keep them from leaving the missions or to force them to come back. They punished people for running away, stealing, fighting and drinking, and for other acts they called disobedience. The missionaries sometimes gave the punishments themselves. They also had soldiers or Native officials deliver them. Soldiers patrolled the countryside around the missions and punished people there too.

In 2023 researchers wrote a report on the missions for the state of California, with advisers from 19 California tribes. It calls flogging "probably the most common" punishment. Flogging is beating a person with a whip, which cuts and bruises the skin. At the missions it was done in the open, in front of others. Some Franciscans, Serra among them, whipped themselves as a religious practice, so whipping seemed to them a fair punishment for others. In a letter of July 31, 1775, Serra asked the military commander Fernando de Rivera y Moncada to have four men who had run from Mission Carmel whipped "two or three" times. He offered to send shackles, iron rings locked around the legs or wrists. The journalist Elias Castillo, who has written against the missions, quotes the letter from Serra's collected writings.

Missionaries and soldiers used other punishments too. People were locked in the stocks, a wooden frame that holds the feet or hands, sometimes for days. People were hobbled, which means their legs were tied so they could only shuffle, and they were made to keep working. Others were beaten with a cudgel, a heavy club, or jailed and put to hard labor. For plotting, murder or armed rebellion, some people were executed. Soldiers raped Native women at missions including Soledad, San Luis Obispo, San Diego and San Gabriel.

From 1769 to 1834, the missionaries recorded 85,840 baptisms of Native people at the 21 missions. In the same years they recorded 59,538 Native deaths. The death records for two missions are missing, so the real number of deaths is higher. These counts come from the Early California Population Project at the Huntington Library, and they run past 1800. Other record collections cover different years and people, so their totals differ: about 101,000 baptisms and 71,000 deaths in one, and 95,493 baptisms and 56,776 deaths in another. The 2023 report for the state of California names the causes of death. They include forced moves, hard labor, the banning of Native ways of life, poor living conditions, violence, and new diseases brought in from outside.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Kumeyaay attack on Mission San Diego, 1775" -->
On the night of November 4 to 5, 1775, Kumeyaay fighters burned Mission San Diego. They came from fifteen of the twenty-five villages nearest the mission. Spanish soldiers estimated that 600 to 1,000 people took part.

The attackers left no account of their own. The Spanish records list what the Kumeyaay had been complaining about. From 1769 to the end of 1774 the friars had baptized only 100 people, and then they pushed much harder. On October 3, 1775, the friars baptized sixty people in a single day. Spanish soldiers had raped Kumeyaay women, and at least one of those women was also murdered. Father Luis Jayme, a friar at the mission, had reported the rapes. Spanish cattle had destroyed village land. Writers at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian list the Spanish destruction of Native food sources and the Spanish taking of Native land among the causes. Disease was also spreading.

The attackers burned the mission and took goods from the church. They killed three people: Father Jayme, and a carpenter and a blacksmith from Mexico. They shot Jayme with an arrow and then beat him to death.

After the attack, Spanish soldiers took violent revenge. They went through the villages in force and singled out the people they believed had led it. The records give no number of the people they punished or killed, and they do not describe what was done to them. The mission was rebuilt.

### Toypurina

> **Who:** A Tongva woman, often called a religious leader, who helped plan an attack on Mission San Gabriel.
> **When and where:** Japchivit and Mission San Gabriel, near what is now Los Angeles, 1785.

Toypurina was a Tongva woman from the village of Japchivit who had never been baptized. She had influence and standing in her community, and writers often call her a shaman, a religious or spiritual leader. In October 1785 she helped plan an attack on Mission San Gabriel. Soldiers at the mission were warned, and they arrested twenty-one of the rebels before the attack began.

The plan started with Nicolás José, a baptized Tongva man from the village of Sibapet. He had lost a son and two wives. He asked Toypurina to call together the leaders of the other villages. Temejasaquichí, the leader of the village of Juvit, carried her message into the mission. People from the mission and from up to eight Tongva villages joined the plan.

When they were questioned, Nicolás José and Toypurina gave their reasons. Nicolás José said that the ban on dancing kept him from holding the ceremonies of mourning for the dead. Toypurina said she was angry with the Spanish settlers. She was also angry with the Native people who had moved to the mission, which stood on her land.

All of the leaders were held in jail for two and a half years. Then Temejasaquichí and Alijivit, the leader of the village of Jajamovit, were freed with a warning. Spanish officials exiled Nicolás José and Toypurina, sending them away from their homes. The records of the plot do not give the year of her birth or tell the rest of her life.
