## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 29 of 37 · Religion · 10 eras · about 200 minutes**

**Freedom of worship in the United States grew one fight at a time, and most of those fights were started by people who were being punished for their faith.**

A statue of a Pueblo man named Po'pay stands in the US Capitol in Washington. New Mexico gave it to the National Statuary Hall Collection in 2005. The sculptor, Cliff Fragua of Jemez Pueblo, carved scars on its back. In 1675 Spanish officials had Po'pay whipped in public for taking part in Pueblo ceremonies. Five years later he led the rising that drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years.

This chapter follows faith in America from the sun posts at Cahokia to the shootings of worshippers at prayer in this century. First, watch who was punished for the way they worshipped, and who did the punishing: Quakers hanged in Boston, Catholic churches burned in Philadelphia, Native dancers whose food was cut off, Black churches bombed. Then watch the people who pushed back, from Baptists who organized against the church tax to families who took their cases to the Supreme Court. Revivals, meetings meant to stir people to a stronger faith, run through the chapter too. By the early 1800s the Presbyterians, Baptists and Methodists who backed them were the largest Protestant churches in the country.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why did colonists who crossed the ocean to worship freely punish other people for their faith?
- How did Americans stop paying taxes to support churches?
- How long did US officials treat Native ceremonies as crimes?

**Five minutes?** Read the Mary Dyer story in The 1600s, "How Virginia ended its church tax" in 1750 to 1800, and "Hiram Price's rules against ceremonies, 1883" in 1850 to 1900.
**Can skip:** "Three more colleges with church backing", "Laws against teaching evolution, 1921 to 1929", "Worship on a screen", "Teaching religion at home". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 5 minutes**

Hundreds of Native nations lived on this land before 1500, and each one had its own religious life. So there were hundreds of Native religions, and many are still practiced today. The nations shared no holy book and no single founder. Each held its ceremonies in a set place, at a set season, with its own community.

No written account of these religions survives from before the 1500s, because the written record of this land begins with the Europeans who came then. What is known comes from two places. One is what people built and buried. The other is the traditions that the nations kept and still keep.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the ground can show, and what it cannot" -->
Archaeologists, scientists who dig up and study old buildings, graves and objects, can measure which way a doorway faces. They can check whether the sun rises in line with it on a certain day. A wall lined up with the midwinter sunrise shows that the builders lined it up on purpose. It does not show what the builders believed.

The nations themselves hold spoken histories that go back before 1500, and the people of each nation decide how much of that to make public. Written descriptions of Native religions begin after 1500. Europeans wrote them from the outside, and most of those writers were hostile to the religions they described. Many were missionaries, people sent to turn others to their own religion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chaco Canyon" -->
Chaco Canyon, in what is now northwestern New Mexico, holds large stone buildings of many stories. People there began building them in the middle and late 800s, and today they are called great houses. Three of the first were Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Peñasco Blanco. Building went on in the canyon for more than 300 years.

The builders lined many great houses up with the sun, the moon, and north, south, east and west. They also built roads out from the canyon. The roads joined it to more than 150 other great houses across the region.

Pueblo people are the descendants of the people of Chaco. They say Chaco was a gathering place where many peoples and clans, groups of families who count themselves as related, came together to share their ceremonies and knowledge. Many Native people of the Southwest also say that Chaco is an important stop on their clans' sacred migration paths. Those are the routes their peoples traveled as they moved from one home to another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia and its circle of posts" -->
Cahokia stood in what is now Illinois, a few miles east of the Mississippi River and of present-day St. Louis, Missouri. People lived there mainly between about 800 and 1400. It was the largest town in North America north of Mexico before Europeans came. National Park Service figures put the number of people living in Cahokia itself at 10,000 to 20,000.

The people of Cahokia built about 120 mounds of earth. Some held public buildings on top, and others held graves. The biggest is now called Monks Mound, and no earth structure that Native people built north of Mexico is larger. It went up in fourteen stages. It rises in four steps, called terraces, to about 100 feet.

**Woodhenge.** Near Monks Mound stood a circle of upright wooden posts, called Woodhenge today. Posts in the circle marked where the sun came up on the longest day of the year and on the shortest. Others marked the two equinoxes, the days in spring and fall when day and night are the same length. The post for the equinox sunrise lines up with the front of Monks Mound.

Bill Iseminger, an archaeologist at the Cahokia site, gives these figures for the circle. The people rebuilt it about five times. It measured from about 240 to 480 feet across. The first circle had 24 posts, and each new circle had twelve more than the one before.

**Not known:** what the people said or believed as they watched the sun rise over the posts. No record of it survives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The earthworks of Ohio" -->
People in the woodlands of eastern North America built long walls of earth between about the years 1 and 400. They laid the walls out in geometric shapes and built mounds of many shapes beside them. Many of these earthworks are in Ohio. In 2023 they were added to UNESCO's list of World Heritage Sites as the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks, ceremonial meaning built for religious gatherings and ceremonies. UNESCO is the part of the United Nations that keeps that list of important places.

Objects found at the earthworks came from far away. There is mica, a shiny rock that splits into thin sheets. There are shark's teeth, sea shells, copper, and obsidian, a black glass made by volcanoes. The builders had contacts with peoples from the Atlantic coast to the Rocky Mountains, and these things reached Ohio through them.

**Whose name?** Archaeologists call the builders the Hopewell culture, but that is not the name of any Native nation. Mordecai C. Hopewell owned a farm near Chillicothe, Ohio, where some of the mounds stood. Warren K. Moorehead dug into twenty-eight mounds on that farm, and the objects he took out went on show at the world's fair in Chicago in 1893. After that, archaeologists used the farmer's name for the builders. The National Park Service's account of the earthworks states that different Native groups built them.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 8 minutes**

Spanish priests, with Spanish soldiers at their side, set out in the 1500s to turn the peoples of this land into Catholics, Christians who follow the pope in Rome. The priests belonged to orders, groups of Catholic men who promised to live by one set of rules. The Jesuits were one order. The Franciscans were another, and their priests and brothers are called friars.

Franciscan friars built missions, churches they ran inside Native towns, among the Timucua and Guale peoples of what is now Florida and Georgia. From 1598 they built missions among the Pueblo nations of what is now New Mexico as well. In each mission town the friars baptized the people, washing them with water in the Christian ceremony that makes a person a member of the church. They also taught them Catholic beliefs.

**Missions and government.** Spanish officials used the missions to bring the Native towns under the Spanish government. The officials demanded that the people promise loyalty to that government, and they ordered each mission town to send men to work for the Spanish. Nearly all of the written record of the missions in this century comes from the missionaries themselves, so it records their view.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The missions in Florida and Georgia" -->
Luis Cáncer de Barbastro, a Dominican friar from Spain, argued that friars should bring Native people to Christianity without soldiers. He had come to the Caribbean in 1518. King Charles V of Spain gave him a written order to try it in Florida, and he sailed from Havana, Cuba, in early June 1549. On June 26 he went ashore near Tampa Bay to greet a group of Tocobaga people, and they killed him. The Tocobaga left no account of why.

The Jesuits tried missionary work in Florida from 1568 to 1571 and left without success. Franciscan friars came next. After a new group of them arrived from Spain in 1587, they began building missions up the coast into what is now Georgia.

San Pedro de Mocama, on the coast of what is now Georgia, was the first successful mission there. By the end of 1595 the friars had missions in at least five main towns of the Guale people, who spoke a Muskogee language.

A mission town with a friar living in it was called a *doctrina*. There the friar baptized people and led Mass, the main Catholic church service. Friars also taught the catechism, a set of questions and answers about Christian beliefs that people learn by heart. The friar Francisco Pareja had a catechism in Spanish and in the Timucua language printed in Mexico City in 1612.

**Workers for St. Augustine.** Spanish officials used the mission towns to get workers as well. Under a system called the *repartimiento*, each mission town had to send a set number of unmarried men to St. Augustine every year to work for the Spanish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Killed for refusing to change faith, 1565" -->
Spanish soldiers killed French Protestants, Christians whose churches had broken away from the pope, in Florida in 1565 because of their religion. It was the first killing over religion between Europeans in what became the United States.

**The first group.** A hurricane wrecked the French ships that year, and about 127 French Protestants came ashore with no food and no weapons. They surrendered to Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, the Spanish commander. Menéndez told them to give up their Protestant faith, and they refused. His soldiers killed 111 of them. Menéndez spared 16 men: a few who said they were Catholic, some sailors forced onto the ships, and four craftsmen St. Augustine needed.

Of the others, Menéndez wrote to the king of Spain on October 15, 1565: "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife." That meant his men killed them with knives.

**The second group.** About two weeks after the first killing, a second group of Frenchmen under Jean Ribault surrendered. They refused the same demand, and Menéndez's soldiers killed 134 of them. The two killings are dated September 29 and October 11 or 12, 1565.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Guale, 1597" -->
Guale people killed five Franciscan friars and destroyed their missions in the autumn of 1597. One of the five was Blas Rodríguez, the friar at the town of Tupiqui. A sixth friar was held captive and freed in 1598.

**Not known:** which Guale people killed the friars, and why.

The historians J. Michael Francis and Kathleen Kole translated the earliest Spanish records of the uprising and published them in 2011. They wrote that those two questions cannot "be answered with any certainty." Older retellings name a young leader and give a reason. In the earliest records, only two of the seven witnesses named that young man at all, and neither said he alone ordered the killings.

**The governor's revenge.** Gonzalo Méndez de Canzo, the Spanish governor of Florida, led soldiers into Guale country himself. On October 27 people at the town of Ospo shot arrows at his men. Over the next ten days the governor and his officer Alonso Díaz burned Ospo and four more Guale towns, with their houses and storehouses, some of them full of corn. The governor could not capture anyone, so he ordered his men to destroy the corn fields and other crops as well. The records describe the burning and give no count of Guale people killed.

**Lucas.** The governor took seven young Guale captives in July 1598. Francis and Kole describe them as boys. One was Lucas, the son of don Felipe, the chief of Tupiqui. Spanish officials questioned Lucas a second time on July 27, now under the threat of torture, and he confessed that he had been there when Blas Rodríguez was killed. They sentenced him to death on July 28. He was hanged at St. Augustine on July 29, 1598, and the records do not name the man who hanged him.

Lucas was the only Guale person the Spanish officially charged and punished for the uprising. Spanish officials divided the other six boys among themselves as personal servants. The boys were captives and had to do forced labor for the men who held them. The Spanish left the northern missions empty until 1604.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate claimed for Spain the land the Spanish called New Mexico in early May 1598, at a crossing of the Rio Grande near what is now El Paso. It was the homeland of the Pueblo nations, and Franciscan friars traveled with him.

That autumn Oñate went to every pueblo, as the Spanish called each Pueblo town. Through translators he demanded that the people of each town promise loyalty to the Spanish government. At those meetings he gave each town a missionary, and the Franciscan missions in New Mexico began that way.

**Acoma.** The friars also gave their approval for a war on Acoma Pueblo. In December 1598 Oñate's nephew, Juan de Zaldívar, went up to Acoma Pueblo with soldiers to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, the soldiers raped an Acoma woman, forcing her into sex. The Acoma killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men.

**What the friars said.** Oñate then asked the Franciscan friars whether a war against Acoma would be just. The friars answered that he "possessed both the authority and sufficient cause." That meant the friars said he had the right to make war and a good enough reason.

**The killing.** Oñate's soldiers attacked in January 1599. They killed Acoma men, women and children and burned much of the town. No one counted the dead, and estimates run from about 300 to about 1,500.

**The sentences.** Oñate sentenced the men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off. He sentenced them and every other captive over twelve to twenty years of forced labor, and he handed the old people to the Plains Apache as slaves. Whether the feet were really cut off is disputed.

**The children.** On Oñate's orders, Acoma children under twelve were taken from their parents and given to the friars. About seventy of the girls were sent south to convents in Mexico, houses of nuns, women who have promised their lives to the Catholic church. The records do not name the men who took them. Almost certainly none of the girls ever came home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dona-maria-melendez" name="Doña María Meléndez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Doña María Meléndez

> **Who:** The Timucua chief of Nombre de Dios, the town beside St. Augustine, and a Catholic.
> **When and where:** Spanish Florida, the late 1500s and early 1600s.

**The friars' success in her town was "largely owing to" her.**

Doña María Meléndez ruled Nombre de Dios, a Timucua town just outside St. Augustine. The Spanish called her the *cacica*, their word for a woman chief. Her mother ruled the town before her and was one of the first Timucua people to become Christian. Doña María was a Christian too. She married a Spanish soldier named Clemente de Vernal, who lived with her and their children at Nombre de Dios.

The Franciscans set up their first *doctrina* at Nombre de Dios after 1587. Staff at the Florida Museum of Natural History write that the Franciscans' success there "was largely owing to" her. By 1606 she ruled the Timucua towns along the coast from St. Augustine north to about Cumberland Island, in what is now Georgia.

**Not known:** how she gained that wider rule. The National Park Service's account says she may have had Spanish help, and the records do not settle it. Spaniards wrote down everything known about her, nothing survives in her own words, and no record says why she became a Christian.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 25 minutes**

English Protestants founded several colonies in the 1600s so that they could worship in their own way, and in some of those colonies the leaders punished people who worshipped differently. The Puritans ran Massachusetts. They were English Protestants who wanted to clear the Church of England of what they saw as Catholic leftovers. Among the people they punished were Quakers, members of a Protestant group begun in England in 1652. Puritan leaders had the ears of some Quakers cut off, and they hanged four Quakers in Boston.

Other colonies set other rules. Maryland's lawmakers protected worship for Christians in a law of 1649, and the same law ordered death for anyone who denied the Trinity, the Christian belief that God is three persons in one. Roger Williams in Rhode Island let people of every faith live as neighbors with equal rights. William Penn wrote freedom of worship into Pennsylvania's Frame of Government, his plan for running the colony.

**New Mexico.** Spanish friars and governors punished Pueblo people for holding their own ceremonies. They whipped religious leaders and hanged some of them. In 1680 the Pueblo nations rose up and drove the Spanish out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the Puritans left England" -->
In England in the 1620s, the men who ran the church and the government ordered the Puritans to follow church practices they hated. Those leaders put Puritan ministers out of their jobs and threatened them with "extirpation from the earth," which means being wiped out.

Bishops, the high officials who ran the Church of England, had their critics punished hard. English judges had Protestants who wrote against the bishops whipped, cut and branded.

**Alexander Leighton.** The judges of a London court called the Star Chamber sentenced a Scottish minister named Alexander Leighton in June 1630, for writing a book against the bishops. They fined him 10,000 pounds and ordered him whipped. He was to stand in the pillory, a wooden frame that locked a person's head and hands in a public place. The judges ordered one of his ears cut off and one side of his nose slit open.

They also ordered his face branded with the letters S.S. Branding means pressing red-hot metal onto the skin to burn in a mark that never goes away. The letters stood for "sower of sedition," a person who stirs people up against the government.

Leighton escaped the night before the punishment, but he was caught again in Bedfordshire. The first part of the sentence was carried out on him on November 26, 1630.

**Not known:** which punishments that first part included, and who carried them out. The account of his life in the Dictionary of National Biography, a British book of life stories printed between 1885 and 1900, does not say.

He was held in the Fleet, a London prison, until 1640. That year the members of Parliament, England's lawmakers, set him free, canceled his fine and voted to pay him 6,000 pounds.

**Crossing the ocean.** As many as 20,000 Puritans left England for America from 1630 on, looking for the freedom to worship as they believed they should. John Winthrop's company of about 1,000 arrived in 1630 and settled on the Shawmut Peninsula, where Boston stands now.

It was the land of the Massachusett people, who called it Mushauwomuk, "the boat landing place." Between 1616 and 1618 an epidemic, a disease that spread fast from person to person, had killed all but about 25,000 of the roughly 100,000 people of that region. That is three people in every four. In 1633 the Massachusett leader Chickataubut signed a treaty with Winthrop that gave up his rights to the peninsula.

The Pilgrims who landed at Plymouth in 1620 were a different group. They had left the Church of England completely and formed churches of their own. The Puritans believed the Church of England was a true church that needed to be fixed.

**Joining a church.** In Massachusetts a person could not simply join a church. At first, only men and women who had gone through a conversion experience, a moment when a person feels that God has changed him or her, could become members. Each new member also had to convince the other members that it was real.

**The Bay Psalm Book.** Richard Mather and two other ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into verses that church members could sing. Stephen Daye printed their book at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640, and it was the first book printed in the English colonies of North America. Eleven copies still exist.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading for God" -->
New England children were taught to read for a religious reason. In 1642 the men of the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmakers, ordered parents and masters to teach their children and apprentices, young people learning a trade from a master, to read. The law required them to learn enough "to read the English tongue" and to know the colony's "Capital Lawes," the laws that set death as the punishment for a crime. The colonists pointed to the Bible as the authority for many of their criminal laws.

**The Old Deluder Satan Act.** A second law, in 1647, ordered towns of 50 households to hire someone to teach reading and writing. Towns of 100 families had to set up a grammar school, a school that prepared young people for "the university," which meant Harvard College. The law's opening words state that it is "one of the chief projects of that old deluder Satan to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures."

A deluder is someone who tricks people, and the law has been called the Old Deluder Satan Act ever since. The colonists wanted every child to be able to read the Bible without a minister in between.

Harvard College was founded in 1636 to train ministers. A pamphlet by its founders, *New Englands First Fruits*, printed in England in 1643, states the college's purpose. The founders were "dreading to leave an illiterate Ministery to the Churches, when our present Ministers shall lie in the Dust." They feared that when their ministers died, no trained ministers would be left to replace them.

New England children learned to read from *The New England Primer*, made by the printer Benjamin Harris. Staff at the American Antiquarian Society, a research library, date its first printing between 1687 and 1690, because the only evidence left is booksellers' advertisements. Harris placed his first advertisement for it in 1690. No copy printed before 1727 survives.

**The picture of the fire.** One page of the Primer showed John Rogers being burned alive at Smithfield, in England, on February 4, 1555. He was the first Protestant put to death under Mary, the Catholic queen of England. The picture showed his wife and ten children beside the fire. A long poem next to it, written as his last advice to his children, told them to "Abhor the arrant Whore of Rome," which meant to hate the Catholic Church.

Library of Congress staff write that generations of New England children read these pages. In their words, the pages "doubtless helped to fuel" New Englanders' prejudice against Catholics, a dislike of a whole group of people without knowing them. That prejudice stayed strong there well into the 1800s.

**By 1650:** Puritan leaders ran Massachusetts by their reading of the Bible, only people who had felt God change them could join its churches, and every town of 50 households had to hire someone to teach reading.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Praying towns and Deer Island" -->
New England ministers also tried to bring the Native nations around them to Christianity. John Eliot, the minister at Roxbury, translated the whole Bible into the Algonquian language of the Massachusett people. It was printed at Cambridge in 1663, the first Bible printed in any language in North America. At one time Eliot ministered to about 1,100 Christian Native people, whom the English called Praying Indians. They lived in fourteen towns laid out in the English style.

**Deer Island.** The English were fighting what they called King Philip's War in 1675. That year the members of the Massachusetts General Court ordered the Christian Native people of Natick taken from their homes and put on Deer Island in Boston Harbor. Daniel Gookin, the Massachusetts official in charge of dealings with the Praying Indians, wrote that the order came from "false informations" given to the court to stir its members up against the people of Natick.

Samuel Shrimpton of Boston held the island, and he agreed to the plan as long as they did not cut down any trees. Captain Thomas Prentiss and a party of horsemen brought the Natick people to the Charles River.

About 200 people from Natick were put on three boats about midnight on October 30, 1675, under the court's order, and carried to Deer Island. Gookin does not name the men who took them. John Eliot met them at the landing and prayed with them. They were afraid they would never come home and would be shipped out of the country.

**The winter.** Massachusetts officials then sent the Punkapog people to the island too, "upon as little cause as the Naticks were," Gookin wrote. By late December about 500 people were held there. Gookin wrote that they lived mostly on clams and shellfish dug out of the sand at low tide. The island was cold, their wigwams, or houses, were poor, and they had few clothes, and thin ones.

Gookin wrote that the island kept them safe "from the fury of the people." His account of that winter gives no count of the dead. In 2025 the Episcopal bishops of Massachusetts and Western Massachusetts wrote that hundreds of Native people from the praying towns died on Deer Island from the cold and from starvation in the winter of 1675 to 1676.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quakers punished in Massachusetts" -->
The Quakers formed around a preacher named George Fox in England in 1652, and they call themselves the Religious Society of Friends. They taught that the Spirit, or "Light of Christ," lives inside every person. Quaker women spoke and preached at Quaker meetings for worship, and other colonists found this offensive. Many people at the time called Quaker teaching heresy, a belief that goes against the teaching of the church.

English officials had put 10,000 Quakers in prison by 1680. According to the Library of Congress, 243 of them died in the king's jails "of torture and mistreatment."

The Puritan lawmakers of Massachusetts passed law after law against Quakers, starting on October 14, 1656. They called Quakers "the cursed sect of heretics." They fined ship captains who brought Quakers into the colony and people who owned Quaker books.

**Ears and tongues.** A law of 1657 set the punishment for a Quaker who came back after being thrown out of the colony. A man would have one ear cut off the first time, and the other ear the second time. A woman would be whipped. A third return was to be punished by boring a hole through the person's tongue with a hot iron.

Christopher Holder, John Copeland and John Rous were Quakers who came back. The judges sentenced each of them to have his right ear cut off by the hangman. On September 16, 1658, the deputy of the colony's marshal, a law officer, came to the prison with a group of men and shut the door. Then he had the right ear of each of the three men cut off. Afterward the three men said: "They that do it Ignorantly, We desire the Lord from our hearts to forgive them."

**Two children for sale.** Massachusetts officials also punished the children of Quakers. Daniel and Provided Southwick were the son and daughter of Lawrence and Cassandra Southwick, Quakers of Salem. Judges at Salem and Ipswich fined them ten pounds for not going to the town's church meetings, and they had no property to pay it with. The members of the General Court then gave the county treasurers the power to sell the two children to any English buyer, in Virginia or Barbados, to pay the fine.

The county treasurer, Edmond Batter, looked for a ship's captain to carry them to Barbados. The captain he found refused. He asked, "will ye offer to make Slaves of so harmless Creatures?" Batter sent the children home, and the order to sell them was never carried out.

**Death for coming back.** The lawmakers set the punishment of death on October 19, 1658, for any Quaker who came back after being banished, which means ordered out of a place and forbidden to return. Between 1659 and 1661 the colony's officials hanged four Quakers in Boston under that law: William Robinson and Marmaduke Stephenson in 1659, Mary Dyer in 1660 and William Leddra in 1661. The governor, John Endecott, sentenced three of them. The story of Mary Dyer, after this section, describes the first three hangings.

King Charles II ordered the Massachusetts lawmakers to stop punishing Quakers on September 9, 1661, after Quakers in England pressed him. By 1677 Quakers could hold their meetings openly in Massachusetts.

Virginia's lawmakers passed their own laws against Quakers from 1659, including death for Quakers who would not obey them. No one was put to death under them. Thomas Jefferson later wrote that the reason was not that the church held back or that the lawmakers went easy. In his words, it "was not owing to the moderation of the church, or the spirit of the legislature."

**By 1677:** Quakers met openly in Massachusetts, and no Quaker had been hanged there since 1661.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-dyer" name="Mary Dyer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mary Dyer

> **Who:** A Quaker woman hanged on Boston Common for returning to Massachusetts after she had been banished.
> **When and where:** Massachusetts and Rhode Island. Hanged in Boston on June 1, 1660.

**She stood on the ladder with the rope around her neck.**

Her first trouble with the Massachusetts leaders came when she supported Anne Hutchinson, a Boston woman who was banished for her religious teaching. In 1638 the Massachusetts leaders forced Dyer and her family out, and they moved to Rhode Island. In the 1650s, in England, she became a Quaker.

She came back to New England to spread Quaker beliefs. The Massachusetts officials arrested her and banished her three times. In the spring of 1657 her husband, William Dyer, got her out of the Boston jail by confronting Governor John Endecott. Endecott let her go on the condition that she never return.

**The first sentence.** She returned anyway, and in October 1659 Endecott sentenced her to death along with two Quaker men, William Robinson, a merchant from London, and Marmaduke Stephenson, a farm worker from Yorkshire. He told them they would "be hanged until you are dead." On October 27, 1659, the officials led all three to the gallows and made Dyer watch the two men hanged. She stood on the ladder with the rope around her neck.

Then the officials announced a reprieve, which called off her hanging. Endecott had agreed to it the day before. The officials sent her out of the colony.

She came back once more in May 1660, and Endecott sentenced her to death a second time. Massachusetts officials hanged her on Boston Common on June 1, 1660.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-dyer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maryland, 1649: freedom for Christians, death for others" -->
Most people in England hated the Catholic Church all through the 1600s, and they made life hard for Catholics and punished them. In 1632 George Calvert got a charter, a written grant of land and of the right to govern it, from King Charles I for the land that became Maryland. According to the Library of Congress, Calvert wanted a safe place for his fellow Catholics.

Two ships, the *Ark* and the *Dove*, carried about 200 settlers there. Two Catholic priests had to sneak on board to get away from England's laws against Catholics.

Maryland's colonists first landed on March 25, 1634. They later bought land from the Yaocomaco people and built St. Mary's City there. The records do not say what the Yaocomaco were paid or how they understood the sale. By 1649 the colony belonged to Cecil Calvert, the second Lord Baltimore.

**The Toleration Act.** Cecil Calvert sent the Maryland assembly, the colony's lawmakers, a set of proposals about religion in 1649. The members met at St. Mary's City from April 2. They rewrote part of what he sent, and on April 21 they voted it into law as An Act Concerning Religion. It has been called the Toleration Act ever since, toleration meaning letting people hold beliefs that others do not share.

The act protected only Christians. It stated that no person "professing to believe in Jesus Christ" should be "in any ways troubled, molested, or discountenanced" for his or her religion. Molested here meant bothered or attacked, and discountenanced meant treated with disapproval.

**Death for other beliefs.** The same act ordered death for anyone who cursed God, or denied that Jesus was the Son of God, or denied the Trinity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost. Such a person "shall be punished with death," and the person's land and goods would be taken and given to Lord Baltimore. Under this law a Jew, or a Christian who did not believe in the Trinity, could be put to death.

The act also punished insults about religion. Calling someone a heretic, a Puritan, a Presbyterian, a "Popish Priest" or a similar name as an insult cost a fine of ten shillings (a shilling was an English coin). Half of the fine went to the person insulted. A person who could not pay was "publickly whipt" and kept in jail until he or she asked forgiveness in public.

Puritans took control of Maryland in 1654 and threw out the act. They took legal rights away from Catholics, Jews, Quakers and others. Maryland's colonists canceled the Puritan laws in 1661, and the act was in force again. After 1689 the Church of England became Maryland's official church by law, and officials there enforced English laws that kept Catholics from voting, holding office or worshipping in public.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two colonies that left worship alone" -->
Roger Williams founded Providence in 1636, on land of the Narragansett people. He wanted it to be "a shelter for persons distressed for conscience." In Rhode Island, Anglicans (members of the Church of England), Puritans, Quakers, Catholics, Jews and people with no religion lived as neighbors with equal rights.

William Penn was a Quaker. In 1681 King Charles II owed a debt to Penn's father, and Penn turned it into a charter for Pennsylvania. The Lenape people, also called the Delaware, lived on both sides of the Delaware River. Beginning in 1682, the Lenape gave up land on the west bank of the river to Penn in exchange for cloth, guns, gunpowder, alcohol and other goods. By 1685 as many as 8,000 Quakers had moved to Pennsylvania.

**Penn's plan.** In 1682 Penn wrote a Frame of Government for the colony. It stated that everyone who believed in "One Almighty and eternal God" would "in no wayes be molested," meaning bothered, for their religion. It also stated that no one would "be compelled at any time to frequent or maintain any Religious Worship, Place or Ministry whatever." That meant no one in Pennsylvania had to attend a church, and no one had to pay for one.

The first group of Germans reached Philadelphia in 1683, from the town of Krefeld. They were Mennonites and possibly some Dutch Quakers. Mennonites, Dunkers, Schwenkfelders, Moravians and German Baptists followed. These were small Protestant groups from Germany and nearby lands, and their members had been punished for their religion in Europe. People described Pennsylvania as "an asylum for banished sects," a safe place for religious groups that had been driven out.

Twenty-three Jewish refugees from Dutch Brazil arrived in New Amsterdam, the Dutch town that became New York, in 1654. Within a year they were holding religious services there. By 1658 Jews had also settled at Newport, Rhode Island, where they could worship freely.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="roger-williams" name="Roger Williams" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Roger Williams

> **Who:** A minister thrown out of Massachusetts who founded Rhode Island, where the government stayed out of religion.
> **When and where:** Born in London about 1603. Massachusetts from 1631. Founded Providence in 1636. Died in Providence in 1683.

**"Forced worship stinks in God's nostrils," he wrote in 1644.**

Roger Williams taught that a government has no right to force religion on anyone. He called the freedom to follow one's own conscience "soul liberty," and he built a colony on that idea.

He had studied at Cambridge University and become a minister in the Church of England, and he disliked the church's ranks of officials, its wealth, its power in government and its decorations. He reached Massachusetts Bay in 1631, one year after the Puritans settled it. There, Puritan religious rules covered every part of daily life, and colony officials enforced them. Williams preached that the government should not enforce anyone's religion, and that a person's spiritual life should be kept separate from public life.

**Thrown out.** The judges of the colony's General Court ordered him out of Massachusetts for preaching these ideas. He fled in the winter, because he might have been put to death if he had been sent back to England. Wampanoag people took him in and kept him alive through that winter. He had traded with them and learned their language.

**Providence.** With help from Wampanoag and Narragansett people, he traveled by canoe to a freshwater spring at a place called Moshassuck. He settled there in 1636 with the permission of the Narragansett and named the place Providence. In 1638 the Narragansett leaders Canonicus and Miantonomi signed a deed with Williams for the settlement, a deed being a paper that hands over land. Under it, the Narragansett kept the right to trade inside the settlement.

In Rhode Island, people of every faith lived as neighbors with the same rights. Williams thought many of them were wrong about religion. He believed that correcting them was not the government's job.

**His books.** In 1643 he published *A Key into the Language of America*, a book about the Native languages of New England, mostly Narragansett, and about Narragansett daily life. In 1644 he published *The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution*, an argument against forced worship. In it he wrote: "God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any civill state." He meant that God does not require everyone to be made to follow the same religion.

Members of England's Parliament granted Rhode Island a patent, a paper that recognized the colony, in 1644. King Charles II granted it a new charter in 1663, at the request of John Clarke, a Rhode Island colonist who had stayed on in England to act for the colony. The charter stated that no one in the colony would be punished "for any differences in opinion in matters of religion."

Williams died in Providence in 1683. In 1965 members of Congress voted to set up the Roger Williams National Memorial in Providence.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roger-williams" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-religion" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** A Boston woman who held religious meetings in her home, taught her own reading of the Bible, and was banished and put out of her church for it.
> **When and where:** Born in Alford, England, about 1591. Boston from 1634. Banished in November 1637. Killed in 1643.

**Her church said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife."**

The leaders of Massachusetts put Anne Hutchinson out of the colony for what she taught about God, and then the members of her church put her out of the church. She had sailed to Boston in the autumn of 1634 with her husband, William, and their children, following their minister, John Cotton.

**The meetings.** From 1636 she held a meeting in her home once a week to talk over the Sunday sermon. At first only women came. Then men came too, including important men of the colony. In Massachusetts the minister's sermon in the meeting house was the official teaching, and a second meeting in a private house, led by a woman, was a rival to it.

**What she taught.** She taught that God's grace, God's free gift of love and forgiveness, alone decides who is saved, meaning who goes to heaven. She said good behavior played no part.

She also said she could tell which ministers and which neighbors had received God's grace and which had not. That second claim put her in judgment over the colony's ministers. Her opponents called her teaching antinomian, a word that means "against the law." They meant that her teaching let a saved person ignore the rules.

At her trial she said that God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation," and that God would curse the Puritans if they harmed her. The judges called that blasphemy, speaking against God.

**Two trials.** She faced two trials, five months apart. In November 1637 the members of the General Court tried her at Newtown, now Cambridge, and banished her. In March 1638 the members of the church at Boston tried her separately and excommunicated her, which means they put her out of the church.

The church's charges said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer." The same charge went on: "and a Magistrate than a Subject," an official who governs or judges rather than a person who is governed.

**Her death.** In the spring of 1638 she moved to Aquidneck Island in what became Rhode Island and founded the town of Portsmouth. After her husband died, she moved in 1642 to land on Long Island Sound that the Dutch claimed for their colony.

A war broke out there the next year. In February 1643 soldiers of the Dutch governor, Willem Kieft, killed at least eighty, and perhaps as many as 120, Munsee people in one night, and that killing began the war. In August or September 1643, during that war, Siwanoy people attacked her home. They killed her and several of her children. One daughter survived.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-religion" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Baptism and slavery in Virginia, 1667" -->
Some colonists argued that a Christian could not be held as a slave. In September 1667 the members of Virginia's lawmaking assembly settled the question in favor of slavery. Their law said that being baptized "doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome," bondage meaning slavery. The law's own words state the lawmakers' reason. Once enslavers were sure that baptism would not free the people they held, they would be more willing to let them be baptized.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico: punished for their ceremonies" -->
The Pueblo nations of New Mexico hold their religious ceremonies in kivas, rooms set aside for worship. Some of their ceremonies are masked dances. For about eighty years the Franciscan friars demanded that the kivas be destroyed and the masked dances ended. The friars called the dances idolatry, which means worshipping false gods.

Alonso de Posada, the head of the Franciscan missionaries from 1656 to 1665, forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered the missionaries to go into the kivas, burn the masks, and take or destroy the sacred objects. In 1661 Spanish soldiers as well as friars raided kivas and burned sacred masks.

**Whippings and hangings.** Will Hansen and Analú María López, who work at the Newberry Library, a research library in Chicago, write that missionaries almost certainly whipped Native people who did not take part in Christian services. Spanish investigations in New Mexico between 1620 and 1680 recorded many cases of missionaries punishing Native people severely. According to the Architect of the Capitol's account of Po'pay, Pueblo people "found practicing their religion were tortured and sometimes executed."

Juan Francisco Treviño, the Spanish governor, ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo medicine men in 1675. The charge was sorcery, using magic to cause harm, which is what Spanish officials called the medicine men's ceremonies. Spanish officials sentenced four of them to be hanged. Three were hanged, and the fourth killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the others in public and put them in prison. The records do not name the men who did the hanging and the whipping.

**Po'pay.** One of the men whipped was Po'pay, of Ohkay Owingeh, also called San Juan Pueblo. He was born about 1630, and his name means "ripe squash" in the Tewa language. He was a farmer and a member of a medicine society, and his work was healing and the spiritual life of his people. Spanish officials had him flogged in public. Flogging is beating a person on the back with a whip. The whip leaves wounds that heal into scars.

When Pueblo leaders heard about the arrests, they sent armed men to Santa Fe, where the prisoners were held. Many Spanish soldiers were away at the time, so Treviño gave in and released the prisoners. Po'pay was one of them.

**The rising.** Po'pay led a rising of the Pueblo nations against the Spanish in 1680, and he planned it from Taos Pueblo. The leaders set it for August 13 and sent runners to the pueblos with knotted cords. Chapter 2, Native Nations, describes the rising in full, including what the knots meant.

On August 9 the Spanish captured two of the runners, Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua of Tesuque Pueblo. Spanish officials tortured them to make them explain the cords, and later hanged them. The records do not name the men who did it. With two of their runners in Spanish hands, the Pueblo leaders began the rising three days early, on August 10, 1680.

The Pueblo fighters killed about 400 people, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in New Mexico, and burned mission churches across the province. The Pueblo nations kept the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years.

The Pueblo nations run their own museum, the Indian Pueblo Cultural Center. Its account of those eighty years of Spanish rule states that "the Pueblo people suffered violence, cruelty, and hunger." It lists "exploitation of labor and resources," and "harassment and persecution for practicing long-held religious traditions that are interwoven in daily life." That means the Spanish forced Pueblo people to work for them, took what they had, and troubled and punished them again and again for keeping their own religion.

**The statue.** In 2005 a statue of Po'pay, given by the state of New Mexico, was placed in the National Statuary Hall Collection in the United States Capitol. Of all the people in that collection who were born on land that later became the United States, Po'pay was born the earliest.

No picture or written description of Po'pay survives, so the face is the sculptor's own work. The sculptor, Cliff Fragua of Jemez Pueblo, wrote: "On his back are the scars that remain from the whipping he received for his participation and faith in the Pueblo ceremonies and religion."

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 14 minutes**

Traveling preachers held revivals, series of meetings meant to stir people to a stronger faith, across the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s. People in many colonies took part in the same religious movement at the same time, and it is called the Great Awakening.

Most colonists already went to church. By one estimate, 75 to 80 percent of colonists attended church between 1700 and 1740, and colonists were building new churches fast. The revival preachers wanted each person to go through the new birth, a conversion that a person could feel happening inside. Church members split into two sides over whether the new birth was real. Critics said many of the conversions were false, and even the work of the devil.

Killings over religion came in these years too. English colonists from Carolina and Creek fighters under James Moore destroyed the Spanish missions among the Apalachee of Florida in 1704. In 1741 New York officials hanged a man they called a Catholic priest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Apalachee missions destroyed, 1704" -->
English colonists from Carolina and their Creek allies destroyed the Spanish missions among the Apalachee people in 1704. The Apalachee lived around what is now Tallahassee, Florida. Many of them were baptized Catholics who lived in fourteen Franciscan mission towns.

The members of the Carolina assembly approved the attack in September 1703 and asked James Moore, a former governor of Carolina, to lead it. Moore promised that the attack would cost the colony nothing, because he would pay for it "by the taking of loot and slaves." That meant stealing goods and capturing people to sell as slaves. He led fifty Englishmen and about one thousand Creek men, also called Muscogee.

**Ayubale.** Moore's men destroyed nearly all of the fourteen mission towns. At the church of Ayubale, on January 25, 1704, a friar named Angel de Miranda and 26 men held off the attackers for about nine hours. They surrendered when they ran out of arrows.

A magazine account based on the work of the historian John Hann describes what the attackers did there. They burned one friar alive and cut off his head, and the account does not give his name. They also tortured two Native people and burned them at the stake.

**Patale.** In a later raid that year, on June 23, 1704, attackers killed the friar Manuel de Mendoza at the Patale mission and burned the church. At Patale they tied prisoners to the Stations of the Cross, a set of markers used in Catholic prayer, and cut them with knives. Then they pushed burning sticks into the wounds and burned the prisoners to death. The records name James Moore as the commander of the January raid. They do not name the men who tortured and burned the prisoners.

**How many were taken.** The counts vary widely. Moore reported that his army killed more than 1,100 men, women and children and captured more than 4,300 people as slaves, most of them women and children. Moore had promised to pay for the raid with captives.

Other counts of the people captured run from hundreds to about 1,400. The historian John Hann counts about 2,000 Apalachee who went into exile after the raids, forced to live far from their homeland, and he writes that an unknown number were enslaved. In 1680 the fourteen mission towns had held about 8,000 people.

Many Apalachee were unhappy with the forced labor the Spanish demanded, which included carrying goods about 100 miles to St. Augustine. The Spanish had also forbidden mission Apalachee to own guns. Many Apalachee left with Moore. In his report, Moore said that most of the people of seven villages chose to join him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Lights and Old Lights" -->
Church members argued over the revival, and many churches divided into two sides. People who supported the revival were called New Lights, and people who opposed it were called Old Lights. Those were the names among the Congregationalists, the members of the churches the Puritans had founded in New England. Among the Presbyterians, another family of Protestant churches, the two sides were called the New Side and the Old Side.

The revival had ended in New England by the middle of the 1740s. Preachers carried it south, where it started revivals that went on into the 1800s. The Presbyterians, Baptists and Methodists had backed the revival, and by the early 1800s they were the largest Protestant churches in the country. According to the Library of Congress, the Anglicans, the Quakers and the Congregationalists "were left behind."

A small number of colonists, among them Thomas Jefferson and John Adams, went a different way. They were deists, who stressed living a good life and rejected the Christian teaching that Jesus was God.

Drawings of New York City from 1690, 1730 and 1771 show the churches multiplying. The 1690 view shows an empty skyline. The 1771 view shows eighteen steeples, the tall towers on top of churches.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A college founded by revival supporters" -->
Only graduates of Harvard or Yale could become Presbyterian ministers in the colonies in these years. Under that rule, the young men taught by William Tennent could not become Presbyterian ministers, so revival supporters set out to found new colleges.

Tennent was a Presbyterian minister, born in Ireland and educated in Edinburgh, Scotland. From 1726 until he died in 1745 he ran a school at Neshaminy, in Bucks County, Pennsylvania. People called it the Log College, because it was a log house about twenty feet long and almost as wide. He taught his students ancient languages and the Bible. Several of them became revival preachers anyway, among them his four sons.

**Princeton.** A group of New Lights planned a college in New Jersey in early 1746 to train young men for the ministry. On October 22, 1746, John Hamilton, the acting governor of New Jersey, granted a charter for the College of New Jersey. It is now Princeton University, and Princeton's own historians call its founding "a direct outgrowth" of the Great Awakening. Six months after the charter, three Log College graduates became trustees, the men in charge of running the college. They were Samuel Blair and two of Tennent's sons, Gilbert Tennent and William Tennent Jr.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Should enslaved people be taught Christianity?" -->
Revival preachers pressed enslavers to let enslaved people be taught Christianity, and enslavers resisted. In 1740 George Whitefield printed an open letter in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* to the enslavers of Maryland, Virginia and the Carolinas. He wrote down their reason for keeping enslaved people from Christianity: that "teaching them Christianity would make them proud, and consequently unwilling to submit to Slavery."

Whitefield called that "a dreadful Reflection" on their own religion. He did not say in the letter that slavery was wrong, and he later held enslaved people himself.

Leah, who was enslaved in the household of the minister Jonathan Edwards, became a full member of the church at Northampton, Massachusetts, during the revival. Kenneth Minkema of the Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale records her membership.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-whitefield" name="George Whitefield" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Whitefield

> **Who:** An English preacher whose tours of the colonies were the high point of the Great Awakening, and who worked to make slavery legal in Georgia.
> **When and where:** Born in Gloucester, England, December 16, 1714. Seven visits to America from 1738. Died in Newburyport, Massachusetts, September 30, 1770.

**People in the crowd threw rotten eggs and pieces of dead cat.**

George Whitefield drew crowds of up to 30,000 people in the Great Awakening, and he also pushed to make slavery legal in Georgia. He was a minister in the Church of England and was often in conflict with its leaders.

**The tour.** His preaching tour of the colonies from 1739 to 1741 was the high point of the Great Awakening in America. A sermon he gave in Boston drew as many as 30,000 people.

Many churches would not let him preach inside, so he preached outdoors from a folding pulpit that he carried with him. The first recorded use of it was at Moorfields, in England, on April 9, 1742, in front of a crowd estimated at twenty or thirty thousand. People in the crowd threw stones, rotten eggs and pieces of dead cat at him. He preached about two thousand sermons from that pulpit, by one estimate.

**Why crowds came.** He preached the new birth. In a printed sermon of 1739 he admitted that "how this glorious Change is wrought in the Soul cannot easily be explained." The Library of Congress lists the reasons crowds came to hear him: a strong voice, a way of speaking like an actor on a stage, the strong feelings he stirred up, simple messages, and clever use of the new ways of advertising.

Someone in London made an engraving, a printed picture, in 1763 that mocked his eye disease by calling him "Dr. Squintum." The picture's maker also accused him of sexual wrongdoing and of getting rich from his preaching.

**Bethesda.** In 1740 he founded an orphanage called Bethesda near Savannah. Slavery was against the law in Georgia at that time. In 1741 he wrote that farming more land than his hired servants and older boys could manage was "impracticable without a few negroes." ("Negroes" was a word of his time for Black people.)

He wrote to a donor in 1747 that friends in South Carolina were giving money toward buying "a plantation and slaves" to support Bethesda. He added: "One negroe has been given me." He wrote that he planned to buy more that week.

**Slavery in Georgia.** From 1747 he pressed the trustees who ran Georgia to lift the ban on slavery. He wrote that "Georgia never can be a flourishing province, unless negroes are employed." He told them that unless the ban ended, he could not "cultivate the plantation in any manner." The trustees gave way.

Slavery became legal in Georgia in the early 1750s. The Penn and Slavery Project at the University of Pennsylvania dates it to 1752, when Georgia became a royal colony, run directly by the king's officials instead of the trustees. Other accounts give 1751.

Whitefield died at Newburyport, Massachusetts, on September 30, 1770. In his will he left 4,000 acres of land in Georgia and fifty enslaved people to the Countess of Huntingdon. Phillis Wheatley, a girl of about seventeen who was enslaved in Boston, wrote a poem about his death that was printed that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-whitefield" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jonathan-edwards" name="Jonathan Edwards" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jonathan Edwards

> **Who:** The minister of the Northampton revival, whom the historian Perry Miller called "the first and greatest homegrown American philosopher." He bought and held enslaved people from 1731 until at least 1756.
> **When and where:** Born in East Windsor, Connecticut, October 5, 1703. Northampton, Massachusetts, from 1726. Died in Princeton, New Jersey, March 22, 1758.

**He compared his listeners to a spider held over a fire.**

Jonathan Edwards led a revival in his own church at Northampton, Massachusetts, and wrote the books that other revival preachers learned from. He was the fifth of eleven children of a minister, and he finished first in Yale's class of 1720.

He joined his grandfather, Solomon Stoddard, as a minister of the church at Northampton in 1726. It was the largest church in Massachusetts outside Boston. He later took his grandfather's place. In 1727 he married Sarah Pierpont, and they had ten children.

A revival took place in his congregation, the people of his church, in 1734 and 1735. Edwards wrote a book about it, *A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God*. Revival preachers in America and Britain used it as a model, and it made him famous.

**The sermon.** On July 8, 1741, he preached a sermon at Enfield, Connecticut, called "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God." He told the people that their own care and goodness had "no more influence to uphold you and keep you out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock."

He also told them that God hated them: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked."

He preached that God controls everything, that people are sinful by nature, that hell is real, and that a person has to be born again. Critics said many of the revival's conversions were false, or even the work of the devil. Edwards answered them in four books between 1741 and 1749, among them *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* in 1746. In them he tried to set out how a person could tell a real change of heart from a false one.

**The people he enslaved.** Edwards bought and held enslaved people from 1731 until at least 1756. Kenneth Minkema, of the Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale, lays out the record. The first was a teenage girl named Venus, whom Edwards bought at Newport in 1731. The last was a child named Titus, in 1756. Others whose names are known were Leah, Rose, and a married couple, Joseph and Sue.

His wife, Sarah, actively tried to get enslaved people from family members and from her husband's fellow ministers. Edwards defended slavery as allowed by the Bible. He argued that it was a way to bring enslaved people to Christianity. Late in his life he came to oppose the slave trade, writing that it was unjust to take away the freedom of people born free. After his death his son, Jonathan Edwards Jr., and his student Samuel Hopkins called for ending slavery completely.

**Stockbridge.** The members of his congregation dismissed him in 1750, after he tried to make the rules stricter for who could take communion, the Christian ceremony of sharing bread and wine in memory of Jesus. From 1751 to 1757 he worked at Stockbridge, a Mohican town on the western edge of Massachusetts. He preached to a small English congregation and was a missionary to about 150 Mohican and Mohawk families.

Stockbridge had 218 Mohican people living there in 1749. By 1774 the Mohicans had lost all but 1,200 of their 23,000 acres there, and in 1783 the Mohicans were forced to leave the rest. Their own history does not name the people who took the land or forced them out. The Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale states that Edwards was "an agent in depriving Native Americans of their lands and culture."

**His death.** In late 1757 Edwards agreed to become president of the College of New Jersey. A few months after he arrived, he was inoculated against smallpox, given a small dose of live smallpox on purpose so that he would have a mild case and be safe afterward. A few of the people inoculated died from it, and Edwards was one of them. He died on March 22, 1758.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jonathan-edwards" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A man hanged as a Catholic priest, New York, 1741" -->
Under a law the members of the New York assembly passed in 1700, any Catholic priest who came to New York faced prison for life.

**The fires of 1741.** After a string of fires in 1741, New York City officials claimed that enslaved people and poor white people had plotted to burn the city. By the end of August the judges had sentenced 30 people to death, by the count of the Historical Society of the New York Courts. They also sentenced 70 people to be sold as slaves in the Caribbean.

The historian Jill Lepore counts 34 people put to death. By her count, officials burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 Black men, two white men and two white women.

**The hunt for priests.** While those trials were going on, James Oglethorpe, the governor of Georgia, wrote to New York's lieutenant governor, the deputy governor. He warned that the Spanish had sent agents to burn English towns in North America, and that "many priests were employed" in disguise, pretending to be doctors or dancing masters. New York officials began hunting for hidden priests. They arrested John Ury, a schoolteacher who knew Latin. Ury said he was a minister of the Church of England.

Mary Burton, a servant, had been a witness in the earlier trials. A judge, Daniel Horsmanden, had promised her money and an end to her years of service. She had never mentioned Ury before. Now she said he was the leader of the plot and had performed Catholic ceremonies for the plotters. Two more witnesses backed her. One was William Kane, a soldier Burton had accused, who went along to save himself. The other was Sarah Hughson, who was brought from the cell where she waited to be executed.

**The trial.** New York's attorney general, the colony's top lawyer, was a man named Bradley. At the trial he attacked the Catholic religion as "murderous" and read Oglethorpe's letter to the jury. Ury had no lawyer. His own witnesses said he was not a Catholic priest and that he had been elsewhere on the nights in question.

The jury took less than fifteen minutes to find him guilty. New York officials hanged John Ury on August 29, 1741. Before he died he said he was innocent and forgave the men who had prosecuted him. He was the last person executed for the supposed plot.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 21 minutes**

Americans spent these years arguing over whether the government should make people pay taxes to support a church. Before 1776, several colonies had an established church, one that the government supported with tax money. In Massachusetts it was the Congregational church. In Virginia it was the Church of England, also called the Anglican church, and people there had to attend its services as well as pay for its ministers.

Baptists and other people outside the tax-supported churches still had to pay those churches' ministers, and they organized against the church taxes. After the Revolution the lawmakers of each state decided for themselves whether to keep a church tax. The Baptists won in Virginia in 1786 and lost in Massachusetts, where the tax lasted until 1833. The First Amendment, the first of the changes added to the national Constitution in 1791, barred Congress, the national lawmakers, from setting up a national church.

**New churches and new missions.** Black Americans who had been born into slavery founded churches of their own in these years. On the Pacific coast, Spanish Franciscans built missions from 1769 on the lands of many Native nations. To keep people in the missions, missionaries and soldiers whipped them, locked them in the stocks, wooden frames that hold the feet or hands, and hunted down those who ran away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three more colleges with church backing" -->
Three more colleges were founded with church backing in these years. Rhode Island College was founded in 1764 with the support of the Philadelphia Baptist Association, a group of Baptist churches, and it was renamed Brown University in 1804. Queen's College, tied to the Dutch Reformed Church, received its charter on November 10, 1766. It is now Rutgers University. Dartmouth College received its charter on December 13, 1769. Its founder was Eleazar Wheelock, a Congregational minister.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How Virginia ended its church tax" -->
A parish was the area served by one church. In Virginia the men who ran each parish also cared for poor people and orphans, so they did the work of a local government as well as running a church.

The members of the Virginia assembly stopped the tax that paid Church of England ministers in 1779. Patrick Henry put forward a bill, a proposed law, for a new religious tax on December 24, 1784. Under his plan, each taxpayer could send his share to the church of his choice.

Some leading Virginians backed a religious tax. Richard Henry Lee wrote that "the experience of all times shows religion to be the guardian of morals." John Marshall, who later became Chief Justice of the United States, voted to move Henry's bill forward rather than put it off. George Washington wrote to George Mason that he did not object to "making people pay towards the support of that which they profess." He thought the bill would stir up trouble, though.

**Madison's protest.** James Madison led the other side. He got the vote put off until the autumn of 1785. On June 20, 1785, he published a long argument against religious taxes, without his name on it, called the *Memorial and Remonstrance*, a remonstrance being a formal protest.

Baptists and most Presbyterians were on his side. They argued that tax money spoils a church, and that a government has no right to control a person's religion. By the autumn of 1785 Madison had the votes, and the members of the assembly voted Henry's bill down.

**Jefferson's law.** In its place Madison brought back a bill that Thomas Jefferson had written in 1777. The members of the assembly passed it on January 16, 1786, and it became law three days later. It is called the Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom, a statute being a law. It states: "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place, or ministry whatsoever."

Jefferson wrote that it was meant to protect "the Jew and the Gentile" (a person who is not Jewish), the "Mahometan" (an old word for a Muslim), the "Hindoo" (an old spelling of Hindu), and the "Infidel of every denomination," meaning people who believe in no religion at all.

Lawmakers in most states did not follow Virginia in these years. Those of Massachusetts, Connecticut and New Hampshire set up religious taxes that people had to pay. Maryland's and Georgia's lawmakers passed religious taxes but never collected them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="No religious test, and the First Amendment" -->
The Constitution written in Philadelphia in 1787 says almost nothing about religion. Its one rule about religion is in Article VI. It states that "no religious Test shall ever be required" for a job in the national government. That meant no one could be made to swear an oath about religious beliefs before taking office. Charles Pinckney of South Carolina proposed the rule on August 20, 1787. The delegates at the convention, the meeting in Philadelphia that wrote the Constitution, adopted it on August 30.

**Franklin's motion.** Benjamin Franklin, who was eighty-one, asked the convention on June 28, 1787, to open each day with prayers. He told the delegates: "the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this Truth." He went on: "that God governs in the Affairs of Men." The delegates did not approve his motion. According to reports of the time, the convention had no money to pay local ministers to lead the prayers.

Some people complained that the Constitution contained "no recognition of his mercies to us . . . or even of his existence," meaning God's. Many of the voters in Madison's part of Virginia were Baptists, and they wanted freedom of religion protected in writing. On June 8, 1789, Madison introduced a set of amendments, or changes, to the Constitution. In his notes for that speech he wrote against a "national" religion.

The members of both houses of Congress voted on September 28, 1789, to send twelve amendments to the states. By December 1791 the lawmakers of three fourths of the states, the number needed, had approved ten of them. The first of the ten begins: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." Those words mean that Congress may not set up an official church, and may not stop people from practicing their religion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last church tax" -->
Massachusetts lawmakers kept their state's church tax for forty-two years after the First Amendment was approved. Article III of the state's 1780 constitution required every town and parish to pay for "public Protestant teachers of piety, religion and morality." It also allowed the lawmakers to order people to go and listen to those teachers.

If a man asked, his church tax went to the minister of his own church, as long as he went to hear that minister preach. If he had no such minister, his tax could go to the minister of the parish where the tax was collected.

**The argument.** Article III was the part of that constitution people argued about most. Phillips Payson, a Congregational minister at Chelsea, argued for it in a sermon on May 27, 1778. He warned that if "the restraints of religion" were "once broken down," nothing could "support and preserve order and government in the state." He meant that without religion, no one could keep order.

Isaac Backus, the leader of the Baptists of New England, answered him that year in a pamphlet. He argued that government money spoils churches, and that the best thing a government can do for religion is to leave it alone.

The Baptists lost. There was strong doubt that Article III had won the two thirds of the vote that it needed, and in 1780 the state's officials declared it adopted anyway.

Article III stood for fifty-three years. In 1833 a new amendment to the state constitution replaced it. Churches could still choose their ministers and agree to pay them, but they had to raise the money themselves. Any member could leave a church by handing its clerk a written notice. Massachusetts was the last state to end its church tax.

**By 1791:** Virginia had ended its church tax and the First Amendment kept Congress from setting up a national church, but Massachusetts towns still had to pay Protestant ministers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Baptists in Savannah" -->
George Liele was born into slavery in Virginia in 1750. In 1775 he was ordained a Baptist minister, which means church leaders officially made him a minister, and he preached at plantations along the Savannah River. In 1778 he gathered a congregation of Black Baptists in Savannah, Georgia.

During the Revolution Liele told his followers to side with the British, who promised to treat African Americans as free people. When the British left their headquarters on Tybee Island, near Savannah, in 1782, many of Liele's followers went with them. They founded settlements and churches in Jamaica, Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone. Liele went to Jamaica.

Andrew Bryan kept the Savannah congregation going. He was born at Goose Creek, South Carolina, and was enslaved by a man named Jonathan Bryan. On January 20, 1788, in a barn at Brampton plantation, a white minister named Abraham Marshall and a Black minister named Jesse Peter baptized more than forty of Bryan's followers. They made the group a church and ordained Bryan as its minister. The church was named the Ethiopian Church of Jesus Christ.

**Whipped for worshipping.** Many white people in Savannah feared that any gathering of enslaved people could be the start of a revolt. A letter written from Savannah in 1790 by Jonathan Clarke describes what they did. White people of Savannah broke up the worship and took many of the worshippers before magistrates, local judges. The worshippers were jailed twice. About fifty were severely whipped. Andrew Bryan was cut and "bled abundantly," which means he bled heavily. The letter does not say who used the whip or who gave the order.

Clarke wrote that Bryan said he "rejoiced not only to be whipped, but would freely suffer death for the cause of Jesus Christ." The chief justice, Henry Osborne, questioned the worshippers along with James Habersham and David Montague, and they released them. Another man, George Walton, said the treatment of the worshippers "would be condemned even among barbarians." Osborne then allowed them to worship between sunrise and sunset.

Bryan counted about 575 followers in 1790. Of those, 225 were full members. About 350 others were converts, and many of them did not have their enslavers' permission to be baptized. Bryan later bought freedom for himself and his family, and he bought land for the church. He died in 1812.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-allen" name="Richard Allen" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Richard Allen

> **Who:** A preacher born into slavery who bought his freedom, walked out of a white-run church in Philadelphia, and founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church.
> **When and where:** Born into slavery on February 14, 1760, held by a Philadelphia lawyer. Delaware, then Philadelphia. Died in Philadelphia, March 26, 1831.

**A trustee pulled a praying man up off his knees.**

Richard Allen founded Bethel Church in Philadelphia after a white trustee, one of the men who ran the church, pulled a Black member up off his knees during prayer. Allen was born enslaved by Benjamin Chew, a Philadelphia lawyer, who sold Allen and his family to Stokely Sturgis, a farmer near Dover, Delaware. Allen began preaching as a teenager, while he was still enslaved.

**Buying freedom.** In 1780 he bought his freedom for $2,000. He earned the money by working in his own time for about five years. The dollars were Continental money, paper money printed during the Revolution, which by 1780 had lost most of its value.

**Philadelphia.** He came to Philadelphia in February 1786. He wrote: "I frequently preached twice a day, at 5 o'clock in the morning and in the evening, and it was not uncommon for me to preach from four to five times a day." That year he gathered a group of forty-two members.

Absalom Jones, William White and Dorus Ginnings joined him in wanting a church for Black Philadelphians. The elder, the church leader in charge at St. George's Methodist Church, where they worshipped, forbade their prayer meetings. Allen wrote that the Black members "were considered as a nuisance."

**The gallery.** At St. George's, Allen wrote, the Black members were moved out of their usual seats and placed along the wall. He does not say who moved them. Then one Sunday the sexton, the church's caretaker, sent them up to the gallery, a balcony above the main floor. As they knelt there to pray, a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones and pulled him up off his knees.

Allen wrote down the words. The trustee said, "You must get up," and then, "you must not kneel here." Jones answered, "Wait until prayer is over, and I will get up and trouble you no more." The trustee said, "No, you must get up now, or I will call for aid and force you away." A second trustee went to pull up William White.

Allen wrote: "By this time, prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church." He added that the Black members had helped pay for the church, "in building the gallery and laying new floors."

**When it happened.** The date of the walkout is disputed, and Allen's own account gives none. The traditional date is 1787. The historian Richard S. Newman argues for 1792 or 1793, which would make it a planned protest rather than a sudden one. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia places it "in the early 1790s."

The group rented a storeroom for worship. Allen wrote that they were threatened with being "read publicly out of meeting," which meant expelled in front of the whole church, if they kept worshipping there. Benjamin Rush and Robert Ralston gave money for a new church. On their first day collecting money, Allen and Jones raised $360. Allen called it "the greatest day's collection that we met with."

**Bethel.** Allen bought a lot at Sixth and Lombard Streets in 1791. The first building was an old blacksmith's shop, and he opened Bethel Church there in July 1794. In 1799 he became the first Black man ordained a Methodist minister.

In Philadelphia in 1816 he brought together African Methodist churches from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland. They formed a new church body, the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and elected Allen its first bishop, a leader over many churches. He died at his home on March 26, 1831, and was buried in the basement of Mother Bethel, as his church came to be called.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-allen" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="absalom-jones" name="Absalom Jones" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Absalom Jones

> **Who:** A man born into slavery who taught himself to read and became the first priest of African descent in the Episcopal Church in the United States.
> **When and where:** Born in Delaware, November 6, 1746. Philadelphia from 1762. Died 1818.

**When he was sixteen, his mother, sister and five brothers were sold.**

Absalom Jones was the first priest of African descent in the United States. He was born into slavery in Delaware and taught himself to read, and the New Testament was one of the books he learned from. When he was sixteen, his mother, his sister and his five brothers were sold. The records do not name who sold them. The man who enslaved Jones took him to Philadelphia in 1762, and there he went to a Quaker night school for Black people.

He bought his freedom in 1784. In 1787 he and Richard Allen started the Free African Society, which worked to end slavery and helped widows, orphans and poor people. In 1792, with help from local Quakers and Episcopalians, Jones and Allen set up the First African Church in Philadelphia.

**St. Thomas.** After the walkout from St. George's, Jones and Allen went separate ways. Jones led the congregation into the Episcopal Church, while Allen stayed a Methodist and opened Bethel Church in 1794. On October 17, 1794, Jones's congregation was formally received into the Episcopal Diocese of Pennsylvania, the group of churches under that state's bishop, as the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. Jones was ordained a deacon, a minister one rank below a priest, in 1795.

Bishop William White, a different man from the William White who walked out of St. George's, ordained Jones, then fifty-six, a priest. The Episcopal Church's own archives give the year as 1802, and Delaware's state historical marker gives 1804.

Jones also started a day school, a Female Benevolent Society and an African Friendly Society. In 1800 he sent a petition, a written request, to Congress about slavery. He died in 1818.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="absalom-jones" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christian Lenape killed at Gnadenhutten, 1782" -->
American militiamen, part-time soldiers from the local area, killed about 96 Christian Lenape and Mohican people at Gnadenhutten, in the Tuscarawas Valley of Ohio, on March 8, 1782. Chapter 22, War, tells the killing in full. The people killed were converts of the Moravian church and lived in Moravian mission villages. They had refused to fight for either side in the Revolution. Colonel David Williamson led between 100 and 200 militiamen.

The militiamen falsely accused the Christian converts of taking their goods in raids. They held them in houses and voted to kill them. Williamson asked the militiamen who wanted to spare the prisoners to stand apart, and only sixteen or eighteen did. The prisoners, knowing what was coming, prayed and sang hymns.

The militiamen tied them up and killed them by smashing their heads with a mallet, one after another, taking turns as each man got tired. Then they cut off their scalps, the skin and hair of the top of the head. They killed 62 adults and 34 children. Two boys escaped. One of them, named Thomas, lived after he was scalped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The California missions" -->
Spanish soldiers and Franciscan friars began building missions in Alta California, which is now the state of California, in 1769. Spain's officials wanted to hold the Pacific coast against Russia and England, and they had little money. They sent missionaries to live on Native homelands because it was a cheap way to claim the land. Soldiers and missionaries came together. The missionaries' job was to make Native people Catholics and to change their whole way of life.

Father Junípero Serra led the Franciscans. He was born in 1713 at Petra, on the island of Mallorca, and he had worked as a missionary in Mexico for seventeen years. He founded Mission San Carlos Borromeo at Monterey in 1770 and lived there until he died in 1784. By the end of 1800 the Franciscans had founded eighteen missions. By 1823 there were twenty-one.

**Whose land.** About 310,000 Native people lived in California in 1769. Each mission was built in the homeland of a people with its own government and language. By 1800 the missions stood on the lands of more than a dozen peoples, among them the Kumeyaay at San Diego, the Tongva at San Gabriel, the Chumash at Santa Bárbara and La Purísima, and different Ohlone peoples around Monterey Bay and San Francisco Bay.

**Baptism and force.** Serra's writings describe a plan to baptize young children first. He expected the children to learn Spanish and translate for their parents. Edward Ketchum, a tribal historian, has said that once a child was baptized, the child was the "property" of the church.

Researchers wrote a report on the missions for the state of California in 2023, with advisers from 19 California tribes. Drawing on the scholars Cook and Madley, it describes persuasion in the early years and more force after about 1790 to 1800. Native accounts make clear that force was used in many baptisms. When Native people kept resisting, the missionaries called in soldiers, who captured runaways and people who had not been baptized.

Missionaries punished baptized Native people to keep them from leaving the missions or to force them to come back. They punished people for running away, stealing, fighting and drinking, and for other acts they called disobedience. The missionaries sometimes gave the punishments themselves. They also had soldiers or Native officials deliver them. Soldiers patrolled the countryside around the missions and punished people there too.

**Whipping.** The 2023 report describes flogging, beating a person with a whip that cuts and bruises the skin, as "probably the most common" form of violence at the missions. It was done in the open, in front of others. Some Franciscans, Serra among them, whipped themselves as a religious practice, so whipping seemed to them a fair punishment for others.

Serra wrote to the military commander Fernando de Rivera y Moncada on July 31, 1775, about four men who had run from Mission Carmel. He asked to have them whipped "two or three" times. He offered to send shackles, iron rings locked around the legs or wrists. The journalist Elias Castillo, who has written against the missions, quotes the letter from Serra's collected writings.

Missionaries and soldiers used other punishments too. People were locked in the stocks, sometimes for days. People were hobbled, which means their legs were tied so they could only shuffle, and they were made to keep working. Others were beaten with a cudgel, a heavy club, or jailed and put to hard labor. For plotting, murder or armed rebellion, some people were executed. Soldiers raped Native women at missions including Soledad, San Luis Obispo, San Diego and San Gabriel.

**Baptisms and deaths.** The missionaries recorded 85,840 baptisms of Native people at the 21 missions from 1769 to 1834, and in the same years they recorded 59,538 Native deaths. That is about seven deaths for every ten baptisms. The death records for two missions are missing, so the real number of deaths is higher. These counts come from the Early California Population Project at the Huntington Library, and they run past 1800.

Other record collections cover different years and people, so their totals differ. One gives about 101,000 baptisms and 71,000 deaths. Another gives 95,493 baptisms and 56,776 deaths. The 2023 report lists the causes of death. They include forced moves, hard labor, the banning of Native ways of life, poor living conditions, violence, and new diseases brought in from outside.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Kumeyaay attack on Mission San Diego, 1775" -->
Kumeyaay fighters burned Mission San Diego on the night of November 4 to 5, 1775. They came from fifteen of the twenty-five villages nearest the mission. Spanish soldiers estimated that 600 to 1,000 people took part.

**Why they attacked.** The attackers left no account of their own, but the Spanish records list what the Kumeyaay had been complaining about. From 1769 to the end of 1774 the friars had baptized only 100 people, and then they pushed much harder. On October 3, 1775, the friars baptized sixty people in a single day.

Spanish soldiers had raped Kumeyaay women, and at least one of those women was also murdered. Father Luis Jayme, a friar at the mission, had reported the rapes. Spanish cattle had destroyed village land. Writers at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian list the Spanish destruction of Native food sources and the Spanish taking of Native land among the causes. Disease was also spreading.

The attackers burned the mission and took goods from the church. They killed three people: Father Jayme, and a carpenter and a blacksmith from Mexico. They shot Jayme with an arrow and then beat him to death.

After the attack, Spanish soldiers took violent revenge. They went through the villages in force and singled out the people they believed had led it. The records give no number of the people they punished or killed, and the 2023 report does not say what the soldiers did to the people they singled out. The mission was rebuilt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Toypurina

> **Who:** A Tongva woman, often called a religious leader, who helped plan an attack on Mission San Gabriel.
> **When and where:** Japchivit and Mission San Gabriel, near what is now Los Angeles, 1785.

**The soldiers were warned and arrested twenty-one rebels before the attack.**

Toypurina was a Tongva woman from the village of Japchivit who had never been baptized. She had influence and standing in her community, and writers often call her a shaman, a religious or spiritual leader. In October 1785 she helped plan an attack on Mission San Gabriel. Soldiers at the mission were warned, and they arrested twenty-one of the rebels before the attack began.

**The plan.** The plan started with Nicolás José, a baptized Tongva man. He had lost a son and two wives. He asked Toypurina to call together the leaders of the other villages. One of those village leaders carried her message into the mission. People from the mission and from up to eight Tongva villages joined the plan.

When they were questioned, Nicolás José and Toypurina gave their reasons. Nicolás José said that the ban on dancing kept him from holding the ceremonies of mourning for the dead. Toypurina said she was angry with the Spanish settlers. She was also angry with the Native people who had moved to the mission, which stood on her land.

All of the leaders were held in jail for two and a half years. Then Spanish officials freed two of the village leaders with a warning. They exiled Nicolás José and Toypurina, sending them away from their homes.

**Not known:** the year of her birth and the rest of her life. The records of the plot do not give them.
