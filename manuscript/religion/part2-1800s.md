## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 27 minutes**

Protestant revivals were held in almost every corner of the country between 1800 and 1850, so many that one religious magazine stopped reporting them in 1827. Its editors wrote that revivals "are becoming too numerous in our country to admit of being generally mentioned in our Record." In 1833 Massachusetts lawmakers stopped using tax money to pay for churches, and no other state still did.

The kind of Protestant faith that spread in these years is called evangelicalism. Evangelical preachers called on each listener to be converted, to turn to God and become a true believer, and they called this change the "new birth." Converted people started societies to hand out Bibles and religious papers, teach children, and send out missionaries. Some of the societies worked to get people to stop drinking, because their members believed a sober person was readier to be converted.

A Library of Congress exhibit lists the six largest of these societies in 1826 and 1827, among them the American Sunday School Union and the American Tract Society.

**Mobs and splits.** In the same fifty years, Protestant mobs burned a school run by Catholic nuns near Boston and Catholic churches in Philadelphia, and a Missouri governor ordered the members of a new church driven from his state. Methodists and Baptists in the South broke away from their national churches over slavery and formed churches of their own. In California the whippings at the missions went on, and the Mexican officials who took the missions away from the friars often whipped people too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Camp meetings" -->
Big revivals began at both ends of the country in 1800, in New England and in Kentucky. A Library of Congress exhibit calls the New England revival "decorous," which means calm and proper, and the Kentucky revival "exuberant," which means full of wild feeling. In Kentucky, Presbyterian ministers started a new kind of gathering, the camp meeting.

The ministers copied the long outdoor "communion seasons" of the Church of Scotland, gatherings for communion, the church service in which believers share bread and wine in memory of Jesus. Families loaded wagons, drove to the meeting, pitched tents, and stayed for several days of preaching.

**Cane Ridge.** One camp meeting was held at Cane Ridge, near Lexington, Kentucky, in the summer of 1801. A Presbyterian minister named Barton Stone organized it. In 1832 Stone and Alexander Campbell left the Presbyterians and formed a new church, the Disciples of Christ.

Lorenzo Dow was a traveling Methodist preacher. While he preached, people in his crowds often jerked their bodies about, which was called the jerking exercise. The Presbyterians and Baptists who had started camp meetings soon turned against them because of the strong feelings they stirred in people. The Methodists kept them and carried them east, and for decades camp meetings were a Methodist practice.

**A drawing from 1809.** Benjamin Henry Latrobe drew a plan of a Methodist camp meeting in Fairfax County, Virginia, on August 8, 1809. His drawing shows the men's seats apart from the women's and the tents of Black worshippers apart from the tents of white worshippers. At one side of the camp, the organizers had built a pen of boards filled with straw. Latrobe wrote that "the converted were thrown" into it "that they might kick about without injuring themselves."

A Library of Congress exhibit calls this drawing an example of separation by race. That separation led Black Methodists, a few years later, to leave and form a Methodist church of their own.

Mother Ann Lee, who founded the Shakers, came to America from England in 1774. The Shakers' full name was the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Coming. They danced in worship, often in circles inside circles, and got their nickname from shaking and whirling during services. In the early 1800s Shakers from New York went to Kentucky to see the revivals, and they gained a small number of converts there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sunday schools taught reading" -->
The first Sunday schools taught poor children to read. Sunday schools began in England in the 1780s. In 1791 a group of pastors and merchants in Philadelphia started the First Day Society. Its members paid schoolmasters to teach boys and girls, and grown men and women who could not read, to read and write. The Bible was the textbook. Sunday was the day poor working children were not at work, and there were no free public schools yet.

A group of Philadelphia businessmen started the Sunday and Adult School Union in 1817, with volunteer teachers instead of paid ones. Within a year they had opened forty-three Sunday schools in the city, with 5,970 pupils. The group grew into the American Sunday School Union. Its leaders hired missionaries who started Sunday schools in cities, in the countryside and on Native reservations, and it became a large publisher of children's books.

**Tracts.** The American Tract Society began in 1825 to print tracts, short papers about religion. A Library of Congress exhibit gives an estimate that its members handed out 35 million books and tracts in its first ten years. Its leaders printed papers that urged readers to be converted and to give up drinking and gambling, which the leaders believed kept people from being converted.

Rebecca Gratz started the first Hebrew Sunday School, a Sunday school for Jewish children, in Philadelphia in 1838.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The African Methodist Episcopal Church grows" -->
The African Methodist Episcopal Church, which Richard Allen and other Black Methodists formed in 1816, grew fast. It began with 8 ministers and 5 churches. By 1846 it had 176 ministers, 296 churches and 17,375 members.

Women preached in the church from its early years. Many people objected to women preaching, in the A.M.E. Church as in the white churches. An engraving made in Philadelphia in 1844 by P. S. Duval, from a painting by Alfred Hoffy, shows one of these women: Juliann Jane Tillman, a preacher of the A.M.E. Church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The convent burned at Charlestown, 1834" -->
Some Protestants in these years turned to violence against Catholics. In Charlestown, Massachusetts, near Boston, Ursuline nuns, Catholic women who make religious promises and live together in a convent, ran a convent school from 1819. The Ursulines are an order of nuns who teach, and their students were girls.

**The stories.** Stories about the convent spread in Boston. Rebecca Reed, a Protestant woman, had entered the convent in 1832 and left after less than six months. Her stories about life inside went around before her book, *Six Months in a Convent*, came out in 1835.

A nun named Sister Mary John, whose name before she joined the order was Elizabeth Harrison, left the convent on July 28, 1834. Bishop Fenwick, the church leader in charge of all the Catholic churches of the area, talked her into going back. Rumors spread that she was being held against her will.

The Protestant minister Lyman Beecher preached against Catholic schools in three Boston churches on Sunday, August 10. The next day a group of Charlestown officials toured the convent, and Sister Mary John guided them. Historians disagree about whether Beecher's sermons led to what happened that night. Ira Leonard argues that they did. Ray Billington points out that the workmen had met before the sermons. Beecher himself said that probably no rioter had heard him.

**The fire.** A mob of mostly Protestant workmen from Charlestown burned the convent school on the night of August 11 to 12, 1834. The nuns and 47 girl students were inside when the mob came, and only six of the girls were Catholic. The mob also broke open the convent's tomb, where several dead nuns were buried. Firemen stood by and watched. No deaths are recorded.

Police arrested 13 men. Juries found 12 of them not guilty, including John R. Buzzell, who admitted he had led the mob. The one man found guilty was 16 years old, and Governor John Davis pardoned him, which means he freed him from his punishment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose Bible? Philadelphia, 1842 to 1844" -->
Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed a law in 1838 that made the King James Bible, the Bible that Protestants used, a required textbook in the public schools. In Philadelphia, Protestant teachers also led their pupils in Protestant hymns, or church songs.

**The bishop's letter.** Bishop Francis Patrick Kenrick, the Catholic bishop of Philadelphia, wrote to the board that ran the city's public schools in 1842. He objected to the hymns and to the Bible readings. He asked that Catholic children be allowed to read the Douay Bible, the translation Catholics use, and that they not be made to join in Protestant worship.

Early in 1843 the board members agreed that children could be excused from the King James Bible and could use any Bible "without note or comment." The Douay Bible has notes and comments. Under the board's rule, the one Bible the Catholic bishop had asked for was still not allowed.

**The rumor.** Hugh Clark, a Catholic school director in the Kensington neighborhood, suggested in February 1844 stopping Bible reading until the board could write a rule both sides accepted. A rumor spread that Clark had ordered a teacher, Miss Bedford, to stop reading the Protestant Bible. Witnesses later showed that the rumor was false.

Nativists, people who opposed Catholic immigration and saw Catholics as a danger to the Protestant faith, spread it anyway. A newspaper editor named Lewis Levin had founded their group in Philadelphia, the American Republican Association, in December 1843.

**The May riots.** The nativists held a rally on May 3, 1844, and marched into Kensington, where Irish Catholic families lived. On May 6 a nativist named George Shiffler was shot dead. By the end of that day a second man, who seems to have been a bystander, had been shot dead. Two more nativists were shot that day and later died of their wounds. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia does not name who shot any of them.

Nativist crowds burned St. Michael's Church, St. Augustine's Church and Irish Catholic homes, including Hugh Clark's house, on May 8. They also burned a Catholic seminary, a school for training priests.

**The July riots.** Fighting started again in July, at the Church of St. Philip de Neri in the Southwark neighborhood. State militia, part-time soldiers called up by the state, fought the crowd with cannon under General George Cadwalader. By the morning of July 7, four militiamen and probably about a dozen rioters were dead, and many more were wounded.

Counts of the dead in the two riots differ. Counts for May run from at least 14 dead to 20, and one count gives at least 100 wounded. Counts for July run from at least 14 dead to about 16.

**Who was blamed.** A grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof for a trial, charged 19 rioters with crimes, and only a few were found guilty. The grand jury blamed the May riots on "the efforts of a portion of the community to exclude the Bible from the public schools." The jurors meant the Catholics who had been attacked.

Catholics answered that the grand jury had called no Catholics to testify. After the riots, Catholics decided to build a school system of their own.

**By 1844:** Protestant mobs had burned a convent school near Boston and two Catholic churches in Philadelphia, and Catholics had decided to build schools of their own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Latter-day Saints driven out of Missouri" -->
Joseph Smith founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints on April 6, 1830, at a farmhouse near Fayette, in western New York. Its members were often called Mormons. In 1831 Smith said that God had chosen western Missouri as the place where his people would gather. Church members moved there, and their Missouri neighbors drove them out twice.

**Jackson County, 1833.** The first time was in Jackson County in 1833. The older settlers feared that the newcomers would control the county's politics and trade, and they objected to the newcomers' religious beliefs. An article in the church newspaper about free Black converts coming to Missouri, a state that allowed slavery, also angered them.

A mob attacked the church's printing office on July 20, 1833, threw the press out of the window, and scattered the type in the street. They tarred and feathered Edward Partridge, the church's bishop in Missouri, and Charles Allen in the public square. In tarring and feathering, men tore off a person's clothes and smeared hot pine tar on the bare skin, which burned it. Then they covered the person with feathers, which stuck to the tar. The church's account does not describe the injuries the two men suffered.

Mobs drove the church members out of the county in November 1833. Three people were killed on November 4, one of them a church member, and the church's account does not say who killed them.

**Caldwell County, 1836 to 1838.** The Missouri lawmakers created Caldwell County in 1836 as a place for church members to live. More converts kept arriving and settled beyond its borders. In 1838 both sides used violence. The Missouri State Archives states that "Old Settler mobs and Mormon paramilitary units roamed the countryside," paramilitary units being armed groups organized like an army.

Some Latter-day Saint men formed the Danites, a group of vigilantes, people who act as their own police and punish others outside the law. In the middle of October, Mormon forces raided the towns of Gallatin and Millport and burned property there. Shortly before the end of October, at Crooked River, church members attacked a unit of the state militia that they took for a mob. One Missourian and two church members were killed. One of the dead was David W. Patten, an apostle, one of the church's highest leaders.

**The governor's order.** Governor Lilburn Boggs issued an order on October 27, 1838. It said the Latter-day Saints must be driven from the state or "exterminated," killed every one, if necessary.

**Haun's Mill.** Three days later, on October 30, between about 200 and 240 armed men attacked the small church settlement at Haun's Mill at about 4 in the afternoon. Colonel Thomas Jennings of Livingston County commanded them, and Captain Nehemiah Comstock led one of the companies.

Boggs had not ordered this attack. The Missouri State Archives says it happened "even without official sanction," which means without the governor's approval. The historian Alma Blair writes that the governor's order "was now widely known."

Many of the Mormon men took cover in the blacksmith shop, and the attackers shot them through the cracks in its walls. The attackers then hacked some of the wounded men to death with corn knives, long blades used for cutting cornstalks. One of them shot Thomas McBride with McBride's own rifle as he surrendered, and then the attackers hacked him.

They killed seventeen or eighteen men and boys. The Missouri State Archives gives eighteen, and Blair and the church's history give seventeen. Among the dead was Sardius Smith, age 10. Charles Merrick, age 9, died of his wounds five weeks later. One woman was wounded. No record shows that anyone was ever tried for the killings at Haun's Mill.

General Samuel D. Lucas arrested Joseph Smith and other church leaders on October 31. Over the next year about 8,000 church members, driven out under the governor's order, went from Missouri to Illinois. The Missouri State Archives describes them as "often ragged and deprived of their property." Governor Boggs's order stayed in force until June 25, 1976, when Governor Bond canceled it.

### Joseph Smith

> **Who:** The founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, whose followers were driven from state to state. A mob killed him in 1844.
> **When and where:** Lived 1805 to 1844. Western New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Nauvoo, Illinois. Killed at Carthage, Illinois, on June 27, 1844.

**About 100 men broke into the jail at Carthage, Illinois.**

Joseph Smith founded a new American church in 1830, and a mob killed him fourteen years later. He grew up in a part of western New York that people called the Burned Over District, because so many revivals had passed through it. A Library of Congress exhibit states that his church did not split off from an older church, and it describes the church as the only one of its kind.

**The Book of Mormon.** E. B. Grandin of Palmyra, New York, published the Book of Mormon for Smith in 1830. Smith said the book came to him through revelations, messages he said came from God, on a set of gold plates written in a language he called reformed Egyptian. He said he had translated it. Eleven men signed statements saying that they had seen and handled the plates.

The book tells of Israelites, the people of ancient Israel in the Bible, who were led out of Jerusalem to the Americas about 600 years before the birth of Jesus. It also tells of Jesus appearing to their descendants in America. Smith's followers believed his church brought back the earliest form of Christianity. Church members accept the Book of Mormon as scripture, a holy book, alongside the Bible.

**Nauvoo.** The church moved from New York to Ohio, from Ohio to Missouri, and from Missouri to Illinois. In 1839 church leaders bought land on the Mississippi River in Illinois, at a nearly empty town called Commerce, and they renamed it Nauvoo in 1840. A large Meskwaki village had stood there, and accounts of how its people lost it differ.

The Illinois Department of Natural Resources runs the state park at Nauvoo. Its page about the park states that the site was signed away in a treaty in 1824, but it does not name who signed it. Another account states that a man named James White bought it from the Sauk leader Quashquame for liquor and corn, and that Quashquame's people moved across the river.

In Illinois, trouble grew again. Neighbors resented the church members' business dealings and the way they all voted together. Some accused them of theft. They were angry about Smith's plans in politics. News spread that some church leaders had more than one wife, and church members also argued among themselves about it.

**Carthage.** Men who opposed Smith started a newspaper in Nauvoo, and Smith ordered its printing press destroyed. He was jailed at Carthage, Illinois, for it. On June 27, 1844, about 100 men broke into the jail and killed Smith and his brother Hyrum. The men came from three units of the Illinois militia: the 59th Regiment, the Warsaw Independent Battalion and the Warsaw Rifle Company.

The militiamen guarding the jail barely resisted. Accounts of the killing differ. A Library of Congress exhibit states that Smith was "apparently" shot, and that one man in the mob came at him meaning to cut off his head and was stopped.

**The trial.** Nine men were charged with the murders in October 1844. Four ran away and were never arrested. Five were tried in May 1845: Thomas C. Sharp, the publisher of an anti-Mormon newspaper, and four militia officers, Levi Williams, Mark Aldrich, William N. Grover and Jacob C. Davis, who was also a state senator. The jury found all five not guilty.

The prosecutor, Josiah Lamborn, said of Davis: "I have no doubt in my own mind, not a particle, that Davis cooperated in the murder, but there is no legal evidence to convict him."

After Smith's death, men attacked Mormon farms around Nauvoo. Brigham Young, who led the church after Smith, planned to move about 15,000 people west. On February 4, 1846, the first wagons crossed the Mississippi into Iowa.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The religion enslavers taught" -->
In the South, enslavers did not let enslaved people attend the regular preaching with them on working days. Charles Colcock Jones was a Presbyterian minister in Liberty County, Georgia. In 1842 he wrote that enslaved people "were not permitted, on working days, to attend the public administration of the word in company with their masters." By "the public administration of the word" he meant the regular preaching. Where they did attend, part of the white church was set apart for them.

Jones wrote a book to urge planters and ministers to have Christianity taught to the people they held in slavery. In *The Religious Instruction of the Negroes in the United States*, finished on July 4, 1842, Jones told ministers which parts of the Bible to use. ("Negro" was the common word for a Black person in his time, and it is no longer used.)

He chose verses about obedience. One verse says "servants" should "count their masters worthy of all honor," and Jones used it to teach enslaved people to obey. He wrote that a minister should teach them "the duties of their station," meaning their duty to obey.

**Frederick Douglass.** Frederick Douglass, who had been enslaved in Maryland, wrote down what this religion looked like to the people it was used on. Thomas Auld, the man who held Douglass, was converted at a Methodist camp meeting in Talbot County, Maryland, in August 1832. Douglass hoped Auld would free him, or at least treat him better. "I was disappointed in both these respects," he wrote. "I believe him to have been a much worse man after his conversion than before."

Douglass wrote that after his conversion, Auld "found religious sanction and support for his slaveholding cruelty," sanction meaning approval. Auld became a leader in his church, and visiting preachers stayed at his house. Douglass wrote: "while he starved us, he stuffed them."

At St. Michael's, Maryland, a young white man named Wilson started a Sunday school to teach enslaved people to read the New Testament. They met three times. Then Garrison West and Wright Fairbanks, two class leaders, each in charge of a small group of church members, came with many other men carrying sticks and other things to throw. The men drove the students away and forbade them to meet again.

**Henny.** Douglass saw Auld whip Henny, a young woman whose hands had been burned so badly in a fire when she was a child that she could not use them. Auld tied her up and whipped her bare shoulders with a heavy cowskin, a whip made of cowhide, "causing the warm red blood to drip." To justify it, he quoted the Bible: "He that knoweth his master's will, and doeth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes."

He left her tied up for four or five hours at a time. He whipped her before breakfast, went to his store, and came back and whipped her again at dinner "in the places already made raw." At last, in Auld's own words, he "set her adrift to take care of herself."

Douglass also named one minister, George Cookman, whom the enslaved people loved and believed was working to free them.

**What Douglass was against.** In 1845 Douglass added a note to the end of his book to explain that he was not against religion. He was against what he called "the slaveholding religion of this land." He wrote: "The man who wields the blood-clotted cowskin during the week fills the pulpit on Sunday." He wrote that the man who called it a religious duty to read the Bible denied him "the right of learning to read the name of the God who made me."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The churches split over slavery" -->
Members of two of the largest Protestant churches in the country divided them over slavery more than fifteen years before the Southern states left the Union.

**The Methodists.** Bishop James Osgood Andrew of Georgia held enslaved people, whom he had gained through his marriage. Methodist leaders met for their national meeting in 1844. The delegates, the men chosen to speak and vote for their churches, voted to stop Andrew from working as a bishop for as long as he held enslaved people.

A few days later the delegates adopted a Plan of Separation. Under the plan, the Methodist conferences in the slave states, the regional groups of churches there, could form a church of their own. Delegates from those conferences met at Louisville, Kentucky, in 1845 and created the Methodist Episcopal Church, South.

**The Baptists.** The Baptists split the same year. Georgia Baptists put forward an enslaver, James E. Reeve, to be a missionary, and Northern Baptists refused to appoint him. From May 8 to 12, 1845, Baptists from the South met at Augusta, Georgia, and formed the Southern Baptist Convention. William Bullein Johnson was its first president.

**By 1845:** Missouri's governor had ordered the Latter-day Saints driven out, a militia mob had killed their founder in Illinois, and Southern Methodists and Baptists had formed churches of their own over slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charles Grandison Finney

> **Who:** A young lawyer who gave up the law after his conversion and became a revival preacher. His book on revivals became a handbook for other revival preachers.
> **When and where:** Born in Warren, Connecticut, August 29, 1792. Upstate New York, then Oberlin, Ohio. Died 1875.

**He told a client he now worked for "the Lord Jesus Christ."**

Charles Grandison Finney taught that a person could choose to be converted, and he built new methods of preaching on that idea. He grew up in Oneida County, New York, which was then, in his words, "to a great extent, a wilderness." His parents did not belong to a church. He wrote that there were few religious books where he grew up and that he "seldom heard a sermon."

**The retainer.** He was training to be a lawyer at Adams, New York, when he was converted in 1821. The next morning a client came to ask whether he was ready to argue the client's case in court. The client was a deacon, an officer of his church. Finney answered with a lawyer's word, retainer, the money paid to a lawyer to take a case: "Deacon B, I have a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead His cause, and I cannot plead yours."

**The anxious seat.** He preached across upstate New York. From September 1830 he spent six months in Rochester. He preached every day of the week and three times on Sunday, and he spent most of each day praying with people one at a time. In Rochester he began using what was called the anxious seat, a set of seats at the front of the church where people who felt worried about their souls came and sat, in front of everyone.

Finney explained that people of the "higher classes" were "too proud to take any position that would reveal them to others as anxious for their souls." He wanted "something that would commit them publicly to the service of Christ."

His methods were called the New Measures. Finney taught that a person could decide to be converted, and his meetings stirred strong feelings in the people who came. Both things angered other ministers. Asahel Nettleton, a New England revival preacher, objected that Finney had changed the older Presbyterian teaching on how God saves people. Lyman Beecher feared that Finney's meetings let feelings run wild.

As early as July 1827, before Rochester, ministers had met at New Lebanon, New York, to look closely at the New Measures. Finney's *Lectures on Revivals*, published in 1835, became a handbook for revival preachers across the country.

**Oberlin.** Oberlin College in Ohio was close to shutting down in 1835. Arthur Tappan, a businessman in New York who worked to end slavery, agreed to rescue it on two conditions. Finney had to be made its teacher of theology, the study of religious belief. And the college had to admit Black students on the same terms as white students.

Finney took the job and also became minister of the Oberlin church. He was president of the college from 1851 to 1866 and stayed on as minister until 1872. He taught that the sign of God's grace in a person "was a person's benevolence toward others," meaning kindness and help given to other people. He died in 1875.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The California missions, 1800 to 1848" -->
The California missions, run by Franciscan friars, went on through these years, and so did the whippings. Missionaries had Native people whipped for running away, stealing, fighting and drinking, for living with a partner without a church marriage, and for other acts that mission officials counted as disobedience. They often had soldiers or Native officials give the whippings, and some of them whipped people themselves. Father de la Peña did at Mission Santa Clara.

**Santa Cruz, 1812.** A 2023 report on the missions for the state of California says that at Mission Santa Cruz, Father Andrés Quintana used a whip tipped with metal for floggings, beatings on the bare back that cut and bruise the skin. In 1812 he had just beaten two men nearly to death with it when a group of Awaswas Ohlone people from the mission killed him. The records name five of them: Lacah, Yaquenonsat, Yachacxi, Ules and Lino.

For two years his death was taken to be a natural one. Then a guard overheard an argument, and the plan came out. Spanish officials tried sixteen men and found nine guilty. Seven of the men died while held at the presidio, the Spanish fort, four of them before they were sentenced. The story of the plan comes from Lorenzo Asisara, an Ohlone man whose father took part in it.

**Punished for having no children.** Diseases brought by the Spanish made Native women less able to have children, or made them lose their babies before birth. Some missionaries believed that women did this on purpose. Lorenzo Asisara told what Father Olbes did at Santa Cruz to a married couple who had no children. Olbes demanded to inspect the couple's genitals, the parts of the body used to make babies.

When the woman resisted, he had her flogged and made her stand in front of the church for nine days holding a wooden doll. Olbes also had her husband put in shackles, iron rings locked around the legs or wrists, and made him stand in front of the church wearing horns. At Mission San Gabriel, Father Zalvidea punished women whose babies were born dead. He had their heads shaved, made them carry a wooden doll, had them flogged, and kept them in iron ankle cuffs for three months.

**The Chumash rising, 1824.** Native people fought back. In February 1824 Chumash people rose against three missions, Santa Inés, La Purísima and Santa Bárbara, in the largest planned revolt in California in the mission years. It began at Santa Inés on February 21, a day earlier than planned, after soldiers whipped a Native man from La Purísima who had come to visit a relative in jail. The Chumash attacked the soldiers and burned mission buildings.

The next day troops from the Santa Bárbara fort burned the adobe houses, made of sun-dried mud bricks, where the rebels were camped. Up to nineteen Chumash people and one Mexican soldier were killed at Santa Inés. At Santa Bárbara the Chumash leader Andrés Sagimomatsee called people to fight. At least two Chumash were killed there, and more than 1,000 people fled the mission for the inland country of the Yokuts people.

At La Purísima, led by Pacomio, the rebels held the mission for almost a month. One Chumash man and four settlers were killed when the rebels took it. In March troops took it back and killed 16 Chumash people. Mexican officials then had seven Chumash men executed for killing the four settlers. They sentenced four leaders to ten years in prison followed by exile from their homeland, and eight more men to eight years.

**Estanislao and Yozcolo.** Hundreds of Yokuts people left Missions San José and Santa Clara in 1828 and 1829 under two mission leaders, Estanislao and Cipriano. They built a fort in the marshes of the San Joaquin River. Mexican officials sent soldiers, Native fighters and armed settlers against them, and these forces defeated them in a battle. The records give no count of the dead and do not name the commander. Estanislao later bargained for a pardon.

A man named Yozcolo freed the young women who were locked in the women's building at Mission Santa Clara in 1839, and fled to the hills. Soldiers defeated him. The records do not say who cut off his head. The Mexican officials there set it outside the mission church for people to see.

**The end of the missions.** Mexicans won their independence from Spain in 1821. Mexican officials then began to take the missions away from the Franciscans and turn them into ordinary parish churches, the local churches of a town. This was called secularization, and Governor José Figueroa put it in place in 1833 and 1834.

The officials who took over the missions often used punishments like the friars' punishments, including whipping. At most missions, colonists got most of the land and supplies. Under Figueroa's rules, Native people had no right to their own homelands. In 1845 officials passed the final law ending the missions.

The historian Cook estimated that about 310,000 Native people lived in California before 1769, give or take about a tenth. By 1845, by his estimate, their number had dropped by about half, to no more than 150,000. That count includes all Native Californians, not only the people held at the missions.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 18 minutes**

Some Protestants met the fast growth of Catholics and Jews after 1850 with mobs, school laws and state constitutions. Catholics were about one person in twenty in the United States in 1850. By 1906 there were 14 million of them, about one person in six. Catholics answered by building their own schools. Jews started schools to train rabbis, the teachers and leaders of Jewish congregations.

**The end of slavery.** Census takers counted 3,953,760 enslaved people in the United States in 1860. Many were freed as the Union army advanced during the Civil War. In December 1865 enough state lawmakers had approved the Thirteenth Amendment, a change to the Constitution, to make it law. It bans slavery everywhere except as a punishment for a crime. Black members already ran some large churches of their own, and in 1865 their ministers spoke for the freed people of Savannah.

**On the reservations.** United States officials made Native religious ceremonies a crime in 1883, and that ban could be enforced until 1978. Churches also ran many of the government's boarding schools for Native children, with government money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bloody Monday in Louisville, 1855" -->
A political party called the Know-Nothings, or the American Party, grew large in the 1850s. Its members opposed Catholic immigration, and they feared that Catholics were a danger to Protestant faith and to democracy.

On election day, Monday, August 6, 1855, in Louisville, Kentucky, mobs led by Know-Nothing members attacked the German immigrant neighborhoods east of downtown and the Irish neighborhoods to the west. The mobs set fires and robbed homes and businesses.

An armed crowd set out to attack St. Martin of Tours, a German Catholic church, and the mayor, John Barbee, who was himself a Know-Nothing, turned it back. The Kentucky Historical Society's marker counts at least 22 dead and does not say who they were. Five people were later charged with crimes, and none was found guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mountain Meadows, 1857" -->
Members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints killed about 120 people at Mountain Meadows, in Utah Territory, about 35 miles southwest of Cedar City, in September 1857. The victims belonged to the Baker-Fancher wagon train, a company of families mostly from Arkansas on their way to California. Only 17 children, all six years old or younger, were spared.

The killers were local militiamen, part-time soldiers of the territory, who were also church members, and Paiutes joined them. A statement from the church identifies five of the leaders: Isaac Haight, John D. Lee, William Dame, John Higbee and Philip Klingensmith. They held both church posts and militia or town posts. Haight was the regional church leader at Cedar City and second in command of the county militia. Dame was the colonel in command of the southern Utah militia. Higbee was the major in command at the meadows.

**The white flag.** The attack began on September 7, by Paiutes and by Mormon men dressed to look like Native Americans. On September 11, John D. Lee and William Bateman came to the wagon train under a white flag, the sign of a truce. They promised the travelers safe passage if they gave up their guns.

The militiamen walked the men of the wagon train out in a single line, each man beside an armed militiaman. Higbee called out, "Halt! Do your duty!" and each guard shot the man beside him. On the other side of the field, Nephi Johnson ordered the killing of the women and the older children.

**Why.** Douglas Linder of the University of Missouri-Kansas City gives three things behind the killings. Federal troops were marching on Utah that year, in what is called the Utah War. That spring, on May 13, 1857, Hector McLean had shot and stabbed Parley Pratt, a church leader, in Arkansas. Eleanor McLean had left her husband and become Pratt's twelfth plural wife, one of several wives he had at the same time. And in 1856 church leaders had held a preaching campaign called the Reformation.

**Disputed:** whether Brigham Young, the church's president, ordered the killings. In 2007 a church leader, Henry B. Eyring, said that Young's message telling local leaders not to interfere with the travelers arrived too late. Linder writes that Young reportedly said afterward, "It is all right. The only fear I have is from traitors."

**The trials.** Nine men were charged with crimes, and only John D. Lee was found guilty. A jury of church members found him guilty of murder on September 20, 1876. He was executed at Mountain Meadows on March 23, 1877, by a firing squad, a group of men who shoot a condemned person at the same time.

Speaking for the church on September 11, 2007, Eyring expressed "profound regret." He called the killings "a terrible and inexcusable departure from Christian teaching and conduct."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Synagogues, schools for rabbis, and an order to expel Jews" -->
About 2,500 Jews lived in the United States in 1800. By 1860 there were between 125,000 and 200,000, and by 1880 between 230,000 and 300,000, about a hundred times as many as in 1800.

Czar Alexander II, the ruler of Russia, was murdered in 1881, and pogroms followed in Russia. A Library of Congress timeline defines a pogrom as a "massacre often with government collusion," which means a mass killing of Jews, often with the help of government officials. After that, Jews from Eastern Europe came to America in great numbers.

**Schools for rabbis.** American Jews divided in these years into a traditional wing and a Reform wing, which was less traditional, and both wings built schools to train rabbis. Isaac Leeser, a leader of the traditional wing, founded Maimonides College in Philadelphia in 1867, the first school in America to train rabbis.

In 1873 thirty-four congregations, groups of Jews who worship together, formed the Union of American Hebrew Congregations. Its founders hoped it would take in every synagogue, or Jewish house of worship, in the country, and it became the union of Reform congregations instead. Two years later Isaac Mayer Wise, a leader of the Reform wing, founded Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati, which became the Reform wing's school for rabbis.

**The dinner.** The food served in 1883 at a dinner for the first class of rabbis from Hebrew Union College was not kosher, which means it broke Jewish food laws. Traditional guests walked out. The argument that followed led to calls for a more traditional seminary, and in 1887 the Jewish Theological Seminary opened in New York.

**General Orders No. 11.** During the Civil War, on December 17, 1862, General Ulysses S. Grant issued General Orders No. 11. It said that "The Jews, as a class," were "hereby expelled from the department within twenty-four hours," and it accused them of "violating every regulation of trade." The order applied to Jewish civilians in Kentucky, Tennessee and Mississippi, the area under his command.

Eleven days later, Cesar Kaskel and all the other Jews known in Paducah, Kentucky, got papers from the army ordering them to leave within twenty-four hours. Kaskel sent a telegram to President Lincoln and went to Washington. He met Lincoln on January 3, 1863, and Lincoln had General Henry Halleck cancel the order. The historian Jonathan Sarna's account of the order gives no count of the people expelled.

That same year, 1862, Jacob Frankel became the first Jewish chaplain in the United States Army, a religious leader who serves soldiers.

**Turned away at Saratoga.** Henry Hilton, the manager of the Grand Union Hotel in Saratoga, New York, turned away Joseph Seligman, a New York banker, and his family in 1877 because they were Jewish. Hilton blamed a drop in the hotel's business on guests who did not want to stay where Jews were let in.

A Library of Congress timeline describes it as a sign of "the growth of social anti-Semitism in America," anti-Semitism being hatred of Jews. That same year New Hampshire's lawmakers gave Jews the same political rights as other citizens.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ministers who spoke for the freedpeople" -->
Secretary of War Edwin Stanton and General William Sherman met twenty Black men in Charles Green's house in Savannah, Georgia, on January 12, 1865, and asked them what freedom should look like. Thirteen of the twenty were ministers, ordained or licensed, which means their churches had made them ministers or given them permission to preach. The others included deacons, class leaders and other church officers.

The notes of the meeting record how large their churches already were. William J. Campbell, who had been freed by a will, was pastor of the First Baptist Church, with about 1,800 members. John Cox, who had bought his own freedom, led the Second African Baptist Church, with 1,222 members.

Another of the twenty, Jacob Godfrey, had been a class leader and a steward, a man who helped look after the church's business, in the Methodist church since 1836. Until the Union army came, he had been held as property by James E. Godfrey, a Methodist preacher who was serving in the Confederate army.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="garrison-frazier" name="Garrison Frazier" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Garrison Frazier

> **Who:** A Baptist minister who had bought his own freedom, chosen by the other Black church leaders at a meeting to tell the Secretary of War what freedom meant.
> **When and where:** Born in Granville County, North Carolina, about 1798. Savannah, Georgia, January 12, 1865.

**He said his people needed "land, and turn it and till it."**

The twenty church leaders at the Savannah meeting chose Garrison Frazier to answer for all of them, and his answers were written down.

He was 67 years old. Eight years before the meeting he had bought his own freedom and his wife's for $1,000 in gold and silver. He had been an ordained Baptist minister for thirty-five years, but by 1865 his health was so poor that he no longer led a congregation of his own.

**Slavery and freedom.** Asked what slavery was, he said it was "receiving by irresistible power the work of another man, and not by his consent." Asked what freedom was, he said it was "taking us from under the yoke of bondage, and placing us where we could reap the fruit of our own labor."

Asked how his people could best take care of themselves, he answered: "land, and turn it and till it by our own labor." He added: "that is, by the labor of the women and children and old men."

Asked whether Black people would rather live among white people or by themselves, he said: "I would prefer to live by ourselves, for there is a prejudice against us in the South that will take years to get over." Each of the other men was then asked the same question, one at a time, and all of them agreed with him. At one point Sherman left the room, and the men were then asked what they thought of him.

**Forty acres.** Four days after the meeting, Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15. In it he set aside about 400,000 acres of land along the coast, from Charleston to northern Florida, for freed families in plots of forty acres, and about 40,000 people settled there. In the fall of 1865 President Andrew Johnson reversed the order and gave the land back to its former owners.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="garrison-frazier" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Chinese temple in Weaverville, 1874" -->
In Weaverville, in Trinity County, California, a temple of Taoism, a Chinese religion and way of life, burned down, and Chinese men there built a new one to replace it. Work began in February 1874, and the temple was dedicated that April. They named it the Temple of the Forest Beneath the Clouds.

It is the oldest Chinese temple in California that has been used without a break, and people still worship there. In 1956 Moon Lim Lee, a descendant of the men who built it, and his wife, Dorothy, gave it to California State Parks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A second school system" -->
In Philadelphia, Bishop Kenrick had asked the public school board to let Catholic children read their own Bible, and under the board's rule the Douay Bible was still not allowed. So Catholics built parish schools, schools run by a local Catholic church. In 1834 there had been fewer than a dozen parish schools in the whole country, for about half a million Catholics. In the 1850s Bishop John Neumann began building parish schools in Philadelphia.

**Baltimore, 1884.** The Catholic bishops of the United States met at the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore from November 9 to December 7, 1884. They declared that every parish that could afford a school should run one. Their letter to Catholics said that parish schools should be as good as "the best schools of the country." It also said: "A Catholic congregation without a Catholic school is like a family without a mother."

**The Blaine amendment.** Congressman James G. Blaine of Maine, at President Grant's suggestion, proposed an amendment to the Constitution in 1875. Its words said that no money raised by taxes for public schools "shall ever be under the control of any religious sect," a sect being a religious group. Members of the House voted for it. In the Senate it fell four votes short. Lawmakers in thirty-seven states later put their own versions into their state constitutions.

Justice Clarence Thomas of the Supreme Court wrote about the amendment in 2000, in the case *Mitchell v. Helms*, for himself and three other justices. He said it came at "a time of pervasive hostility to the Catholic Church," a time when dislike of Catholics was everywhere. He wrote that "it was an open secret that 'sectarian' was code for 'Catholic,'" an open secret being something many people know but few say out loud.

Blaine ran for president in 1884. A pastor who supported him called the Democrats the party of "rum, Romanism and rebellion," Romanism being an insulting word for the Catholic Church. Blaine did not reject the remark. Lyle Denniston, writing for the National Constitution Center, says the remark and Blaine's amendment cost him many Irish Catholic votes. Blaine lost the election.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Belief and practice: the Reynolds case" -->
George Reynolds, a Latter-day Saint in Utah Territory, married a second wife while his first wife was still living, which was the crime of bigamy. A court found him guilty and sentenced him to two years in prison at hard labor, forced heavy work, and a $500 fine. He argued that his religion required him to have more than one wife. In a case decided in the Supreme Court's 1878 term, *Reynolds v. United States*, the justices ruled against him.

Chief Justice Waite wrote that laws "cannot interfere with mere religious belief and opinions," but "they may with practices." He wrote that excusing a crime because of religious belief would "permit every citizen to become a law unto himself."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hiram Price's rules against ceremonies, 1883" -->
Hiram Price, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued a set of rules on March 30, 1883, for the Court of Indian Offenses, which tried Native people for breaking them. He wrote them after the Secretary of the Interior, Henry M. Teller, sent him a letter on December 2, 1882. In it Teller objected to the work of medicine men, Native healers and religious leaders. He called Native dances "heathenish," his insult for the ways of people who were not Christian.

Price's rules named ceremonies as crimes: "The 'sun-dance,' the 'scalp-dance,' the 'war-dance,' and all other so-called feasts assimilating thereto, shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" The words "assimilating thereto" mean "like those dances." For a first offense, officials took away the person's rations for up to ten days. For a second, they took away rations for fifteen to thirty days, or jailed the person for up to thirty days.

**Food as punishment.** Rations were the food that United States officials had agreed by treaty to give to Native nations whose hunting lands United States officials had taken. So the punishment for dancing was that a family went without food for as long as a month.

Another rule said that "the usual practices of so-called 'medicine-men' shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" A medicine man could be jailed for at least ten days, and kept there until he showed that he had given up his practices. These rules could be enforced for ninety-five years, until 1978, when members of Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act.

**Alaska.** Missionaries worked against Native ceremonies in Alaska too. In 1885 missionaries of the Moravian Church, a Protestant church, arrived at Quinhagak, a Yup'ik village. They called Yup'ik masked dancing "heathen rites" and "idol worship," and they recruited Yup'ik helpers as missionaries. After the missionaries came, no traditional dancing took place in Quinhagak for more than a hundred years, until 2013. The 2015 *Archaeology* magazine report on the village does not say who stopped the dancing or how.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The churches and the boarding schools" -->
Federal officials handed many of the Indian boarding schools, schools where children live as well as study, to churches to run, and they paid the churches to do it. Federal officials used these schools to assimilate Native children, which means to make them give up their own nations' ways of life. In a report published in 2024, investigators at the Department of the Interior counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools. A religious group ran 210 of them, about half.

At least 59 different religious groups received federal money to run or support the schools. That count leaves out the schools that churches ran without any federal money.

**What staff did to the children.** The same federal investigation recorded what staff at the schools did to the children. Staff cut the children's hair, took away their clothes for uniforms, gave them English names and forbade their own languages. Punishments included beatings, solitary confinement, which means being locked up alone, withheld food, and mouths washed out with soap.

At least 973 children died at the schools, and investigators found at least 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 schools. The report states that the true number of deaths is higher.

**Which churches.** The investigators counted 132 schools tied to Protestant churches, 77 to the Catholic Church, and 5 to other groups. Some schools were tied to more than one. The Presbyterians had the most, with 31 schools. The Quakers, the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions, the Methodists and the Episcopalians each had 12 or more.

On the Catholic side, the Jesuits, an order of priests, had 10. Many orders of Catholic nuns are on the list, among them the Sisters of Mercy, the Sisters of St. Joseph, the Ursulines and the Grey Nuns.

**The money.** The payments began long before. In the Civilization Fund Act of March 3, 1819, members of Congress set aside $10,000 a year to teach Native nations "the habits and arts of civilization." Officials often gave that money to religious groups, until members of Congress stopped the yearly payment in 1873. From 1871 to 1969, by the investigators' estimate, members of Congress set aside more than $23.3 billion, in 2023 dollars, for the boarding schools and related programs.

Government records show how much power officials handed to the churches. Each reservation was run from an office called an agency, by a government agent. In 1872 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs wrote that the agencies had been divided among the churches. Each church could put forward the person to be the agent in charge and could "assume charge of the intellectual and moral education of the Indians."

A Senate report of 1969 describes this as "an unprecedented delegation of power by the U.S. Government to church bodies," a handing over of power never done before. The report states that the "military was frequently called in to reinforce the missionaries' orders," which means soldiers were often brought in to back up what the missionaries ordered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance, 1889 and 1890" -->
**Wovoka's vision.** A Northern Paiute man named Wovoka, whose English name was Jack Wilson, had a vision on January 1, 1889, during an eclipse of the sun. By one account, he saw a world made new, with the dead alive again, the buffalo back, the land restored, the settlers gone and everyone living in peace.

A young Arapaho man, Casper Edson, wrote down Wovoka's message for a group of visitors. In it Wovoka said: "You must not hurt anybody or do harm to anyone. You must not fight. Do right always." He said that "the dead are all alive again" and told people to dance every six weeks.

He also warned: "Do not refuse to work for the whites and do not make any trouble with them until you leave them." The dance and its teaching spread across the reservations of the Plains, and the religion became known as the Ghost Dance.

The teaching changed as it spread. At the end of October 1890, the Lakota leader Short Bull told a gathering near Pine Ridge that "the whites were interfering so much in the religious affairs of the Indians" that he would make the great change, the new world of Wovoka's vision, come sooner. In that change, he said, "the white race itself would soon be annihilated," wiped out completely. He told the dancers to keep dancing even if soldiers surrounded them, because "the guns of the soldiers would be rendered harmless."

**The agent's fear.** Daniel F. Royer became the government agent at Pine Ridge in October 1890. On October 12 he reported that more than half of the 6,000 Lakota people at his agency were dancing, and he suggested calling in the army. General Miles told him the excitement would die down. On October 30 Royer wrote that 600 or 700 soldiers were needed.

Royer sent a telegram on November 15 saying "that the Indians were wild and crazy and that at least a thousand soldiers were needed." The Lakota called Royer "Young-man-afraid-of-Indians." Officials in the Indian Office passed his request to the War Department. In the middle of November 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered troops onto the reservations.

**Wounded Knee.** Soldiers killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890, most of them women and children. Chapter 2, Native Nations, tells what happened there.
