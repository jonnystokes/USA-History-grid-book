## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Protestant preachers held revivals that reached almost every corner of the country. A revival is a series of religious meetings meant to bring people to a stronger faith. By 1827 there were so many revivals that one religious magazine stopped reporting them. Its editors wrote that revivals "are becoming too numerous in our country to admit of being generally mentioned in our Record." In 1833 Massachusetts became the last state to stop collecting taxes for churches.

The kind of Protestant faith that spread in these years is called evangelicalism. Evangelical preachers called on each listener to be converted, which means to turn to God and become a true believer. They called this change the "new birth." Converted people started societies to hand out Bibles and religious papers, teach children, and send missionaries. Some of the societies worked to get people to stop drinking, because their members believed a sober person was readier to be converted.

The Library of Congress names the six largest societies of 1826 and 1827. They were the American Education Society, the American Board of Foreign Missions and the American Bible Society. The others were the American Sunday-School Union, the American Tract Society and the American Home Missionary Society.

In the same fifty years, Protestant mobs burned a convent school near Boston and Catholic churches in Philadelphia, and a Missouri governor ordered the members of a new church driven from his state. Methodists and Baptists in the South broke away from their national churches over slavery and formed churches of their own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Camp meetings" -->
In 1800 big revivals began at both ends of the country, in New England and in Kentucky. The Library of Congress calls the New England revival "decorous," which means calm and proper, and the Kentucky revival "exuberant," which means full of wild feeling. In Kentucky, Presbyterian ministers started a new kind of gathering, the camp meeting.

The ministers copied the long outdoor "communion seasons" of the Church of Scotland. Communion is the church service in which believers share bread and wine in memory of Jesus. Families loaded wagons with food and bedding, drove to a field, pitched tents, and stayed for several days of preaching. One camp meeting was held at Cane Ridge, near Lexington, Kentucky, in the summer of 1801. A Presbyterian minister named Barton Stone organized it. In 1832 Stone and Alexander Campbell left the Presbyterians and formed a new church, the Disciples of Christ.

Lorenzo Dow was a traveling Methodist preacher. While he preached, people in his crowds often moved their bodies in strange ways, and people called one of these movements the jerking exercise. The Presbyterians and Baptists who had started camp meetings soon turned against them because of the strong feelings they stirred in people. The Methodists kept them and carried them east, and for decades camp meetings were a Methodist practice.

On August 8, 1809, Benjamin Henry Latrobe drew a plan of a Methodist camp meeting in Fairfax County, Virginia. His drawing puts the men's seats apart from the women's and the tents of Black worshippers apart from the tents of white worshippers. At one side of the camp, the organizers had built a pen of boards filled with straw. Latrobe wrote that "the converted were thrown" into it "that they might kick about without injuring themselves." A Library of Congress exhibit points to this drawing as an example of the separation by race that led Black Methodists, a few years later, to leave and form a Methodist church of their own.

The Shakers had come from England with Mother Ann Lee in 1774. Their full name was the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Coming. They danced in worship, often in circles inside circles, and got their nickname from shaking and whirling during services. In the early 1800s Shakers from New York went to Kentucky to see the revivals, and they gained a small number of converts there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sunday schools taught reading" -->
The first Sunday schools taught poor children to read. Sunday schools began in England in the 1780s. In 1791 a group of pastors and merchants in Philadelphia started the First Day Society. It paid schoolmasters to teach boys and girls, and grown men and women who could not read, to read and write. The Bible was the textbook. Sunday was the day poor working children were not at work, and there were no free public schools yet.

In 1817 a group of Philadelphia businessmen started the Sunday and Adult School Union. They used volunteer teachers instead of paid ones. Within a year they had opened forty-three Sunday schools in the city, with 5,970 pupils. The group grew into the American Sunday School Union. It hired missionaries who started Sunday schools in cities, in the countryside and on Native reservations, and it became a large publisher of children's books. The American Tract Society began in 1825. A tract is a short printed paper about religion. A Library of Congress exhibit gives an estimate that the society handed out 35 million books and tracts in its first ten years. Its papers preached against drinking and gambling as well as for conversion.

In 1838 Rebecca Gratz started the first Hebrew Sunday School, a Sunday school for Jewish children, in Philadelphia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The African Methodist Episcopal Church grows" -->
The African Methodist Episcopal Church, which Richard Allen and other Black Methodists formed in 1816, grew fast. It began with 8 ministers and 5 churches. By 1846 it had 176 ministers, 296 churches and 17,375 members. Women preached in the church from its early years. Many people objected to women preaching, in the A.M.E. Church as in the white churches. An engraving made in Philadelphia in 1844 by P. S. Duval, from a painting by Alfred Hoffy, shows one of these women: Juliann Jane Tillman, a preacher of the A.M.E. Church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The convent burned at Charlestown, 1834" -->
Some Protestants in these years turned to violence against Catholics. In Charlestown, Massachusetts, near Boston, Ursuline nuns ran a convent school from 1819. Nuns are Catholic women who make religious promises and live together in a house called a convent. The Ursulines are an order of nuns who teach. Their students were girls.

Stories about the convent spread in Boston. Rebecca Reed, a Protestant woman, had entered the convent in 1832 and left after less than six months. Her stories about life inside went around before her book, *Six Months in a Convent*, came out in 1835. On July 28, 1834, a nun named Sister Mary John, whose name before she joined the order was Elizabeth Harrison, left the convent. Bishop Fenwick talked her into going back. Rumors spread that she was being held against her will.

On Sunday, August 10, the Protestant minister Lyman Beecher preached against Catholic schools in three Boston churches. The next day a group of Charlestown officials toured the convent, and Sister Mary John guided them. Historians disagree about whether Beecher's sermons led to what happened that night. Ira Leonard argues that they did. Ray Billington points out that the workmen had met before the sermons. Beecher himself said that probably no rioter had heard him.

On the night of August 11 to 12, 1834, a mob of mostly Protestant workmen from Charlestown burned the convent school. The nuns and 47 girl students were inside when the mob came. Only six of the girls were Catholic. The mob also broke open the convent's tomb, where several dead nuns were buried. Firemen stood by and watched. No deaths are recorded. Police arrested 13 men. Juries found 12 of them not guilty, including John R. Buzzell, who admitted he had led the mob. The one man found guilty was 16 years old, and Governor John Davis pardoned him, which means he freed him from his punishment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose Bible? Philadelphia, 1842 to 1844" -->
In 1838 the lawmakers of Pennsylvania passed a law that made the King James Bible, the Bible that Protestants used, a required textbook in the public schools. In Philadelphia, Protestant teachers also led their pupils in Protestant hymns.

In 1842 Bishop Francis Patrick Kenrick, the Catholic bishop of Philadelphia, wrote to the board that ran the city's public schools. He objected to the hymns and to the Bible readings. He asked that Catholic children be allowed to read the Douay Bible, the translation Catholics use, and that they not be made to join in Protestant worship. Early in 1843 the board members agreed that children could be excused from the King James Bible and could use any Bible "without note or comment." The Douay Bible has notes and comments. Under the board's rule, the one Bible the Catholic bishop had asked for was still not allowed.

In February 1844 Hugh Clark, a Catholic school director in the Kensington neighborhood, suggested stopping Bible reading until the board could write a rule both sides accepted. A rumor spread that Clark had ordered a teacher, Miss Bedford, to stop reading the Protestant Bible. Witnesses later showed that the rumor was false. Nativists spread it anyway. Nativists were people who wanted the country kept for Protestants born in America. A newspaper editor named Lewis Levin had founded their group in Philadelphia, the American Republican Association, in December 1843.

On May 3, 1844, the nativists held a rally and marched into Kensington, where Irish Catholic families lived. On May 6 a nativist named George Shiffler was shot dead. By the end of that day a second man, who seems to have been a bystander, had died. Two more nativists were wounded that day and later died. The records do not name who killed any of them. On May 8 nativist crowds burned St. Michael's Church, St. Augustine's Church, a Catholic seminary, which is a school for training priests, and Irish Catholic homes, including Hugh Clark's house.

Fighting started again in July, at the Church of St. Philip de Neri in the Southwark neighborhood. State militia, part-time soldiers called up by the state, fought the crowd with cannon under General George Cadwalader. By the morning of July 7, four militiamen and probably about a dozen rioters were dead, and many more were wounded. The sources give different totals for the two riots. Counts for May run from at least 14 dead to 20, and one count gives at least 100 wounded. Counts for July run from at least 14 dead to about 16.

A grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof for a trial, charged 19 rioters with crimes, and only a few were found guilty. The grand jury blamed the May riots on "the efforts of a portion of the community to exclude the Bible from the public schools." The jurors meant the Catholics who had been attacked. Catholics answered that the grand jury had called no Catholics to testify. After the riots, Catholics decided to build a school system of their own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Latter-day Saints driven out of Missouri" -->
Joseph Smith founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints on April 6, 1830, at a farmhouse near Fayette, in western New York. Its members were often called Mormons. In 1831 their founder, Joseph Smith, said that God had chosen western Missouri as the place where his people would gather. Church members moved there, and their Missouri neighbors drove them out twice.

The first time was in Jackson County in 1833. The older settlers feared that the newcomers would control the county's politics and trade. They objected to the newcomers' religious beliefs. They were also angry at an article in the church newspaper about free Black converts coming to Missouri, a state that allowed slavery. On July 20, 1833, a mob attacked the church's printing office, threw the press out of the window, and scattered the type in the street. They tarred and feathered Edward Partridge, the church's bishop in Missouri, and Charles Allen in the public square. In tarring and feathering, men tore off a person's clothes and smeared hot pine tar on the bare skin, which burned it. Then they covered the person with feathers, which stuck to the tar. The church's account does not describe the injuries the two men suffered. In November 1833 mobs drove the church members out of the county. On November 4 three people were killed, one of them a church member. The church's account does not say who killed them.

In 1836 the Missouri lawmakers created Caldwell County as a place for church members to live. More converts kept arriving and settled beyond its borders. In 1838 violence came from both sides. The Missouri State Archives states that "Old Settler mobs and Mormon paramilitary units roamed the countryside." Paramilitary units are armed groups organized like an army. Some Latter-day Saint men formed a vigilante group called the Danites. Vigilantes are people who act as their own police and punish others outside the law. In the middle of October, Mormon forces raided the towns of Gallatin and Millport and burned property there. Shortly before the end of October, at Crooked River, church members attacked a unit of the state militia that they took for a mob. One Missourian and two church members died. One of the dead was David W. Patten, an apostle, one of the church's highest leaders.

On October 27, 1838, Governor Lilburn Boggs issued an order. It said the Latter-day Saints must be driven from the state or "exterminated," if necessary. To exterminate means to kill every one.

Three days later, on October 30, between about 200 and 240 armed men attacked the small church settlement at Haun's Mill at about 4 in the afternoon. Boggs had not ordered this attack. The Missouri State Archives says it happened "even without official sanction," which means without the governor's approval. The historian Alma Blair writes that the governor's order "was now widely known." Colonel Thomas Jennings of Livingston County commanded the attackers, and Captain Nehemiah Comstock led one of the companies.

Many of the Mormon men took cover in the blacksmith shop, and the attackers shot them through the cracks in its walls. The attackers then hacked some of the wounded men to death with corn knives, long blades used for cutting cornstalks. One of them shot Thomas McBride with McBride's own rifle as he surrendered, and then the attackers hacked him. They killed seventeen or eighteen men and boys. The Missouri State Archives gives eighteen, and Blair and the church's history give seventeen. Among the dead was Sardius Smith, age 10. Charles Merrick, age 9, died of his wounds five weeks later. One woman was wounded. No record shows that anyone was ever tried for the killings at Haun's Mill.

On October 31 General Samuel D. Lucas arrested Joseph Smith and other church leaders. Over the next year about 8,000 church members left Missouri for Illinois. The Missouri State Archives describes them as "often ragged and deprived of their property." Governor Boggs's order was still in force until June 25, 1976, when Governor Bond canceled it.

### Joseph Smith

> **Who:** The founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, whose followers were driven from state to state. A mob killed him in 1844.
> **When and where:** Lived 1805 to 1844. Western New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Nauvoo, Illinois. Killed at Carthage, Illinois, on June 27, 1844.

Joseph Smith founded a new American church in 1830, and a mob killed him fourteen years later. He grew up in a part of western New York that people called the Burned Over District, because so many revivals had passed through it. A Library of Congress exhibit states that his church did not split off from an older church. It describes the church as the only one of its kind.

In 1830 E. B. Grandin of Palmyra, New York, published the Book of Mormon for Smith. Smith said the book came to him through revelations, messages he said came from God, on a set of gold plates written in a language he called reformed Egyptian. He said he had translated it. Eleven men signed statements saying that they had seen and handled the plates. The book tells of Israelites who were led out of Jerusalem to the Americas about 600 years before the birth of Jesus. It also tells of Jesus appearing to their descendants in America. Smith's followers believed his church brought back the earliest form of Christianity. Church members accept the Book of Mormon as scripture, a holy book, alongside the Bible.

The church moved from New York to Ohio, from Ohio to Missouri, and from Missouri to Illinois. In 1839 church leaders bought land on the Mississippi River in Illinois, at a nearly empty town called Commerce. They renamed it Nauvoo in 1840. A large Meskwaki village had stood there, and accounts of how its people lost it differ. The Illinois Department of Natural Resources, which runs the state park at Nauvoo, says the village had 400 to 500 lodges, or homes, and was given up by a treaty in 1824 for 200 sacks of corn. Another account says it was a Sauk and Meskwaki village of up to 1,000 lodges, and that in 1823 or 1824 a man named James White bought it from the Sauk leader Quashquame for some liquor and 2,000 bushels of corn. In that account, Quashquame's people moved across the river.

In Illinois, trouble grew again. Neighbors resented the church members' business dealings and the way they all voted together. Some accused them of theft. They were angry about Smith's plans in politics. News spread that some church leaders had more than one wife, and church members also argued among themselves about it.

Men who opposed Smith started a newspaper in Nauvoo, and Smith ordered its printing press destroyed. He was jailed at Carthage, Illinois, for it. On June 27, 1844, about 100 men broke into the jail and killed Smith and his brother Hyrum. The men came from three units of the Illinois militia: the 59th Regiment, the Warsaw Independent Battalion and the Warsaw Rifle Company. The militiamen guarding the jail barely resisted. Accounts of the killing differ. The Library of Congress states that Smith was "apparently" shot, and that one man in the mob tried to cut off his head and was stopped.

In October 1844 nine men were charged with the murders. Four ran away and were never arrested. Five were tried in May 1845. One was Thomas C. Sharp, the publisher of an anti-Mormon newspaper. The other four were militia officers: Levi Williams, Mark Aldrich, William N. Grover and Jacob C. Davis, who was also a state senator. The jury found all five not guilty. The prosecutor, Josiah Lamborn, said of Davis: "I have no doubt in my own mind, not a particle, that Davis cooperated in the murder, but there is no legal evidence to convict him."

After Smith's death, men attacked Mormon farms around Nauvoo. Brigham Young, who led the church after Smith, planned to move about 15,000 people west. On February 4, 1846, the first wagons crossed the Mississippi into Iowa.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The religion enslavers taught" -->
In the South, enslavers kept enslaved people out of their own church services. Charles Colcock Jones was a Presbyterian minister in Liberty County, Georgia. In 1842 he wrote that enslaved people "were not permitted, on working days, to attend the public administration of the word in company with their masters." That means they could not go to the regular preaching alongside the people who held them. Where they did attend, part of the white church was set apart for them.

Jones wrote a book to urge planters and ministers to have Christianity taught to the people they held in slavery. In *The Religious Instruction of the Negroes in the United States*, finished on July 4, 1842, Jones told ministers which parts of the Bible to use. He chose verses about obedience. One verse says "servants" should "count their masters worthy of all honor." Jones used it to teach enslaved people to obey. He wrote that a minister should teach them "the duties of their station," meaning their duty to obey.

Frederick Douglass, who had been enslaved in Maryland, wrote down what this religion looked like to the people it was used on. In August 1832, Thomas Auld, the man who held Douglass, was converted at a Methodist camp meeting in Talbot County, Maryland. Douglass hoped Auld would free him, or at least treat him better. "I was disappointed in both these respects," he wrote. "I believe him to have been a much worse man after his conversion than before." Douglass wrote that after his conversion, Auld "found religious sanction and support for his slaveholding cruelty." Sanction means approval. Auld became a leader in his church, and visiting preachers stayed at his house. Douglass wrote: "while he starved us, he stuffed them."

At St. Michael's, Maryland, a young white man named Wilson started a Sunday school to teach enslaved people to read the New Testament. They met three times. Then two class leaders, Garrison West and Wright Fairbanks, came with many other men carrying sticks and other things to throw. A class leader led a small group of church members. The men drove the students away and forbade them to meet again.

Douglass saw Auld whip Henny, a young woman whose hands had been burned so badly in a fire when she was a child that she could not use them. Auld tied her up and whipped her bare shoulders with a heavy cowskin, a whip made of cowhide, "causing the warm red blood to drip." To justify it, he quoted the Bible: "He that knoweth his master's will, and doeth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes." He left her tied up for four or five hours at a time. He whipped her before breakfast, went to his store, and came back and whipped her again at dinner "in the places already made raw." At last, in Auld's own words, he "set her adrift to take care of herself." Douglass also named one minister, George Cookman, whom the enslaved people loved and believed was working to free them.

In 1845 Douglass added a note to the end of his book to explain that he was not against religion. He was against what he called "the slaveholding religion of this land." He wrote: "The man who wields the blood-clotted cowskin during the week fills the pulpit on Sunday." He wrote that the man who called it a religious duty to read the Bible denied him "the right of learning to read the name of the God who made me."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The churches split over slavery" -->
Members of two of the largest Protestant churches in the country divided them over slavery more than fifteen years before the Southern states left the Union.

Bishop James Osgood Andrew of Georgia held enslaved people, whom he had gained through his marriage. In 1844 Methodist leaders met for their national meeting. The delegates, the men chosen to speak and vote for their churches, voted to stop Andrew from working as a bishop for as long as he held enslaved people. A few days later the delegates adopted a Plan of Separation. Under the plan, the Methodist conferences in the slave states, the regional groups of churches there, could form a church of their own. In 1845 delegates from those conferences met at Louisville, Kentucky, and created the Methodist Episcopal Church, South.

The Baptists split the same year. Georgia Baptists put forward an enslaver, James E. Reeve, to be a missionary, and Northern Baptists refused to appoint him. From May 8 to 12, 1845, Baptists from the South met at Augusta, Georgia, and formed the Southern Baptist Convention. William Bullein Johnson was its first president.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charles Grandison Finney

> **Who:** A young lawyer who gave up the law after his conversion and became a revival preacher. His book on revivals became a handbook for other revival preachers.
> **When and where:** Born in Warren, Connecticut, August 29, 1792. Upstate New York, then Oberlin, Ohio. Died 1875.

Charles Grandison Finney taught that a person could choose to be converted, and he built new methods of preaching on that idea. He began his own account of his life this way: "I was born in Warren, Litchfield county, Connecticut, August 29, 1792. When I was about two years old, my father removed to Oneida county, New York, which was, at that time, to a great extent, a wilderness." His parents did not belong to a church. He wrote that there were few religious books where he grew up and that he "seldom heard a sermon."

He was training to be a lawyer at Adams, New York, when he was converted in 1821, which means he turned to God and became a believer. The next morning a client came to ask whether he was ready to argue the client's case in court. The client was a deacon, an officer of his church. Finney answered: "Deacon B, I have a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead His cause, and I cannot plead yours." A retainer is money paid to a lawyer to take a case.

He preached across upstate New York. From September 1830 he spent six months in Rochester. He preached every day of the week and three times on Sunday, and he spent most of each day praying with people one at a time. In Rochester he began using what was called the anxious seat. It was a set of seats at the front of the church where people who felt worried about their souls came and sat, in front of everyone. Finney explained that people of the "higher classes" were "too proud to take any position that would reveal them to others as anxious for their souls." He wanted "something that would commit them publicly to the service of Christ."

His methods were called the New Measures. Finney taught that a person could decide to be converted, and his meetings stirred strong feelings in the people who came. Both things angered other ministers. Asahel Nettleton, a New England revival preacher, objected to the changes Finney made to his church's older teachings about how God saves people. Lyman Beecher feared that Finney's meetings let feelings run wild. As early as July 1827, before Rochester, ministers on both sides had met at New Lebanon, New York, to argue about the New Measures. Finney's *Lectures on Revivals*, published in 1835, became a handbook for revival preachers across the country.

In 1835 Oberlin College in Ohio was close to shutting down. Arthur Tappan, a businessman in New York who worked to end slavery, agreed to rescue it on two conditions. Finney had to be made its teacher of theology, the study of religious belief. And the college had to admit Black students on the same terms as white students. Finney took the job and also became minister of the Oberlin church. He was president of the college from 1851 to 1866 and stayed on as minister until 1872. He died in 1875. Finney taught that the sign of God's grace in a person "was a person's benevolence toward others," which means kindness and help given to other people.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The California missions, 1800 to 1848" -->
The California missions were Catholic settlements run by Franciscan friars. Franciscans are members of a Catholic religious order, and their priests and brothers are called friars. The missions went on through these years, and so did the whippings. Missionaries had Native people whipped for running away, stealing, fighting and drinking. They often had soldiers or Native officials give the whippings, and some of them whipped people themselves. Father de la Peña did at Mission Santa Clara.

Flogging is beating a person on the bare back with a whip, which cuts and bruises the skin. A 2023 report on the missions for the state of California says that at Mission Santa Cruz, Father Andrés Quintana used a whip tipped with metal for floggings. In 1812 he had just beaten two men nearly to death with it when a group of Awaswas Ohlone people from the mission killed him. The records name five of them: Lacah, Yaquenonsat, Yachacxi, Ules and Lino. For two years his death was taken to be a natural one, and the records do not say who first called it that. Then a guard overheard an argument, and the plan came out. Spanish officials tried sixteen men and found nine guilty. Seven of the men died at the presidio, the Spanish fort, four of them before they were sentenced. The story of the plan comes from Lorenzo Asisara, an Ohlone man whose father took part in it.

Diseases brought by the Spanish made Native women less able to have children, or made them lose their babies before birth. Some missionaries believed that women did this on purpose. Lorenzo Asisara told what Father Olbes did at Santa Cruz to a married couple who had no children. Olbes demanded to inspect the couple's genitals, the parts of the body used to make babies. When the woman resisted, he had her flogged and made her stand in front of the church for nine days holding a wooden doll. Olbes also had her husband put in shackles, iron rings locked around the legs or wrists, and made him stand in front of the church wearing horns. At Mission San Gabriel, Father Zalvidea punished women whose babies were born dead. He had their heads shaved, made them carry a wooden doll, had them flogged, and kept them in iron ankle cuffs for three months.

Native people fought back. On February 21, 1824, Chumash people rose against the missions at Santa Inés, La Purísima and Santa Bárbara. It was the largest planned revolt in California in the mission years. It began a day early at Santa Inés, after soldiers whipped a Native man from La Purísima who had come to visit a relative in jail. The Chumash attacked the soldiers and burned mission buildings. The next day troops from the Santa Bárbara fort burned the adobe houses where the rebels were camped. Up to nineteen Chumash people and one Mexican soldier died at Santa Inés. At Santa Bárbara the Chumash leader Andrés Sagimomatsee called people to fight. At least two Chumash were killed there and four soldiers were wounded, and more than 1,000 people fled the mission for the inland country of the Yokuts people. At La Purísima, led by Pacomio, the rebels held the mission for almost a month. One Chumash man and four settlers were killed when the rebels took it. In March troops took it back and killed 16 Chumash people. Mexican officials then had seven Chumash men executed for killing the four settlers. They sentenced four leaders to ten years in prison followed by exile, and eight more men to eight years.

In 1828 and 1829, hundreds of Yokuts people left Missions San José and Santa Clara under two mission leaders, Estanislao and Cipriano. They built a fort in the marshes of the San Joaquin River. Mexican officials sent soldiers, Native fighters and armed settlers against them, and these forces defeated them in a bloody battle. The records give no count of the dead and do not name the commander. Estanislao later bargained for a pardon. In 1839 a man named Yozcolo freed the young women who were locked in the women's building at Mission Santa Clara, and fled to the hills. Soldiers defeated him. The records do not say who cut off his head. The Mexican officials there set it outside the mission church for people to see.

Mexicans won their independence from Spain in 1821. Mexican officials then began to take the missions away from the Franciscans and turn them into ordinary parish churches, the local churches of a town. This was called secularization. Governor José Figueroa put it in place in 1833 and 1834. The officials who took over the missions often used punishments like the friars' punishments, including whipping. At most missions, colonists got most of the land and supplies. Figueroa's rules did not recognize the rights of Native people to their own homelands. In 1845 officials passed the final law ending the missions.

The historian Cook estimated that about 310,000 Native people lived in California before 1769, give or take about a tenth. By 1845, by his estimate, their number had dropped by about half, to no more than 150,000. That count includes all Native Californians, not only the people held at the missions.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1850 Catholics were about one person in twenty in the United States. By 1906 there were 14 million of them, about one person in six. The number of Jews grew from between 125,000 and 200,000 in 1860 to between 230,000 and 300,000 in 1880. Some Protestants fought the change with mobs, school laws and state constitutions. Catholics answered by building their own schools, and Jews built synagogues and schools to train rabbis.

The census of 1860 counted 3,953,760 enslaved people in the United States. Many were freed as the Union army advanced during the Civil War, and the Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution ended slavery everywhere in December 1865, except as a punishment for a crime. Black members already ran some large churches of their own. In Savannah, the First Baptist Church had about 1,800 members. In January 1865, when the Secretary of War and General Sherman met Black leaders in Savannah to ask what freedom should mean, most of the men who came were ministers.

On the reservations, United States officials made Native religious ceremonies a crime in 1883. That ban could be enforced until 1978. Churches also ran many of the government's boarding schools for Native children, with government money.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bloody Monday in Louisville, 1855" -->
In the 1850s a political party called the Know-Nothings, or the American Party, grew large. Its members opposed Catholic immigration, and they feared that Catholics were a danger to Protestant faith and to democracy. On election day, Monday, August 6, 1855, in Louisville, Kentucky, mobs led by Know-Nothing members attacked the German immigrant neighborhoods east of downtown and the Irish neighborhoods to the west. The mobs set fires and robbed homes and businesses. An armed crowd set out to attack St. Martin of Tours, a German Catholic church, and the mayor, John Barbee, who was himself a Know-Nothing, turned it back. At least 22 people were killed, according to the Kentucky Historical Society. Five people were later charged with crimes, and none was found guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mountain Meadows, 1857" -->
In September 1857 members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints killed about 120 people at Mountain Meadows, in Utah Territory, about 35 miles southwest of Cedar City. The victims belonged to the Baker-Fancher wagon train, a company of families mostly from Arkansas on their way to California. Only 17 children, all six years old or younger, were spared.

The killers were local militiamen, part-time soldiers of the territory, who were also church members, and Paiutes joined them. The church itself names five of the leaders: Isaac Haight, John D. Lee, William Dame, John Higbee and Philip Klingensmith. They held both church posts and militia or town posts. Haight was the regional church leader at Cedar City and second in command of the county militia. Dame was the colonel in command of the southern Utah militia. Higbee was the major in command at the meadows.

The attack began on September 7, by Paiutes and by Mormon men dressed to look like Native Americans. On September 11, John D. Lee and William Bateman came to the wagon train under a white flag, the sign of a truce. They promised the emigrants safe passage if they gave up their guns. The militiamen walked the emigrant men out in a single line, each man beside an armed militiaman. Higbee called out, "Halt! Do your duty!" and each guard shot the man beside him. On the other side of the field, Nephi Johnson ordered the killing of the women and the older children.

Douglas Linder of the University of Missouri-Kansas City gives three things behind the killings. Federal troops were marching on Utah that year, in what is called the Utah War. That spring, on May 13, 1857, Hector McLean had shot and stabbed Parley Pratt, a church leader, in Arkansas. Eleanor McLean had left her husband and become Pratt's twelfth plural wife, which means one of several wives he had at the same time. And in 1856 the church had held a preaching campaign called the Reformation. Whether Brigham Young, the church's president, ordered the killings is disputed. In 2007 a church leader, Henry B. Eyring, said that Young's message telling local leaders not to interfere with the emigrants arrived too late. Linder writes that Young reportedly said afterward, "It is all right. The only fear I have is from traitors."

Nine men were charged with crimes. Only John D. Lee was found guilty. On September 20, 1876, a jury of church members found him guilty of murder. He was executed at Mountain Meadows on March 23, 1877, by a firing squad, a group of men who shoot a condemned person at the same time. On September 11, 2007, Eyring spoke for the church and said it felt "profound regret." He called the killings "a terrible and inexcusable departure from Christian teaching and conduct."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Synagogues, schools for rabbis, and an order to expel Jews" -->
About 2,500 Jews lived in the United States in 1800. By 1860 there were between 125,000 and 200,000, and by 1880 between 230,000 and 300,000. In 1881 Czar Alexander II, the ruler of Russia, was murdered, and pogroms followed in Russia. The Library of Congress defines a pogrom as a "massacre often with government collusion," which means a mass killing of Jews in which government officials secretly took part. After that, Jews from Eastern Europe came to America in great numbers.

A rabbi is a teacher and leader of a Jewish congregation, and a synagogue is a Jewish house of worship. Isaac Leeser founded Maimonides College in Philadelphia in 1867, the first school in America to train rabbis. Six years later, thirty-four congregations joined to form the Union of American Hebrew Congregations, and in 1875 Isaac Mayer Wise founded Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati. Etz Chaim, the first yeshiva in the United States, opened in New York in 1886. A yeshiva is a school for studying the Talmud, the collection of Jewish law and teaching. The Jewish Theological Seminary opened in New York the next year, and from 1897 the Rabbi Isaac Elchanan Theological Seminary trained Orthodox rabbis, who keep to traditional Jewish law.

American Jews also divided into a traditional wing and a Reform wing, which was less traditional. The Union's founders hoped it would take in every synagogue in the country, and it became the union of Reform congregations instead. Wise became a leader of the Reform wing, and Leeser of the traditional wing. In 1883, at a dinner for the first class of rabbis from Hebrew Union College, the food was not kosher, which means it broke Jewish food laws. Traditional guests walked out. The argument that followed led to calls for a more traditional seminary. In 1885 the Pittsburgh Platform set out the beliefs of American Reform Judaism.

On December 17, 1862, during the Civil War, General Ulysses S. Grant issued General Orders No. 11. It said: "The Jews, as a class violating every regulation of trade established by the Treasury Department and also department orders, are hereby expelled from the department within twenty-four hours from the receipt of this order." The order applied to Jewish civilians in Kentucky, Tennessee and Mississippi, the area under his command. The order reached Paducah, Kentucky, eleven days later. Cesar Kaskel and all the other Jews known in the town got papers from the army ordering them to leave within twenty-four hours. Kaskel sent a telegram to President Lincoln and went to Washington. On January 3, 1863, he met Lincoln, and Lincoln had General Henry Halleck cancel the order. No record gives a count of the people expelled. That same year, 1862, Jacob Frankel became the first Jewish chaplain in the United States Army. A chaplain is a religious leader who serves soldiers.

In 1877 Henry Hilton, the manager of the Grand Union Hotel in Saratoga, New York, turned away Joseph Seligman, a New York banker, and his family because they were Jewish. Hilton blamed a drop in the hotel's business on guests who did not want to stay where Jews were let in. The Library of Congress marks it as a sign of "the growth of social anti-Semitism in America." Anti-Semitism is hatred of Jews. That same year New Hampshire's lawmakers gave Jews the same political rights as other citizens.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ministers who spoke for the freedpeople" -->
On January 12, 1865, in Charles Green's house in Savannah, Georgia, Secretary of War Edwin Stanton and General William Sherman met twenty Black men. The two officials asked them what freedom should look like. Thirteen of the twenty were ministers, ordained or licensed, which means their churches had made them ministers or given them permission to preach. The others were deacons, class leaders and other church officers. A deacon is an officer who helps run a church, and a class leader led a small group of members.

The notes of the meeting record each man's age, where he was born, and how he became free. They show how large their churches already were. William J. Campbell, 51, had been freed by a will in 1849. He was pastor of the First Baptist Church, with about 1,800 members and church property worth $18,000. John Cox, 58, had bought his own freedom in 1849 for $1,100, and he led the Second African Baptist Church, with 1,222 members. James Porter, 39, was born free because his mother had bought his freedom. He was a lay reader, a church member who leads prayers, at St. Stephen's Protestant Episcopal Colored Church, with about 200 members.

Jacob Godfrey, 57, had been a class leader and steward in the Methodist church since 1836. A steward helped look after a church's business. Until the Union army came, he had been held as property by James E. Godfrey, a Methodist preacher who was serving in the Confederate army.

### Garrison Frazier

> **Who:** A Baptist minister who had bought his own freedom, chosen by twenty Black church leaders to tell the Secretary of War what freedom meant.
> **When and where:** Born in Granville County, North Carolina, about 1798. Savannah, Georgia, January 12, 1865.

Garrison Frazier answered for twenty Black church leaders when Secretary of War Edwin Stanton and General William Sherman asked them what freedom meant. At the meeting in Savannah on January 12, 1865, the men chose him to speak for all of them, and the answers he gave were written down.

He was 67 years old and had been born in Granville County, North Carolina. Eight years before the meeting he had bought his own freedom and his wife's for $1,000 in gold and silver. He had been an ordained Baptist minister for thirty-five years. By 1865 his health was so poor that he no longer led a congregation of his own.

Asked what slavery was, he said it was "receiving by irresistible power the work of another man, and not by his consent." Asked what freedom was, he said it was "taking us from under the yoke of bondage, and placing us where we could reap the fruit of our own labor."

Asked how his people could best take care of themselves, he answered: "land, and turn it and till it by our own labor." He added: "that is, by the labor of the women and children and old men."

Asked whether Black people would rather live among white people or by themselves, he said: "I would prefer to live by ourselves, for there is a prejudice against us in the South that will take years to get over." Each of the other men was then asked the same question, one at a time. All of them agreed with him. At one point Sherman left the room, and the men were then asked what they thought of him.

Four days after the meeting, Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15. In it he set aside about 400,000 acres of land along the coast, from Charleston to northern Florida, for freed families in plots of forty acres, and about 40,000 people settled there. In the fall of 1865 President Andrew Johnson reversed the order and gave the land back to its former owners.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Chinese temple in Weaverville, 1874" -->
In Weaverville, in Trinity County, California, a Chinese Taoist temple burned down, and Chinese men there built a new one to replace it. Taoism is a Chinese religion and way of life. Work began in February 1874, and the temple was dedicated that April. They named it the Temple of the Forest Beneath the Clouds. It is the oldest Chinese temple in California that has been used without a break, and people still worship there. In 1956 Moon Lim Lee, a descendant of the men who built it, and his wife, Dorothy, gave it to California State Parks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A second school system" -->
In Philadelphia, Bishop Kenrick had asked the public school board to let Catholic children read their own Bible, and the board's rule still kept it out. So Catholics built schools of their own. A parish school is a school run by a local Catholic church. In 1834 there had been fewer than a dozen parish schools in the whole country, for about half a million Catholics. In the 1850s Bishop John Neumann began building parish schools in Philadelphia.

From November 9 to December 7, 1884, the Catholic bishops of the United States met at the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore. They declared that every parish that could afford a school should run one. Their letter to Catholics said that parish schools should be as good as "the best schools of the country." It also said: "A Catholic congregation without a Catholic school is like a family without a mother."

In 1875 Congressman James G. Blaine of Maine, at President Grant's suggestion, proposed an amendment to the Constitution. Its words said that no money raised by taxes for public schools "shall ever be under the control of any religious sect." A sect is a religious group. Members of the House voted for it. In the Senate it fell four votes short. Lawmakers in thirty-seven states later put their own versions into their state constitutions. In 2000 Justice Clarence Thomas of the Supreme Court, writing for himself and three other justices in *Mitchell v. Helms*, said the amendment came at "a time of pervasive hostility to the Catholic Church," a time when dislike of Catholics was everywhere. He wrote that "it was an open secret that 'sectarian' was code for 'Catholic.'" In other words, everyone knew that "sectarian" schools meant Catholic schools.

Blaine ran for president in 1884. A pastor who supported him called the Democrats the party of "rum, Romanism and rebellion." Romanism was an insulting word for the Catholic Church. Blaine did not reject the remark. Lyle Denniston, writing for the National Constitution Center, says the remark and Blaine's amendment cost him many Irish Catholic votes. Blaine lost the election.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Belief and practice: the Reynolds case" -->
George Reynolds, a Latter-day Saint in Utah Territory, married a second wife while his first wife was still living. That is called bigamy, and it was a crime. A court found him guilty and sentenced him to two years in prison at hard labor, which means forced heavy work, and a $500 fine. He argued that his religion required him to have more than one wife. In a case decided in the Supreme Court's 1878 term, *Reynolds v. United States*, the justices ruled against him. Chief Justice Waite wrote that laws "cannot interfere with mere religious belief and opinions," but "they may with practices." He wrote that excusing a crime because of religious belief would "permit every citizen to become a law unto himself."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hiram Price's rules against ceremonies, 1883" -->
On March 30, 1883, Hiram Price, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued a set of rules for the Court of Indian Offenses, which tried Native people for breaking them. He wrote them after the Secretary of the Interior, Henry M. Teller, sent him a letter on December 2, 1882. In it Teller called Native dances "heathenish" and objected to the work of medicine men. A medicine man was a Native healer and religious leader. Teller used "heathenish" as an insult for the ways of people who were not Christian.

Price's rules named ceremonies as crimes: "The 'sun-dance,' the 'scalp-dance,' the 'war-dance,' and all other so-called feasts assimilating thereto, shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" The words "assimilating thereto" mean "like those dances." For a first offense, officials took away the person's rations for up to ten days. For a second, they took away rations for fifteen to thirty days, or jailed the person for up to thirty days.

Rations were the food that United States officials had agreed by treaty to give to Native nations whose hunting lands the United States had taken. So the punishment for dancing was that a family went without food for as long as a month.

Another rule said that "the usual practices of so-called 'medicine-men' shall be considered 'Indian offenses.'" A medicine man could be jailed for at least ten days, and kept there until he showed that he had given up his practices. These rules could be enforced for ninety-five years, until 1978, when members of Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act.

Missionaries worked against Native ceremonies in Alaska too. In 1885 missionaries of the Moravian Church, a Protestant church, arrived at Quinhagak, a Yup'ik village. They called Yup'ik masked dancing "heathen rites" and "idol worship," and they recruited Yup'ik helpers as missionaries. No traditional dancing took place in Quinhagak for more than a hundred years, until 2013.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The churches and the boarding schools" -->
Federal officials handed many of the Indian boarding schools to churches to run, and they paid the churches to do it. A boarding school is a school where children live as well as study. Federal officials used these schools to assimilate Native children, which means to make them give up their own nations' ways of life. In a report published in 2024, investigators at the Department of the Interior counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools. A religious group ran 210 of them. At least 59 different religious groups received federal money to run or support the schools. That count leaves out the schools that churches ran without any federal money.

The same federal investigation recorded what staff at the schools did to the children. Staff cut the children's hair, took away their clothes for uniforms, gave them English names and forbade their own languages. Punishments included beatings, solitary confinement, which means being locked up alone, withheld food, and mouths washed out with soap. At least 973 children died at the schools, and investigators found at least 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 schools. The report states that the true number of deaths is higher.

The investigators counted 132 schools tied to Protestant churches, 77 to the Catholic Church, and 5 to other groups. Some schools were tied to more than one. The Presbyterians had the most, with 31 schools. The American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions had 20, the Methodists 15, the Episcopalians 12, and the Quakers 21 under two different names. The Quakers are a Protestant group also called the Religious Society of Friends. The Jesuits, an order of Catholic priests, had 10. Many orders of Catholic nuns are on the list, among them the Sisters of Mercy, the Sisters of St. Joseph, the Ursulines and the Grey Nuns.

The payments began long before. In the Civilization Fund Act of March 3, 1819, members of Congress set aside $10,000 a year to teach Native nations "the habits and arts of civilization." Officials often gave that money to religious groups, until members of Congress stopped the yearly payment in 1873. From 1871 to 1969, by the investigators' estimate, members of Congress set aside more than $23.3 billion, in 2023 dollars, for the boarding schools and related programs.

Government records show how much power officials handed to the churches. Each reservation was run from an office called an agency, by a government agent. In 1872 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs wrote that the agencies had been divided among the churches. Each church could name the agent in charge and could "assume charge of the intellectual and moral education of the Indians." A Senate report of 1969 called this "an unprecedented delegation of power by the U.S. Government to church bodies." Unprecedented means never done before. The report states that the "military was frequently called in to reinforce the missionaries' orders," which means soldiers were often brought in to back up what the missionaries ordered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance, 1889 and 1890" -->
On January 1, 1889, during an eclipse of the sun, a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka had a vision. His English name was Jack Wilson. A young Arapaho man, Casper Edson, wrote down Wovoka's message for a group of visitors. It said: "You must not hurt anybody or do harm to anyone. You must not fight. Do right always." It said that "the dead are all alive again." It said: "Do not refuse to work for the whites and do not make any trouble with them until you leave them." It told people to dance every six weeks. The dance and its teaching spread across the reservations of the Plains. The religion became known as the Ghost Dance.

The teaching changed as it spread. At the end of October 1890, the Lakota leader Short Bull told a gathering near Pine Ridge that "the whites were interfering so much in the religious affairs of the Indians" that the great change would come sooner. He said the dancers must keep dancing even if soldiers surrounded them, because "the guns of the soldiers would be rendered harmless and the white race itself would soon be annihilated." Annihilated means wiped out completely.

Daniel F. Royer became the government agent at Pine Ridge in October 1890. On October 12 he reported that more than half of the 6,000 Lakota people at his agency were dancing, and he suggested calling in the army. General Miles told him the excitement would die down. On October 30 Royer wrote that 600 or 700 soldiers were needed. On November 15 he sent a telegram saying "that the Indians were wild and crazy and that at least a thousand soldiers were needed." The Lakota called Royer "Young-man-afraid-of-Indians." Officials in the Indian Office passed his request to the War Department. In the middle of November 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered troops onto the reservations. On December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee, soldiers killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children.
