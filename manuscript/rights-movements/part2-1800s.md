## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, many groups of Americans organized to change rules that held them back. Women formed antislavery societies, gathered signatures on petitions to Congress and spoke to crowds. Ministers, lawmakers and mobs attacked them for doing it. Some of those women then turned the same methods to their own rights. In July 1848, more than 300 people met at Seneca Falls, New York. One hundred of them signed a declaration that listed the wrongs men had done to women, and the people at the meeting voted to demand the vote. Black families in Boston campaigned to get their children into the city's schools. In 1817 teachers at a new school in Hartford, Connecticut, began teaching deaf children together in sign language.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaf children taught together, 1817" -->
The American School for the Deaf opened at Hartford, Connecticut, on 15 April 1817, in a building called Bennett's City Hotel. Seven pupils came full time in the first classes. Teachers taught them in sign language, using a method from France.

Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet founded the school with Laurent Clerc, a deaf French teacher whom Gallaudet brought back from Paris. A Hartford doctor, Mason Fitch Cogswell, and ten other Hartford men backed them. Cogswell's deaf daughter, Alice Cogswell, was one of the first pupils.

Public money came early. In October 1816, before the school opened, Connecticut's lawmakers voted the school $5,000. An essay by John Crowley for the Disability History Museum calls that grant the first public money for educating Americans with disabilities. In 1819 members of Congress voted to give the school 23,000 acres of public land in Alabama, after a motion by Henry Clay. It was land the United States had taken from Native nations. The surviving accounts do not say which nation's land it had been. The school's leaders sold the land to build up an endowment, a sum of money kept and invested so that its earnings pay for the school's work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Girls ask for the same lessons as boys" -->
Some of the first organized demands for women between 1800 and 1850 were about schooling. Girls' schools usually taught manners and fine arts. A few women opened schools that taught girls the subjects boys learned.

In 1819 Emma Willard wrote *A Plan for Improving Female Education* and addressed it to New York's lawmakers. She argued that young women needed the same subjects young men were taught. With the support of Governor DeWitt Clinton, she opened the Troy Female Seminary in Troy, New York, in 1821. A seminary was a school for older girls or young women.

Catharine Beecher and her sister Mary opened the Hartford Female Seminary in Connecticut on 20 May 1823. Their pupils studied geography, world history, Latin, rhetoric (the art of speaking and writing well), philosophy and the natural sciences. Within three years the school had 100 full-time pupils.

Mary Lyon began raising money in 1834 for a school for women. She opened Mount Holyoke Female Seminary at South Hadley, Massachusetts, in 1837. Gifts ran from six cents to $1,000. Women's sewing circles sent quilts and bedding, and the school's trustees, the men in charge of its money and buildings, gave stoves and furniture. Tuition was $60 a year. A history page by Mount Holyoke College says Lyon "endured ridicule from those who felt her ambitious undertaking would be 'wasted' on women." It adds that "The idea of a college for women was horrifying to many people in the 1830s."

A college degree for women came in two steps, at Oberlin in Ohio. Oberlin stands on land that the college's own art museum calls "unceded ancestral lands" of the Erie, Wyandotte, Mingo and other Native nations. By "unceded" the museum means that, in its view, those nations never agreed to give the land up.

Women studied beside men at Oberlin from its opening in 1833, but in a Ladies Course that ended with a diploma, not a degree. In 1837 four women entered the course that led to a bachelor's degree: Mary Kellogg, Mary Caroline Rudd, Mary Hosford and Elizabeth Prall. Rudd, Hosford and Prall graduated in 1841. Kellogg ran out of money, left, and came back later. Oberlin was the first college to give bachelor's degrees to women in classes with men.

In 1835 Oberlin also became the first college with a rule of admitting Black students.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston's Black families and the city's schools" -->
Boston's Black families opened the African School in 1798, in Primus Hall's house. In 1808 they moved it into the basement of the African Meeting House. In the mid-1830s it moved into the Abiel Smith School. The Smith School became the segregated school where most of Boston's Black pupils were taught. Segregated means kept apart by race. The men of Boston's Primary School Committee kept separate schools for Black and white children.

In the late 1840s William Cooper Nell and others organized a boycott of the Smith School, which means families kept their children out of it in protest. They also gathered signatures on petitions against it. A father, Benjamin Roberts, asked the Primary School Committee four times to let his five-year-old daughter, Sarah, into the school nearest their home. To reach the Smith School she had to walk past five schools for white children. The committee refused each time, and in 1848 he sued the city of Boston.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women petition Congress, 1829 to 1830" -->
A petition is a written request to people in power, signed by the people who are asking. Women had no vote, and petitions were the way they first spoke to Congress about national questions. The House of Representatives' own historian says women first petitioned on political issues in 1829 and 1830.

The question was Indian removal, the plan to force the Cherokee and other Native nations off their land. On 31 December 1829, Catharine Beecher wrote a *Circular Addressed to Benevolent Ladies of the United States*. "It may be, that female petitioners can lawfully be heard, even by the highest rulers of our land," she wrote. More than 1,500 women signed petitions to Congress against removal. On 15 February 1830, women of Steubenville, Ohio, called themselves "the feeblest of the feeble" in theirs.

Members of Congress mocked the women in public. Senator Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri told "these ladies" to "sit down on the way side, and wait for the coming of the conquerors." In 1830 a majority in Congress voted for the Indian Removal Act.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maria W. Stewart speaks in Boston, 1832" -->
In September 1832, Maria W. Stewart, a free Black woman, spoke at Franklin Hall in Boston to an audience that included men. A National Park Service page calls it one of the first recorded times an American woman of any race spoke in public.

Stewart was born in 1803 in Hartford, Connecticut. In her own words she "was left an orphan at five years of age" and "was bound out in a clergyman's family," meaning she was sent to work as a servant in a minister's house. In Boston she and her husband, James W. Stewart, lived among the city's free Black families. In 1831 William Lloyd Garrison printed her essay "Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality" in his antislavery newspaper, *The Liberator*. In her speeches she asked, "How long shall the fair daughters of Africa be compelled to bury their minds and talents beneath a load of iron pots and kettles?" She urged her listeners, "Sue for your rights and privileges."

Audiences turned against her. She scolded Black men in public for drinking too much, and some people objected that it was improper for a woman to speak in front of men. On 21 September 1833 she gave a farewell speech. She asked, "What if I am a woman," and said it was "no use for me as an individual, to try to make myself useful among my color in this city." In 1834 she moved to New York and taught school. She later worked as matron, the woman in charge of housekeeping and care, at the Freedmen's Hospital in Washington, where she died on 17 December 1879.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Prudence Crandall and Sarah Harris

> **Who:** A young Black woman who asked to be taught, and the white teacher who was arrested, tried and attacked for teaching her and other Black girls.
> **When and where:** Canterbury, Connecticut, 1832 to 1834.

In 1832 Prudence Crandall let a young Black woman, Sarah Harris, into her school for girls in Canterbury, Connecticut. For the next two years the men of the town and the state fought to close the school, and in the end armed men attacked it.

Sarah Harris had finished primary school. Her father handed out *The Liberator*, Garrison's antislavery newspaper. She wanted enough schooling to teach Black children in the town of Norwich. She told Crandall, "I want to get a little more learning, if possible enough to teach colored children." She added, "If you think it will be the means of injuring you, I will not insist on the favor."

Crandall admitted her. The white families took their daughters out of the school.

In the spring of 1833 Crandall went to Boston to see Garrison. Then she traveled around New England asking Black families to send their daughters. Her advertisements said the school would reopen on 1 April 1833 for "young ladies and little misses of color." About 24 pupils came, and most of them boarded in the house.

The man who led the fight against the school was Andrew T. Judson, a lawyer and politician who lived next door. He told the minister Samuel J. May, "we mean there shall not be such a school set up anywhere in our State." He also said, "They are an inferior race of beings, and never can or ought to be recognized as the equals of whites."

On 24 May 1833 the lawmakers of the Connecticut General Assembly passed what people called the Black Law. Under it, a school in the state that took Black pupils from outside Connecticut without the town's permission was breaking the law. They wrote it to close Crandall's school.

Connecticut officials arrested Crandall on 27 June 1833, and she spent a night in jail. Judson led the case against her. At her first trial the jury could not agree. At the second, in October 1833, Judge David Daggett told the jury that Black Americans were not citizens, so the protections her lawyers pointed to did not cover her pupils. The jury found her guilty. On 26 July 1834 the judges of the Connecticut Supreme Court threw out the guilty verdict on a technical point. The judges did not rule on the law itself, so the Black Law still stood.

Canterbury's town officials went after the pupils too. Ann Eliza Hammond was a 16-year-old pupil from Providence, Rhode Island, who arrived in April 1833. The town officials used the state's vagrancy law against her, a law against people with no home or work in the town. Its punishment was whipping "on the naked body not exceeding ten stripes," which means up to ten lashes of a whip on bare skin. Crandall paid so that no one whipped her.

Shopkeepers would not sell to the school, and people threw stones and eggs at the pupils. Someone hung a dead cat at the school door. In January 1834 a fire set by an unknown person damaged the building.

On the night of 9 September 1834, a mob of armed men broke the windows of the house and went through it with weapons while Crandall and her pupils were inside. A National Park Service page counts more than ninety windows broken. Another account says five large windows on the ground floor. No one was ever charged, and no record names the attackers. Crandall closed the school the next morning.

Crandall married a minister of the Baptist church, Calvin Philleo, and moved west to Illinois and then Kansas. In 1886 Connecticut's lawmakers voted her a pension of $400 a year. She died in Kansas in 1890, aged 87. Sarah Harris became an abolitionist, a person working to end slavery, and a conductor on the Underground Railroad, helping people escape from slavery. In 1877 she traveled to Kansas to visit Crandall, and the two women wrote to each other about their old pupils.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="prudence-crandall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs attack the organizers, 1835 to 1838" -->
Between 1835 and 1838, white mobs in Boston, Alton and Philadelphia attacked people who organized against slavery. The mobs broke up meetings, destroyed printing presses, burned a hall and killed a newspaper editor. No one was convicted for any of the three attacks.

On 21 October 1835, a mob of about 5,000 men surrounded a meeting of the Boston Female Anti-Slavery Society at its room on Washington Street, in Boston. Maria Weston Chapman, her sisters Caroline, Anne and Deborah Weston, and eight other women had founded the society in 1834. Deborah Weston called it "the day when 5,000 men mobbed 45 women." A newspaper of the time, the *Hampshire Gazette*, described the crowd as men "calling themselves 'respectable.'"

When Mayor Theodore Lyman asked the women to leave, Maria Weston Chapman answered, "If this is the last bulwark of freedom, we may as well die here as anywhere." The women walked out through the hissing crowd and finished their meeting at the Chapman house. The mob caught William Lloyd Garrison and dragged him through the streets at the end of a rope. Mayor Lyman and other men took him to the Leverett Street Jail and kept him there overnight so the mob could not reach him. No record names a man in the mob or shows that anyone was charged.

On 7 November 1837, in Alton, Illinois, a mob shot and killed Elijah Lovejoy, a Presbyterian minister who printed an antislavery newspaper. After threats to hurt him, he had moved from St. Louis to Alton, 25 miles away, for safety. Mobs in Alton smashed three of his printing presses. On the night of 7 November, Lovejoy and a group of men guarded a fourth press in a warehouse. About 20 or 30 men of the town surrounded the building. Men in the armed mob shot Lovejoy five times as he stood in an open doorway. He died within the hour, two days before his 35th birthday.

In January 1838 Judge William Martin and a jury in Alton's city court tried the men who had defended the press, for riot. A riot is a violent disturbance by a crowd. The jury found the warehouse owner, Winthrop Gilman, not guilty in about fifteen minutes, and the prosecutor dropped the charges against the other eleven defenders. Then eleven men of the mob, among them John Solomon, Levi Palmer and James M. Rock, were tried for riot for breaking in and destroying the press. On 20 January 1838 the jury found them not guilty. No one was charged with killing Lovejoy, and the court record does not name who fired the shots.

On 17 May 1838, in Philadelphia, a mob burned down Pennsylvania Hall, a new meeting hall where antislavery groups met. The Pennsylvania Hall Association had raised about $40,000 to build it, and the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society raised much of that money. The hall opened on 14 May 1838. That week the Anti-Slavery Convention of American Women met in it. Three days after it opened, the mob set the hall on fire, using its own gas lights. Between 10,000 and 15,000 people watched, and 200 to 300 of them took part in the attack.

Mayor John Swift made what one history of the city calls "half-hearted efforts" to clear the crowd. Sheriff John G. Watmough arrested about a dozen men that night, and the crowd took them away from him. In all, officials arrested dozens of men and investigated five, and no court found any of them guilty. The same history records the rioters only as "dock workers and white rioters," and names none of them. In 1847 the judges of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court ordered the county to pay $27,942.27 for the damage.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Sarah and Angelina Grimké

> **Who:** Two sisters from a South Carolina slaveholding family who spoke against slavery to crowds of men and women, and were condemned from the pulpit for speaking at all.
> **When and where:** Charleston, then Philadelphia and New England. Their speaking tour ran in 1837 and 1838.

Sarah and Angelina Grimké were the first women to work as agents, or organizers, for the American Anti-Slavery Society. In 1837 Congregational ministers in Massachusetts condemned them because they were women speaking in public, and the sisters answered with arguments for the rights of women.

Sarah was born on 26 November 1792 and Angelina on 20 February 1805. Their father, John Faucheraud Grimké, was a judge and cotton planter in Charleston, South Carolina, who held people in slavery. Both sisters became Quakers, members of a Christian group that taught that men and women were equal before God. Angelina left Charleston for Philadelphia in November 1829. In 1835 the sisters joined the American Anti-Slavery Society.

In July 1835 a mob in Charleston broke into the post office and burned antislavery papers. Angelina wrote a letter to William Lloyd Garrison about it, and he printed it in *The Liberator*. After that the sisters were not welcome in South Carolina.

At Poughkeepsie, New York, they spoke to a crowd of men and women together. In 23 weeks across 1837 and 1838 they spoke at 88 or more meetings in 67 towns, to more than 40,000 people.

In July 1837 the Congregational ministers of Massachusetts issued a Pastoral Letter, a message from church leaders to the members of their churches, and ministers read it aloud from their pulpits. The letter states that women like the sisters put "the female character" in danger of "wide spread and permanent injury." It criticizes "females" who take "an obtrusive and ostentatious part in measures of reform." Obtrusive and ostentatious mean pushing forward to be seen. The ministers attacked the sisters for being women who spoke in public.

Sarah Grimké answered the same month. The first of her "Letters on the Equality of the Sexes" ran in the *New England Spectator* in July 1837. At a convention of antislavery women in 1837, Angelina proposed that "the right of petition is natural and inalienable," meaning a right that nobody can take away.

At that time the House of Representatives had a rule, which its members adopted in 1836, that set aside every antislavery petition without reading it. People called it the gag rule. On 14 February 1838 women of Brookline, Massachusetts, petitioned the House to end it. They called the rule "an assumption of authority, at once dangerous and destructive to the fundamental principles of republican government, to the rights of minorities, to the sovereignty of the People." The first two names on the petition were Sarah M. Grimké and Angelina E. Grimké. Members of the House voted to renew the gag rule each time a new Congress met, every two years, until 1844.

On 21 February 1838, Angelina Grimké went to the Massachusetts State House in Boston. She gave a committee of the state's lawmakers antislavery petitions signed by about 20,000 Massachusetts women. She spoke to the committee in front of an overflowing crowd. The House of Representatives' historian calls her "the first woman in the United States to speak before a legislative body." She told the lawmakers, "I stand before you as a southerner, exiled from the land of my birth by the sound of the lash and the piteous cry of the slave." She also said, "We are citizens of this republic and as such our honor, happiness, and well-being are bound up in its politics, government, and laws."

Both sisters lived to see the end of slavery in the United States. Sarah died in 1873 and Angelina in 1879, before American women won the vote.

### Dorothea Dix

> **Who:** A Massachusetts woman who visited every jail and poorhouse in her state, wrote down what was being done to people with mental illness, and handed the record to the state's lawmakers.
> **When and where:** Massachusetts, 1841 to 1843. Born 1802, died 1887.

Dorothea Dix went and looked at how Massachusetts towns were treating people with mental illness. Then she wrote down what she saw and put it in front of the state's lawmakers. The word used then was *insane*. It meant a person whose mind worked in ways that their family or their town could not manage.

On 28 March 1841 Dix went to the jail at East Cambridge, Massachusetts, to teach a Sunday school class. She found women with mental illness locked in with convicted criminals, in "a bare, foul-smelling, unheated jail room." The jail officials denied her account. Then they improved the women's conditions.

Between 1841 and 1843 Dix visited every jail, poorhouse and house of correction in Massachusetts. A poorhouse was a building where a town kept people who had no money. A house of correction was a kind of jail for people found guilty of small crimes. She wrote down what she saw in each one. In January 1843 she gave the Massachusetts lawmakers a memorial, a formal written statement, of what she had found.

"I come as the advocate of the helpless, forgotten, insane men and women held in cages, closets, cellars, stalls, pens!" she wrote. "Chained, naked, beaten with rods, and lashed into obedience!" A stall and a pen are enclosures built for animals. A rod is a stick used for beating, and lashed means whipped. Her notes named places. At Lincoln she found a woman in a cage. At Medford she found one person "chained, and one in a close stall for seventeen years."

Printers reprinted the memorial as a pamphlet, and newspapers printed parts of it. The lawmakers voted money to make the state hospital at Worcester bigger. In 1843 the United States had 13 hospitals for people with mental illness. By 1880 it had 123, and Dix had helped found 32 of them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dorothea-dix" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coverture begins to break, 1837 to 1848" -->
Coverture was the old English rule, still the law in every state, that a married woman had no legal existence apart from her husband. She did not control property, even what she brought into the marriage. The first law in any state that let a married woman own property in her own name passed in Mississippi in 1839. It came out of a court case about a Chickasaw woman, and the property in that case was an enslaved man.

Betsy Love was Chickasaw, through her mother, Sally Colbert. She had inherited an enslaved man named Toney from her father, Thomas Love, and had given him to her daughter, Susan. Betsy Love's husband owed money to a man named John Fisher. Fisher went to court to have Toney sold at auction to pay the husband's debt. In 1837 the judges of the Mississippi Supreme Court ruled that Betsy Love was the legal owner, and that her husband had no rights over her property. They gave three reasons. Chickasaw families trace their family lines and pass on property through the mother. Since 1830, contracts made under Chickasaw law, including marriage contracts, had counted as binding in Mississippi. The third, in the words of the National Park Service's account, was that "Property in the Chickasaw Nation was owned by the women of the tribe."

In 1839 Mississippi's lawmakers passed the Married Women's Property Act. A married woman could now own property she got by gift, will, purchase or inheritance, as long as it did not come from her husband.

New York's lawmakers passed their own Married Women's Property Act in 1848, months before the Seneca Falls convention. Under it, the property a woman owned when she married stayed hers. Her husband could not sell it, and the people he owed money to could not take it to pay his debts. Lawmakers in other states copied New York's law. It ended one part of coverture, the part about property a wife brought into a marriage. The rest of coverture stayed the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sojourner Truth takes her name, 1843" -->
The woman born Isabella Bomfree in Ulster County, New York, in 1797 changed her name in 1843. She said the Spirit had called her to preach the truth, and she named herself Sojourner Truth. A sojourner is a traveler who stays in a place for a while. She traveled through Long Island, Connecticut and Massachusetts, speaking and singing.

In 1844 she joined the Northampton Association of Education and Industry in Massachusetts. Abolitionists had founded it as a community. Its members supported women's rights and religious tolerance and opposed war, and the community was a stop on the Underground Railroad. It broke up in 1850. Her formal lecture tour began the next year, in 1851.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott

> **Who:** Two women who were refused seats as delegates at an antislavery meeting in London because they were women, and who called the first women's rights convention eight years later.
> **When and where:** London, 1840. Waterloo and Seneca Falls, New York, July 1848.

In July 1848, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott led the first women's rights convention in the United States, at Seneca Falls, New York. The people at the meeting voted to demand that women be allowed to vote. James Gordon Bennett, publisher of the *New York Herald*, printed their declaration in his paper to make fun of it.

Lucretia Mott was born on Nantucket, Massachusetts, in 1793. She was raised a Quaker and went to a Quaker boarding school called Nine Partners. In 1833 she helped found the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society. Elizabeth Cady Stanton was born at Johnstown, New York, on 12 November 1815.

The two women met in London in 1840, at the World Anti-Slavery Convention. American antislavery groups had elected women as delegates. The women "were refused admission because of their sex." After an argument, the men at the convention decided that the women "would sit at the rear of the hall, but not participate." Stanton was in London on her honeymoon. She found Mott in the women's section, and the two agreed to hold a women's rights convention when they got home.

Stanton moved to Seneca Falls in 1847. The town is on Cayuga land. In 1789 the Cayuga Nation kept a 64,000-acre reservation there by a treaty, a written agreement, with New York. New York officials bought almost all of it in 1795 and the last three square miles in 1807. In 2017 a federal judge in western New York ruled that Congress had never approved those purchases, as a law of 1790 required.

Stanton later wrote about what pushed her to act: "The general discontent I felt with women's portion as wife, mother, housekeeper ... impressed me with a strange feeling that some active measures should be taken."

On 9 July 1848 five women met for tea at Waterloo, New York, and decided to hold the convention. They were Stanton, Mott, Mary Ann M'Clintock, Martha Coffin Wright and Jane Hunt. They put a notice in the local paper calling people to "a Convention to discuss the social, civil and religious rights and condition of woman."

The convention, a large organized meeting, met on 19 and 20 July 1848 at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, three miles east of Waterloo. More than 300 people came. The first day was meant for women only. Men took part on the second day. Lucretia Mott's husband, James Mott, chaired the convention, which means he ran the meeting. Mary M'Clintock was the secretary.

Stanton wrote a Declaration of Sentiments with the M'Clintock family. She modeled it on the Declaration of Independence. It listed, one by one, the wrongs men had done to women. "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise," meaning the right to vote. "He has taken from her all right in property, even to the wages she earns." That was coverture, still the law in 1848. "He has denied her the facilities for obtaining a thorough education," it went on, "all colleges being closed against her."

The people at the convention voted on a set of resolutions, written statements of what they wanted. Eleven passed with no one against them. The resolution demanding the vote met opposition and passed only narrowly. Frederick Douglass, who had escaped from slavery in Maryland ten years before and now published an antislavery newspaper, the *North Star*, stood with Stanton and argued for it. One hundred people signed the Declaration of Sentiments, 68 women and 32 men. A Library of Congress exhibition counts seventy-two years of organized campaigning for women's votes from that resolution to the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.

The signers had written that they expected "misconception, misrepresentation, and ridicule," and it came. Bennett printed the whole declaration in the *Herald*. Some signers later took their names off after people criticized them harshly for signing. The records do not say how many.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans who were denied rights learned, in the second half of the 1800s, to plan court cases on purpose. They chose a law to challenge, raised money, hired a lawyer and found a person willing to be arrested or refused. Susan B. Anthony voted in 1872, the Tape family sued a school board in 1884, and a committee in New Orleans arranged an arrest on a train in 1892. The judge at Anthony's trial told the jury to find her guilty. The Tapes won in court, and school officials still kept their daughter out of the white school. Homer Plessy, the man the committee chose, lost in the Supreme Court.

After the Civil War, members of Congress and state lawmakers added three amendments to the Constitution. The 13th bans slavery, the 14th states that people born in the country are citizens, and the 15th bars states from denying the vote because of race. In 1869 the leaders of the women's movement split over whether Black men or women should get the vote first. Women first voted under territorial law in Wyoming and Utah. Members of Congress voted for laws that shut Chinese immigrants out, and Chinese Americans fought those laws in court. In Memphis, the newspaper editor Ida B. Wells began counting the people killed by lynch mobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sojourner-truth" name="Sojourner Truth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sojourner Truth

> **Who:** A woman born into slavery in New York who spoke in public for more than thirty years for the rights of Black people and of women.
> **When and where:** Akron, Ohio, May 1851. Washington, D.C., 1864. Died at Battle Creek, Michigan, 26 November 1883.

Sojourner Truth spoke for women's rights and Black Americans' rights from 1851 on. Her most famous speech survives in two versions, and the famous one was written by Frances Dana Gage twelve years after the speech.

In 1850 she published *The Narrative of Sojourner Truth: a Northern Slave* and bought a house in Northampton, Massachusetts. She paid for part of it by selling photographs of herself. On them she printed the words "I sell the shadow to support the substance."

On 28 May 1851 she spoke at the Ohio Women's Rights Convention in the Old Stone Church at Akron, Ohio. She had not been invited. (One account gives the date as 29 May. The convention ran for two days.)

Marius Robinson, one of the convention's secretaries, printed a report of her speech in the *Anti-Slavery Bugle* on 21 June 1851, about three weeks later. Truth was staying with Robinson and his wife, Emily, at the time. A National Park Service page says it is believed he went over the text with her. His version begins, "May I say a few words? I want to say a few words about this matter. I am a woman's rights. I have as much muscle as any man, and can do as much work as any man. I have plowed and reaped and husked and chopped and mowed, and can any man do more than that?" Later she said, "You need not be afraid to give us our rights for fear we will take too much, for we can't take more than our pint will hold." She ended, "But man is in a tight place, the poor slave is on him, woman is coming on him, and he is surely between a hawk and a buzzard." Robinson wrote that it was "impossible to transfer it to paper, or convey any adequate idea of the effect it produced upon the audience."

The famous version, with the question "And ain't I a woman?" repeated through it, was written by Frances Dana Gage, who had run the convention. Gage published it in 1863, twelve years after the speech, and wrote it from memory while she was in South Carolina during the war. She did not check it with Truth. She called it "but a faint sketch of her speech." Gage's version gives Truth a Southern way of speaking.

Sojourner Truth grew up in Ulster County, New York, nearly 100 miles north of New York City. She spoke only Dutch until she was nine, and she was never enslaved in the South. Gage's version also has her say she bore thirteen children. She had five. A National Women's History Museum exhibit says Gage "fabricated" her version, which means she made it up. Robinson's version is the one written down that summer.

After 1857 Truth lived in Michigan. During the Civil War she recruited Black men for the Union Army, among them her grandson James Caldwell, who joined the 54th Massachusetts regiment, an Army unit, in April 1863. In 1863 officials in Indiana arrested her under a state law that barred Black people from entering the state, and held her for ten days.

In 1864 she worked in Washington, D.C., for the National Freedmen's Relief Association, which helped people who had just been freed from slavery. She rode the city's streetcars to force the streetcar companies to carry Black passengers. When a streetcar conductor tried to stop her from riding by force, she had him arrested and won her case. On 29 October 1864 she met President Abraham Lincoln at the White House.

From 1870 she campaigned for the federal government to give land in the West to people freed from slavery. The campaign failed. In 1872 she went to the polls in Battle Creek, Michigan, to vote, and election officials turned her away. That same year, Susan B. Anthony voted in Rochester, New York, and was arrested for it.

Sojourner Truth died at Battle Creek on 26 November 1883, about 86 years old. She believed she was 105.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts lawmakers outlaw segregated schools, 1855" -->
In Boston, William Cooper Nell and other Black Bostonians kept up their boycott of the segregated Smith School and their petition drives. In 1787 Prince Hall and other Black Bostonians had asked the state's lawmakers for schools for their children. In 1855, 68 years after that first petition, Massachusetts lawmakers voted to outlaw segregated public schools. A National Park Service page records that race-based segregation policies continued in Boston's public schools for decades afterward, even though state and federal laws forbade them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A wife's wages, New York, 1860" -->
Until 1860, a married woman in New York did not own the money she earned. Under coverture, her wages belonged to her husband. Women's rights campaigners in New York organized to change that. They carried petitions to every county in the state. They held conventions in 40 of its 60 counties and gave talks in 150 towns. Frances D. Gage, Antoinette Brown Blackwell and Susan B. Anthony were among the speakers.

Anthony gave a draft bill, a proposed law, to a state senator, Andrew J. Colvin, and he brought it to the state's lawmakers. State senators passed it on 28 February 1860, and members of the assembly on 20 March. Governor Edwin D. Morgan signed it. One newspaper of the time printed the date of passage as 30 March. The law states that "the earnings of any married woman, from her trade, business, labor or services, shall be her sole and separate property, and may be used or invested by her in her own name."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three amendments, and the violence around them" -->
Members of Congress and state lawmakers added three amendments to the Constitution after the Civil War. An amendment becomes part of the Constitution when enough states ratify it, which means approve it. The 13th, ratified on 6 December 1865, bans slavery. The 14th, part of the Constitution from 9 July 1868, states that "All persons born or naturalized in the United States" are citizens. Naturalized means made a citizen after coming from another country. It also says no state may "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws," meaning a state must apply its laws the same way to everyone. The 15th, ratified on 3 February 1870, states that the right to vote cannot be denied "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Servitude means slavery.

In these same years white men attacked Black Americans who were living as free people. In May 1866, mobs that included Memphis police officers, firemen and businessmen killed 46 Black residents of the city. By the count of a committee of Congress, the mobs also burned 91 homes, 4 churches and 8 schools and raped five women. Rape means forcing a person into sex. On 13 April 1873, at Colfax, Louisiana, more than 150 armed white men led by Christopher Columbus Nash killed between 62 and 153 Black men. In April 1877 President Rutherford B. Hayes ordered federal soldiers out of the Louisiana state house, the last state house in the South that they guarded. Black Americans went on organizing after 1877, and white men went on attacking them.

In 1875 members of Congress voted for a Civil Rights Act. Under it, no one could lawfully deny anyone "the full and equal enjoyment of any of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters and other places of public amusement." Public conveyances are trains, boats and coaches for hire. In 1883 the justices of the Supreme Court struck the law down. They held that the 14th Amendment did not let Congress stop private individuals from treating people unfairly because of race.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The women's movement splits, 1869" -->
In 1869 the people who had worked together against slavery split over who should get the vote next. The 14th Amendment was the first place the Constitution used the word "male." Under its second section, the number of seats a state got in Congress depended on the number of its "male inhabitants" who could vote. The 15th Amendment, which members of Congress passed on 26 February 1869, bars denying the vote because of race. It says nothing about sex.

The break came on 12 May 1869, at the third annual meeting of the American Equal Rights Association. People working to end slavery and women's rights campaigners had formed that group together in 1866. At the 1866 meeting, the Black campaigner Frances Ellen Watkins Harper had told the white women there, "You white women speak here of rights. I speak of wrongs."

At the 1869 meeting, Frederick Douglass argued that Black men needed the vote first. "I do not see how any one can pretend that there is the same urgency in giving the ballot to woman as to the negro," he said. "With us, the question is a matter of life and death, at least, in fifteen States of the Union." He described Black families "in danger of having their homes burnt down over their heads," and children "not allowed to enter schools."

Susan B. Anthony answered, "If you will not give the whole loaf of suffrage to the entire people, give it to the most intelligent first." Suffrage means the right to vote. Elizabeth Cady Stanton said, "If intelligence, justice, and morality are to have precedence in the government, let the question of women brought up first and that of the negro last." A National Park Service page records that Stanton used racist language in this fight. She called Black Americans "ignorant of the laws and customs" of the country. Stanton and Anthony argued that educated white women should vote before Black men. Their words angered Black campaigners, among them Douglass and Harper.

Two rival groups formed in 1869. Stanton and Anthony started the National Woman Suffrage Association, which worked for a change to the Constitution. Lucy Stone, Henry Blackwell and Julia Ward Howe started the American Woman Suffrage Association, which worked state by state and supported the 15th Amendment. Members of the two groups stayed apart until 1890, when they joined as the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Two daughters of the founders, Alice Stone Blackwell and Harriot Stanton Blatch, helped bring them together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women vote first in the West, 1869 and 1870" -->
The lawmakers of Wyoming Territory passed the first law in United States history that openly gave women the vote, and they had mixed reasons for it. William H. Bright, a Democrat from South Pass City, brought the bill to the territory's first group of lawmakers. Governor John Allen Campbell approved it on 10 December 1869. Under the act, women could vote and hold office. A National Park Service page records that some of the lawmakers also wanted to draw more women to a territory that had very few.

South Pass City was a gold-mining camp that grew up after gold was found in 1867. It stood in country that an 1863 treaty had recognized as the Eastern Shoshone's, a homeland of about 44 million acres. White prospectors, men searching for gold, had been slipping into that country since the 1850s, in spite of the treaties. In the 1868 treaty of Fort Bridger, United States officials left the Shoshone only a reservation of about 3.2 million acres on the Wind River.

The lawmakers of Utah Territory passed a women's vote law two months later, and the acting governor signed it on 12 February 1870. Two days after that, on 14 February 1870, a Salt Lake City schoolteacher named Seraph Young voted in the city election. She was the first woman in the United States to vote under a law that gave women the vote on the same terms as men.

On 17 February 1870, Esther Hobart Morris was appointed justice of the peace at South Pass City, a local judge for small cases. She was the first woman in the country to hold that office. In the spring of 1870, women in Laramie were called to serve on juries, the first women in the country to do so. Later, Wyoming officials read the jury rule to mean "male citizens over 21 years of age," and that kept women off Wyoming juries until 1949. In Wyoming, Louisa Swain of Laramie voted on the morning of 6 September 1870.

In the Edmunds-Tucker Act of 1887, members of Congress voted to take Utah women's vote away, as part of a federal campaign against the Mormon church. Utah women got the vote back in the state constitution when Utah became a state on 4 January 1896. Wyoming kept women's vote when it became a state in 1890, so Wyoming was the first state where women could vote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Susan B. Anthony

> **Who:** One of fifteen women who voted in Rochester, New York, in 1872, and the only one put on trial for it.
> **When and where:** Rochester, New York, 5 November 1872. Trial at Canandaigua, New York, 17 to 19 June 1873.

Susan B. Anthony voted in 1872 to test whether the Constitution gave women the vote. She was arrested and tried, and the judge told the jury to find her guilty.

In 1871 the leaders of the National Woman Suffrage Association had decided that women should try to register to vote, be refused, and then sue in federal court. Their argument was that the 14th Amendment made voting one of the rights of every citizen. Anthony went to register in Rochester, and the officials let her. On election day she went back, and the officials let her vote.

On 5 November 1872, in the first district of Rochester's Eighth Ward, Anthony and fourteen other women voted in the national election. A poll watcher, a man watching the voting for one party, named Sylvester Lewis challenged her. The three election inspectors did what New York law required. They put her under oath and asked whether she was a citizen, whether she lived in the district and whether anyone had paid her. Her answers satisfied them, and they put her ballots in the boxes.

Nine days later, a federal official, Commissioner William Storrs, issued warrants, papers ordering an arrest, for all fifteen women. On 18 November 1872 a deputy marshal, a federal officer who makes arrests, came to Anthony's house. She told the story many times. "'What for?' I asked. 'To arrest you,' he said. 'Is that the way you arrest men?' 'No.' Then I demanded that I should be arrested properly." He took her downtown on the streetcar, and the government paid her fare.

All fifteen women were arrested, and only Anthony was tried. The charge stated that she had voted "without having a lawful right to vote," being "then and there a person of the female sex." At a hearing on 29 November 1872, the assistant prosecutor, John Pound, asked the election inspectors, "Was Miss Anthony dressed in the apparel of a woman and had she the appearance of a woman?" Her lawyer objected. The inspectors answering that question had been arrested themselves for letting the women vote.

Federal courts at that time did not let a person on trial for a crime speak to the jury. So in March and April 1873 Anthony spoke to the people the jurors would come from. She gave a lecture, "Is It a Crime for a U.S. Citizen to Vote?", in twenty-nine villages and towns of Monroe County. The federal prosecutor, Richard Crowley, then had the trial moved to Ontario County. Anthony scheduled a speech in every village there too.

The trial opened at Canandaigua on 17 June 1873, before a jury of twelve men. The judge was Ward Hunt, a justice of the Supreme Court who was also hearing cases in that part of New York. When the prosecution finished, Hunt read out an opinion he had already written. He said voting was not one of the rights the 14th Amendment protected, and that Anthony had known New York let only men vote. Then he told the jury to bring in a verdict of guilty. Her lawyer, Henry Selden, asked for each juror to be polled, which means asked one by one for his own verdict. The clerk refused. The jury never talked the case over.

On 19 June Selden asked for a new trial, because Hunt had taken the decision away from the jury. Hunt refused. Then he asked the question a court asks before it sets a punishment. Three reports of what Anthony said next survive, and none comes from the court's own record of the trial. The words below are from the version she published herself.

Hunt asked, "Has the prisoner anything to say why sentence shall not be pronounced?" Anthony answered, "Yes, your honor, I have many things to say, for in your ordered verdict of guilty, you have trampled under foot every vital principle of our government." She went on, "Robbed of the fundamental privilege of citizenship, I am degraded from the status of a citizen to that of a subject."

"The Court cannot allow the prisoner to go on," Hunt said. She kept going. "May it please the Court to remember that since the day of my arrest last November, this is the first time that either myself or any person of my disfranchised class has been allowed a word of defense before judge or jury." Disfranchised means denied the vote. "The prisoner must sit down," said Hunt. "The Court cannot allow it."

She said that every man who had judged her was her "political superior," whether "native or foreign born, white or black, rich or poor, educated or ignorant, awake or asleep, sober or drunk." Hunt answered, "The Court must insist." He said she had been tried "according to the established forms of law." She replied, "Yes, your honor, but by forms of law all made by men, interpreted by men, administered by men, in favor of men, and against women."

Hunt fined her $100 and the costs of the trial. "May it please your honor, I shall never pay a dollar of your unjust penalty," she said. Hunt answered, "Madam, the Court will not order you committed until the fine is paid." If he had jailed her for not paying, she could have appealed to the Supreme Court. In July 1873 the deputy marshal sent to collect reported that he could "find no goods or chattles land or tenements" to take. Chattels are belongings, and tenements are buildings. Federal officials dropped the matter, and she never paid.

The three election inspectors were tried after her, found guilty and fined $25 each. They refused to pay too, and on 25 February 1874 the marshal put them in jail. Anthony sent telegrams to Representative Benjamin Butler and Senator Aaron Sargent. On 3 March 1874 President Ulysses S. Grant pardoned the inspectors, which means he canceled their punishment. The same day, the voters of the Eighth Ward elected all three to their posts again.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Comstock Act, 1873" -->
On 3 March 1873 members of Congress voted for "An Act for the Suppression of, trade in, and circulation of obscene literature and articles of immoral use," known as the Comstock Act. Under it, sending certain things by mail became a federal crime. They included anatomy books, some novels, photographs of boxing matches and information about contraception, which means ways to prevent pregnancy. Under the act, it was a crime to mail a woman the information she needed about her own body.

Obscene means indecent or shocking, but the act never said what counted as obscene. Anthony Comstock had pushed members of Congress to pass it, and he was appointed a special agent of the Post Office. In practice, he decided what the word meant. In court he often refused to let jurors see the item he had seized, saying it was too vile to show them. He claimed that over his life he seized 150 tons of books, made 4,000 arrests and drove 15 people to suicide.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Harvard doctor says school will harm girls, 1873" -->
In 1873 Edward H. Clarke, a professor at Harvard Medical School, published a book that said studying would damage girls' bodies. The book was *Sex in Education*, with the subtitle *A Fair Chance for the Girls*. Clarke argued that a girl's body spends the teenage years building the organs used to carry a pregnancy. Studying the way boys studied, he said, would take the energy that work needed. "Unless the work is accomplished at that period, unless the reproductive mechanism is built and put in good working order at that time, it will never be perfectly accomplished afterwards," he wrote. He warned that the result would be "monstrous brains and puny bodies."

A Smith College history page lists the harms Clarke said would follow. They were atrophy of the uterus and ovaries, which means those organs shrinking and stopping working, and masculinization, which means a girl's body taking on a man's traits. The list also named sterility, which means being unable to have children, and insanity and death. He said women should study no more than four hours a day, and not at all during their monthly bleeding. He described a patient he called Miss A: "She graduated at nineteen, the first scholar, and an invalid." An invalid is a person too sick to live a normal life.

Leaders of the new women's colleges answered him. Smith College opened in 1875, and its first president, Laurenus Clark Seelye, said, "It is to preserve her womanliness that this College has been founded." Smith's leaders built supervised houses for its students in answer to worries like Clarke's.

Mary Putnam Jacobi answered with evidence. She had earned a pharmacy degree, a degree in preparing medicines, in 1863, the first woman in the country to do so. She earned a medical degree in 1864 and a second medical degree in Paris in 1871. Jacobi sent out a thousand printed lists of questions through women's rights groups and collected answers from hundreds of women, rich and poor, in towns and on farms. Her essay *The Question of Rest During Menstruation* won Harvard's Boylston Prize in 1876, and she was the first woman to win it. She found no evidence that monthly bleeding made women unfit to study or work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Supreme Court says citizens do not have a right to vote" -->
Virginia Minor, a woman in St. Louis, Missouri, used the same argument Anthony had used. The registrar, Reese Happersett, refused to register her to vote, and she sued him. She argued in court that she was a citizen and that citizens have the right to vote.

The Supreme Court justices ruled against her, all of them, in *Minor v. Happersett*, decided in the court term that began in October 1874. They held that "the Constitution of the United States does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one," and that a state could give the vote to men only. Women were citizens, but in the justices' reading, being a citizen did not include a right to vote. After that ruling, the only way left to win the vote everywhere at once was a new amendment to the Constitution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Getting into college, and into court as a lawyer" -->
Colleges for women opened in these years. Matthew Vassar founded Vassar College at Poughkeepsie, New York, in 1861, and it opened on 26 September 1865 with 353 students. Smith, founded in 1871, opened in the fall of 1875 with 14 students. Pauline and Henry Fowle Durant founded Wellesley in 1870, and it opened on 8 September 1875 with 314 students. Bryn Mawr, a Quaker college for women, opened in September 1885.

Spelman began on 11 April 1881 in the basement of Friendship Baptist Church in Atlanta, Georgia. Two New England teachers, Sophia B. Packard and Harriet E. Giles, founded it with help from the church's pastor, Frank Quarles. Its first class was eleven students: ten women, some of them formerly enslaved, and one girl.

Some state universities opened to women too, though admitting women and letting them finish a degree often came years apart. A University of Iowa history page says women were admitted from its founding, and "on an equal basis with men" from 1860. In February 1870 Madelon Stockwell enrolled at the University of Michigan, the first woman there since it opened in 1817. The first woman to enter Cornell, Jennie Spencer of Cortland, came in 1870. She had won a state scholarship, and she left because there was nowhere near the campus for a woman to live. Women entered Cornell regularly from 1872.

Rebecca Lee Crumpler received the degree "Doctress of Medicine" from the New England Female Medical College on 1 March 1864. She was the first African American woman in the United States to earn a medical degree. After the war she moved to Richmond, Virginia, and worked with the Freedmen's Bureau, the federal office that helped people freed from slavery. She treated patients who had no other doctor.

Women who studied law could pass the examination and still be refused a license. Arabella Mansfield passed the Iowa bar examination on 15 June 1869 and became the first woman admitted to a state bar. The bar is the body of lawyers allowed to practice in a court. Iowa's law described "any white male citizen" as eligible, and she argued that nothing in it forbade a woman. Charlotte E. Ray graduated from Howard University School of Law in 1872 and was admitted to the District of Columbia bar, the first Black woman lawyer in the country. A page by the District of Columbia attorney general's office says she later had to close her law office because, as a woman of color, she could not get enough clients.

Myra Bradwell passed the Illinois bar examination in 1869. The judges of the Illinois Supreme Court refused her a license because she was married. She took her case to the Supreme Court of the United States and lost, eight to one, in *Bradwell v. The State*, in the term that began in October 1872. Justice Samuel F. Miller wrote for the Court that each state decides who may practice law, and that the 14th Amendment does not protect that right. Justice Joseph Bradley wrote separately, "The paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother. This is the law of the Creator." He also wrote, "A married woman is incapable, without her husband's consent, of making contracts which shall be binding on her or him." That sentence states the rule of coverture.

In 1876 the justices of the Supreme Court refused to let Belva Lockwood argue cases before them. They said "none but men are permitted to practice before [us] as attorneys and counselors." Lockwood lobbied Congress, which means she pressed its members in person, until they voted for "An Act to relieve certain legal disabilities of women." On 3 March 1879 she became the first woman admitted to the bar of the Supreme Court.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Laws that shut Chinese immigrants out, 1875 and 1882" -->
While Americans argued about who should be added to the voters, members of Congress voted for laws to keep Chinese immigrants out. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the Page Act on 3 March 1875. A National Park Service page calls it the first federal law to restrict immigration. Under it, agents at the ports could stop and question "any subject of China, Japan, or any Oriental country" to find out whether the person had come "without their free and voluntary consent." Under it, no one could bring in women "for the purposes of prostitution," which means to be paid for sex, and officials decided which women those were. The officers applied it to Asian women in general, and in practice almost no Chinese women could enter the country.

President Chester A. Arthur signed the Chinese Exclusion Act on 6 May 1882. Under it, Chinese laborers could not enter the country for ten years. Section 14 states that "no State court or court of the United States shall admit Chinese to citizenship." Members of Congress extended the law in 1892, made it permanent in 1902 and repealed it, which means canceled it, in 1943. Britannica calls it "the first and only major federal legislation to explicitly suspend immigration for a specific nationality."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Frances Thompson arrested for living as a woman, 1876" -->
On 10 July 1876, Memphis police arrested a Black woman named Frances Thompson for wearing women's clothing. Writers today, among them reporters at CNN, describe her as a transgender woman, meaning a woman whose body was counted as male at birth. She told police and reporters she had lived her whole life as a woman.

Ten years earlier, in 1866, Thompson had told a committee of Congress that seven white men had raped her and another woman, Lucy Smith, during the Memphis massacre.

After the 1876 arrest, four doctors, among them Dr. Joseph Nuttall, a doctor working for the city, examined her body against her will at the central police station on Adams Street and declared her a man. The newspaper reports do not say how they examined her. The city's recorder, an official who judged small cases, fined her $50. She could not pay, so the recorder sentenced her to 100 days on the city chain gang, a group of prisoners chained together and made to work outdoors. She cleaned the streets for 50 cents a day toward her fine. Crowds gathered to stare at her. Officials made her pose for photographs in men's and women's clothing, and newspapers printed them. After harassment from other prisoners and the public, city officials took her off the chain gang and held her at the police station for the rest of her sentence.

Memphis newspapers printed the doctors' finding. Writers for several of the papers used it to try to show that her 1866 testimony about the rape was false. Her neighbors later found her sick and took her to the city hospital. She died there of dysentery, a disease of the gut, on 1 November 1876.

### Sarah Winnemucca

> **Who:** A Northern Paiute woman who worked as an interpreter for the United States government and the Army, saw what the government did to her people, and spent the rest of her life saying so in public.
> **When and where:** Nevada and Oregon, 1844 to 1891. The removal to the Yakama Reservation, 1878 to 1879.

Sarah Winnemucca worked for the United States government until President Rutherford B. Hayes had her people forced off their reservation in the winter of 1878. After that she campaigned for their return in speeches, in a petition to Congress and in a book.

Her Paiute name was Thocmetony, which means Shell Flower. She was born in 1844 in what later became Nevada. She was the daughter of Chief Winnemucca and the granddaughter of Chief Truckee. Both men had guided white travelers across Paiute country.

A National Park Service page lists what she saw growing up: "the murder of two uncles, the attempted rape of her sister, the Pyramid Lake War and Bannock War, and the burning of Paiute food supplies."

She spoke several languages. In the 1870s she worked as an interpreter for the Indian Office and as a go-between for the Paiute and the United States Army. Part of that work was persuading Paiute bands to move onto reservations. During the Bannock War of 1878, she found her father's band, who were being held by Bannock fighters, and led them out to the Army's protection. She traveled 230 miles in three days.

Army soldiers then removed Paiute people who had not fought in the war at all. The House of Representatives' historian writes that the Paiute were punished for the part a small number of them had taken in the fighting. Winnemucca wrote that "just a little before Christmas" in 1878 she learned that President Hayes had ordered it. "My people were only given one week to get ready in," she wrote.

Soldiers took them off the Malheur Reservation, which lay inside their own homeland, and sent them 350 miles north to the Yakama Reservation, the Yakama Nation's land. About 550 Paiute men, women and children made the journey. It took nearly a month, through snow and over two mountain ranges. The women and children rode in wagons. Some of the men walked in leg irons, metal rings locked around the ankles and chained together. The records do not say which soldiers or officials put the irons on them. Five children, one woman and an old man died on the road. Their bodies were left beside it, because the ground was too frozen to dig graves.

The historian Sally Zanjani estimated that more than a fifth of the group died at Yakama afterward, most of them from hunger and disease. W. V. Rinehart, the government agent at Malheur, wrote a report in August 1879. In it he complained about what the removal had cost. He also wrote that officials had sent the Paiute off without enough supplies, even though supplies were on the way.

After the removal, Winnemucca stopped working as an interpreter and became a campaigner. In the early 1880s she gave more than 300 speeches. In 1880 she met President Hayes and the Secretary of the Interior, Carl Schurz, in Washington. Hayes did not change his decision. Schurz gave her a written promise that her people could go back to Malheur, and then he did not keep it. In 1882 and 1883, President Chester Arthur opened the Malheur land to white settlers. In 1884 she petitioned Congress to give Malheur back to her people. She asked the government to "sweep away the [Indian] agency system" and "give us homes to live in, for God's sake and for humanity's sake."

In 1883 she published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims*, with help from the reformers Elizabeth Peabody and Mary Peabody Mann. A page by the Oregon Historical Society calls it the first autobiography published by a Native American woman.

She also started a school for Paiute children and taught them in Paiute and in English. It closed after three years, when the money ran out. In the same years, United States officials were opening boarding schools, where Native children lived far from their families. An Army officer, Richard Henry Pratt, opened the first of them, Carlisle, in Pennsylvania in 1879. Staff at these schools cut the children's hair, gave them English names and forbade their languages, and punished children who spoke them with beatings, solitary confinement and withheld food. A federal count published in 2024 found that at least 973 children died at the schools.

Sarah Winnemucca died in 1891. Her statue stands in the United States Capitol for the state of Nevada. It shows her at 35, holding up a shell flower in her right hand, with a book at her side.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standing Bear goes to court, 1879" -->
In 1879 a Ponca leader named Standing Bear went to federal court and won a ruling that a Native American is a person under the law. Then he toured the country arguing his case in public.

The Ponca lived at the mouth of the Niobrara River in Nebraska. They had never fought the United States and had never sold that land. But in 1868 United States officials made a treaty with the Sioux that created the Great Sioux Reservation, and the Ponca land lay inside its lines. In 1876 members of Congress set aside $25,000 to move the Ponca to Indian Territory "if they consented to go." Indian Territory was the land where United States officials sent Native nations they forced from their homes. Officials took eight Ponca chiefs south to choose new land. The chiefs turned down every place they saw and asked to go home. When officials refused, the chiefs walked more than 500 miles back to Nebraska in winter, in 40 days, "with only a few dollars and one blanket each."

Federal officials ordered the move anyway, "using Army troops if necessary." An Indian Office inspector, E. C. Kemble, led the first 170 people south on 30 April 1877. He ordered the agent, E. A. Howard, to move the rest of the Ponca. They left on 16 May 1877, with 25 soldiers guarding them, and left behind their homes, crops and burial grounds. Standing Bear's daughter died on the way. In July they crossed into Indian Territory onto Quapaw land. Later they chose 101,894 acres that had been the Cherokee Nation's land. A third of the Ponca died after they arrived.

In late December 1878, Standing Bear's son, Bear Shield, died of a fever at sixteen. In early January 1879 Standing Bear set out on foot for Nebraska with a small group of his people, mostly women and children, carrying his son's bones to bury at home. The walk took about two months. The group was arrested and held at Fort Omaha, an Army post, under General George Crook.

Two Omaha lawyers, John L. Webster and Andrew J. Poppleton, took his case. They asked for a writ of habeas corpus. That is an old legal rule under which a person being held can make the government show its legal reason for holding him. The case was heard on 1 and 2 May 1879 before Judge Elmer S. Dundy of the United States District Court in Omaha. Standing Bear told the court, "That hand is not the color of yours. But if I pierce it, I shall feel pain ... I am a man. The same god made us both."

On 12 May 1879 Dundy ruled that "an Indian is a person within the meaning of the laws of the United States," and ordered the Ponca released. From June 1879 Standing Bear toured the country. He spoke in Chicago, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Boston, New York, Washington and Baltimore. The journalist Thomas Tibbles went with him, and so did Susette La Flesche of the Omaha Nation, called Bright Eyes, who interpreted, and her brother Frank.

The ruling covered Standing Bear and the people held with him. Native people did not become citizens because of it. In April 1880 John Elk, a Native man who had left his nation and lived in Omaha, tried to register to vote. Charles Wilkins, the registrar of his ward, refused him because he was Native. On 3 November 1884 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled against Elk, seven to two, in *Elk v. Wilkins*. Justice Horace Gray wrote that a Native person born into a Native nation did not become a citizen by being born in the United States. Justices John Marshall Harlan and William B. Woods dissented, which means they disagreed with the ruling.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reformers who asked for allotment" -->
Standing Bear's speeches reached white reformers in the East. They organized, lobbied and petitioned. What they won was a law under which Native nations lost about two-thirds of the land they still held.

In 1879 Helen Hunt Jackson heard Standing Bear speak in Boston. In 1881 she published *A Century of Dishonor*, a book about how the United States government had treated Native nations. She sent a copy to every member of Congress with a note: "Look upon your hands: they are stained with the blood of your relations."

In 1879 two Philadelphia women, Mary Lucinda Bonney and Amelia Stone Quinton, founded the Women's National Indian Association. On 15 December 1882 a group of wealthy Philadelphia men founded the Indian Rights Association, after Herbert Welsh and Henry S. Pancoast came back from a visit to the Sioux in Dakota Territory. They said their aim was to secure for Native people "the political and civil rights already guaranteed to them by treaty and statutes." Its members believed that Native people would survive only by becoming Protestant Christians, members of the Christian churches that had broken from the Catholic Church, and by living like white Americans. From 1883 a Quaker hotel owner, Albert K. Smiley, gathered these reformers every year at his resort at Lake Mohonk, New York. The account of the conferences that describes them names no Native people taking part. The reformers asked members of Congress to break reservation land into plots owned by single families. This was called allotment.

Members of Congress did it in the Dawes Act, signed on 8 February 1887 and named for Senator Henry Dawes of Massachusetts. Under the act, the head of each family got 160 acres, and each person who took a plot became a citizen. Federal officials could buy the land that was "not allotted" from the Native nations and sell it to settlers. Under the Dawes Act and the laws that followed it, the land held by Native nations went from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934. Another count gives about 137 million and 47 million.

### Mary and Joseph Tape

> **Who:** Two Chinese American parents who took San Francisco's school leaders to court because they would not let their eight-year-old daughter into the school near their home. They won, and school officials still kept her out.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, 1884 and 1885.

In 1884 the principal of a San Francisco school refused to take eight-year-old Mamie Tape because her parents were Chinese. Her parents sued and won. Then San Francisco's school officials reopened a separate school for Chinese children, and Mamie became one of its first two pupils.

San Francisco's schools had been separated by race from the start. The first public schools, opened in 1851, were for white children only. A school for Black children opened in 1854, and one for Chinese children in 1859. In 1870 the city's school officials closed the Chinese school, which left Chinese American children with no public school at all.

In January 1874 a Black mother, Harriet A. Ward, had taken the same question to court for her daughter, Mary Frances, who was eleven. Noah F. Flood, the principal of the Broadway Grammar School, had refused to take her. California law said, "The education of children of African descent, and Indian children, shall be provided for in separate schools." The judges of the California Supreme Court ruled against the Wards. They held that separate schools were lawful, and that a Black child could be kept out of a white school wherever a separate school existed. San Francisco had one.

Mary Tape was born near Shanghai, China, in 1857. She came to the United States alone in 1868, when she was eleven. Women of the Ladies' Protection and Relief Society took her in. She learned English there and took the name Mary McGladery, after one of the women who cared for her. Joseph Tape was born Jeu Dip and arrived in 1864. He worked as a servant, then ran a milk delivery business, then worked as a broker and as an interpreter for China's consulate. They had four children, Mamie, Frank, Emily and Gertrude, and lived in the Cow Hollow part of San Francisco.

In 1884 they took Mamie to Spring Valley Primary School, the all-white school near their home. The principal, Jennie Hurley, refused her because her family came from China. Joseph Tape sued the San Francisco Board of Education, the school superintendent Andrew Jackson Moulder, and Hurley herself.

On 9 January 1885 the judge of the Superior Court of California ruled for the Tapes. He held that keeping out "children of Chinese parents" broke California law and the 14th Amendment's promise of equal protection. The members of the Board of Education appealed, and the judges of the California Supreme Court agreed with the lower court.

Moulder sent a telegram to the state's lawmakers as soon as the decision came down. The lawmakers passed a law that let school officials set up "separate schools for children of Mongolian or Chinese descent." On 8 April 1885, officials at Spring Valley refused Mamie again. This time they said she had no vaccination certificate and the classes were full.

That day Mary Tape wrote to the Board of Education. The *Daily Alta California* printed her letter on its front page on 16 April 1885. She was writing in English, her second language, and the newspaper printed her letter with her own spelling.

"I see that you are going to make all sorts of excuses to keep my child out of the Public schools. Dear sirs, Will you please to tell me! Is it a disgrace to be Born a Chinese? Didn't God make us all!!! What right have you to bar my children out of the school because she is a chinese Decend. ... It seems no matter how a Chinese may live and dress so long as you know they Chinese. Then they are hated as one. There is not any right or justice for them. ... Mamie Tape will never attend any of the Chinese schools of your making! Never!!! I will let the world see sir What justice there is When it is govern by the Race prejudice men! ... I guess she is more of a American then a good many of you that is going to prevent her being Educated." She signed it "Mrs. M. Tape."

On 13 April 1885, Mamie and her brother Frank became the first two pupils at the new Chinese Primary School in Chinatown. San Francisco's school district says today that its officials reopened the separate Chinese school "as a way to avoid integrating white schools," meaning to keep Chinese children out of white children's schools.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chinese Americans take the law to court, 1886 to 1898" -->
Chinese Americans used the 14th Amendment to challenge laws aimed at them, and sometimes they won. In 1885 San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, the men who made the city's laws, required a permit to run a laundry in a wooden building. About 200 Chinese laundry owners applied, and the supervisors refused every one. About 80 owners who were not Chinese applied, and all but one got a permit. Officials arrested more than 150 Chinese men for working without a permit. One of them was Yick Wo, who had run a laundry at 349 Third Street for 22 years. The sheriff, Peter Hopkins, held him in jail.

On 10 May 1886, in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, all the justices of the Supreme Court ruled for Yick Wo. Justice Stanley Matthews wrote that a law "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand" denied people the equal protection of the laws.

In 1892 members of Congress voted for the Geary Act. Under it, Chinese exclusion went on, and every Chinese laborer had to register and carry a certificate of residence at all times. A man caught without one could be sentenced to up to a year of hard labor and then deported, which means sent out of the country by force. To prove he had lived in the country, he needed a white witness. The act, as the Supreme Court quoted it, asked for "at least one credible white witness." A news account says two.

The leaders of the Chinese Six Companies, a Chinese American association, told people not to register. They collected a dollar from each Chinese resident to pay lawyers and put out leaflets that said, "The Geary Act is an unjust law." About 100,000 Chinese people lived in the United States, and only about 13,000 registered in the first period. A 2026 KQED news report, drawing on the historians Kelly Lytle Hernández, Alexander Jin and Russell Jeung, calls the refusal the first mass resistance to deportation in the United States.

Three men became the test case: Fong Yue Ting, Wong Quan and Lee Joe. Lee Joe had lived in the country since about 1874. He had tried to get a certificate in April 1893 and was refused, because his only witnesses were Chinese. On 15 May 1893 the justices of the Supreme Court upheld the law, five to three, in *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*. Justice David Brewer wrote in dissent that deportation meant "removal from home, from family, from business, from property."

Wong Kim Ark was born at 751 Sacramento Street in San Francisco in 1873, to Chinese parents. In August 1895, when he came home from a visit to China, officials at the port of San Francisco refused to let him back in. On 28 March 1898 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled six to two that he was a citizen under the 14th Amendment, because he was born in the United States. Justice Horace Gray wrote for the Court. Chief Justice Melville Fuller dissented, and Justice John Marshall Harlan joined him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A committee plans an arrest, New Orleans, 1891 to 1896" -->
In 1891 a group of Black men in New Orleans formed a committee to fight a new law in court, and they planned the arrest that started the case. On 10 June 1890 Louisiana's lawmakers had passed Act 111, the Separate Car Act. Under it, railroads had to give "equal but separate" seats to Black and white passengers. Black New Orleanians protested, and 16 of the state's lawmakers at the time were Black. The majority passed it anyway. A passenger who sat in the wrong car could be fined $25 or jailed for 20 days.

In September 1891, French-speaking men of African descent in New Orleans formed the Comité des Citoyens, which means the Citizens' Committee. Its full name was the Citizens' Committee to Test the Constitutionality of the Separate Car Law. The members raised money and hired a lawyer, Albion W. Tourgée.

Homer Plessy volunteered to be arrested. He was born Homère Patris Plessy in New Orleans on 17 March 1863, to free parents of color, and he worked as a shoemaker. On 7 June 1892 he bought a ticket to Covington, Louisiana, on the East Louisiana Railroad and sat in the car for white passengers. Railroad officials were in on the plan, and the company did not want to pay the cost of running extra cars. As planned, a private detective hired for the job arrested him, so that he would be charged with exactly the offense the committee wanted to fight. Judge John H. Ferguson of the Orleans Parish criminal court ruled against Plessy, and so the case carries his name.

On 18 May 1896 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled against Plessy, seven to one, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*. Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote that laws "permitting, and even requiring, their separation, in places where they are liable to be brought into contact, do not necessarily imply the inferiority of either race to the other." If Black Americans felt that such a law marked them as inferior, he wrote, "it is not by reason of anything found in the act, but solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it."

Justice John Marshall Harlan disagreed, alone. He wrote, "But in view of the constitution, in the eye of the law, there is in this country no superior, dominant, ruling class of citizens." He also wrote, "Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." The ruling stood for nearly sixty years, until *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954. Homer Plessy died in New Orleans in 1925.

### Ida B. Wells

> **Who:** A Memphis newspaper editor who counted the people killed by lynch mobs, published the count, showed that the reason given for the killings was false, and was driven out of the South for it.
> **When and where:** Born at Holly Springs, Mississippi, 16 July 1862. Memphis, Tennessee, 1884 to 1892.

Ida B. Wells counted the lynchings in the South, printed the numbers and showed that the excuse given for them did not match the facts. A lynching is a killing by a crowd, outside the law, with no trial. After she wrote about it, white men in Memphis came looking for her paper's editors and she could not go home.

She was born into slavery at Holly Springs, Mississippi, on 16 July 1862, to James and Lizzie Wells, and was freed in 1865. In 1884 she bought a first-class railroad ticket, and the train crew ordered her into the car for Black passengers. She refused, and they removed her from the train by force. She sued the railroad and won $500 in the circuit court. The judges of the Tennessee Supreme Court overturned the award. She taught in a segregated public school in Memphis, and in 1891 Memphis school officials fired her for writing about conditions in the schools.

Her friend Thomas Moss was part owner of the People's Grocery, a store run by Black owners in a mostly Black part of Memphis. It was taking business from a white-owned store nearby. William Barrett, one of the rival owners, got a judge to declare the People's Grocery a public nuisance and to make him a deputy. Barrett and a group of deputies in ordinary clothes marched on the store. Shots were fired, and three deputies were wounded. Wells wrote that "Thirty-one men were arrested and thrown in jail as 'conspirators.'" Moss and two others from the store were among them.

In the early hours of 9 March 1892, a mob took Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell and a third man out of their cells in the Memphis jail. The men of the mob took them to the rail yards and shot them to death. Calvin McDowell was shot with a shotgun at close range. The third man was shot in the neck. Records give his name two ways, as Will Stewart and as Henry Steward. Moss's last words were reported as "Tell my people to go west. There is no justice for them here." Wells wrote that Black Memphis "urged and waited for the authorities to act in the matter and bring the lynchers to justice. No attempt was made to do so." No one was ever arrested for the killings.

Black Memphians organized in answer. Wells wrote that "the black men left the city by thousands." The people who stayed stopped riding the streetcars. The streetcar company's superintendent, manager and treasurer came to the office of Wells's newspaper, the *Free Speech*, and asked its editors to tell people to ride again.

On 21 May 1892 Wells printed an editorial in the *Free Speech*: "Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread-bare lie that Negro men rape white women." The excuse given in public for lynching was that Black men had raped white women. Wells wrote that white men were killing Black men over relationships that white women had chosen.

She described what happened next in a pamphlet later that year. On 24 May 1892, after editorials in the Memphis daily papers, men of the city held a meeting at the Cotton Exchange Building. "A committee was sent for the editors of the Free Speech," she wrote, "and the only reason the open threats of lynching that were not carried out was because they could not be found." Her business partner, J. L. Fleming, "had to leave town to escape the mob, and was afterwards ordered not to return." Wells was on vacation in New York. Letters and telegrams warned her "that bodily harm awaited my return." Then "Creditors took possession of the office and sold the outfit, and the Free Speech was as if it had never been." She did not go back. She wrote that her return home was "forbidden under penalty of death."

Her pamphlet *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* came out in 1892. In it she wrote that "the dark and bloody record of the South shows 728 Afro-Americans lynched during the past eight years." She also wrote: "only one-third of the 728 victims to mobs have been charged with rape, to say nothing of those of that one-third who were innocent." Two out of three had never been accused of the crime that lynching was said to punish. Using the *Chicago Tribune*'s yearly lists, she later counted 241 people lynched in 1892.

In 1893 she wrote *The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian Exposition*, printed in three languages, for the world's fair in Chicago. In 1894 she toured Britain speaking about lynching, and people there formed a London Anti-Lynching Committee. In 1895 she married Ferdinand Barnett, and they had four children. The same year she published *A Red Record*, 100 pages of lynching figures and history.

One white reformer fought her in public. Frances Willard was president of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, a women's group that campaigned against alcohol. In 1890 Willard had told a newspaper, the *New York Voice*, that "The colored race multiplies like the locusts of Egypt," and that "The safety of woman, of childhood, the home, is menaced in a thousand localities." Menaced means threatened. Wells printed those words in Britain in 1894. At her group's convention in Cleveland on 5 November 1894, Willard said Wells had "put an imputation upon half the white race in this country that is unjust." An imputation is an accusation. Wells answered her in *A Red Record*.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Suffragists remember the clan mothers, 1891 and 1893" -->
A National Park Service page records that the power of Haudenosaunee women, the women of a union of Native nations in New York, inspired leaders of the women's rights movement. In 1891 Elizabeth Cady Stanton told the National Council of Women that Haudenosaunee women "did not hesitate, when occasion required, 'to knock off the horns,' as it was technically called, from the head of a chief." She meant the clan mothers' power to remove a chief. Matilda Joslyn Gage wrote in the *New York Evening Post* that among the Haudenosaunee, "In the home the wife was absolute." In 1893 the Wolf Clan of the Mohawk Nation adopted Gage as an honorary member and gave her the name Ka-ron-ien-ha-wi, "She who holds the sky."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A city government overthrown, Wilmington, 1898" -->
In Wilmington, North Carolina, on 10 November 1898, armed white men overthrew the city's elected government. Black and white Republicans held the city's offices, and Alex Manly edited the *Daily Record*, the city's Black newspaper. Manly had printed an editorial that challenged white beliefs about relationships between Black men and white women, and Democratic Party leaders used it to stir up white voters. Before the election two days earlier, the Red Shirts, a group of Democratic Party supporters on horseback, had frightened Black people and forced white voters to vote for Democrats.

Alfred Moore Waddell, a former Confederate officer and congressman, led the attack. About 500 armed white men gathered at the armory, and the crowd grew to perhaps 2,000. They broke into the *Daily Record* office and set the building on fire. Violence broke out across the town, and the white men murdered at least 14 and perhaps as many as 60 men. All of the dead who were reported were Black. The coroner counted 14. The state's commission report of 2006 gives as many as sixty. The records do not give an exact count.

That afternoon Waddell's men forced the Republican mayor, Silas P. Wright, the aldermen, who were the members of the city council, and other city officers to resign. The new aldermen made Waddell mayor. The new city leaders banished Black leaders and white Republicans from the city, which means they forced them to leave and not come back. Manly escaped. Governor Daniel Russell did not ask for federal help, and President William McKinley sent no troops. Lawyers in the United States Attorney General's office investigated and closed the case in 1900 without charging anyone. The records do not name the men who fired.
