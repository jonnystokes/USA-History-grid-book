## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, the people who organized for their rights worked out the methods the movements of the 1960s later used. Alice Paul put women on a picket line at the White House gates in 1917. Charles Hamilton Houston planned a chain of court cases, one law school at a time, starting in 1935. Disabled New Yorkers sat down in a city relief office in 1935 and would not leave. A. Philip Randolph told two presidents he would bring a march to Washington, and each time he got a signed order without holding it.

Women won the vote in 1920, and white officials in the South kept most Black women from using it. White mobs attacked Black Americans in Springfield in 1908, in 25 cities in 1919, and in Tulsa in 1921, where white Tulsans burned the Black district of Greenwood and killed as many as 300 people. State governments had more than 60,000 people operated on so that they could never have children. In 1942 the federal government removed about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from their homes and locked them in camps without charging any of them with a crime. The Supreme Court upheld the operations in 1927 and the removal in 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A mob in Lincoln's town, and the NAACP, 1908 and 1909" -->
A white mob's attack on Black residents of Springfield, Illinois, in August 1908 led a group of Black and white reformers to found the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, the NAACP. Springfield was Abraham Lincoln's home town.

On 14 August 1908, Springfield's newspapers reported that a Black man had tried to attack a white woman in her home the night before. Then they reported a second attack. A white mob went looking for the men accused. When it could not find them, it turned on the city's Black neighborhoods. The violence lasted nearly three days. The mob lynched two men, Scott Burton and William Donegan. Neither had anything to do with any crime. The rioters destroyed dozens of homes. The state militia, the state's own part-time soldiers, stopped the violence. About 150 people were arrested, and few of them were ever convicted of anything. Nearly 2,000 Black residents left Springfield and never came back.

The NAACP was founded on 12 February 1909, a hundred years after Lincoln was born. Its white founders included Mary White Ovington, Oswald Garrison Villard, William English Walling and Dr. Henry Moscowitz. About 60 people signed the call for its first conference. Seven of them were Black, among them the scholar W. E. B. Du Bois, Mary Church Terrell and Ida B. Wells-Barnett, who had counted the South's lynchings in the 1890s. The founders said their aim was to "secure for all people the rights guaranteed in the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women march in Washington, 1913" -->
On 3 March 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson became president, women marched down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington to demand the vote. Alice Paul and Lucy Burns organized the march. They ran the Congressional Committee of the National American Woman Suffrage Association, and they chose the same route the president's parade would take the next day. At least 250,000 people came to watch.

The crowd pushed off the sidewalks and blocked the street. Men shoved the marchers, spat on them and shouted insults and sexual propositions at them. The police did little to stop it. United States Army troops arrived about an hour later and cleared the street. Afterward, members of Congress investigated why the police had done so little.

More than forty Black women marched, in their state groups or with women of their professions. Ida B. Wells-Barnett came with the Illinois group. As the march was forming, she was asked to walk at the back instead. She refused and left. She waited at the side of the avenue until the Illinois women came past, and then she stepped into the line between two white marchers, Belle Squire and Virginia Brooks. The same year, Black women in Chicago formed the Alpha Suffrage Club, the first Black women's suffrage club in Illinois. Ida B. Wells-Barnett died in Chicago on 25 March 1931.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Silent Sentinels and the Occoquan Workhouse, 1917" -->
From 10 January 1917, women stood at the gates of the White House six days a week, holding banners that asked President Wilson to support their vote. The National Park Service says Alice Paul invented picketing the White House. To picket is to stand outside a place with signs, as a public protest. One banner read "Mr President What Will You Do For Woman Suffrage?" Another read "How Long, Mr President, Must Women Wait For Liberty?" Reporters called the women the Silent Sentinels.

In 1917 police began arresting the women on a charge of "obstructing traffic." Over the whole campaign, more than 150 women were convicted of it. In November 1917 alone, 41 women were arrested, among them Lucy Burns, Dorothy Day, Dora Lewis and Mary Nolan. Their sentences ran from six days to six months.

Most of them were sent to the Occoquan Workhouse, a prison in Virginia. On the night of 14 November 1917, on the orders of the superintendent, W. H. Whittaker, the guards attacked them. The guards threw the women around, hung some of them by their arms and refused them food and medical care. Lucy Burns kept a daily log on scraps of paper and got it smuggled out of the workhouse. Her entry for that night reads: "Seized by guards from behind, flung off my feet, and shot out of the room. All of us were seized by men guards and dragged to cells in men's part. Dorothy Day was roughly used-back twisted. Mrs. Mary A. Nolan (73-year-old picket from Jacksonville, Florida) flung into cell." Burns was handcuffed all night, and part of the time her hands were chained to the bars of her cell, for asking the other women how they were. The women called it the Night of Terror.

Many of the prisoners went on hunger strike, which means they refused to eat as a protest. The prison doctor, Dr. Gannon, answered by feeding them by force. Forcible feeding meant holding a woman down and pushing a rubber tube through her nose or mouth, down her throat and into her stomach, and then pouring liquid food through the tube. Burns wrote in her log what was done to her:

"I was held down by five people at legs, arms, and head. I refused to open mouth. Gannon pushed tube up left nostril. I turned and twisted my head all I could, but he managed to push it up. It hurts nose and throat very much and makes nose bleed freely. Tube drawn out covered with blood. Operation leaves one very sick. Food dumped directly into stomach feels like a ball of lead. Left nostril, throat and muscles of neck very sore all night."

Dora Lewis was held down by five people while Gannon forced the tube through her lips. "I didn't know where to breathe from and everything turned black when the fluid began pouring in," she wrote. Alice Paul and Rose Winslow were fed this way three times a day for three weeks, until they were let out. Winslow got a note out of the prison: "We think of the coming feeding all day. It is horrible."

### Alice Paul

> **Who:** The organizer who put the first picket line outside the White House, and who served six prison terms for women's right to vote.
> **When and where:** Born at Mount Laurel, New Jersey, 11 January 1885. Washington, D.C., 1913 to 1920.

Alice Paul's method was to embarrass the president in public and keep doing it until he gave way. When officials jailed her for it, she refused to eat, and a prison doctor moved her to a hospital ward for people about to be committed to an asylum.

She was born on 11 January 1885 at Mount Laurel, New Jersey, into a Quaker family. She graduated from Swarthmore College in 1905, earned a master's degree in 1907 and a doctorate in economics from the University of Pennsylvania in 1912. She also studied in London and Birmingham. In England she joined Emmeline Pankhurst's campaign for the vote and was arrested for it more than once.

Back home, she became chairman of the National American Woman Suffrage Association's Congressional Committee in 1913. She then founded the Congressional Union and, in 1916, the National Woman's Party. She organized the 1913 march in Washington, and on 10 January 1917 she started the picket line at the White House gates.

In October 1917 she was arrested for picketing and sentenced to seven months for obstructing traffic. On her way to serve the sentence, she told reporters, "We are being imprisoned, not because we obstructed traffic, but because we pointed out to the President the fact that he was obstructing the cause of democracy at home, while Americans were fighting for it abroad." The United States was then fighting in the First World War.

In the District of Columbia jail she went on hunger strike. Commissioner Gardner, one of the officials who governed the District of Columbia, told Dr. Gannon, "Go ahead, take her and feed her." She was carried on a stretcher to the jail's psychopathic ward, the part of the hospital for people thought to be insane. Gannon ordered one of her windows nailed shut and her door replaced with a barred cell door. A prisoner on a ladder nailed heavy boards across the other window from outside. A nurse was ordered to check on her every hour through the night by turning an electric light onto her face. Under the rules of the time, the signatures of two doctors were enough to send a person to St. Elizabeth's, the government asylum. She wrote later, "No trial, no counsel, no protest from the outside world!"

The psychiatrist sent to examine her, Dr. William A. White, did not find her insane. He wrote, "I felt myself in the presence of an unusually gifted personality," and praised the "most admirable, coherent, logical and forceful way" in which she argued her case. A lawyer, Dudley Field Malone, forced his way in and got her out of the ward. She was released after five weeks of her seven-month sentence. She served six prison terms for the vote in all, three in England and three in the United States.

After the 19th Amendment passed in 1920, she kept working. She earned a law degree in 1922. She wrote the Equal Rights Amendment, which said that men and women must have equal rights under the law. It was introduced at Seneca Falls on 20 July 1923, where it was called the Lucretia Mott Amendment. In 1945 she worked on the words about women in the United Nations Charter, and she worked on the clause about sex in the Civil Rights Act of 1964. She died at Moorestown, New Jersey, on 9 July 1977, aged 92. The Equal Rights Amendment she wrote was not part of the Constitution when she died.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Margaret Sanger and the Comstock laws, 1914 to 1936" -->
Margaret Sanger went to jail for giving women information about birth control, which means ways to keep from becoming pregnant. The Comstock Act of 1873 made it a crime to send that information by mail. In 1914 Sanger was charged under the Comstock laws for publishing *The Woman Rebel*, a paper about birth control, and she left for England.

On 16 October 1916 she opened a birth control clinic in Brooklyn, New York. Police arrested her about a week later, and she spent 30 days in jail. A court ruling in 1918 allowed doctors to prescribe contraceptives for medical reasons. In 1921 Sanger founded the American Birth Control League. In 1936 a federal court allowed doctors to prescribe birth control more widely. The National Women's History Museum records that Sanger also supported parts of the eugenics movement, whose members wanted to stop people they judged unfit from having children.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attacks in 25 cities, and a court case out of Elaine, 1919 to 1925" -->
In 1919 white mobs attacked Black Americans in 25 major cities, and the NAACP's lawyers turned one of those attacks into a Supreme Court ruling. The year's violence is called the Red Summer. The Equal Justice Initiative, which documents racial violence, says white mobs and police especially targeted Black veterans coming home from the First World War.

In Chicago on 27 July 1919, a 17-year-old Black boy named Eugene Williams was swimming off a raft near the 29th Street beach. A 24-year-old white man, George Stauber, threw stones at the Black boys, and Eugene Williams drowned. A police officer, Daniel Callahan, refused to arrest Stauber. White youth gangs and neighborhood "athletic clubs" then attacked Black neighborhoods, and the fighting went on until 3 August. Thirty-eight people were killed, 23 Black and 15 white. About 537 were injured, and two out of three of them were Black. Two out of three of the 138 people charged with crimes afterward were Black too.

In Elaine, Arkansas, Black sharecroppers had joined a union. A sharecropper farmed land owned by someone else and paid for it with a share of the crop. A union is a group of workers who bargain together. This one, the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America, was led by Robert Lee Hill, and its members wanted a better price for their cotton from the white landowners. On the night of 30 September 1919, about 100 of them met in a church at Hoop Spur. Men in a car outside and the union's armed guards exchanged shots. A white security officer, W. A. Adkins, was killed, and a deputy sheriff, Charles Pratt, was wounded.

Between 500 and 1,000 armed white men came to Elaine from the surrounding Arkansas counties and from across the river in Mississippi. A white witness, H. F. Smiddy, swore in 1921 that "several hundred of them" began "to hunt negroes" and shoot them as they found them. Governor Charles Hillman Brough sent more than 500 soldiers from Camp Pike, who arrived on 2 October, and some accounts say the soldiers also killed Black people at random. Four brothers, David, Gibson, Lewis and Leroy Johnston, were killed on 2 October on their way home from a hunting trip. The count of Black dead is disputed. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas says it "ranged into the hundreds." The National Archives says more than 100. The Army commander, Colonel Isaac Jenks, reported that his soldiers had killed two. Five white people died.

Phillips County officials charged 122 Black people with crimes. By 5 November 1919, twelve men had been sentenced to death. One of their white defense lawyers admitted he "had not interviewed any witnesses." Walter White of the NAACP went to Arkansas to investigate, and a Black lawyer from Little Rock, Scipio Africanus Jones, defended the twelve. On 19 February 1923, in *Moore v. Dempsey*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the trials had been unfair. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that one trial "lasted about three-quarters of an hour and in less than five minutes the jury brought in a verdict of guilty of murder in the first degree." An angry crowd filled the courthouse and the streets around it. Sworn statements in the case said Black witnesses had been "whipped, placed in the electric chair and strangled by something put in their noses to make them testify." In January 1925 Governor Thomas McRae freed the last of the twelve.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thirty years of anti-lynching bills, 1900 to 1922" -->
The NAACP's main demand of Congress was a federal law against lynching, and in the whole of this era its members never passed one. A federal law would have let federal courts try lynch mobs when state courts did not.

Representative George Henry White of North Carolina brought the first federal anti-lynching bill to Congress on 20 January 1900. He was the last Black member of Congress of his generation. His bill went nowhere.

On 8 April 1918, Representative Leonidas Dyer of Missouri introduced a bill "to protect citizens of the United States against lynching in default of protection by the States." Under it, members of a lynch mob would have been tried in federal court for murder. The county where a lynching happened would have been fined. Any sheriff or jailer who handed a prisoner to a mob, or failed to stop one, could have been jailed and fined up to $5,000. People who had been part of a mob could not sit on the jury.

NAACP leaders worked to pass it. In April 1919 they published a report, *Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889-1918*. The report recorded 3,224 people killed by lynch mobs in those thirty years. Of them, 2,522 were Black, and 50 of the Black victims were women. They sent James Weldon Johnson to Washington to press members of Congress in person. "I tramped the corridors of the Capitol and the two office buildings so constantly that toward the end, I could, I think, have been able to find my way about blindfolded," he wrote.

On 14 June 1922 NAACP organizers held a silent march in Washington, with more than 5,000 Black marchers, for the bill. Members of the House of Representatives had passed it on 26 January 1922, by 230 votes to 119, with several hundred Black visitors crowded into the public gallery. Senators never voted on it. Southern Democratic senators filibustered it, which means they kept talking so that no vote could be held. They did the same in 1923 and 1924, and Republican senators let the bill drop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 19th Amendment, 1920, and the women it did not reach" -->
The 19th Amendment to the Constitution was ratified in August 1920. It barred the United States and the states from denying citizens the vote because of their sex. It is one sentence: "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Abridged means cut down.

The same words had been in front of Congress for 42 years. Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced them on 10 January 1878. Senators voted them down in 1887, 1914, 1918 and February 1919. Susan B. Anthony died in Rochester, New York, on 13 March 1906, and suffragists named the measure the Anthony Amendment in her memory. Representatives passed it on 21 May 1919, and senators passed it on 4 June 1919, by 56 votes to 25. Then three-fourths of the states had to ratify it, which means approve it. Tennessee became the 36th state to do so on 18 August 1920, the last one needed. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified it on 26 August 1920, 72 years after the meeting at Seneca Falls.

Under the amendment, state officials could no longer refuse a woman the vote because she was a woman. They could still refuse her for other reasons. In the South, white officials already kept Black men from voting, by rules and by violence. The National Park Service records that Black women in the South "encountered the very same disfranchisement strategies and anti-Black violence that led to the disfranchisement of Black men." The National Archives says many women "remained unable to vote long into the 20th century because of discriminatory state voting laws."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa, 1 June 1921: white Tulsans burn Greenwood" -->
On 1 June 1921, thousands of armed white Tulsans went into Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and burned it to the ground. They killed as many as 300 people. The historian Scott Ellsworth writes that "credible estimates of deaths range from fifty to three hundred." In 2025 the United States Department of Justice wrote that the attack "killed as many as 300 Tulsans, perhaps even more." The attackers threw bodies into the Arkansas River, carried them off on railroad flatcars and buried them in unmarked graves, so the true count may never be known. The Red Cross counted about 700 people injured.

About 10,000 Black people lived in Greenwood. It had two newspapers, more than a dozen churches, a library, two hotels, four drug stores and many grocery stores. Loula Williams owned the Dreamland Theater. J. B. Stradford owned a hotel of 54 rooms. Doctors, lawyers and teachers lived on Detroit Avenue, and families owned pianos and good furniture. Black Tulsans spent their money in Greenwood because most white businesses in Tulsa would not serve them or treated them with contempt. City laws kept Black families out of white blocks unless they worked there as servants. White officials controlled the city's tax money and spent almost none of it on running water, sewers or paved streets in Greenwood. All of Tulsa, Greenwood included, stood on land in the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's reservation.

The attack began after police arrested Dick Rowland, a 19-year-old Black man, on 31 May. A white woman, Sarah Page, ran the elevator he used, and a white man had accused him of attacking her. She did not press charges. That evening a white crowd gathered at the courthouse to lynch Rowland, and armed Black men went there to stop it. Shooting broke out. Tulsa police then deputized hundreds of white men, which means they swore them in as temporary police officers. Many of those men had been calling for a lynching and had been drinking.

At about 1 in the morning, white men set the first fires in Greenwood. When firemen came, armed white men threatened them and told them to let the Black homes burn. A little after 5 in the morning, a whistle blew. White men who had been organized into companies during the night gathered at three railroad stations and went into Greenwood. The 2001 Oklahoma state commission put their number at about 10,000. A scholar writing in 1946 said 8,000 to 10,000. The state's adjutant general, Charles Barrett, told the *New York Times* there were 25,000, a figure many historians think was a misprint for 2,500.

The attackers went from house to house. They took what they wanted, piled bedding and furniture in the middle of a room, poured kerosene over it and lit it. They backed trucks up to houses and loaded "everything moveable and of value." White women came with shopping bags and took clothes, silverware and jewelry. The attackers opened safes and destroyed people's legal papers before burning the buildings. They burned more than 1,200 houses and 35 city blocks. Among the buildings they burned were a dozen churches, five hotels, 31 restaurants, four drug stores, eight doctors' offices, more than two dozen grocery stores and the library.

They killed people who ran and people who gave up. The Justice Department's review describes a grandfather shot in front of his family in a wagon. It describes an old man who could not walk, shot in his home when he said he could not "march." Four white men tied a blind man who had lost both legs to the back of a car and dragged him through the streets until he died. An old couple on Greenwood Avenue were shot in the back of the head while they prayed. A man was shot as he came out of a burning building and thrown back into the fire. Survivors could not name the men who did these things. The Red Cross's relief director in Tulsa, Maurice Willows, who was white, wrote: "This was NOT a RIOT."

Some Black men fought back. A group fired from the bell tower of the new Mount Zion Baptist Church until National Guard soldiers turned a machine gun on it. J. B. Stradford shot from the second floor of his hotel. He gave up when the attackers promised not to burn it, and then they burned it.

The National Guard is a state's own army of part-time soldiers. Three Guard units were in Tulsa, and before the governor took control the guardsmen took orders from Police Chief John Gustafson. By their own reports, they helped the police organize white men into companies and went into Greenwood to round up Black residents. The Justice Department found that capturing Black men was the Guard's main work, and that this left Black homes empty for other white men to loot and burn. Some guardsmen tried to hold back white attackers. Governor James Robertson declared martial law, which put the Guard in charge of the city, at 11:30 that morning.

Airplanes flew over Greenwood during the attack. The Justice Department calls it "undisputed" that the men in them watched where Black residents ran and told the police. Many survivors said men in the planes also dropped firebombs or shot at people fleeing. The lawyer B. C. Franklin, who saw it, described turpentine bombs. The Justice Department calls that part "hotly disputed."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The camps, the passes and the grand jury, June 1921" -->
After the burning, police, deputies and guardsmen arrested Black people all over Tulsa, whether or not they were suspected of anything. The Justice Department found that the arrests were not based on "probable cause or even reasonable suspicion." Guardsmen also arrested Black servants in the servants' rooms of white homes. They held the people they arrested under armed guard in three places: Convention Hall, McNulty Park, which was a baseball park, and the stalls of the fairgrounds. At the fullest, between 4,000 and 6,000 people were held. Some stayed a few days and some up to two weeks. Guardsmen arrested some white men for looting, but they held no white person in the camps.

To leave a camp, a Black person needed an identification card, and a white person had to vouch for it. People who worked in white homes and businesses got green cards that did not run out. People without jobs got red cards that were good for one day. Anyone found without a card was arrested again. By 7 June, officials had given out 7,500 of these cards, and the rule lasted at least a month. People forced to live under it said they were treated "as though they [were] dogs."

The day after the burning, the Guard commander ordered "all able bodied [N]egro men" in the camps to "render such service and perform such labor as is required." The men cleared the ruins and buried the dead. The Red Cross paid up to 25 cents an hour, and a meal cost 20 cents. For several weeks Black Tulsans were not allowed to own or buy guns. Most of Greenwood's people had no home left. Thousands lived through the winter of 1921 in Red Cross tents, many of them with no floor, and many stayed in tents for more than a year.

A state grand jury heard almost 200 witnesses. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to charge someone with a crime. This one charged about 70 men, many of them Black leaders of Greenwood, with starting the "riot." On 26 June 1921 the *Tulsa Daily World* printed its report under the headline "Grand Jury Blames Negroes for Inciting Race Rioting: Whites Clearly Exonerated." Exonerated means cleared of blame. Most of the cases were later dropped, including the case against Dick Rowland. Some white men were charged with looting. No white man went to prison for the killings or the burning. The grand jury also charged Police Chief Gustafson with dereliction of duty, which means failing to do the job he was paid to do, and with corruption from before the massacre. He was convicted and removed from his post.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building Greenwood again, against the city" -->
Greenwood's people rebuilt their district with their own money, after winning a court fight against city leaders who tried to stop them. On 7 June 1921, Tulsa's city commissioners passed Fire Ordinance No. 2156. It put most of Greenwood inside the city's fire limits, and it required every new building there to be "of concrete, brick, or steel and ... at least two stories high." Few families who had lost everything could pay for that. The first newspaper story about the rule said the land would "never again be a [N]egro quarter but will become a wholesale and industrial center." In 2025 the Justice Department found that "those who drafted the ordinance planned to drive Black people out of Greenwood."

B. C. Franklin, a Black lawyer, set up an office in a tent on Archer Street with two partners, I. H. Spears and T. O. Chappelle. Before they could get to court, police had arrested many people for trying to rebuild. The lawyers won a court order that stopped the rule for a time, and then, in September 1921, an order that stopped it for good.

Franklin's firm also filed about $4 million in claims against the city and against insurance companies. Officials of the insurance companies refused to pay, because their policies did not cover damage done in a "riot." In 1926 the justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court ruled that no one could sue the insurance companies over it. Judges threw out the lawsuits against the city, Mayor T. D. Evans, the police and the airplane company.

By 1925 Greenwood was hosting the National Negro Business League's national meeting, and by the 1940s it had 242 Black-owned businesses. From the 1950s, city officials took land along its edges for highways. The Inner Dispersal Loop and the Cherokee Expressway, finished in the 1970s, run along Greenwood's south and east sides.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The survivors ask to be paid, 1997 to 2026" -->
Survivors of the massacre asked for more than eighty years to be paid for what was taken from them, and none of them ever was. In 1997 Oklahoma's lawmakers set up an 11-member commission to study the massacre. Its report came out on 28 February 2001. It asked the state to pay the survivors and their descendants directly, and to fund scholarships, business growth in the area and a memorial. On 1 June 2001 the lawmakers passed the 1921 Tulsa Race Riot Reconciliation Act. It paid the survivors nothing.

In 2003 survivors and descendants sued the state and the city. Federal judges threw the case out in 2004, ruling that they had waited too long to sue. In 2020 the last three known survivors, Viola Fletcher, her brother Hughes Van Ellis and Lessie Benningfield Randle, sued the City of Tulsa, the Tulsa Regional Chamber, the county's commissioners, Sheriff Vic Regalado and the Oklahoma Military Department. On 19 May 2021 the three spoke to a committee of Congress.

On 1 June 2021 President Joe Biden met the three survivors and spoke at the Greenwood Cultural Center. He was the first president in office to visit Greenwood about the massacre. "It wasn't a riot, it was a massacre," he said. He did not call for the survivors to be paid.

On 12 June 2024 the justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court ended the survivors' lawsuit, by eight votes to one. They wrote that the harm the massacre left behind "can only be resolved by policymakers, not the courts." Hughes Van Ellis had died on 9 October 2023, aged 102. Viola Fletcher died on 24 November 2025, aged 111. Lessie Benningfield Randle, born on 10 November 1914, was the last known survivor in 2026, and she had never been paid.

In 2018 Tulsa's mayor, G. T. Bynum, started a search for the massacre's unmarked graves. By September 2026 the searchers had dug up the remains of 55 people in Oaklawn Cemetery. They have named two victims of the massacre. C. L. Daniel was identified by DNA in July 2024. James Goings was identified from Veterans Administration records in June 2025, and his grave has not been found. Both men had served in the Army in the First World War.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="viola-fletcher" name="Viola Fletcher" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Viola Fletcher

> **Who:** A seven-year-old girl whose family fled Greenwood in 1921, who never went to school past the fourth grade, and who at 107 asked Congress for justice.
> **When and where:** Tulsa, Oklahoma, 1921. Washington, D.C., 19 May 2021.

Viola Fletcher was seven when white Tulsans burned her neighborhood, and she spent the last years of her long life asking a court to pay the survivors. She never saw them paid.

She was born Viola Ford on 10 May 1914, the daughter of Lucinda Ellis and John Wesley Ford of Tulsa. She grew up in Greenwood with her parents and five brothers and sisters. "The neighborhood I fell asleep in that night was rich," she told Congress in 2021, "not just in terms of wealth, but in culture, community, and heritage." "My family had a beautiful home. We had great neighbors and I had friends to play with. I felt safe."

On the night of 31 May 1921, her family woke her and told her they had to leave. "I will never forget the violence of the white mob when we left our house," she said. "I still hear airplanes flying overhead. I hear the screams. I live through the Massacre every day."

"When my family was forced to leave Tulsa, I lost my chance at an education," she said. "I never finished school past the fourth grade." During the Second World War she worked in the shipyards in California. "For most of my life, I was a domestic worker serving white families," she said. A domestic worker cleans and cooks in other people's homes.

In 2020, at 106, she joined her brother and Lessie Benningfield Randle in suing the City of Tulsa and others. On 19 May 2021 she spoke to the House of Representatives' subcommittee on civil rights. "Today, I am in Washington, D.C. for the first time in my life. I am here seeking justice," she told them. "I am 107 years old and have never seen justice." She asked them to "open the courtroom doors to us."

In 2023 she and her grandson, Ike Howard, published a book about her life, *Don't Let Them Bury My Story*. The Oklahoma Supreme Court ended the survivors' lawsuit in June 2024. Viola Fletcher died on 24 November 2025, aged 111.

### Grace Fryer

> **Who:** A watch-dial painter who sued the company whose paint had poisoned her, and who had to fight a state time limit that ran out before she knew she was ill.
> **When and where:** Orange, New Jersey, 1917 to 1928.
> **Movie:** Radium Girls (2018)

Grace Fryer and four other women sued the U.S. Radium Corporation in 1927 for poisoning them. The company argued that the law's time limit had already run out.

From 1917 to 1920, Fryer painted numbers on watch dials at the company's plant in Orange, New Jersey. The paint glowed in the dark because it had radium in it. Radium is a radioactive metal, which means it gives off radiation, energy that can damage the body's cells. To keep a fine point on the brush, the painters put the tip between their lips. The physicist Robley Evans wrote down why: a cloth or their fingers "were too harsh, but by wiping the brush clean between their lips the proper erasing point could be obtained."

The body takes up radium as if it were calcium and builds it into the bones and teeth. Once there, it stays and keeps giving off radiation from the inside. When doctors examined the bodies of dial painters after death, they found 99 percent of the radiation in the skeleton. The radium caused the bone marrow to stop making blood properly. It caused the jaw bone to die and break apart. It caused bone cancers.

Fryer left the plant in 1920. In 1924 she went to a dentist, Theodor Blum, who found that her jaw bone was wasting away. He had never seen anything like it. Doctors came to call it radium jaw.

In May 1927 a lawyer, Raymond Berry, filed suit against the company for Fryer and four women she had worked with: Katherine Schaub, Quinta Maggia Macdonald, Albina Maggia Larice and Edna Hussman. The company's lawyers argued that New Jersey's statute of limitations cleared it. A statute of limitations is a time limit after which a person can no longer sue. New Jersey's limit for injuries at work was two years. Radium poisoning took longer than two years to show itself, and Fryer had left the plant seven years before.

On 4 June 1928 a federal judge, William Clark, brought the two sides to a settlement outside court. The company paid the sick women and did not admit it had done anything wrong.

Ten years later, women who had painted dials for the Radium Dial Company in Ottawa, Illinois, fought the same fight. One of them, Catherine Wolfe Donohue, died in July 1938, aged 35. The last ruling in her case came that October, after her death, and her family received a small payment.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native Americans become citizens and fight for the vote, 1911 to 1957" -->
Native Americans organized their own national rights groups in these years, and it took them 33 years after they became citizens to win the vote in every state. On 12 October 1911, at Columbus, Ohio, Native people founded the Society of American Indians, the first national rights organization run by Native people. Charles Eastman and Carlos Montezuma were among its leaders, and Zitkala-Ša edited its journal. In 1926 Zitkala-Ša founded the National Council of American Indians. On 16 November 1944, 80 delegates from more than 50 tribes met in Denver and founded the National Congress of American Indians.

On 2 June 1924 Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act, and President Calvin Coolidge signed it. It made American Indians citizens. It did not put them on the voter rolls, because each state made its own rules about who could vote.

Arizona barred from voting anyone who was an "idiot, insane person, person non compos mentis, or under guardianship." Non compos mentis is Latin for "not of sound mind." In 1928 the justices of the Arizona Supreme Court ruled that Native people living on reservations were under the guardianship of the federal government, and so could not vote. In New Mexico, officials used the state constitution's phrase "Indians not taxed." They argued that people on reservations paid no state taxes and so could not vote.

Both rules fell in 1948. On 15 July 1948, in *Harrison v. Laveen*, the Arizona Supreme Court threw out its own 1928 ruling. Justice Levi Udall wrote that a Native person who met the other requirements could not be kept off the rolls for living on a reservation. In August 1948, in *Trujillo v. Garley*, three federal judges in New Mexico ruled that the "Indians not taxed" rule broke the Constitution. Utah was the last state to let Native people vote, in 1957.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A society for gay rights in Chicago, 1924" -->
On 10 December 1924 Henry Gerber founded the Society for Human Rights in Chicago. The National Park Service calls it the first organization in the United States set up only to work for the civil rights of gay people. A gay person is a man who loves men or a woman who loves women.

Gerber was born Josef Heinrich Dittmar in Bavaria, in Germany, on 29 June 1892. He came through Ellis Island in October 1913 and served in the United States Army. He worked for the Post Office. He got a charter for the society from the State of Illinois by describing its members as people "abused and hindered in the legal pursuit of happiness." Its members printed two issues of a newsletter, *Friendship and Freedom*. No copy survives.

Chicago police arrested Gerber and other members without a warrant, a court paper that allows an arrest. They took his typewriter, his writings and his diaries, and they gave back only the typewriter. The Post Office fired him "for conduct unbecoming a postal employee." The society ended in 1925. Gerber died in 1972. His house in Chicago became a National Historic Landmark in June 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eugenics: operating on people so they could not have children" -->
Eugenics was the belief that a country could improve its people by deciding who was fit to have children and stopping the rest. The English statistician Francis Galton made up the word in 1883. The National Human Genome Research Institute calls eugenics "the scientifically erroneous and immoral theory of 'racial improvement' and 'planned breeding.'" It gained followers in the early 1900s.

American eugenicists pushed for laws that forced people to be sterilized. To sterilize a person is to operate on them so that they can never have a child. The operation cannot be undone. Harry H. Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office wrote a model sterilization law for states to copy. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum says that "in part due to Laughlin's efforts, dozens of American states enacted laws that forced certain people to be sterilized against their will." It says that "more than 62,000 sterilizations had been performed in the United States by the 1960s," and that about six in ten of the people operated on were women. The National Human Genome Research Institute counts at least 60,000 people sterilized under the laws of 30 states by the 1970s. The people targeted included Latinos, Native Americans, African Americans, poor white people and disabled people.

In 1933 Nazi Germany passed the Law for the Prevention of Offspring with Hereditary Diseases. The Holocaust Museum says it was "based in part on the practice of forced sterilization in the US." Doctors in Nazi Germany sterilized about 400,000 people under it. In 1936 the University of Heidelberg in Germany gave Harry Laughlin an honorary degree. On 28 May 1936 he wrote back that "To me this honor will be doubly valued because it will come from a nation which for many centuries nurtured the human seed-stock which later founded my own country."

### Carrie Buck

> **Who:** A young Virginia woman chosen as a test case by the man who wanted to sterilize her, and defended by a lawyer who wanted the same thing.
> **When and where:** Virginia, 1924 to 1927. Died at Waynesboro, Virginia, 28 January 1983.

Carrie Buck's case was arranged so that Virginia would win it. The official who wanted to sterilize her picked her case to test the state's new law, and her own lawyer supported sterilization.

Virginia's sterilization law passed on 20 March 1924. Aubrey Strode, a lawyer, wrote it. Albert Priddy ran the Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feeble-Minded, a state institution. Feeble-minded was a label then given to people who scored low on intelligence tests. He wanted to know whether the new law would hold up in court, so he picked one of the people held there, Carrie Buck, and had a case brought over her. Priddy died on 13 January 1925, and Dr. John H. Bell took his place at the Colony and in the case. That is why it is called *Buck v. Bell*.

Strode, who had written the law, argued for the Colony in court. Carrie Buck's lawyer was Irving P. Whitehead. He had sat on the Colony's own board, and he supported sterilization. The historian Paul Lombardo found that Whitehead did not seriously question any of the witnesses against her and did not call one witness for her. Both lawyers wanted the court to uphold the law.

A eugenics researcher, Arthur Estabrook, interviewed the family for one day. Then he testified that Carrie, her mother Emma and her baby daughter Vivian were "a defective strain." Emma Buck had been committed to the Colony in April 1920. Vivian was born on 28 March 1924. A social worker said of the baby that she had "a look about it that is not quite normal."

Judge Bennett T. Gordon of the trial court ruled for the Colony on 18 November 1924. The Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals agreed on 12 November 1925. The justices of the Supreme Court of the United States heard the case on 22 April 1927 and ruled for the Colony, eight to one, on 2 May 1927.

On 19 October 1927 Dr. Bell performed a salpingectomy on Carrie Buck. He cut out a section of each of her two tubes that carry an egg from an ovary to the womb. After that, no egg could reach her womb, so she could never become pregnant. The operation could not be undone. The staff released her from the Colony the next month. Her sister, Doris Buck, had been brought to the Colony in June 1926. An order to sterilize Doris was signed in December 1927, and she was operated on in January 1928.

Between 1927 and 1972, about 8,300 people were sterilized in Virginia. Virginia's lawmakers repealed the law in 1974. In 1980 the American Civil Liberties Union sued for the survivors, and the case was settled out of court. On 2 May 2002, 75 years to the day after the ruling, Governor Mark Warner apologized for what he called "a shameful effort in which state government never should have been involved." Carrie Buck married twice. She died in a nursing home in Waynesboro, Virginia, on 28 January 1983. The Supreme Court has never overruled *Buck v. Bell*.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Citizens sent to Mexico, 1929 to 1936" -->
During the Great Depression, federal, state and county officials pushed hundreds of thousands of people of Mexican ancestry out of the United States, and many of them were American citizens. Counts run from about 500,000 to about 2 million. The State of California found in 2005 that about 1.2 million of the 2 million people it counted had been born in the United States, which made them citizens.

William N. Doak, President Herbert Hoover's Secretary of Labor, led a national campaign to remove Mexicans, saying they were taking jobs. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel, head of the city's unemployment relief committee, planned the publicity. On 26 February 1931, immigration agents closed the exits of La Placita park in Los Angeles with about 400 people inside. Los Angeles County welfare officers told families their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico. Relief was the public money that poor families lived on during the Depression. The first county train left on 23 March 1931. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks bound for the border. Local officials sold the property that families left behind.

On 7 October 2005, California's governor signed a law in which the state's lawmakers apologized "for the fundamental violations of their basic civil liberties and constitutional rights committed during the period of illegal deportation and coerced emigration." The Los Angeles County supervisors apologized in 2012. The federal government has not apologized.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two California school cases, 1931 and 1946" -->
Mexican American parents in California won the first two school desegregation cases in the country, fifteen years apart. On 5 January 1931 the principal of Lemon Grove Grammar School, near San Diego, Jerome T. Green, stood in the doorway. He let in every child except the Mexican American ones and told them to go to a separate two-room building. The school board had planned this the summer before. The school had 169 pupils: 87 counted as European American, about 75 Mexican American and 7 Japanese American.

The parents formed a committee, the Comité de Vecinos de Lemon Grove, which means the Lemon Grove Neighbors Committee. They kept their children out of the separate building and hired two lawyers, Fred Noon and A. C. Brinkley. They sued in the name of one of the pupils, Roberto Alvarez. On 30 March 1931 Judge Claude Chambers of the San Diego County Superior Court ordered the district to admit the children "on a basis of equality with other children of said school district, and without separation or segregation in a separate school because of race or nationality." He said that putting "all the Mexicans in one group can only be done by infringing the laws of the State of California." Some accounts date the ruling 31 March.

Fifteen years later, five families in Orange County, the Mendez, Guzman, Palomino, Estrada and Ramirez families, sued four school districts. Their lawyer was David C. Marcus, who had won a case in 1944 against segregated pools and parks in San Bernardino. On 18 February 1946 Judge Paul J. McCormick ruled for the families. He wrote, "A paramount requisite in the American system of public education is social equality."

When the districts appealed, other groups joined the fight. NAACP lawyers filed a friend-of-the-court brief, an argument from people who are not part of a case. Thurgood Marshall, Robert L. Carter and Loren Miller wrote it. The Japanese American Citizens League, the League of United Latin American Citizens, the American Jewish Congress and the American Civil Liberties Union filed briefs too. The federal appeals court ruled for the families on 14 April 1947. In June 1947 Governor Earl Warren signed the Anderson Bill, which took segregated schools out of California's school laws. California was the first state to do so. Warren became Chief Justice of the United States in 1953.

The Mendez family were farming 40 acres in Westminster that belonged to Seima Munemitsu and his family. From May 1942 to September 1945, the federal government held the Munemitsus at the Poston camp in Arizona.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Parents of children the schools refused, 1893 to 1933" -->
A public school could refuse a disabled child in these years, because no law required it to teach one. The rule came from a Massachusetts case decided on 4 January 1893. In 1885 the school committee of Cambridge had put a boy named John A. Watson out of the public schools. Its record said he was excluded "because he was too weak-minded to derive profit from instruction," and that he was "troublesome to other children, making uncouth noises, pinching others." The judges of the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled that the committee's decision was final and that no court could review it. That rule held through the whole of this era.

In 1933 parents in Cleveland, Ohio, founded the Cuyahoga County Council for the Retarded Child. "Retarded" was the word then used for children with intellectual disabilities. The county's own history describes it as a group led by parents and "founded to assist children of the region who had been excluded from their public schools."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A sit-in for jobs, New York, 1935" -->
In May 1935 six disabled New Yorkers sat down in the city's Home Relief Bureau and refused to leave until they were given a fair chance at government jobs. During the Depression, the federal Works Progress Administration, the WPA, gave out jobs to people who were out of work. The Home Relief Bureau stamped disabled people's applications "PH," for "physically handicapped." The stamp marked them as unable to work and kept them out of WPA jobs.

About 300 disabled people formed the League of the Physically Handicapped. Most of them lived with the effects of polio, a disease that can paralyze muscles, or of cerebral palsy, a condition from birth that affects movement. After the bureau's director refused to see them, six of them began the sit-in. Others picketed outside. Later they sat in at WPA headquarters too. One member said afterward: "What started it was finding out that jobs were available, that the government was handing out jobs ... everybody was getting jobs ... those of us who were militant just refused to accept the fact that we were the only people who were looked upon as not worthy, not capable of work."

By 1936 about 1,500 jobs had been created for disabled people in New York City. The league broke up by 1938. In August 1935 lawmakers in Congress passed the Social Security Act. It defined disability as "inability to engage in substantial gainful work," so a person who received disability insurance could not easily take a job.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charles Hamilton Houston's plan: law schools first, 1935 to 1949" -->
Charles Hamilton Houston planned the NAACP's attack on segregated schools as a series of court cases, starting with law schools. He was born in Washington, D.C., on 3 September 1895. He was the first Black editor of the *Harvard Law Review*. In 1929 he became dean of Howard University's law school, where he trained Black lawyers to fight segregation in court. One of his students was Thurgood Marshall. In the 1930s Houston became the NAACP's first special counsel, its chief lawyer. His plan, in his words, was to "start with the law schools and higher education, and integrate schools from the top down."

The first case was Donald Gaines Murray's. On 24 January 1935 he applied to the University of Maryland School of Law and was refused because he was Black. Houston, Marshall and a Baltimore lawyer, Nicholas Gosnell, took his case to the Baltimore City Court. Judge Eugene O'Dunne ordered the university's president, Raymond A. Pearson, to admit him. The Maryland Court of Appeals agreed on 15 January 1936, and Murray graduated in 1938. The second case, Lloyd Gaines's in Missouri, went to the Supreme Court in 1938.

Ada Lois Sipuel was born in Chickasha, Oklahoma, on 8 February 1924. On 14 January 1946 she applied to the University of Oklahoma College of Law. The university's president, George Lynn Cross, put in writing that there was no academic reason to reject her. He refused her because state law forbade teaching Black and white students in the same room. Thurgood Marshall took her case, and the Supreme Court decided it on 12 January 1948. Oklahoma's leaders answered by setting up a separate law school for Black students in five days, in rooms at the State Capitol. She was admitted to the University of Oklahoma on 18 June 1949, three years and five months after she applied, and graduated in August 1952. Houston died on 22 April 1950.

### Lloyd Gaines

> **Who:** A history graduate who sued to study law in his own state, won the NAACP's first school case at the Supreme Court, and then disappeared.
> **When and where:** Missouri, 1935 to 1938. Last seen in Chicago, March 1939.

Lloyd Gaines won the first school case the NAACP ever won at the Supreme Court. Missouri's leaders answered by building a separate law school, and a few months later Gaines disappeared. He has never been found.

He was born in 1911 at Water Valley, Mississippi. His father, Henry Richard Gaines, was a tenant farmer who had been a teacher. His mother was Callie Gaines. He had ten brothers and sisters. He finished Vashon High School in St. Louis in 1931 and spent a year at Stowe Teachers College. In August 1935 he graduated with honors from Lincoln University, Missouri's college for Black students, with a degree in history.

He then applied to the University of Missouri School of Law. The registrar, S. W. Canada, sent him to the president of Lincoln University. Lincoln's president pointed him to a Missouri law that paid a Black student's fees to study law in a neighboring state. Missouri's officials offered to pay for him to attend law school in Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa or Illinois rather than let him into its own.

He refused the offer and sued. Charles Hamilton Houston of the NAACP and a St. Louis lawyer, Sidney Redmond, took his case. They filed it in the circuit court on 24 January 1936. The circuit court ruled against him in July 1936, and the Missouri Supreme Court ruled against him on 9 December 1937.

The justices of the Supreme Court of the United States heard the case on 9 November 1938 and decided it on 12 December 1938, six to two, for Gaines. Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote: "The State was bound to furnish him within its borders facilities for legal education substantially equal to those which the State there afforded for persons of the white race." A state could keep its promise of equal protection, he wrote, "only where its laws operate, that is, within its own jurisdiction." Justice James McReynolds dissented, which means he disagreed with the ruling, and Justice Pierce Butler joined him.

Missouri's lawmakers did not let Gaines into the university. In 1939 they created a separate law school for Black students at Lincoln University. Gaines never attended either school. He was last seen in Chicago in March 1939, at a fraternity house, and he left a bag of his belongings behind. No one formally reported him missing until October 1939. In 2007 FBI agents reopened the case as part of their work on unsolved civil rights crimes.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marian Anderson sings at the Lincoln Memorial, 1939" -->
In 1939 the Daughters of the American Revolution barred the Black singer Marian Anderson from singing in their hall in Washington, Constitution Hall. Its rules allowed only white performers. Interior Secretary Harold Ickes arranged for her to sing on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial instead. On Easter Sunday, 9 April 1939, about 75,000 people came. Ickes introduced her with the words "Genius draws no color line." She opened with "My Country 'Tis of Thee." At the memorial's dedication in 1922, the crowd had been seated by race, and the one Black speaker, Robert Russa Moton, had had his speech checked in advance.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="a-philip-randolph-rights-movements" name="A. Philip Randolph" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### A. Philip Randolph

> **Who:** A union leader who twice got a president to sign an order against discrimination by threatening to bring a march to Washington.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1941 and 1948.

A. Philip Randolph got President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1941, and President Harry Truman in 1948, to sign orders against racial discrimination. Each time he did it by planning a march on Washington, and he called off the first march once he had the order.

He was born on 15 April 1889 at Crescent City, Florida. In 1925 he founded the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, a union of the Black Pullman porters who worked on railroad sleeping cars. In 1935 it became the first African American union admitted to the American Federation of Labor, a national group of unions.

In 1941 the country was building weapons for the Second World War, and defense factories were hiring. Randolph and Bayard Rustin began organizing a march on Washington against discrimination in the defense industries. Randolph and other Black leaders met Eleanor Roosevelt and members of the president's cabinet. Randolph told them he would bring "ten, twenty, fifty thousand Negroes on the White House lawn."

On 25 June 1941 President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 8802. An executive order is a written order from the president to the people who work for the federal government. This one said, "There shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries and in Government, because of race, creed, color, or national origin." It set up a Committee on Fair Employment Practice to take complaints and investigate them. It covered federal agencies, companies with defense contracts and the unions working on them. Randolph called off the march. The National Archives says most historians judge that the committee did little, especially in the South. After the war, members of Congress refused to make it permanent.

In 1948 Randolph planned another march, this time against segregation in the armed forces. Black soldiers had fought the Second World War in units separated by race. On 26 July 1948 President Truman signed Executive Order 9981: "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." The military services resisted it. Nearly all of them were integrated by about 1953, the end of the Korean War.

Randolph did hold a march fifteen years later. He was the chief organizer of the March on Washington in 1963. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and died at his home in New York City on 16 May 1979, aged 90.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="About 120,000 people held without charges, 1942 to 1945" -->
In 1942 the federal government removed about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from their homes on the west coast and held them in camps. Not one of them was charged with a crime or given a trial. Japan had attacked Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on 7 December 1941.

On 19 February 1942 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It let Army commanders order people out of any area they chose. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, who commanded the Army on the west coast, used it against people of Japanese ancestry. He set curfews that applied only to Japanese Americans, and he gave families as little as 48 hours' notice to leave their homes. A curfew is an order to stay indoors during set hours. On 21 March 1942 Congress passed a law making it a crime to disobey his orders, punishable by up to a year in prison and a $5,000 fine. The federal courts count more than 120,000 people removed, two out of three of them American citizens. The National Archives counts about 122,000, of whom nearly 70,000 were citizens.

They were held first in temporary centers such as the Tanforan racetrack near San Francisco, and then in ten camps built inland, in Wyoming, California, Utah, Arizona, Colorado, Idaho and Arkansas. One of the ten was Topaz, the Central Utah Relocation Center, in the desert 15 miles northwest of Delta, Utah. It opened on 11 September 1942 and closed on 31 October 1945. More than 11,000 people passed through it, most of them from around San Francisco, and more than 8,000 lived there at once at its fullest. The camp had 42 blocks, each with 12 barracks and 250 to 300 people. The barracks were built of pine boards covered with tarpaper. Each family was issued a pot-bellied stove, army cots, blankets and mattress covers. The people of each block shared one dining hall and one building of washrooms, toilets and laundry. A fence with seven watchtowers surrounded the camp.

On 11 April 1943, James Hatsuki Wakasa, a 63-year-old man born in Japan, was walking his dog inside the fence at Topaz. Private First Class Gerald Philpott, the military policeman in Guard Tower 8, shot him dead. Philpott said Wakasa had been too close to the fence and had not turned back when warned. People who knew Wakasa believed he was hard of hearing. Investigators found that his body lay several feet inside the fence and that he had been facing the guard when he was shot. Army officers tried Philpott at a court-martial, a military trial, for manslaughter, which means killing a person without planning to, and found him not guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four cases at the Supreme Court, and the one that won, 1943 and 1944" -->
Four Japanese Americans took the government's orders to the Supreme Court. Three of them lost, and one, Mitsuye Endo, won. Gordon Hirabayashi and Minoru Yasui each broke the curfew on purpose so that they could challenge it in court. In 1943 the justices upheld the curfew in both cases and put off the question of whether the removal itself was lawful.

They answered that question on 18 December 1944, in Fred Korematsu's case, and they upheld the removal by six votes to three. Justice Hugo Black wrote for the majority that "all legal restrictions which curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect" and needed "the most rigid scrutiny." Then he allowed this one. Justice Frank Murphy dissented. He called the removal "the legalization of racism." He compared it to the "abhorrent and despicable treatment of minority groups by the dictatorial tyrannies which this nation is now pledged to destroy." Justice Robert Jackson also dissented. He wrote that Korematsu had been convicted for "being present in the state whereof he is a citizen."

On the same day, the justices ruled in Mitsuye Endo's case, and the government lost. Endo was born in Sacramento, California, on 10 May 1920, and worked as a clerk for the state of California. A lawyer, James Purcell, chose her to bring the case because a judge would find it hard to call her disloyal. She worked for the state, she was a Christian, she spoke only English, she had never been to Japan, and her brother was in the United States Army. On 12 July 1942 Purcell filed a petition for habeas corpus for her in the federal court in San Francisco.

While the case went on, the War Relocation Authority, the agency that ran the camps, offered to let her out if she dropped it. She refused and stayed in the camps, because leaving would have ended the case for everyone else. She was moved from the Sacramento assembly center to Tule Lake and then to Topaz. Justice William O. Douglas wrote for the Court that "Mitsuye Endo should be given her liberty." The government, he wrote, "has no authority to subject citizens who are concededly loyal to its leave procedure." On 17 December 1944, the day before the rulings, the Army had issued Public Proclamation No. 21, which ended the exclusion from the west coast on 2 January 1945. The last camp, Tule Lake, closed on 20 March 1946.

Endo moved to Chicago in May 1945 and worked as a secretary for the Mayor's Committee on Race Relations. She married Kenneth Tsutsumi, raised three children and died in Chicago on 14 April 2006.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fred-korematsu" name="Fred Korematsu" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Fred Korematsu

> **Who:** A welder who refused to leave his home when Japanese Americans were ordered out, lost at the Supreme Court, and had his conviction thrown out in 1983.
> **When and where:** Oakland, California, 1942. San Francisco, 1983.

Fred Korematsu stayed in his home town when the Army ordered people of Japanese ancestry out, and he went to prison and then to court for it. He lost in 1944, and a federal judge threw out his conviction 39 years later.

He was born in Oakland, California, on 30 January 1919, and named Toyosaburo. A teacher suggested he call himself Fred. His father, Kakusaburo, came to the United States in 1905, and his mother, Kotsui, came through the immigration station at Angel Island in 1914. He had three brothers. The family ran the Stonehurst Flower Nursery in East Oakland.

When the orders came in 1942, his family went to the camps and he did not. He was 23. He had a girlfriend, Ida Boitano, and a job as a welder. He got identification papers in a false name, Clyde Sarah, and kept working. On 30 May 1942 FBI agents arrested him on the street with his girlfriend. The charge was breaking Civilian Exclusion Order No. 34, the Army's order for people of Japanese ancestry to leave his area.

Ernest Besig, a lawyer with the American Civil Liberties Union, visited him in the Army stockade at the Presidio in San Francisco and asked to take his case to the higher courts. Korematsu agreed. He was convicted and given five years' probation, which meant he stayed free under a court's watch. Then Army officers sent him to the Tanforan racetrack on 28 June 1942, and to the Topaz camp with his family that September. The Supreme Court heard his case on 11 October 1944 and ruled against him on 18 December 1944.

After he left the camp, he moved to Detroit and married Kathryn Pearson. They came back to California and raised two children.

In the early 1980s, researchers found that the government's own lawyers had hidden evidence from the Supreme Court. Intelligence reports had told them that Japanese Americans were no military threat, and they had kept those reports out of the case. A team of lawyers who worked without pay reopened his case. On 10 November 1983, in the same San Francisco courthouse where he had been convicted, Judge Marilyn Hall Patel ruled that "the conviction that was handed down in this court and affirmed by the Supreme Court in *Korematsu v. United States* is ... vacated and the underlying indictment dismissed." Vacated means cancelled.

When people asked him about a pardon, he said, "I don't want a pardon. If anything, I should be pardoning the government." He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998. He died at Larkspur, California, on 30 March 2005, aged 86. California marks 30 January as Fred Korematsu Day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fred-korematsu" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elizabeth Peratrovich and the Alaska law, 1945" -->
On 16 February 1945 Alaska's territorial government approved the Alaska Anti-Discrimination Act, after the Tlingit leader Elizabeth Peratrovich spoke for it before the territory's lawmakers. It was the first law against discrimination in the United States or its territories. Businesses in Alaska had put up signs such as "No Natives Allowed," and the act was aimed at them. Peratrovich belonged to the Lukaax.ádi clan of the Tlingit. For half a century the Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded at Sitka in 1912, and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, founded in 1915, were the only civil rights groups for Alaska Natives. Alaska marks 16 February each year as Elizabeth Peratrovich Day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A soldier blinded on his way home, and the president's committee, 1946" -->
On 12 February 1946 Sergeant Isaac Woodard was discharged from the Army after four years and took a bus home to South Carolina. After he argued with the bus driver, police at Batesburg, South Carolina, took him off the bus. Police Chief Lynwood Shull and Officer Elliot Long beat him with blackjacks, short heavy clubs, and he said they drove the ends of the clubs into his eyes. He was blind for the rest of his life. In November 1946 an all-white federal jury took thirty minutes to find Shull not guilty.

On 19 September 1946, Walter White of the NAACP led a group to see President Harry Truman and told him what had been done to Woodard. "My God! I had no idea that it was as terrible as that! We've got to do something!" Truman said. On 5 December 1946 he signed Executive Order 9808, which set up the President's Committee on Civil Rights. The committee's report, *To Secure These Rights*, came out in 1947.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1950-2000" order="09" chapter="rights-movements" label="1950 to 2000" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Black Americans, women, Mexican Americans, gay Americans and disabled Americans all ran organized campaigns in the second half of the 1900s, and each won changes in the country's laws. In 1954 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled segregated public schools unconstitutional, and in 1956 they struck down segregation on Montgomery's buses. Members of Congress outlawed literacy tests for voting in 1965. They barred sex discrimination in schools that took federal money in 1972. In 1990 they made it illegal to refuse a disabled person a job or a way into a public building.

Each of those changes followed years of organizing, and the organizers paid for it. People who signed lawsuits lost their jobs. Police beat marchers with nightsticks, and firemen turned high-pressure hoses on children. Members of the Ku Klux Klan killed four girls with a bomb in a Birmingham church in 1963, and a Neshoba County deputy sheriff was among the men convicted over the killing of three civil rights workers in 1964. An Alabama state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson in 1965. Doctors operated on Black women and girls without their consent so that they could never have children. Federal officials pushed thousands of gay people out of government jobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The law school cases, 1950" -->
The court campaign that Charles Hamilton Houston planned in the 1930s won two more cases in June 1950, six weeks after he died. On 5 June 1950 the justices of the Supreme Court decided both cases, and all of them agreed on both.

Heman Marion Sweatt had applied to the University of Texas Law School in February 1946 and was turned away because he was Black. Texas officials then opened a separate law school for Black students in Austin in February 1947. It had four part-time teachers borrowed from the university and no full-time librarian. It had ordered about 10,000 books, and few had arrived. No body of lawyers had approved it as a real law school. Chief Justice Fred Vinson wrote that the education it offered was not "substantially equal" to what Sweatt would get at the university. "Equal protection of the laws is not achieved through indiscriminate imposition of inequalities," he wrote. Texas had to admit Sweatt.

George W. McLaurin already had a master's degree and wanted a doctorate in education. The University of Oklahoma let him in and then made rules for him alone. The Court's opinion lists them. He "was required to sit apart at a designated desk in an anteroom adjoining the classroom." He had to sit at an assigned desk on the library's balcony floor, "but not to use the desks in the regular reading room." In the cafeteria he had "to sit at a designated table and to eat at a different time from the other students." Before the case was argued, university officials eased the rules a little. McLaurin could sit in a marked row in the classroom and eat at the usual hours, still at a table of his own. The justices struck down the whole arrangement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What signing cost in Clarendon County, 1949 and 1950" -->
In Clarendon County, South Carolina, 107 Black parents and children signed a petition against the county's schools, and white employers, merchants and lenders punished them for it. School District 22 ran more than 30 school buses for white children and none for Black children. The parents began by asking for one bus. The court record describes the all-white Summerton High School as "modern, safe, sanitary, well equipped, lighted and healthy." It describes the Black schools as "inadequate…unhealthy…old and overcrowded and in a dilapidated condition."

The Reverend Joseph A. DeLaine, a minister and teacher, brought the NAACP together with the Pearson brothers to start the case. Six white men later attacked DeLaine. He got away by pretending he had a gun in his pocket and jumping into the passing truck of another man who had signed.

The petition went round for signatures on 11 November 1949, and the lawsuit was filed on 16 May 1950. It is named for Harry Briggs, whose name was first on the list. The National Park Service records what happened to the signers. They were "fired from their jobs, denied service when trying to purchase supplies for their farms, or being turned down for loans on their properties."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-johns" name="Barbara Rose Johns" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara Rose Johns

> **Who:** A sixteen-year-old who got her principal out of the building with a false phone call and led her whole school out on strike, three years before *Brown*.
> **When and where:** Farmville, Prince Edward County, Virginia, 23 April 1951.

Barbara Rose Johns was sixteen when she led the students of her all-Black high school out on strike against their building. The strike led to a lawsuit that became one of the five cases the Supreme Court decided as *Brown v. Board of Education*.

She was born in New York City on 6 March 1935 and grew up in Prince Edward County, Virginia. She went to Robert Russa Moton High School in Farmville, the county's high school for Black students. The building had been made for 180 students, and it held between 360 and 450. Classes met in the auditorium, in school buses and in three wooden sheds wrapped in tar paper, which people compared to chicken coops. The school had no cafeteria, no science laboratory and no gym. One day a half-empty school bus for white children passed her without stopping, and she decided to act.

On 23 April 1951 a student named John Watson telephoned the principal's office and disguised his voice. He said Moton students were making trouble at the bus station downtown. The principal, M. Boyd Jones, left to see. While he was gone, Johns called the whole school to the auditorium and led the students out. They stayed out for two weeks.

She wrote to two NAACP lawyers in Richmond, Oliver W. Hill and Spottswood William Robinson III. They agreed to take the case on one condition. The parents had to sue to end segregation itself, rather than ask for a better Black school. On 3 May 1951 the Reverend L. Francis Griffin called the parents to a meeting at the First Baptist Church in Farmville to hear the offer. They agreed. The lawsuit, *Davis v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, was filed on 23 May 1951 by 74 parents for 118 Moton students.

Her parents sent her out of the county. She finished her last year of high school in Montgomery, Alabama, living with her uncle, the Reverend Vernon Johns, and his wife, Altona. Her family's house in Prince Edward County later burned down, and the family believed someone had set the fire. Barbara Rose Johns died of bone cancer in Philadelphia on 25 September 1991, aged 56.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-johns" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Brown v. Board of Education, 17 May 1954" -->
On 17 May 1954, in *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka*, all nine justices of the Supreme Court ruled that separating children by race in public schools broke the 14th Amendment. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote, "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." Inherently means by their very nature. With it, the justices rejected the reasoning of *Plessy v. Ferguson* for public schools, 58 years after that case.

*Brown* was five lawsuits bundled together by the Court. They were *Brown v. Board of Education* from Topeka, Kansas, *Briggs v. Elliott* from Clarendon County, South Carolina, *Davis v. County School Board* from Prince Edward County, Virginia, *Belton v. Gebhart* from Delaware, and *Bolling v. Sharpe* from Washington, D.C.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rosa-parks-mlk" name="Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr." movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr.

> **Who:** The NAACP branch secretary whose arrest set off the Montgomery bus boycott, and the 26-year-old minister the boycotters chose to lead it.
> **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, December 1955 to December 1956.

Rosa Parks had been the secretary of the Montgomery NAACP for twelve years on the day she was arrested for keeping her bus seat. Black women in Montgomery had been organizing about the buses for six years, and they turned her arrest into a boycott that lasted more than a year.

She was born in Tuskegee, Alabama, on 4 February 1913, and married Raymond Parks in 1932. In the early 1940s she started the Montgomery NAACP's Youth Council, and she was the branch's secretary from 1943 to 1956. As secretary of the NAACP's Alabama State Conference, she traveled the state interviewing victims of discrimination and witnesses to lynchings. She had broken the bus seating rules many times before, and a driver had once put her off a bus for it.

Nine months before Parks's arrest, on 2 March 1955, a fifteen-year-old student named Claudette Colvin refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus when white passengers got on. She told police she had a constitutional right to sit anywhere on the bus, as white passengers did. The officers swore at her, hit her with a nightstick and dragged her off the bus. E. D. Nixon, a Montgomery organizer, and others decided not to build a campaign around her case. Nixon thought she was too young, and when she became pregnant later that year he looked for someone who fit the conservative customs of the time. Colvin believed the choice also had to do with her family's standing outside Montgomery's Black middle class.

Police arrested Rosa Parks on 1 December 1955 for refusing to give up her bus seat. The Women's Political Council, a group of Black professional women formed in 1949, had already been pressing the city about the buses. Its leader, Jo Ann Robinson, had proposed a boycott more than a year before. After Parks's arrest, council members printed 50,000 leaflets calling for a one-day boycott on Monday, 5 December.

After that Monday, Black riders stayed off the buses. The same day, Montgomery's Black ministers and organizers formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and elected Martin Luther King Jr. as its president. He was 26, the pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, and still finishing his doctorate at Boston University. About 40,000 Black people lived in Montgomery. For a year, most of them walked to work or school, shared cars, took taxis or hitchhiked.

City leaders indicted nearly 100 of the boycott's leaders on charges of conspiracy. On 30 January 1956, someone bombed King's house while his wife, Coretta Scott King, their seven-week-old daughter, Yolanda, and a neighbor were inside. No one was hurt, and no one was ever prosecuted for the bombing. On 1 February a bomb went off in E. D. Nixon's yard.

The lawsuit that ended bus segregation in Montgomery was brought by four other women. On 1 February 1956 the lawyer Fred Gray filed *Browder v. Gayle* for Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Mary Louise Smith and Claudette Colvin, who was then sixteen. In June 1956 federal judges in Montgomery struck down Alabama's bus segregation laws, and in November the Supreme Court agreed. Black riders went back on the buses on 21 December 1956. Colvin later worked as a nurse's aide in New York for fifty years. Her 1955 arrest record was erased on 16 December 2022, and she died on 13 January 2026, aged 86.

Rosa Parks lost her job as a tailor and received death threats. She and Raymond moved to Detroit in 1957. She worked in the office of Representative John Conyers from 1965 to 1988, and she died in Detroit on 24 October 2005.

King led the movement for twelve more years. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, at 35. In 1968 he went to Memphis, Tennessee, twice to support 1,300 Black sanitation workers on strike. On 1 February 1968, two of them, Echol Cole and Robert Walker, had been crushed to death in the compactor of their garbage truck. The strikers carried signs that read "I AM A MAN." King was shot in Memphis on 4 April 1968 and died at 7:05 that evening at St. Joseph's Hospital. He was 39. After his death, city officials recognized the workers' union. James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to King's murder in March 1969 and was sentenced to 99 years. For the rest of his life Ray tried to take back his plea, until he died in April 1998. In 1999 a jury in a civil case brought by the King family found that a Memphis man, Jowers, and "others, including government agencies" had plotted the killing. In 2000 a Justice Department investigation found those claims not credible.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rosa-parks-mlk" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="After Brown: Little Rock, Prince Edward County and New Orleans, 1957 to 1964" -->
After *Brown*, Black families still had to force their way into white schools one district at a time, and white officials fought them with the police, the city council and the county budget.

In Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1957, nine Black students entered Central High School. Daisy Bates organized them. She was president of the Arkansas State Conference of the NAACP, and with her husband, L. C. Bates, she published a newspaper, the *Arkansas State Press*. The nine students met at her house every school day and left for Central High from there. In August 1957 a rock came through her living room window with a note tied to it: "STONE THIS TIME. DYNAMITE NEXT." Crosses were burned in her front yard twice, and someone fired shots into the house from a passing car. The records do not name the people who did it.

Then the city councils of Little Rock and North Little Rock passed rules that made every organization hand its list of members to the city clerk, to be made public. Daisy Bates kept the records of the Little Rock branch of the NAACP. She refused to hand over the list and was convicted for refusing. On 23 February 1960 the Supreme Court threw out her conviction. Justice Potter Stewart wrote that keeping a group's members private "may in many circumstances be indispensable to preservation of freedom of association, particularly where a group espouses dissident beliefs." Dissident beliefs are beliefs that go against those of the people in power.

Prince Edward County, Virginia, was home to the Moton students' case. On 26 June 1959 the county's board of supervisors voted to stop paying for public schools. The county had no public schools until 8 September 1964. White children went to a private school, Prince Edward Academy. About 2,000 Black children went without formal schooling for five years. In 1963 Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy said "the only places on earth not to provide free public education are Communist China, North Vietnam, Sarawak, Singapore, British Honduras." Then he added, "and Prince Edward County, Virginia." On 25 May 1964 the Supreme Court ordered the county to reopen its schools, in a case named for the Reverend L. Francis Griffin.

In New Orleans, a federal judge, J. Skelly Wright, ordered the city's schools desegregated in the spring of 1960. On 14 November 1960, four Black six-year-old girls went into two white schools. Ruby Bridges went alone into William Frantz Elementary. Leona Tate, Tessie Prevost and Gail Etienne went together into McDonogh 19, in the Lower Ninth Ward, with federal marshals walking them in. White parents took their children out. By the end of the month, fewer than ten white children attended the two schools combined. From January to May 1961, Ruby Bridges was the only pupil at William Frantz.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Levittown, 1957: a family moves in" -->
In August 1957 Daisy and William Myers became the first Black family to live in Levittown, Pennsylvania, and white neighbors attacked their home for nearly three months. William Myers was an Army veteran of the Second World War, and Daisy Myers was a teacher. They had three young children.

In the first Levittown, in New York, the standard lease had barred anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race" from living in a house, except white servants. In 1948 the Supreme Court ruled in *Shelley v. Kraemer* that courts could not enforce clauses like that. The builder, William Levitt, dropped the written clause and kept selling only to white buyers.

When the Myers family moved in, mobs gathered outside the house. People threw rocks through the windows, burned crosses and made death threats by telephone. Delivery men stopped bringing milk, bread and heating oil. State police guarded the house. A documentary, *Crisis in Levittown*, was filmed while it was happening, in 1957. Daisy Myers later wrote a book about it, *Sticks 'n Stones*, published in 2005.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sit-ins and Freedom Rides, 1960 and 1961" -->
In 1960 and 1961, Black and white students used their own bodies to test segregation, first at lunch counters and then on buses. On 1 February 1960, four freshmen from the Agricultural and Technical College of North Carolina walked into the Woolworth store in Greensboro. Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil, David Richmond and Ezell Blair Jr. sat at the lunch counter, which was for white customers only, ordered coffee and waited. The waitress and the manager ignored them. A police officer walked up and down behind them. Some white customers shouted at them, and others encouraged them.

The next day 19 students came. The day after that 85 came, from several colleges. Before the week was out there were 400, taking turns so they could still go to class. Students started sit-ins in seven other North Carolina cities and in Hampton, Virginia, and Nashville, Tennessee. The Greensboro counter was opened to Black customers on 25 July 1960. By that summer 33 Southern cities had opened their restaurants and lunch counters, and a year later 126 had.

In 1960, in *Boynton v. Virginia*, the Supreme Court struck down Virginia laws that required segregation in travel between states. The Congress of Racial Equality, known as CORE, organized riders to find out whether bus stations in the South would obey. On 4 May 1961, eleven Black and white Freedom Riders left Washington, D.C., in two groups.

On 14 May 1961, at Anniston, Alabama, a white mob that included members of the Ku Klux Klan attacked the Greyhound bus. They slashed its tires, followed it out of town and firebombed it. The riders were trapped on the burning bus. They got out after an explosion, and then the mob beat them. The Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth organized a line of cars and got them to Birmingham within a few hours. Accounts name different men as the mob's leaders, and the records found do not show that anyone went to prison for burning the bus.

The same day, young white men climbed onto the second bus, a Trailways bus, at Anniston and beat the riders until they moved into segregated seats. When that bus reached Birmingham, Klansmen attacked the riders at the station. Bull Connor, Birmingham's public safety commissioner, had ordered his police to stay away from the station while it happened. Photographs of the burning bus were printed around the world. Over the summer of 1961 the number of Freedom Riders grew to more than 400.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fannie-lou-hamer" name="Fannie Lou Hamer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fannie Lou Hamer

> **Who:** A plantation timekeeper in Mississippi who tried to register to vote, lost her job the same day, was beaten in a jail for her organizing, and told the country about it on national television.
> **When and where:** Ruleville, Sunflower County, Mississippi, 1962 and 1963. Atlantic City, New Jersey, 22 August 1964.

Fannie Lou Hamer was fired for trying to register to vote, and beaten in a Mississippi jail for helping others do it. In 1964 she described the beating on national television, to the Democratic Party's leaders, while they decided whether to seat her delegation.

She was born in Montgomery County, Mississippi, on 6 October 1917, the youngest of the twenty children of Ella and James Lee Townsend. She began working in the cotton fields at six, weeding first and then picking. In 1944 she married Perry Hamer, whom people called Pap. For eighteen years she worked on the W. D. Marlow plantation outside Ruleville, keeping the time records for the other workers.

In 1961 she went into a hospital, and a doctor performed a hysterectomy on her without asking her or telling her. A hysterectomy is an operation that cuts out the womb, the organ in which a baby grows. After it, a woman can never become pregnant, and it cannot be undone. Hamer's name for these operations, a "Mississippi appendectomy," came into use for them. The records do not name the surgeon.

On 31 August 1962 she and seventeen others went 26 miles to the Sunflower County courthouse in Indianola to register to vote. Police let only two of them in to take the literacy test, a reading and writing test that registrars used to turn Black voters away. That evening the plantation owner told her, "If you don't go down and withdraw your registration, you will have to leave." She answered, "I didn't try to register for you. I tried to register for myself." She lost her job that day. On 10 September someone fired sixteen bullets into the Tucker family's house in Ruleville, and other houses were shot at the same night.

On 9 June 1963 she was one of ten people coming home by Continental Trailways bus from a voter registration workshop. At the bus station in Winona, Mississippi, some of the group tried to use the restaurant and the washroom. Winona's police chief, Thomas Herrod, ordered them arrested. In the jail, state highway patrolmen made two Black prisoners, Roosevelt Knox and Sol Poe, beat her with a blackjack. The patrolmen ordered her to lie face down on a bunk. The first prisoner beat her until he was exhausted. Then the patrolman gave the blackjack to the second prisoner and made the first one sit on her feet so she could not move them. "I began to scream and one white man got up and began to beat me in my head and tell me to hush," she said. The officers also had June Johnson, who was fifteen, and Annell Ponder beaten. Hamer lost the sight of one eye and had kidney damage for the rest of her life. "After I got out of jail, half dead, I found that Medgar Evers had been shot down in his own yard," she said.

In December 1963, federal prosecutors tried five white Winona and Montgomery County officers, among them Chief Herrod, for violating the prisoners' civil rights. An all-white, all-male jury found them not guilty on every count.

Mississippi's Democratic Party leaders kept Black people out of the party. Hamer and others founded the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, and in August 1964 they went to the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City to challenge the all-white Mississippi delegation. She was the group's vice chair. On 22 August she told the convention's credentials committee what had been done to her, and television carried it across the country. The National Archives records that her testimony "was interrupted by President Lyndon Johnson, in an effort to suppress the truth and stabilize the Democratic Party." She ended, "All of this is on account of we want to register, to become first-class citizens. And if the Freedom Democratic Party is not seated now, I question America. Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave, where we have to sleep with our telephones off the hooks because our lives be threatened daily, because we want to live as decent human beings, in America?"

In 1969 she started the Freedom Farm to grow food and give families in Sunflower County a living. She died on 14 March 1977, at 59, of heart failure brought on by cancer, diabetes and high blood pressure. The Equal Justice Initiative records that the kidney damage from the beating contributed to her death. She used to say, "Nobody's free until everybody's free."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fannie-lou-hamer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Birmingham, 1963: the children and the church" -->
In May 1963 Birmingham, Alabama, police arrested hundreds of Black children for marching against segregation, and firemen turned hoses on them. That September, members of the Ku Klux Klan killed four girls with a bomb at the church the marches had started from.

On 2 May 1963, students aged eight to eighteen gathered at the 16th Street Baptist Church and marched downtown to ask the mayor to end segregation. On the orders of Bull Connor, the public safety commissioner, police arrested them. The National Park Service gives two counts for that day. One page says 600 children were arrested, some as young as six. Another says 1,200. By the next day at least 1,000 children were in jail. Over the following days Connor's police jailed more than 3,000 demonstrators.

On 3 May Connor ordered fire hoses and police dogs turned on the marchers. The National Park Service describes it: "Firemen blasted the protestors, including children, with high-pressure fire hoses, and police used their nightsticks indiscriminately. Police K-9 units loosed their dogs into crowds of peaceful demonstrators, pinning down one woman and severely wounding three teenagers." These were the hoses firemen used on burning buildings, and the water came out hard enough to knock people off their feet.

On Sunday, 15 September 1963, just before 11 in the morning, a bomb went off under the steps of the 16th Street Baptist Church. It killed four girls: Addie Mae Collins, Carole Robertson and Cynthia Wesley, who were fourteen, and Denise McNair, who was eleven. A few minutes before, they had been together in the basement women's room in their Sunday clothes, ready to sing and to show people to their seats at the adult service, talking about the new school year. Addie Mae's sister, Sarah, survived and lost her right eye.

The bombers were members of the Ku Klux Klan. FBI agents named Robert Chambliss as their main suspect. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover closed the investigation in 1968 without charging anyone and ordered the evidence sealed. Investigators reopened the case after Hoover died in 1972. Chambliss was convicted on 14 November 1977, fourteen years after the bombing, and died in prison in 1985. Thomas Blanton was convicted on 1 May 2001 and given four life sentences. He died in prison in 2020. Bobby Frank Cherry was convicted in 2002, 39 years after the bombing. A fourth Klansman, Herman Cash, died in 1994 without being tried.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organizers killed in Mississippi, 1963 and 1964" -->
White men in Mississippi killed civil rights organizers in 1963 and 1964, and for decades no jury in the state convicted anyone of murder for it.

Medgar Evers was born at Decatur, Mississippi, on 2 July 1925, and fought in Germany and France in the Second World War. From 1954 he was the NAACP's field secretary for the state of Mississippi. He led marches, prayer vigils, boycotts and drives to register voters. He worked to get James Meredith admitted to the University of Mississippi in 1962.

In the early hours of 12 June 1963, Byron De La Beckwith, a member of the White Citizens' Council, shot Evers in the back in the driveway of his home in Jackson. The Citizens' Council was a group founded in Mississippi in 1954, after *Brown*, to keep segregation in place. Evers's wife, Myrlie, and their children had waited up for him and were watching television. When they heard the shot, the children did what they had practiced: they dropped to the floor and crawled to the bathroom. Myrlie found her husband collapsed and bleeding, still holding the T-shirts he had carried in from the car. He died at University Hospital from his wounds and loss of blood. Beckwith was tried twice in 1964, and both juries failed to agree on a verdict. On 5 February 1994, 31 years and three trials after the killing, a jury convicted him, and he was sentenced to life in prison. He died on 21 January 2001, aged 80.

In the summer of 1964, hundreds of volunteers came to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools. It was called Freedom Summer. On 21 June 1964, three of the workers went to look at a Black church that had been burned after a civil rights meeting was held there. James Chaney, 21, was a Black college student from Mississippi. Andrew Goodman, 20, and Michael Schwerner, 24, were white New Yorkers working with CORE. Local police arrested the three for "speeding" and held them in jail. The National Archives records that they were "released after dark into the hands of the Ku Klux Klan."

Searchers found their bodies on 4 August 1964, buried near Old Jolly Farm in Neshoba County. Goodman and Schwerner had each been shot once in the head. Chaney had two broken arms, a broken jaw, a crushed right shoulder and injuries to his groin.

Twenty-one men were arrested. On 20 October 1967 a federal jury convicted seven of the eighteen men on trial of violating the three workers' civil rights. They included Cecil Price, the Neshoba County deputy sheriff, and Samuel Bowers, the imperial wizard, or top leader, of the White Knights of the Ku Klux Klan of Mississippi. Their sentences ran from three to ten years. One juror would not convict Edgar Ray Killen, a former preacher and Klan leader who had organized the killings. On 21 June 2005, 41 years to the day after the killings, a Mississippi jury convicted Killen of manslaughter. He was sentenced to 60 years and died in the state prison at Parchman on 11 January 2018.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The March on Washington, 28 August 1963" -->
About 250,000 people came to Washington on 28 August 1963 for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. The National Park Service counts about 190,000 of them as Black and about 60,000 as white. A. Philip Randolph, who had threatened a march on Washington in 1941 and called it off, led this one. Bayard Rustin organized it in less than three months.

The organizers issued ten demands, among them fair wages, fair hiring and desegregated schools. All the speakers were men: Roy Wilkins of the NAACP, Whitney Young of the National Urban League, Martin Luther King Jr. of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, James Farmer of CORE, John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, the union leader Walter Reuther, and the clergymen Joachim Prinz, Eugene Carson Blake and Matthew Ahmann. Dorothy Height of the National Council of Negro Women helped plan the march. The National Park Service says she "operated in the background of this male dominated, leadership group."

John Lewis's speech was "toned down...at the request of white liberals and moderate black allies," the Park Service records. What he said included, "we do not want our freedom gradually but we want to be free now."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bayard-rustin" name="Bayard Rustin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bayard Rustin

> **Who:** The organizer who planned the March on Washington in under three months, and whom a California court had jailed for being gay.
> **When and where:** Born at West Chester, Pennsylvania, 17 March 1912. Los Angeles, 1953. Washington, D.C., 1963.

Bayard Rustin planned the 1963 March on Washington, and he did most of his work out of public view because a 1953 conviction for having sex with men was used against him for the rest of his life. California's governor pardoned him in 2020, 33 years after he died.

He was born in West Chester, Pennsylvania, on 17 March 1912. He was raised a Quaker, in a family already doing civil rights work, and he was a pacifist all his life, a person who refuses to take part in war or violence. In 1942, in California, he visited Japanese Americans held at the Manzanar camp and reported on the conditions there.

In 1947 he and George Houser organized the Journey of Reconciliation, a bus trip to test a Supreme Court ruling that banned racial discrimination in travel between states. The National Park Service calls it the first of the Freedom Rides. Rustin was arrested for breaking a state law on segregated seating and served 22 days on a chain gang, a line of prisoners chained at the ankles and made to work outdoors.

On 22 January 1953 the Los Angeles County Superior Court convicted him of vagrancy, a crime that means wandering with no job or home. What he had done was have sex with other men who agreed to it. The judge sentenced him to 60 days in jail and ordered him to register as a sex offender. California's governor wrote in 2020 that "Mr. Rustin was sentenced pursuant to a charge commonly used to punish gay men for engaging in consensual adult sexual conduct." The same document says his conviction caused "an injustice that was compounded by his political opponents' use of the record of this case to try to undermine him, his associates, and the civil rights movement."

He taught Martin Luther King Jr. how to practice nonviolent resistance, and he worked for years with A. Philip Randolph. Randolph made him deputy director of the March on Washington, and Rustin planned the whole of it.

He died in New York City on 24 August 1987, of a burst appendix, aged 75. On 8 August 2013 President Barack Obama awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom and called him "an unyielding activist for civil rights, dignity, and equality for all." On 4 February 2020 Governor Gavin Newsom of California pardoned him and began a process for pardoning others convicted in the same way.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two men climb the Gateway Arch, 1964" -->
On 14 July 1964, Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed 125 feet up the unfinished north leg of the Gateway Arch in St. Louis, Missouri. They were protesting the fact that Black workers were kept out of the jobs building it. Green belonged to CORE and later founded a group called ACTION. The National Park Service, which runs the Arch, now documents the protest itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selma and the Voting Rights Act, 1965" -->
In 1965 marchers in Selma, Alabama, campaigned for the right to vote, and state troopers, other officers and Klansmen attacked them. The 15th Amendment had barred states from denying the vote because of race since 1870. In much of the South, officials still used literacy tests to keep Black citizens from registering.

On 18 February 1965, during a march for voting rights in Marion, Alabama, State Trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Jimmie Lee Jackson in the stomach inside a cafe. Jackson was 26 and had been protecting his mother and grandfather. He died of an infection from the wound on 26 February. In May 2007 Fowler was indicted for murder. In 2010 he pleaded guilty to misdemeanor manslaughter, a minor charge, and served five months of a six-month sentence.

On Sunday, 7 March 1965, about 600 people, most of them Black, walked out of Brown Chapel in Selma to march to Montgomery, the state capital. At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, Major John Cloud of the Alabama State Troopers gave them two minutes to turn back. Then state troopers and other officers attacked them "with nightsticks and teargas," in the National Park Service's words. At least 50 marchers needed hospital care. The day is called Bloody Sunday.

On 9 March about 2,000 people marched again, led by King, and he turned them back at the bridge. That evening four men attacked three ministers outside a restaurant in Selma. One of the attackers hit the Reverend James Reeb of Boston in the head with a club, and Reeb died two days later. Elmer L. Cook, William Stanley Hoggle and Namon O'Neil Hoggle were tried for his murder, and on 10 December 1965 the jury acquitted all three. The prosecutor had allowed a man whose brother was a suspect in the attack to sit on the jury.

The march set out again on 21 March 1965 with more than 8,000 people. By the time it reached the capitol in Montgomery on 25 March, the crowd had grown to nearly 25,000. That night, Viola Liuzzo was driving on Highway 80 between Selma and Montgomery. She was a 39-year-old white mother of five from Detroit who had come to help. In Lowndes County, four members of the Ku Klux Klan fired into her car and killed her. Her passenger, Leroy Moton, a 19-year-old Black volunteer, survived. The four men were Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., William Orville Eaton, Eugene Thomas and Gary Thomas Rowe. Rowe was a paid FBI informant, and he was given immunity. Alabama juries did not convict the other three. A federal jury convicted them of violating her civil rights, and each was sentenced to ten years. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover called Liuzzo an "outside agitator" and spread rumors about her to the press. Her family sued the FBI in 1979, and a federal judge dismissed the suit in 1983.

President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965. It outlawed literacy tests and sent federal examiners into the places that had used them, with the power to register voters themselves. States and counties covered by the act had to get approval from federal officials in Washington before changing any voting rule. By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had registered, a third of them through the federal examiners. By the end of 1966, only four of the thirteen Southern states still had fewer than half of their Black citizens registered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chicano movement, 1954 to 1970" -->
Mexican Americans ran their own rights campaigns from the 1950s. Many called themselves Chicanos, and their campaigns the Chicano movement.

Their first major win came at the Supreme Court. A jury in Jackson County, Texas, convicted a Mexican American man, Pete Hernandez, of murder and sentenced him to life in prison. About 14 of every 100 people in the county had Mexican or Latin American surnames. In 25 years, no one with such a name had served on a jury commission, the board that chose jurors. More than 6,000 jurors had been called in that time, and none had such a name. On the courthouse grounds, one of the two men's toilets was marked "Colored Men" and "Hombres Aqui," which means "Men Here." On 3 May 1954, in *Hernandez v. Texas*, all nine justices ruled that shutting Mexican Americans out of juries broke the 14th Amendment. The ruling came two weeks before *Brown*.

From 1 to 8 March 1968, about 15,000 students walked out of seven high schools in East Los Angeles. About 60 of every 100 Mexican American students there left high school without finishing. Staff forbade students to speak Spanish and steered them into trade classes instead of classes that prepared them for college. The students asked for teaching in both languages, changes to what they were taught, and Mexican American principals. The Board of Education turned them down, saying the changes would cost too much. On 31 March police arrested thirteen organizers on charges of felony conspiracy, which means planning together to commit a serious crime. One of them, the teacher Sal Castro, was fired. He got his job back after months of sit-ins at the board's offices. On 2 October police arrested 35 people there.

On 29 August 1970, between 20,000 and 30,000 people marched through East Los Angeles in the National Chicano Moratorium. They were protesting how many Mexican Americans were being drafted, wounded and killed in the Vietnam War. About 1,500 police officers came to Laguna Park, fired tear gas into the crowd and beat protesters. Four people died that day, and 150 were arrested. Los Angeles County Sheriff's Deputy Thomas Wilson fired a tear gas projectile into the Silver Dollar Bar on Whittier Boulevard. It hit Rubén Salazar, a reporter for the *Los Angeles Times*, in the head and killed him. The coroner ruled his death a homicide, a killing by another person. District Attorney Evelle Younger decided not to charge Wilson. The city later renamed Laguna Park for Salazar.

In the same years, farmworkers organized under Larry Itliong, Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The women's movement and the Equal Rights Amendment, 1966 to 1982" -->
Women organized in the late 1960s for equal treatment at work and under the law, and they won some laws and lost a constitutional amendment by three states.

Since 1964, federal law had banned sex discrimination at work. On 30 June 1966, at a government conference on women in Washington, some delegates wanted the conference to demand that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the federal office in charge of that ban, enforce it. Conference officials ruled the motion out of order. That night 28 people met in the hotel room of Betty Friedan, the author of *The Feminine Mystique*, and founded the National Organization for Women, known as NOW. They set it up "to take action to bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society NOW."

The Equal Rights Amendment that Alice Paul wrote is one sentence long: "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Representative Martha Griffiths of Michigan pushed it through the House in 1971. Senators passed it on 22 March 1972, by 84 votes to 8. Three-fourths of the states, 38 of them, had to ratify it within seven years. Hawaii ratified first, and 35 states had ratified by 1977.

Phyllis Schlafly led the campaign against it, called STOP ERA, from 1972. She argued that the amendment would end women's exemption from the military draft, take away Social Security benefits paid to widows as dependent wives, and end mothers' advantage in custody cases. Lawmakers in Nebraska, Tennessee, Idaho, Kentucky and South Dakota voted to take back their states' approval. Members of Congress extended the deadline to 1982, and no more states ratified before it ran out. The amendment ended three states short.

In October 1974 members of Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. It made it illegal for a lender to refuse a woman credit because of her sex or because she was married or single.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Title IX, 1972" -->
Title IX, one sentence long, became law on 23 June 1972. It reads: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." It says nothing about sports. It covers any school or college program paid for even in part with federal money.

Representative Patsy Takemoto Mink of Hawaii, Representative Edith Green of Oregon and Senator Birch Bayh of Indiana worked together to pass it. Green, a former teacher, led the hearings in the House. After Mink died on 28 September 2002, the law was renamed the Patsy T. Mink Equal Opportunity in Education Act.

Before Title IX, fewer than 300,000 girls played high school sports. By 2012 more than 3 million did. That year the White House reported that American high schools still offered girls 1.3 million fewer chances to play sports than boys. In the 2013-14 school year, the Government Accountability Office found that girls were 49 of every 100 students at public high schools that offered sports, and 43 of every 100 players.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gay Americans organize, 1953 to 1979" -->
From the 1950s, gay Americans organized openly against being fired, arrested and photographed for who they were. On 27 April 1953 President Dwight Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450. It listed "sexual perversion," the government's term then for being gay, among the reasons to remove a person from a federal job. The historian David K. Johnson estimates that "somewhere between 5,000 and 10,000 people lost their jobs" under it. Frank Kameny, a scientist at the Army Map Service, was fired in 1957 and fought his firing as a civil rights case. On 21 May 1965 he and others picketed the White House against the firings. In 1975 the Civil Service Commission, which set the rules for federal jobs, announced that it would stop firing federal workers for being gay.

In New York in the 1960s, bars were forbidden to serve alcohol to gay people. Police raided gay bars, arrested the customers and photographed them. In the early hours of 28 June 1969, police led by Deputy Inspector Seymour Pine raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in Greenwich Village. This time the crowd outside fought back. People threw coins, bottles, bricks, garbage cans and burning trash. The police shut themselves inside the bar until the Tactical Patrol Force, a police unit trained to break up riots, arrived. The protests went on for six nights. Police arrested 13 people the first night. The National Park Service counts fifty to sixty gay groups in the country before Stonewall and at least 1,500 a year later.

On 28 June 1970, the first anniversary of the raid, thousands of people marched from Greenwich Village to Central Park in the Christopher Street Liberation Day March. Craig Rodwell led the organizing.

Harvey Milk was one of the first openly gay people elected to public office in the United States, as a member of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors. On 27 November 1978, Dan White, a former supervisor, shot Mayor George Moscone and then shot Milk five times at City Hall. In May 1979 a jury convicted White of voluntary manslaughter, a lesser crime than murder, and he was sentenced to seven years and eight months. He served about five. Crowds rioted at City Hall after the verdict.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native activists hold their ground, 1969 to 1974" -->
Native activists occupied land and got themselves arrested on purpose. They held Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay from 20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971. In 1973 they held the village of Wounded Knee, on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota, the Oglala Lakota's land, from 27 February to 8 May.

In Washington State, Native fishermen fished where their treaties said they could, and got arrested for it on purpose. Billy Frank Jr. of the Nisqually Nation was arrested more than 50 times, starting at age fourteen, at Frank's Landing. On 12 February 1974, in *United States v. Washington*, Judge Boldt ruled that the tribes were entitled to half of the salmon that could be caught. The Supreme Court upheld the ruling in 1979. President Barack Obama awarded Frank the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015, after his death.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Done to people without their consent, 1965 to 1973" -->
Between 1965 and 1973, doctors, nurses and staff paid with public money harmed poor Black girls and disabled children who had no way to refuse. In June 1973, in Montgomery, Alabama, nurses from the family planning clinic of Montgomery Community Action, which was paid for with federal money, told Minnie Relf that her daughters would get birth control "shots." She could not read or write, and she signed the form with an "X." Surgeons then sterilized the two girls, who were 12 and 14, by tubal ligation. In that operation a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so that the girls could never become pregnant. Their father, Lonnie Relf, saw the surgical scars when they came home from three days in the hospital. The records do not name the surgeons. The Southern Poverty Law Center sued for the family. The judge in the case found that an estimated 100,000 to 150,000 poor people were being sterilized each year under programs paid for with federal money. The judge barred federal money for sterilizations done against people's will.

Willowbrook State School, on Staten Island in New York City, was a state institution for children with intellectual disabilities. It was built for 4,000 people and held 6,000 by 1965. Senator Robert F. Kennedy toured it that year and said the residents were "living in filth and dirt, their clothing in rags." In 1972 the television reporter Geraldo Rivera showed crowded wards, filthy bathrooms, and staff beating and sexually abusing residents. On 17 March 1972, parents of 5,000 residents sued New York State. New York officials agreed to a court order in 1975, and the last children left Willowbrook on 17 September 1987.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Parents sue to get their children into school, 1971 to 1975" -->
Parents of disabled children sued in 1971 and 1972 to get their children into public schools, and they won. Pennsylvania's school law let a school board "refuse to accept or retain beginners who have not attained a mental age of five years." It also allowed the "temporary or permanent exclusion from the public school of children who are found to be uneducable and untrainable." A child who scored under 30 on an intelligence test was called uneducable and sent to the state welfare department. In practice the child never went to school.

The parents' organization was the Pennsylvania Association for Retarded Children. "Retarded" was the word used then, in laws and in the case, for people with intellectual disabilities. On 7 January 1971 the association's lawyer, Thomas K. Gilhool of Philadelphia, sued the state for fourteen children. It estimated that about 100,000 children in Pennsylvania were being denied a free education. Three federal judges approved a settlement on 8 October 1971 and a final order on 5 May 1972. Pennsylvania officials had to find those children, enroll them in school, and give parents a hearing before a child was moved or kept out. On 1 August 1972 a federal judge ruled the same way for seven families in Washington, D.C., in *Mills v. Board of Education*.

Parents were bringing cases like these in twenty states. President Gerald Ford signed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act on 29 November 1975. The federal Bureau of Education for the Handicapped estimated that in 1975 about a million disabled children were still shut out of school completely, and at least 300,000 more were getting schooling that did not meet their needs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Section 504, Gallaudet and the Americans with Disabilities Act, 1973 to 1990" -->
In 1977 disabled Americans occupied federal offices to force officials to enforce a law that had been passed four years before. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 was the first federal law protecting the civil rights of disabled people. It says that no qualified person with a disability "shall, solely by reason of his or her disability, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

Federal officials could not enforce the law until someone signed the rules for carrying it out. For four years no one did. Joseph A. Califano Jr., the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, refused to sign them after schools and hospitals objected to what they would cost.

On 5 April 1977 disabled people demonstrated at federal offices in eleven cities: San Francisco, Washington, Seattle, Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, Dallas, Atlanta, New York, Boston and Philadelphia. In San Francisco, about 100 of them occupied the department's offices at 50 UN Plaza and stayed. The National Park Service gives the length of the sit-in as 26 days in one account and 24 days in another. The Black Panther Party brought in one hot meal a day and food for two more. The International Association of Machinists, a union, paid bills and paid to fly a group to Washington. A lesbian cooperative called the Brick Hut and Glide Memorial Church also helped. Judy Heumann, Kitty Cone and Dennis Billups led the occupation from inside. Califano signed the rules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Judy Heumann

> **Who:** A woman refused a place at school as a child and a teaching license as an adult, who sued, won, and then helped lead the 1977 sit-in that won the Section 504 rules.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1949 to 1970. San Francisco, April 1977.

Judy Heumann was turned away by New York City's schools as a child and by its Board of Education as a teacher, because she used a wheelchair. She sued for her license and won, and in 1977 she helped lead the San Francisco sit-in.

She was born in Philadelphia on 18 December 1947. At eighteen months old she got polio, which paralyzed her, and she used a wheelchair from early childhood. New York City's public schools refused to take her. Her mother, Ilse, spent years pressing them. Judy got in at about fourth grade, into a separate program for disabled students called Health Conservation 21.

She finished college and applied for a license to teach. In 1970 the New York Board of Education turned her down because she used a wheelchair. She asked the American Civil Liberties Union for help, and its lawyers refused, saying that turning someone down for a medical reason was not discrimination. That year the *New York Times* put her story on its front page. A lawyer, Roy Lucas, read it and offered to represent her. She sued the Board of Education at 22. The judge was Constance Baker Motley. Board officials settled before trial and gave her the license. She and her supporters then started a group called Disabled in Action.

In April 1977 she was one of about a hundred disabled people who occupied the federal offices at 50 UN Plaza in San Francisco to force the government to sign the Section 504 rules. She and Dennis Billups flew to Washington during the sit-in, with money from the machinists' union.

She spent the rest of her life on the same work. From 1993 to 2001 she was the federal official in charge of special education and rehabilitation. She advised the World Bank on disability from 2001 to 2005, and from 2010 to 2017 she was the federal government's special adviser on international disability rights. She died in Washington on 4 March 2023. She once said, "Some people say that what I did changed the world. But really, I simply refused to accept what I was told about who I could be. And I was willing to make a fuss about it."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaf President Now, 1988, and the Capitol Crawl" -->
Gallaudet University, in Washington, D.C., is the country's university for deaf students. In March 1988 its board of trustees chose a hearing candidate, Elisabeth Zinser, as president over deaf candidates. Students marched, stopped going to classes and let the air out of the tires of the buses parked across the gates. They made four demands. Zinser had to go and a deaf president be chosen. The board's chair, Jane Spilman, had to resign. At least 51 of every 100 board members had to be deaf. No one who protested could be punished. After eight days the trustees gave in. I. King Jordan became Gallaudet's first deaf president. He said, "A Deaf person can do anything a hearing person can, except hear."

While Congress was considering the Americans with Disabilities Act, disabled protesters left their wheelchairs, walkers and crutches at the bottom of the steps of the Capitol and pulled themselves up the steps by hand. It is called the Capitol Crawl. President George H. W. Bush signed the Americans with Disabilities Act on 26 July 1990. It made it illegal to refuse a disabled person a job, or to shut a disabled person out of buses, shops, restaurants and public buildings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Busing, Detroit and Boston, 1974" -->
When Black parents in the North sued over segregated schools in the 1970s, they won in Boston and lost at the Supreme Court over Detroit's suburbs. On 25 July 1974, in *Milliken v. Bradley*, five justices against four ruled that a judge could not order children bused between a city's school district and its suburbs unless it was proved that one district had caused segregation in the other. Under the ruling, a judge's plan that would have covered Detroit and 53 suburban districts could not go ahead. Justice Thurgood Marshall, who had argued *Brown* as the NAACP's lawyer, was one of the four who dissented.

In Boston, Tallulah Morgan, a Black mother of three, and thirteen other families sued the Boston School Committee in 1972. On 21 June 1974 Judge W. Arthur Garrity ruled that "the entire school system of Boston was unconstitutionally segregated." He found that school officials "had knowingly carried out a systematic program of segregation affecting all of the city's students, teachers and school facilities." Busing began on 9 September 1974 and paired South Boston with Roxbury. Protesters threw rocks and bottles at the buses, broke the windows and shouted racial insults at the children inside. Of the 800 Black students assigned to South Boston High that first day, 56 came. Garrity handed the monitoring of the schools to the state in 1982 and issued his final order in 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Warren County lies down in the road, 1982" -->
In September 1982, residents of mostly Black Warren County, North Carolina, lay down in front of dump trucks to stop a landfill for poisoned soil. In 1978 soil along North Carolina's roadsides had been contaminated with chemicals called PCBs, dumped illegally. State officials chose Afton, in mostly Black, rural Warren County, as the place to bury it. After the courts failed them, residents and civil rights leaders, among them the Reverend Benjamin Chavis of the United Church of Christ's Commission for Racial Justice, blocked the trucks. About 500 people were arrested over six weeks. The state built the landfill anyway. The protest is widely named as the start of the environmental justice movement, the campaign against placing pollution where poor and nonwhite people live. In 1987 the United Church of Christ published a report, *Toxic Wastes and Race*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An apology and $20,000, 1980 to 1990" -->
Japanese Americans who had been held in the camps of the Second World War campaigned for an apology and payment, and in 1988 they won both. The Japanese American Citizens League and the National Council for Japanese American Redress led the campaign.

On 31 July 1980 President Jimmy Carter signed a law creating a commission to study the camps. In 1981 its members held 20 days of hearings in 11 places and heard more than 750 witnesses. Their report, in February 1983, found that the causes were "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership." In 1983 a federal judge also threw out Fred Korematsu's conviction.

Senators Daniel Inouye and Spark Matsunaga and Representatives Norman Mineta and Robert Matsui led the bill through Congress. President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act on 10 August 1988. It gave an apology and $20,000 to each person still living who had been held. The first letters and checks went out on 1 October 1990.
