## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, the people who organized for their rights worked out the methods that organizers in the 1960s later used. In 1917 Alice Paul put women on a picket line at the White House gates, and in 1935 disabled New Yorkers sat down in a city relief office and would not leave. Starting in 1935, Charles Hamilton Houston planned a chain of court cases, one law school at a time. A. Philip Randolph planned marches on Washington in 1941 and in 1948. Both times a president signed an order: the first against unfair hiring by race in defense factories, the second against unequal treatment in the armed forces.

Women won the vote in 1920, and white officials in the South kept many Black women from voting, with the same rules and violence they used against Black men. White mobs attacked Black Americans in Springfield in 1908 and in 25 cities in 1919. In Tulsa in 1921, white Tulsans burned the Black district of Greenwood and killed as many as 300 people. Officials in 30 states had at least 60,000 people operated on so that they could never have children. In 1942 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed an order, and Army officers forced about 120,000 people whose families came from Japan out of their homes and into camps. Officials locked them up without charging them with any crime. Those who disobeyed the orders, such as Fred Korematsu, were charged with breaking the orders themselves. The justices of the Supreme Court ruled in 1927 that the operations were allowed, and in 1944 that the removal was allowed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A mob in Lincoln's town, and the NAACP, 1908 and 1909" -->
After a white mob attacked Black residents of Springfield, Illinois, in August 1908, a group of Black and white reformers founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, the NAACP. Colored was a common word for Black people at the time. Springfield was Abraham Lincoln's home town.

On 14 August 1908, reporters for Springfield's newspapers wrote that a Black man had tried to attack a white woman in her home the night before. Then they wrote about a second attack. A white mob went looking for the men accused. When the men in it could not find them, they turned on the city's Black neighborhoods. The violence lasted nearly three days. The mob lynched two men, Scott Burton and William Donegan. To lynch is to kill a person without any trial, as a mob, often by hanging. Neither man had anything to do with any crime. The rioters destroyed dozens of homes. Part-time soldiers of the state militia, the state's own army, stopped the violence. Officials arrested about 150 people, and few of them were ever found guilty of anything. Nearly 2,000 Black residents left Springfield and never came back.

The NAACP's founders started it on 12 February 1909, a hundred years after Lincoln was born. Its white founders included Mary White Ovington, Oswald Garrison Villard, William English Walling and Dr. Henry Moscowitz. About 60 people signed the call for its first conference. Seven of them were Black. They included the scholar W. E. B. Du Bois, Mary Church Terrell and Ida B. Wells-Barnett, who had counted the South's lynchings in the 1890s. The founders said their aim was to "secure for all people the rights guaranteed in the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women march in Washington, 1913" -->
On 3 March 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson became president, women marched down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington to demand the vote. Alice Paul and Lucy Burns organized the march. They ran the Congressional Committee of the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Suffrage means the right to vote. They chose the same route the president's parade would take the next day. At least 250,000 people came to watch.

The crowd pushed off the sidewalks and blocked the street. Men shoved the marchers, spat on them and shouted insults and crude requests for sex at them. The police did little to stop it. United States Army troops arrived about an hour later and cleared the street. Afterward, members of Congress investigated why the police had done so little.

More than forty Black women marched, in their state groups or with women of their professions. Ida B. Wells-Barnett came with the Illinois group. As the march was forming, the parade's national organizers told the Illinois women that their group was to be "entirely white" and that Black women were to march at the tail end of the parade. The leader of the Illinois group went along with it. Wells-Barnett refused and left. She waited at the side of the avenue until the Illinois women came past. Then she stepped into the line between two white marchers, Belle Squire and Virginia Brooks. The same year, Black women in Chicago formed the Alpha Suffrage Club, the first Black women's suffrage club in Illinois. Ida B. Wells-Barnett died in Chicago on 25 March 1931.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Silent Sentinels and the Occoquan Workhouse, 1917" -->
From 10 January 1917, women stood at the gates of the White House six days a week, holding banners that asked President Wilson to support their vote. A National Park Service article states that Alice Paul invented picketing the White House. To picket is to stand outside a place with signs, as a public protest. One banner read "Mr President What Will You Do For Woman Suffrage?" Another read "How Long, Mr President, Must Women Wait For Liberty?" The women became known as the Silent Sentinels.

In 1917 police began arresting the women on a charge of "obstructing traffic," which means blocking the road. Over the whole campaign, judges found more than 150 women guilty of it. In November 1917 alone, 41 women were arrested, among them Lucy Burns, Dorothy Day, Dora Lewis and Mary Nolan. Their sentences ran from six days to six months.

Many of them were sent to the Occoquan Workhouse, a prison in Virginia. On the night of 14 November 1917, on the orders of the superintendent, W. H. Whittaker, the guards attacked them. The guards threw the women around, hung some of them by their arms and refused them food and medical care. Lucy Burns kept a daily log on scraps of paper and got it smuggled out of the workhouse. Her entry for that night reads: "Seized by guards from behind, flung off my feet, and shot out of the room. All of us were seized by men guards and dragged to cells in men's part. Dorothy Day was roughly used-back twisted. Mrs. Mary A. Nolan (73-year-old picket from Jacksonville, Florida) flung into cell." The guards handcuffed Burns all night, and for part of the time they chained her hands to the bars of her cell. By her own account, they did it because she had asked the other women how they were. That night is known as the Night of Terror.

Many of the prisoners went on hunger strike, which means they refused to eat as a protest. The prison doctor, Dr. Gannon, answered by feeding them by force. Forcible feeding meant holding a woman down and pushing a tube through her nose or mouth, down her throat and into her stomach. Then the doctor poured liquid food through the tube. Burns wrote in her log what was done to her:

"I was held down by five people at legs, arms, and head. I refused to open mouth. Gannon pushed tube up left nostril. I turned and twisted my head all I could, but he managed to push it up. It hurts nose and throat very much and makes nose bleed freely. Tube drawn out covered with blood. Operation leaves one very sick. Food dumped directly into stomach feels like a ball of lead. Left nostril, throat and muscles of neck very sore all night."

Dora Lewis was held down by five people while Gannon forced the tube through her lips. "I didn't know where to breathe from and everything turned black when the fluid began pouring in," she wrote. Alice Paul and Rose Winslow were fed this way three times a day for three weeks, until they were let out. Winslow got a note out of the prison: "We think of the coming feeding all day. It is horrible."

### Alice Paul

> **Who:** The organizer who put the first picket line outside the White House, and who served six prison terms for women's right to vote.
> **When and where:** Born at Mount Laurel, New Jersey, 11 January 1885. Washington, D.C., 1913 to 1920.

Alice Paul started the picket line outside the White House, and when officials jailed her for it, she refused to eat. A prison doctor then had her moved to a ward for people thought to be insane, where two doctors' signatures could send a person to an asylum, a hospital where such people were locked up.

She was born on 11 January 1885 at Mount Laurel, New Jersey, into a Quaker family. Quakers are members of a Christian group. She graduated from Swarthmore College in 1905, earned a master's degree in 1907 and a doctorate, the highest university degree, in economics from the University of Pennsylvania in 1912. She also studied in London and Birmingham. In England she joined Emmeline Pankhurst's campaign for the vote and was arrested for it more than once.

Back home, she became chairman of the National American Woman Suffrage Association's Congressional Committee in 1913. She then founded the Congressional Union and, in 1916, the National Woman's Party. She organized the 1913 march in Washington, and on 10 January 1917 she started the picket line at the White House gates.

In October 1917 she was arrested for picketing and sentenced to seven months for obstructing traffic. On her way to serve the sentence, she told reporters, "We are being imprisoned, not because we obstructed traffic, but because we pointed out to the President the fact that he was obstructing the cause of democracy at home, while Americans were fighting for it abroad." The United States was then fighting in the First World War.

In the District of Columbia jail she went on hunger strike. Commissioner Gardner, one of the officials who governed the District of Columbia, told Dr. Gannon, "Go ahead, take her and feed her." She was carried on a stretcher to the jail's psychopathic ward, the part of the hospital for people thought to be insane. Gannon ordered one of her windows nailed shut and her door replaced with a barred cell door. A prisoner on a ladder nailed heavy boards across the other window from outside. A nurse was ordered to check on her every hour through the night by turning an electric light onto her face. Under the rules of the time, the signatures of two doctors were enough to send a person to St. Elizabeth's, the government asylum. She wrote later, "No trial, no counsel, no protest from the outside world!" Counsel means a lawyer.

The psychiatrist sent to examine her, Dr. William A. White, did not find her insane. A psychiatrist is a doctor who treats illnesses of the mind, and White ran St. Elizabeth's. He wrote, "I felt myself in the presence of an unusually gifted personality," and praised the "most admirable, coherent, logical and forceful way" in which she argued her case. A lawyer, Dudley Field Malone, forced his way in and got her out of the ward. She was released after five weeks of her seven-month sentence. She served six prison terms for the vote in all, three in England and three in the United States.

After the 19th Amendment passed in 1920, she kept working. She earned a law degree in 1922. She wrote the Equal Rights Amendment, which said that men and women must have equal rights under the law. She had it introduced at Seneca Falls on 20 July 1923, where it was called the Lucretia Mott Amendment. In 1945 she worked on the words about women in the United Nations Charter, and she worked on the part about sex in the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The Equal Rights Amendment she wrote was still not part of the Constitution when she died at Moorestown, New Jersey, on 9 July 1977, aged 92.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Margaret Sanger and the Comstock laws, 1914 to 1936" -->
Margaret Sanger went to jail for giving women information about birth control, which means ways to keep from becoming pregnant. The Comstock Act of 1873 made it a crime to send that information by mail. In 1914 Sanger was charged under the Comstock laws for publishing *The Woman Rebel*, a paper about birth control, and she left for England.

On 16 October 1916 she opened a birth control clinic in Brooklyn, New York. Police arrested her about a week later, and she spent 30 days in jail. In 1918 judges ruled that doctors could give women birth control for medical reasons. In 1921 Sanger founded the American Birth Control League. In 1936 the judges of a federal court ruled that doctors could give their patients birth control. A National Women's History Museum biography states that Sanger also supported parts of the eugenics movement, whose members wanted to stop people they judged unfit from having children.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attacks in 25 cities, and a court case out of Elaine, 1919 to 1925" -->
In 1919 white mobs attacked Black Americans in 25 major cities, and the NAACP's lawyers turned one of those attacks into a Supreme Court ruling. The year's violence is called the Red Summer. Writers at the Equal Justice Initiative, a group that records racial violence, state that white mobs and police especially targeted Black veterans coming home from the First World War.

In Chicago on 27 July 1919, a 17-year-old Black boy named Eugene Williams was swimming off a raft near the 29th Street beach. A 24-year-old white man, George Stauber, threw stones at the Black boys, and Eugene Williams drowned. A police officer, Daniel Callahan, refused to arrest Stauber. White youth gangs and neighborhood "athletic clubs" then attacked Black neighborhoods, and the fighting went on until 3 August. Thirty-eight people were killed, 23 Black and 15 white. About 537 were hurt, and two out of three of them were Black. Two out of three of the 138 people charged with crimes afterward were Black too.

In Elaine, Arkansas, Black sharecroppers had joined a union. A sharecropper farmed land owned by someone else and paid for it with a share of the crop. A union is a group of workers who bargain together. This one, the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America, was led by Robert Lee Hill, and its members wanted a better price for their cotton from the white landowners. On the night of 30 September 1919, about 100 of them met in a church at Hoop Spur. Men in a car outside and the union's armed guards exchanged shots. A white security officer, W. A. Adkins, was killed, and a deputy sheriff, Charles Pratt, was wounded.

Between 500 and 1,000 armed white men came to Elaine from the surrounding Arkansas counties and from across the river in Mississippi. A white witness, H. F. Smiddy, swore in 1921 that "several hundred of them" began "to hunt negroes" and shoot them as they found them. Negro was the common word for a Black person at the time. Governor Charles Hillman Brough sent more than 500 soldiers from Camp Pike. They arrived on 2 October. Some accounts say the soldiers also killed Black people at random. Four brothers, David, Gibson, Lewis and Leroy Johnston, were killed on 2 October on their way home from a hunting trip. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas does not say who killed them. The count of Black dead is disputed. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas gives a count that "ranged into the hundreds." A National Archives page gives more than 100. The Army commander, Colonel Isaac Jenks, reported that his soldiers had killed two. Five white people were killed.

Phillips County officials charged 122 Black people with crimes. By 5 November 1919, twelve men had been tried in Phillips County and sentenced to death. One of their white defense lawyers admitted he "had not interviewed any witnesses." Walter White of the NAACP went to Arkansas to investigate, and a Black lawyer from Little Rock, Scipio Africanus Jones, defended the twelve. On 19 February 1923, in *Moore v. Dempsey*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the trials had been unfair. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that one trial "lasted about three-quarters of an hour and in less than five minutes the jury brought in a verdict of guilty of murder in the first degree." A verdict is a jury's decision. An angry crowd filled the courthouse and the streets around it. Sworn statements in the case said Black witnesses had been "whipped, placed in the electric chair and strangled by something put in their noses to make them testify." In January 1925 Governor Thomas McRae freed the last of the twelve.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thirty years of anti-lynching bills, 1900 to 1922" -->
NAACP leaders pushed Congress for a federal law against lynching, and in the whole of this era members of Congress never passed one. Under such a law, federal judges could have tried the members of a lynch mob when state officials would not.

Representative George Henry White of North Carolina brought the first federal anti-lynching bill to Congress on 20 January 1900. He was the last Black member of Congress of his generation. Members of Congress did not pass his bill.

On 8 April 1918, Representative Leonidas Dyer of Missouri introduced a bill "to protect citizens of the United States against lynching in default of protection by the States." In default of protection meant wherever the states failed to protect them. Under it, members of a lynch mob would have been tried in federal court for murder. The county where a lynching happened would have been fined. Any sheriff or jailer who handed a prisoner to a mob, or failed to stop one, could have been jailed and fined up to $5,000. People who had been part of a mob could not sit on the jury.

NAACP leaders worked to pass it. In April 1919 they published a report, *Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889-1918*. The report recorded 3,224 people killed by lynch mobs in those thirty years. Of them, 2,522 were Black, and 50 of the Black victims were women. They sent James Weldon Johnson to Washington to press members of Congress in person. "I tramped the corridors of the Capitol and the two office buildings so constantly that toward the end, I could, I think, have been able to find my way about blindfolded," he wrote.

Members of the House of Representatives passed the bill on 26 January 1922, by 230 votes to 119, with several hundred Black visitors crowded into the public gallery. On 14 June 1922 NAACP organizers held a silent march in Washington for it, with more than 5,000 Black marchers. Senators never voted on it. Southern Democratic senators filibustered it, which means they kept talking so that no vote could be held. They did the same in 1923 and 1924, and Republican senators let the bill drop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 19th Amendment, 1920, and the women it did not reach" -->
The 19th Amendment to the Constitution was ratified, which means approved by the states, in August 1920. Under it, no official of the United States or of any state could refuse citizens the vote because of their sex. Its first section is one sentence: "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Abridged means cut down.

The same words had been in front of Congress for 42 years. Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced them on 10 January 1878. Senators voted them down in 1887, 1914, 1918 and February 1919. Susan B. Anthony died in Rochester, New York, on 13 March 1906, and suffragists named the measure the Anthony Amendment in her memory. Representatives passed it on 21 May 1919, and senators passed it on 4 June 1919, by 56 votes to 25. Then lawmakers in three-fourths of the states had to ratify it. Tennessee's lawmakers made it the 36th state to do so on 18 August 1920, the last one needed. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby signed the paper declaring it part of the Constitution on 26 August 1920, 72 years after the meeting at Seneca Falls.

Under the amendment, state officials could no longer refuse a woman the vote because she was a woman. They could still refuse her for other reasons. In the South, white officials already kept Black men from voting, by rules and by violence. A National Park Service article states that Black women in the South "encountered the very same disfranchisement strategies and anti-Black violence that led to the disfranchisement of Black men." Disfranchisement means taking away a person's vote. A National Archives page states that many women "remained unable to vote long into the 20th century because of discriminatory state voting laws." Discriminatory means unfair to people because of who they are, such as their race or sex.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa, 1 June 1921: white Tulsans burn Greenwood" -->
On 1 June 1921, thousands of armed white Tulsans went into Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and burned it to the ground. They killed as many as 300 people. The historian Scott Ellsworth writes that "credible estimates of deaths range from fifty to three hundred." In 2025 lawyers at the United States Department of Justice wrote that the attack "killed as many as 300 Tulsans, perhaps even more." The attackers threw bodies into the Arkansas River, carried them off on railroad flatcars and buried them in unmarked graves, so the true count may never be known. Red Cross workers counted about 700 people hurt.

About 10,000 Black people lived in Greenwood. It had two newspapers, more than a dozen churches, a library, two "fine hotels," four drug stores and many grocery stores. Loula Williams owned the Dreamland Theater. J. B. Stradford owned a hotel of 54 rooms. Doctors, lawyers and teachers lived on Detroit Avenue, and families owned pianos and good furniture. Black Tulsans spent their money in Greenwood because most white business owners in Tulsa would not serve them or treated them with contempt. Under city laws, Black families could not live in white blocks unless they worked there as servants. White officials controlled the city's tax money and spent none of it in Greenwood on running water or on carrying away sewage and garbage, and paved fewer of its streets. All of Tulsa, Greenwood included, stood on land in the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's reservation.

The attack began after police arrested Dick Rowland, a 19-year-old Black man, on 31 May. A white woman, Sarah Page, ran the elevator he used, and a white man had accused him of attacking her. She did not press charges. That evening a white crowd gathered at the courthouse to lynch Rowland, and armed Black men went there to stop it. Shooting broke out. Tulsa police then deputized hundreds of white men, which means they swore them in as temporary police officers. Many of those men had been calling for a lynching and had been drinking.

At about 1 in the morning, white men set the first fires in Greenwood. When firemen came, armed white men threatened them and told them to let the Black homes burn. A little after 5 in the morning, a whistle blew. White men who had been organized into companies during the night gathered at three railroad stations and went into Greenwood. The 2001 Oklahoma state commission put their number at about 10,000. A scholar writing in 1946 said 8,000 to 10,000. The state's adjutant general, the officer at the head of Oklahoma's National Guard, was Charles Barrett. The National Guard is a state's own army of part-time soldiers. Barrett told the *New York Times* there were 25,000. The Justice Department's 2025 review states that many take that figure to be a misprint for 2,500.

The attackers went from house to house. They took what they wanted, piled bedding and furniture in the middle of a room, poured kerosene over it and lit it. They backed trucks up to houses and loaded "everything moveable and of value." White women came with shopping bags and took clothes, silverware and jewelry. The attackers opened safes and destroyed people's legal papers before burning the buildings. They burned more than 1,200 houses and 35 city blocks. Among the buildings they burned were a dozen churches, five hotels, 31 restaurants, four drug stores, eight doctors' offices, more than two dozen grocery stores and the library.

They killed people who ran and people who gave up. The Justice Department's review describes a grandfather shot in front of his family in a wagon. It describes an old man who could not walk, shot in his home when he said he could not "march." Four white men tied a blind man who had lost both legs to the back of a car and dragged him through the streets until he died. An old couple on Greenwood Avenue were shot in the back of the head while they prayed. A man was shot as he came out of a burning building and thrown back into the fire. Survivors could not name the men who did these things. The Red Cross's relief director in Tulsa, Maurice Willows, who was white, wrote: "This was NOT a RIOT."

Some Black men fought back. A group fired from the bell tower of the new Mount Zion Baptist Church until National Guard soldiers turned a machine gun on it. J. B. Stradford shot from the second floor of his hotel. He gave up when the attackers promised not to burn it, and then they burned it.

Three Guard units were in Tulsa, and before the governor took control the guardsmen took orders from Police Chief John Gustafson. By their own reports, they helped the police organize white men into companies and went into Greenwood to round up Black residents. The Justice Department's review states that the guardsmen's main work was capturing Black men. That left Black homes empty, and other white men looted and burned them. Some guardsmen tried to hold back white attackers. Governor James Robertson declared martial law, which put the Guard in charge of the city, at 11:30 that morning.

Airplanes flew over Greenwood during the attack. The Justice Department's review calls it "undisputed" that the men in them watched where Black residents ran and told the police. Many survivors said men in the planes also dropped firebombs or shot at people fleeing. The lawyer B. C. Franklin, who saw it, described turpentine bombs. The review calls that part "hotly disputed."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The camps, the passes and the grand jury, June 1921" -->
After the burning, police, deputies and guardsmen arrested Black people all over Tulsa, whether or not they were suspected of anything. The Justice Department's review states that the arrests were not based on "probable cause or even reasonable suspicion." Those are legal words for a good reason to believe a person has done something wrong. Guardsmen also arrested Black servants in the servants' rooms of white homes. They held the people they arrested under armed guard in three places: Convention Hall, McNulty Park, which was a baseball park, and the stalls of the fairgrounds. At the fullest, between 4,000 and 6,000 people were held. Some stayed a few days and some up to two weeks. Guardsmen arrested some white men for looting, but they held no white person in the camps.

To leave a camp, a Black person needed an identification card, and a white person had to vouch for it. People who worked in white homes and businesses got green cards that did not run out. People without jobs got red cards that were good for one day. Anyone found without a card was arrested again. By 7 June, officials had given out 7,500 of these cards, and the rule lasted at least a month. People forced to live under it said they were treated "as though they [were] dogs."

The day after the burning, the Guard commander ordered "all able bodied [N]egro men" in the camps to "render such service and perform such labor as is required." The men cleared the ruins and buried the dead. Red Cross officials paid up to 25 cents an hour, and a meal cost 20 cents. For several weeks officials did not allow Black Tulsans to own or buy guns. Most of Greenwood's people had no home left. Many lived in Red Cross tents for more than a year, and many of the tents had no floor.

A state grand jury heard almost 200 witnesses. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to charge someone with a crime. This one charged about 70 men, many of them Black leaders of Greenwood, with starting the "riot." On 26 June 1921 the *Tulsa Daily World* printed its report under the headline "Grand Jury Blames Negroes for Inciting Race Rioting: Whites Clearly Exonerated." Exonerated means cleared of blame. Most of the cases were later dropped, including the case against Dick Rowland. Some white men were charged with looting. No white man went to prison for the killings or the burning. The grand jury also charged Police Chief Gustafson with neglect of duty, which means failing to do the job he was paid to do, and with dishonest dealings from before the massacre. He was found guilty in court and removed from his post.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building Greenwood again, against the city" -->
Greenwood's people rebuilt their district with no rebuilding money from the city, the state or the federal government, after winning a court fight against city leaders who tried to stop them. On 7 June 1921, Tulsa's city commissioners passed Fire Ordinance No. 2156. An ordinance is a city law. Under it, most of Greenwood fell inside the city's fire limits, an area with strict building rules meant to stop fires spreading. Every new building there had to be "of concrete, brick, or steel and ... at least two stories high." The first newspaper story about the rule said the land would "never again be a [N]egro quarter but will become a wholesale and industrial center." In 2025 the Justice Department's review stated that "those who drafted the ordinance planned to drive Black people out of Greenwood."

B. C. Franklin, a Black lawyer, set up an office in a tent on Archer Street with two partners, I. H. Spears and T. O. Chappelle. Before they could get to court, police had arrested many people for trying to rebuild. The lawyers won a court order that stopped the rule for a time, and then, in September 1921, an order that stopped it for good.

Between June 1921 and June 1922, Greenwood's people filed more than $1.8 million in damage claims with the city. City officials turned down all but one, and the one they paid went to a white shop owner for guns taken from his store. Franklin's firm filed about $4 million in claims against the city and against insurance companies. Officials of the insurance companies refused to pay, because their policies did not cover damage done in a "riot." In 1926 the justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court ruled that no one could sue the insurance companies over it. Judges threw out the lawsuits against the city, Mayor T. D. Evans, the police and the airplane company.

By 1925 the National Negro Business League held its national meeting in Greenwood, and by the 1940s Greenwood had 242 Black-owned businesses. From the 1950s, city officials took land along its edges for highways. The Inner Dispersal Loop and the Cherokee Expressway, finished in the 1970s, run along Greenwood's south and east sides.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The survivors ask to be paid, 1997 to 2026" -->
Greenwood's survivors began asking to be paid for what was taken from them in June 1921, and in more than a hundred years neither the city nor the state paid them. In 1997 Oklahoma's lawmakers set up an 11-member commission to study the massacre. Its members handed in their report on 28 February 2001. In it they asked the state to pay the survivors and their descendants directly, and to fund scholarships, business growth in the area and a memorial. On 1 June 2001 the lawmakers passed the 1921 Tulsa Race Riot Reconciliation Act. They voted no money for the survivors in it.

In 2003 survivors and descendants sued the state and the city. Federal judges threw the case out in 2004, ruling that they had waited too long to sue. In 2020 the last three known survivors sued again. They were Viola Fletcher, her brother Hughes Van Ellis and Lessie Benningfield Randle. They sued the City of Tulsa, the Tulsa Regional Chamber, the county's commissioners, Sheriff Vic Regalado and the Oklahoma Military Department. On 19 May 2021 the three spoke to a committee of Congress.

On 1 June 2021 President Joe Biden met the three survivors and spoke at the Greenwood Cultural Center. He was the first president in office to visit Greenwood about the massacre. "It wasn't a riot, it was a massacre," he said. He did not call for the survivors to be paid.

On 12 June 2024 the justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court ended the survivors' lawsuit, by eight votes to one. They wrote that the harm the massacre left behind "can only be resolved by policymakers, not the courts." Hughes Van Ellis had died on 9 October 2023, aged 102. Viola Fletcher died on 24 November 2025, aged 111. Lessie Benningfield Randle, born on 10 November 1914, was the last known survivor in 2026, and neither the city nor the state had ever paid her.

In 2018 Tulsa's mayor, G. T. Bynum, started a search for the massacre's unmarked graves. By September 2026 the searchers had dug up the remains of 55 people in Oaklawn Cemetery. They have named two victims of the massacre. C. L. Daniel was identified by DNA in July 2024. James Goings was identified from Veterans Administration records in June 2025, and his grave has not been found. Both men had served in the Army in the First World War.

### Viola Fletcher

> **Who:** A seven-year-old girl whose family fled Greenwood in 1921, who never went to school past the fourth grade, and who at 107 asked Congress for justice.
> **When and where:** Tulsa, Oklahoma, 1921. Washington, D.C., 19 May 2021.

Viola Fletcher was seven when white Tulsans burned her neighborhood, and she spent the last years of her long life asking a court to pay the survivors. She never saw them paid.

She was born Viola Ford on 10 May 1914, the daughter of Lucinda Ellis and John Wesley Ford of Tulsa. She grew up in Greenwood with her parents and five brothers and sisters. "The neighborhood I fell asleep in that night was rich," she told Congress in 2021, "not just in terms of wealth, but in culture, community, and heritage." "My family had a beautiful home. We had great neighbors and I had friends to play with. I felt safe."

On the night of 31 May 1921, her family woke her and told her they had to leave. "I will never forget the violence of the white mob when we left our house," she said. "I still hear airplanes flying overhead. I hear the screams. I live through the Massacre every day."

"When my family was forced to leave Tulsa, I lost my chance at an education," she said. "I never finished school past the fourth grade." During the Second World War she worked in the shipyards in California. "For most of my life, I was a domestic worker serving white families," she said. A domestic worker cleans and cooks in other people's homes.

In 2020, at 106, she joined her brother and Lessie Benningfield Randle in suing the City of Tulsa and others. On 19 May 2021 she spoke to the House of Representatives' subcommittee on civil rights. "Today, I am in Washington, D.C. for the first time in my life. I am here seeking justice," she told them. "I am 107 years old and have never seen justice." She asked them to "open the courtroom doors to us."

In 2023 she and her grandson, Ike Howard, published a book about her life, *Don't Let Them Bury My Story*. The justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court ended the survivors' lawsuit in June 2024. Viola Fletcher died on 24 November 2025, aged 111.

### Grace Fryer

> **Who:** A watch-dial painter who sued the company whose paint had poisoned her, and who had to fight a state time limit that ran out before she knew she was ill.
> **When and where:** Orange, New Jersey, 1917 to 1928.
> **Movie:** Radium Girls (2018)

Grace Fryer and four other women sued the U.S. Radium Corporation in 1927 for poisoning them. The company's lawyers argued that the law's time limit had already run out.

From 1917 to 1920, Fryer painted numbers on watch dials at the company's plant in Orange, New Jersey. The paint glowed in the dark because it had radium in it. Radium is a radioactive metal, which means it gives off radiation, energy that can damage the body's cells. To keep a fine point on the brush, the painters put the tip between their lips. The physicist Robley Evans wrote down why: a cloth or their fingers "were too harsh, but by wiping the brush clean between their lips the proper erasing point could be obtained."

The body takes up radium as if it were calcium and builds it into the bones and teeth. Once there, it stays and keeps giving off radiation from the inside. When doctors examined the bodies of dial painters after death, they found 99 percent of the radiation in the skeleton. Radium in the bones stopped the marrow from making blood properly, killed the jaw bone so that it broke apart, and caused bone cancers.

Fryer left the plant in 1920. In 1924 she went to a dentist, Theodor Blum, who found that her jaw bone was wasting away. He had never seen anything like it. Doctors came to call it radium jaw.

In May 1927 a lawyer, Raymond Berry, filed suit against the company for Fryer and four women she had worked with: Katherine Schaub, Quinta Maggia Macdonald, Albina Maggia Larice and Edna Hussman. The company's lawyers argued that New Jersey's statute of limitations meant the company could not be blamed. A statute of limitations is a time limit after which a person can no longer sue. New Jersey's limit for injuries at work was two years. Radium poisoning took longer than two years to show itself, and Fryer had left the plant seven years before.

On 4 June 1928 a federal judge, William Clark, brought the two sides to a settlement outside court, and they signed it on 8 June. Each woman got $10,000, though some sources give as much as $15,000. Each also got $600 a year for as long as she lived, and her doctors' bills paid. The settlement papers contain no admission that the company was to blame.

Ten years later, women who had painted dials for the Radium Dial Company in Ottawa, Illinois, fought the same fight. One of them, Catherine Wolfe Donohue, collapsed at a hearing on 10 February 1938 and was carried out of the room. On 5 April 1938 the officer hearing her case ruled that her work had made her ill and awarded her $5,661, plus $277 a year for life. On 6 July the members of the Illinois Industrial Commission, the state board that heard workers' claims, threw out the company's appeal and added $730. She died on 27 July 1938, aged 35. The last ruling in her case came that October, after her death, and her family received a small payment.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native Americans become citizens and fight for the vote, 1911 to 1957" -->
Native Americans organized their own national rights groups in these years, and it took them 33 years after they became citizens to win the vote in every state. On 12 October 1911, at Columbus, Ohio, Native people founded the Society of American Indians, the first national rights organization run by Native people. Charles Eastman and Carlos Montezuma were among its leaders, and Zitkala-Ša edited its journal. In 1926 Zitkala-Ša founded the National Council of American Indians. On 16 November 1944, 80 delegates from more than 50 tribes met in Denver and founded the National Congress of American Indians.

On 2 June 1924 members of Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act, and President Calvin Coolidge signed it. Under the act, American Indians became citizens. That did not put them on the voter rolls, the lists of people allowed to vote, because officials in each state set their own rules about who could vote.

Under Arizona's law, no one could vote who was an "idiot, insane person, person non compos mentis, or under guardianship." Non compos mentis is Latin for "not of sound mind." A person under guardianship has been placed in someone else's care by law. In 1928 the justices of the Arizona Supreme Court ruled that Native people living on reservations were under the guardianship of the federal government, and so could not vote. In New Mexico, officials used the state constitution's phrase "Indians not taxed." They argued that people on reservations paid no state taxes and so could not vote.

Judges threw out both rules in 1948. On 15 July 1948, in *Harrison v. Laveen*, the justices of the Arizona Supreme Court threw out their own court's 1928 ruling. Justice Levi Udall wrote that a Native person who met the other requirements could not be kept off the rolls for living on a reservation. In August 1948, in *Trujillo v. Garley*, three federal judges in New Mexico ruled that the "Indians not taxed" rule broke the Constitution. Utah's officials were the last in any state to let Native people vote, in 1957.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A society for gay rights in Chicago, 1924" -->
On 10 December 1924 Henry Gerber founded the Society for Human Rights in Chicago. A National Park Service article calls it the first organization in the United States set up only to work for the civil rights of gay people. A gay person is a man who loves men or a woman who loves women.

Gerber was born Josef Heinrich Dittmar in Bavaria, in Germany, on 29 June 1892. He came through Ellis Island in October 1913 and served in the United States Army. He worked for the Post Office. He got a charter for the society from the State of Illinois, a paper that made it a legal group, by describing its members as people "abused and hindered in the legal pursuit of happiness." Its members printed two issues of a newsletter, *Friendship and Freedom*. No copy survives.

Chicago police arrested Gerber and other members without a warrant, a court paper that allows an arrest. They took his typewriter, his writings and his diaries, and they gave back only the typewriter. Post Office officials fired him "for conduct unbecoming a postal employee." The society ended in 1925. Gerber died in 1972. His house in Chicago became a National Historic Landmark in June 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eugenics: operating on people so they could not have children" -->
Eugenics was the belief that a country could improve its people by deciding who was fit to have children and stopping the rest. Francis Galton, an English scientist who studied numbers about people, made up the word in 1883. A fact sheet from the National Human Genome Research Institute calls eugenics "the scientifically erroneous and immoral theory of 'racial improvement' and 'planned breeding.'" Erroneous means wrong. The idea became popular in the early 1900s.

American eugenicists pushed for laws under which officials could force people to be sterilized. To sterilize a person is to operate on them so that they can never have a child. The operation cannot be undone. When Dr. John H. Bell operated on Carrie Buck in Virginia in 1927, he cut out a piece of each of the two tubes that carry eggs to her womb. Harry H. Laughlin of the Eugenics Record Office wrote a model sterilization law for lawmakers in each state to copy. A page from the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum states that "in part due to Laughlin's efforts, dozens of American states enacted laws that forced certain people to be sterilized against their will." The same page states that "more than 62,000 sterilizations had been performed in the United States by the 1960s," and that about six in ten of the people operated on were women. The National Human Genome Research Institute's fact sheet counts at least 60,000 people sterilized under the laws of 30 states by the 1970s. The people targeted included Latinos, Native Americans, African Americans, poor white people and disabled people.

In 1933 Germany's Nazi rulers passed the Law for the Prevention of Offspring with Hereditary Diseases. Offspring means children, and hereditary diseases are illnesses passed from parents to children. The Holocaust Museum's page states that the law was "based in part on the practice of forced sterilization in the US." Doctors in Nazi Germany sterilized about 400,000 people under it. In 1936 leaders of the University of Heidelberg in Germany gave Harry Laughlin an honorary degree, a degree given as an honor without study. On 28 May 1936 he wrote back that "To me this honor will be doubly valued because it will come from a nation which for many centuries nurtured the human seed-stock which later founded my own country." Seed-stock means the animals or plants that farmers keep for breeding. Laughlin used the word for people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Carrie Buck

> **Who:** A young Virginia woman chosen as a test case by the man who wanted to sterilize her, and defended by a lawyer who wanted the same thing.
> **When and where:** Virginia, 1924 to 1927. Died at Waynesboro, Virginia, 28 January 1983.

Carrie Buck's case was arranged so that Virginia's officials would win it. The official who wanted to sterilize her picked her case to test the state's new law, and her own lawyer supported sterilization.

Virginia's lawmakers passed the state's sterilization law on 20 March 1924. Aubrey Strode, a lawyer, wrote it. Albert Priddy ran the Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feeble-Minded, a place where Virginia's officials held people they had ordered locked up. Epileptics are people with epilepsy, an illness that causes seizures. Feeble-minded was a label then given to people who scored low on intelligence tests. Carrie Buck was born in Charlottesville on 2 July 1906 and raised by foster parents. Encyclopedia Virginia states that she was "allegedly raped" by their nephew and became pregnant. Rape means forcing a person to have sex. Her foster parents said the pregnancy showed that she was feeble-minded, and on 23 January 1924 they asked a court in Charlottesville to have her sent to the Colony. Priddy wanted to know whether the new law would hold up in court. So he picked one of the people held there, Carrie Buck, and had a case brought over her. Priddy died on 13 January 1925, and Dr. John H. Bell took his place at the Colony and in the case. That is why it is called *Buck v. Bell*.

Strode, who had written the law, argued for the Colony in court. Carrie Buck's lawyer was Irving P. Whitehead. He had sat on the Colony's own board, and he supported sterilization. The historian Paul Lombardo found that Whitehead did not seriously question any of the witnesses against her and did not call one witness for her. Both lawyers wanted the judges to rule that the law was allowed.

A eugenics researcher, Arthur Estabrook, interviewed the family for one day. Then he testified that Carrie, her mother Emma and her baby daughter Vivian were "a defective strain." Officials had committed Emma Buck to the Colony in April 1920, which means they had ordered her held there. Vivian was born on 28 March 1924. A social worker said of the baby that she had "a look about it that is not quite normal."

Judge Bennett T. Gordon of the trial court ruled for the Colony on 18 November 1924. The judges of the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals agreed with him on 12 November 1925. The justices of the Supreme Court of the United States heard the case on 22 April 1927 and ruled for the Colony, eight to one, on 2 May 1927.

On 19 October 1927 Dr. Bell performed a salpingectomy on Carrie Buck. He cut out a section of each of her two tubes that carry an egg from an ovary to the womb. After that, no egg could reach her womb, so she could never become pregnant. The operation could not be undone. The staff released her from the Colony the next month. Officials had brought her sister, Doris Buck, to the Colony in June 1926. Colony officials signed an order to sterilize Doris in December 1927, and doctors operated on her in January 1928.

Between 1927 and 1972, Virginia doctors sterilized about 8,300 people under the law. Virginia's lawmakers ended the law in 1974. In 1980 lawyers for the American Civil Liberties Union sued for the survivors, and the two sides settled the case out of court. On 2 May 2002, 75 years to the day after the ruling, Governor Mark Warner apologized for what he called "a shameful effort in which state government never should have been involved." Carrie Buck married twice. She died in a nursing home in Waynesboro, Virginia, on 28 January 1983. The justices of the Supreme Court have never overturned *Buck v. Bell*, which means they have never ruled that it was wrong.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Citizens sent to Mexico, 1929 to 1936" -->
During the Great Depression, the years of hard times after 1929, federal, state and county officials sent or forced hundreds of thousands of people whose families came from Mexico out of the United States. Many of them were American citizens. Counts run from about 500,000 to about 2 million. In a 2005 law, California's lawmakers found that about 1.2 million of the 2 million people they counted had been born in the United States, which made them citizens.

William N. Doak, President Herbert Hoover's Secretary of Labor, led a national campaign to remove Mexicans, saying they were taking jobs. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel, head of the city's unemployment relief committee, planned the publicity. On 26 February 1931, immigration agents closed the exits of La Placita park in Los Angeles with about 400 people inside. Los Angeles County welfare officers told families their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico. Relief was the public money that poor families lived on during the Depression. The first county train left on 23 March 1931. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks bound for the border. Local officials often sold the property that families left behind.

On 7 October 2005, California's governor signed a law in which the state's lawmakers apologized. They apologized "for the fundamental violations of their basic civil liberties and constitutional rights committed during the period of illegal deportation and coerced emigration." That means people were sent out of the country against the law, or forced to leave it. The Los Angeles County supervisors, who run the county, apologized in 2012. In 2006 Representative Hilda Solis called on the federal government to apologize. No president and no Congress has apologized.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two California school cases, 1931 and 1946" -->
Mexican American parents in California won two school desegregation cases, fifteen years apart, and the first, in 1931, was the first school desegregation case won in the United States. Segregation means keeping people of different races apart, and desegregation means ending it. On 5 January 1931 the principal of Lemon Grove Grammar School, near San Diego, Jerome T. Green, stood in the doorway. He let in every child except the Mexican American ones and told them to go to a separate two-room building. Members of the school board had planned this the summer before. The school had 169 pupils: 87 counted as European American, about 75 Mexican American and 7 Japanese American.

The parents formed a committee, the Comité de Vecinos de Lemon Grove, which means the Lemon Grove Neighbors Committee. They kept their children out of the separate building and hired two lawyers, Fred Noon and A. C. Brinkley. They sued in the name of one of the pupils, Roberto Alvarez. On 30 March 1931 Judge Claude Chambers of the San Diego County Superior Court ordered the district to admit the children "on a basis of equality with other children of said school district, and without separation or segregation in a separate school because of race or nationality." He said that putting "all the Mexicans in one group can only be done by infringing the laws of the State of California." Infringing means breaking. Some accounts date the ruling 31 March.

Fifteen years later, five families in Orange County, the Mendez, Guzman, Palomino, Estrada and Ramirez families, sued four school districts: Westminster, Garden Grove, Santa Ana and El Modena. Their lawyer was David C. Marcus, who had won a case in 1944 against segregated pools and parks in San Bernardino. On 18 February 1946 Judge Paul J. McCormick ruled for the families. He wrote, "A paramount requisite in the American system of public education is social equality." He meant that treating all children as equals came first in American public schools.

When the districts' officials appealed, which means they asked a higher court to change the ruling, other groups joined the fight. NAACP lawyers filed a friend-of-the-court brief, an argument from people who are not part of a case. Thurgood Marshall, Robert L. Carter and Loren Miller wrote it. The Japanese American Citizens League, the League of United Latin American Citizens, the American Jewish Congress and the American Civil Liberties Union filed briefs too. The judges of the federal appeals court ruled for the families on 14 April 1947. In June 1947 Governor Earl Warren signed the Anderson Bill. With it, California's lawmakers took segregated schools out of the state's school laws, the first lawmakers in any state to do so. Warren became Chief Justice of the United States in 1953.

While the Mendez family fought school segregation, they were farming land whose Japanese American owners federal officials had locked up. The farm was 40 acres in Westminster that belonged to Seima Munemitsu and his family. From May 1942 to September 1945, federal officials held the Munemitsus at the Poston camp in Arizona.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Parents of children turned away from school, 1893 to 1933" -->
School officials could turn a disabled child away in these years, because no law required them to teach one. The rule came from a Massachusetts case decided on 4 January 1893. In 1885 members of the Cambridge school committee had put a boy named John A. Watson out of the public schools. Their record states that he was excluded "because he was too weak-minded to derive profit from instruction," and that he was "troublesome to other children, making uncouth noises, pinching others." Uncouth means rude and rough. The judges of the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled that the committee's decision was final and that no court could review it. That rule held through the whole of this era.

In 1933 parents in Cleveland, Ohio, founded the Cuyahoga County Council for the Retarded Child. "Retarded" was the word then used for children with intellectual disabilities. The county's own history describes it as a group led by parents and "founded to assist children of the region who had been excluded from their public schools."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A sit-in for jobs, New York, 1935" -->
In May 1935 six disabled New Yorkers sat down in the city's Home Relief Bureau and refused to leave until they were given a fair chance at government jobs. During the Depression, federal officials gave out jobs to people who were out of work through the Works Progress Administration, the WPA. Workers at the city's Home Relief Bureau stamped disabled people's applications "PH," for "physically handicapped." Officials treated anyone with the stamp as unable to work, and gave them no WPA jobs.

About 300 disabled people formed the League of the Physically Handicapped. Most of them lived with the effects of polio, a disease that can paralyze muscles, or of cerebral palsy, a condition from birth that affects movement. After the bureau's director refused to see them, six of them began the sit-in. Others picketed outside. Later they sat in at WPA headquarters too. One member said afterward: "What started it was finding out that jobs were available, that the government was handing out jobs ... everybody was getting jobs ... those of us who were militant just refused to accept the fact that we were the only people who were looked upon as not worthy, not capable of work." Militant means ready to fight hard for a cause.

By 1936 officials had made about 1,500 jobs for disabled people in New York City. The league had ended by 1938. In August 1935 lawmakers in Congress passed the Social Security Act. It defined disability as "inability to engage in substantial gainful work," so a person who received disability insurance could not easily take a job.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charles Hamilton Houston's plan: law schools first, 1935 to 1949" -->
Charles Hamilton Houston planned the NAACP's attack on segregated schools as a series of court cases, starting with law schools. He was born in Washington, D.C., on 3 September 1895. He was the first Black editor of the *Harvard Law Review*. In 1929 he became dean, the head, of Howard University's law school, where he trained Black lawyers to fight segregation in court. One of his students was Thurgood Marshall. In the 1930s Houston became the NAACP's first special counsel, its chief lawyer. His plan, in his words, was to "start with the law schools and higher education, and integrate schools from the top down." To integrate schools is to let Black and white students learn in the same schools.

The first case was Donald Gaines Murray's. On 24 January 1935 he applied to the University of Maryland School of Law and was refused because he was Black. Houston, Marshall and a Baltimore lawyer, Nicholas Gosnell, took his case to the Baltimore City Court. Judge Eugene O'Dunne ordered the university's president, Raymond A. Pearson, to admit him. The judges of the Maryland Court of Appeals agreed with him on 15 January 1936, and Murray graduated in 1938. The second case, Lloyd Gaines's in Missouri, went to the Supreme Court in 1938.

Ada Lois Sipuel was born in Chickasha, Oklahoma, on 8 February 1924. On 14 January 1946 she applied to the University of Oklahoma College of Law. The university's president, George Lynn Cross, put in writing that there was no academic reason to reject her. He refused her because state law forbade teaching Black and white students in the same room. Thurgood Marshall took her case, and the justices of the Supreme Court decided it on 12 January 1948. Oklahoma's leaders answered by setting up a separate law school for Black students in five days, in rooms at the State Capitol. She was admitted to the University of Oklahoma on 18 June 1949, three years and five months after she applied, and graduated in August 1952. Houston died on 22 April 1950.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lloyd-gaines" name="Lloyd Gaines" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lloyd Gaines

> **Who:** A history graduate who sued to study law in his own state, won the NAACP's first school case at the Supreme Court, and then disappeared.
> **When and where:** Missouri, 1935 to 1938. Last seen in Chicago, March 1939.

Lloyd Gaines won the first school case the NAACP ever won at the Supreme Court. Missouri's leaders answered by building a separate law school, and a few months later Gaines disappeared. He has never been found.

He was born in 1911 at Water Valley, Mississippi. His father, Henry Richard Gaines, was a tenant farmer, a farmer who rents the land he works, and had been a teacher. His mother was Callie Gaines. He had ten brothers and sisters. He finished Vashon High School in St. Louis in 1931 and spent a year at Stowe Teachers College. In August 1935 he graduated with honors from Lincoln University, Missouri's college for Black students, with a degree in history.

He then applied to the University of Missouri School of Law. The registrar, the official in charge of signing up students, was S. W. Canada. He sent Gaines to the president of Lincoln University. Lincoln's president pointed him to a Missouri law that paid a Black student's fees to study law in a neighboring state. Missouri's officials offered to pay for him to attend law school in Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa or Illinois rather than let him into its own.

He refused the offer and sued. Charles Hamilton Houston of the NAACP and a St. Louis lawyer, Sidney Redmond, took his case. They filed it in the circuit court, a state court in Missouri, on 24 January 1936. The circuit judge ruled against him in July 1936, and the judges of the Missouri Supreme Court ruled against him on 9 December 1937.

The justices of the Supreme Court of the United States heard the case on 9 November 1938 and decided it on 12 December 1938, six to two, for Gaines. Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote: "The State was bound to furnish him within its borders facilities for legal education substantially equal to those which the State there afforded for persons of the white race." A state could keep its promise of equal protection, he wrote, "only where its laws operate, that is, within its own jurisdiction." He meant that a state had to give a Black student a law school as good as the white one, inside the state itself. Justice James McReynolds dissented, which means he disagreed with the ruling, and Justice Pierce Butler joined him.

Missouri's lawmakers did not let Gaines into the university. In 1939 they created a separate law school for Black students at Lincoln University. Gaines never attended either school. He was last seen in Chicago in March 1939, at a fraternity house, and he left a bag of his belongings behind. No one formally reported him missing until October 1939. In 2007 FBI agents reopened the case as part of their work on unsolved civil rights crimes.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marian Anderson sings at the Lincoln Memorial, 1939" -->
In 1939 leaders of the Daughters of the American Revolution, a women's group, barred the Black singer Marian Anderson from singing in their hall in Washington, Constitution Hall. Under the hall's rules, only white performers could appear there. Interior Secretary Harold Ickes arranged for her to sing on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial instead. On Easter Sunday, 9 April 1939, about 75,000 people came. Ickes introduced her with the words "Genius draws no color line." She opened with "My Country 'Tis of Thee." At the memorial's opening ceremony in 1922, the organizers had seated the crowd by race, and they had checked the speech of the one Black speaker, Robert Russa Moton, in advance.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="a-philip-randolph-rights-movements" name="A. Philip Randolph" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### A. Philip Randolph

> **Who:** A union leader who twice got a president to sign an order against discrimination by threatening to bring a march to Washington.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1941 and 1948.

A. Philip Randolph got President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1941, and President Harry Truman in 1948, to sign orders against racial discrimination, which means treating people worse because of their race. Each time he did it by planning a march on Washington, and he called off the first march once he had the order.

He was born on 15 April 1889 at Crescent City, Florida. In 1925 he founded the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, a union of the Black Pullman porters who worked on railroad sleeping cars. A union is a group of workers who bargain together. In 1935 the Brotherhood became the first African American union admitted to the American Federation of Labor, a national group of unions.

In 1941 Americans were building weapons for the Second World War, and defense factories were hiring. Randolph and Bayard Rustin began organizing a march on Washington against discrimination in the defense industries. Randolph and other Black leaders met Eleanor Roosevelt and members of the president's cabinet. Randolph told them he would bring "ten, twenty, fifty thousand Negroes on the White House lawn."

On 25 June 1941 President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 8802. An executive order is a written order from the president to the people who work for the federal government. This one said, "There shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries and in Government, because of race, creed, color, or national origin." Creed means religion. With the order, Roosevelt set up a Committee on Fair Employment Practice to take complaints and look into them. Federal agencies, companies with defense contracts and the unions working on them all had to follow the order. Randolph called off the march. A National Archives page states that most historians judge that the committee did little, especially in the South. After the war, members of Congress refused to make it permanent.

In 1948 Randolph planned another march, this time against segregation in the armed forces, where officers still kept Black and white troops apart. On 26 July 1948 President Truman signed Executive Order 9981: "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." Military leaders resisted the order. By about 1953, the end of the Korean War, Black and white troops served together in nearly all of the armed services.

In 1963 Randolph was the chief organizer of the March on Washington. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and died at his home in New York City on 16 May 1979, aged 90.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="a-philip-randolph-rights-movements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="About 120,000 people held without charges, 1942 to 1945" -->
In 1942 Army officers, acting on President Roosevelt's order, forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry out of their homes on the west coast, and federal officials held them in camps. People of Japanese ancestry are people whose families came from Japan. Officials locked them up without charging them with any crime or giving them a trial. Fred Korematsu and others who disobeyed the orders were charged with breaking them. Japan had attacked Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on 7 December 1941.

On 19 February 1942 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. Under it, Army commanders could mark off "military areas," as large as they decided, and order "any or all persons" out of them. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, who commanded the Army on the west coast, used it against people of Japanese ancestry. He set curfews that applied only to Japanese Americans, and he gave families as little as 48 hours' notice to leave their homes. A curfew is an order to stay indoors during set hours. On 21 March 1942 members of Congress passed a law making it a crime to disobey his orders, punishable by up to a year in prison and a $5,000 fine. A page from the federal courts counts more than 120,000 people removed, two out of three of them American citizens. A National Archives page counts about 122,000, of whom nearly 70,000 were citizens.

Officials held them first in temporary centers such as the Tanforan racetrack near San Francisco, and then in ten camps built inland, in Wyoming, California, Utah, Arizona, Colorado, Idaho and Arkansas. One of the ten was Topaz, the Central Utah Relocation Center, 15 miles northwest of Delta, Utah. It opened on 11 September 1942 and closed on 31 October 1945. More than 11,000 people passed through it, most of them from around San Francisco. At its fullest, more than 8,000 lived there at once. The camp had 42 blocks, each with 12 barracks and 250 to 300 people. The barracks were built of pine boards covered with tarpaper. The furniture handed out was pot-bellied stoves, army cots, blankets and mattress covers. The people of each block shared one dining hall and one building of washrooms, toilets and laundry. A fence with seven watchtowers surrounded the camp.

On 11 April 1943, James Hatsuki Wakasa, a 63-year-old man born in Japan, was walking his dog inside the fence at Topaz. Private First Class Gerald Philpott, the military policeman in Guard Tower 8, shot him dead. Philpott said Wakasa had been too close to the fence and had not turned back when warned. Witnesses believed Wakasa was hard of hearing. Investigators found that his body lay several feet inside the fence and that he had been facing the guard when he was shot. Army officers tried Philpott at a court-martial, a military trial, for manslaughter, which means killing a person without planning to, and found him not guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four cases at the Supreme Court, and the one that won, 1943 and 1944" -->
Four Japanese Americans took the government's orders to the Supreme Court. Three of them lost, and one, Mitsuye Endo, won. Gordon Hirabayashi and Minoru Yasui each broke the curfew on purpose so that they could challenge it in court. On 28 March 1942 Yasui, a lawyer in Portland, Oregon, walked the streets after curfew, asked police officers to arrest him, and then turned himself in at the police station. Hirabayashi, a student at the University of Washington, stayed out past curfew, refused to report for removal, and went to the FBI himself on 16 May 1942. In 1943 the justices ruled in both cases that the curfew was allowed, and they put off the question of whether the removal itself was lawful.

They answered that question on 18 December 1944, in Fred Korematsu's case, and they ruled by six votes to three that the removal was allowed. Justice Hugo Black wrote for the majority that "all legal restrictions which curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect" and needed "the most rigid scrutiny," which means the closest possible look, and then he allowed this one. Justice Frank Murphy dissented. He called the removal "the legalization of racism." He compared it to the "abhorrent and despicable treatment of minority groups by the dictatorial tyrannies which this nation is now pledged to destroy." By dictatorial tyrannies he meant the countries the United States was fighting, which were ruled by dictators. Justice Robert Jackson also dissented. He wrote that Korematsu had been found guilty for "being present in the state whereof he is a citizen."

On the same day, the justices ruled in Mitsuye Endo's case, and the government lost. Endo was born in Sacramento, California, on 10 May 1920, and worked as a clerk for the state of California. A lawyer, James Purcell, chose her to bring the case because a judge would find it hard to call her disloyal. She worked for the state, she was a Christian, she spoke only English, she had never been to Japan, and her brother was in the United States Army. On 12 July 1942 Purcell filed a petition for habeas corpus for her in the federal court in San Francisco. A petition for habeas corpus asks a judge to order that a person held prisoner be brought to court, and freed if no one can show a lawful reason to hold her.

While the case went on, officials of the War Relocation Authority, the agency that ran the camps, offered to let her out if she dropped it. She refused and stayed in the camps, because leaving would have ended the case for everyone else. Officials moved her from the Sacramento assembly center to Tule Lake and then to Topaz. Justice William O. Douglas wrote for the Court that "Mitsuye Endo should be given her liberty." The government, he wrote, "has no authority to subject citizens who are concededly loyal to its leave procedure." He meant that officials could not keep holding citizens whom they themselves agreed were loyal. On 17 December 1944, the day before the rulings, Army commanders had issued Public Proclamation No. 21, which ended the exclusion from the west coast on 2 January 1945. The exclusion was the order keeping people of Japanese ancestry away from the coast. The last camp, Tule Lake, closed on 20 March 1946.

Endo moved to Chicago in May 1945 and worked as a secretary for the Mayor's Committee on Race Relations. She married Kenneth Tsutsumi, raised three children and died in Chicago on 14 April 2006.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Fred Korematsu

> **Who:** A welder who refused to leave his home when Japanese Americans were ordered out, lost at the Supreme Court, and had his conviction thrown out in 1983.
> **When and where:** Oakland, California, 1942. San Francisco, 1983.

Fred Korematsu stayed in his home town when Army commanders ordered people of Japanese ancestry out, and he went to prison and then to court for it. He lost in 1944, and a federal judge threw out his conviction, the finding that he was guilty, 39 years later.

He was born in Oakland, California, on 30 January 1919, and named Toyosaburo. A teacher suggested he call himself Fred. His father, Kakusaburo, came to the United States in 1905, and his mother, Kotsui, came through the immigration station at Angel Island in 1914. He had three brothers. The family ran the Stonehurst Flower Nursery in East Oakland.

When the orders came in 1942, his family went to the camps and he did not. He was 23. He had a girlfriend, Ida Boitano, and a job as a welder. He got identification papers in a false name, Clyde Sarah, and kept working. On 30 May 1942 police stopped him and his girlfriend on a street in San Leandro, California, and arrested him. The charge was breaking Civilian Exclusion Order No. 34, the Army's order for people of Japanese ancestry to leave his area.

Ernest Besig, a lawyer with the American Civil Liberties Union, visited him in the Army stockade, a military jail, at the Presidio in San Francisco and asked to take his case to the higher courts. Korematsu agreed. A federal judge found him guilty and gave him five years' probation, which meant he stayed free under a court's watch. Then Army officers sent him to the Tanforan racetrack on 28 June 1942, and to the Topaz camp with his family that September. The justices of the Supreme Court heard his case on 11 October 1944 and ruled against him on 18 December 1944.

After he left the camp, he moved to Detroit and married Kathryn Pearson. They came back to California and raised two children.

In the early 1980s, a team of lawyers who worked without pay found that the government's own lawyers had hidden evidence from the Supreme Court. Intelligence reports had told them that Japanese Americans were no military threat, and they had kept those reports out of the case. The team reopened his case. On 10 November 1983, Judge Marilyn Hall Patel ruled in the same San Francisco courthouse where he had been found guilty. She wrote that "the conviction that was handed down in this court and affirmed by the Supreme Court in *Korematsu v. United States* is ... vacated and the underlying indictment dismissed." Vacated means cancelled, and the indictment was the criminal charge against him.

About a pardon, a president's official forgiveness of a crime, he said, "I don't want a pardon. If anything, I should be pardoning the government." He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998. He died at Larkspur, California, on 30 March 2005, aged 86. In California, 30 January is Fred Korematsu Day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fred-korematsu" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elizabeth Peratrovich and the Alaska law, 1945" -->
On 16 February 1945 Alaska's lawmakers approved the Alaska Anti-Discrimination Act, after the Tlingit leader Elizabeth Peratrovich spoke for it before them. Alaska was then a territory, land ruled by the United States that was not yet a state. The act was the first law against discrimination in the United States or its territories. Signs such as "No Natives Allowed" hung in Alaska, and the lawmakers wrote the act against them. Peratrovich belonged to the Lukaax.ádi clan of the Tlingit, a clan being a group of related families. For half a century the Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded at Sitka in 1912, and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, founded in 1915, were the only civil rights groups for Alaska Natives. In Alaska, 16 February is Elizabeth Peratrovich Day every year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A soldier blinded on his way home, and the president's committee, 1946" -->
On 12 February 1946 Sergeant Isaac Woodard was discharged from the Army after four years and took a bus home to South Carolina. After he argued with the bus driver, police at Batesburg, South Carolina, took him off the bus. Police Chief Lynwood Shull and Officer Elliot Long beat him with blackjacks, short heavy clubs. Woodard said they tried to gouge out his eyes, which means to dig them out. A local judge fined Woodard himself $50. At the veterans' hospital in Aiken, doctors found bleeding inside both of his eyeballs and a torn cornea, the clear front of the eye, in his right eye. He was blind for the rest of his life. Justice Department lawyers charged Shull with breaking Woodard's civil rights. In November 1946 an all-white federal jury took thirty minutes to find Shull not guilty.

On 19 September 1946, Walter White of the NAACP led a group to see President Harry Truman and told him what had been done to Woodard. "My God! I had no idea that it was as terrible as that! We've got to do something!" Truman said. On 5 December 1946 he signed Executive Order 9808, which set up the President's Committee on Civil Rights. The committee's report, *To Secure These Rights*, came out in 1947.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Black Americans, women, Mexican Americans, gay Americans and disabled Americans all ran organized campaigns after 1950. Each group won changes in the country's laws. In 1954 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that segregated public schools broke the Constitution, and in 1956 they struck down segregation on Montgomery's buses. Members of Congress then passed three laws in 25 years. In 1965 they banned literacy tests for voting. In 1972 they banned sex discrimination in schools that took federal money. In 1990 they banned refusing a disabled person a job or a way into a public building. A literacy test was a reading and writing test that officials used to keep Black citizens from voting.

Each of those changes followed years of organizing, and the organizers paid for it. People who signed lawsuits lost their jobs, police beat marchers with nightsticks, and firemen turned high-pressure hoses on children. In 1963 members of the Ku Klux Klan, a secret white group that used terror against Black Americans, killed four girls with a bomb in a Birmingham church. The next year, white men in Mississippi killed three civil rights workers, and a Neshoba County deputy sheriff was among the men later found guilty over it. Civil rights are the rights every citizen has to equal treatment under the law and to vote. An Alabama state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson in 1965. Doctors operated on Black women such as Fannie Lou Hamer, and on poor girls, without asking them, so that they could never have children. Federal officials fired thousands of gay people from government jobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The law school cases, 1950" -->
NAACP lawyers following the plan Charles Hamilton Houston made in the 1930s won two more cases in June 1950, six weeks after he died. On 5 June 1950 the justices of the Supreme Court decided both cases, and all of them agreed on both.

Heman Marion Sweatt had applied to the University of Texas Law School in February 1946, and university officials turned him away because he was Black. Texas officials then opened a separate law school for Black students in Austin in February 1947. Its four teachers were part-timers borrowed from the university, and it had no full-time librarian. About 10,000 books were on order, and few had arrived. No body of lawyers had approved it as a real law school. Chief Justice Fred Vinson wrote that the education it offered was not "substantially equal" to what Sweatt would get at the university. "Equal protection of the laws is not achieved through indiscriminate imposition of inequalities," he wrote. He meant that a state could not call it equal treatment to give Black students something worse. The justices ordered Texas officials to admit Sweatt.

George W. McLaurin already had a master's degree and wanted a doctorate in education. University of Oklahoma officials let him in and then made rules for him alone. The justices' written opinion lists them. He "was required to sit apart at a designated desk in an anteroom adjoining the classroom," which means a desk set aside for him in a small room next to the classroom. He had to sit at an assigned desk on the library's upper floor, "but not to use the desks in the regular reading room." In the cafeteria he had "to sit at a designated table and to eat at a different time from the other students." Before the case was argued, university officials eased the rules a little. McLaurin could sit in a marked row in the classroom and eat at the usual hours, still at a table of his own. The justices struck down the whole arrangement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What signing cost in Clarendon County, 1949 and 1950" -->
In Clarendon County, South Carolina, 107 Black parents and children signed a petition against the county's schools. For it, employers fired them, merchants refused to sell to them and lenders turned them down for loans. A petition is a written request, signed by many people, asking officials to change something. School District 22 ran more than 30 school buses for white children and none for Black children. The parents began by asking for one bus. The court record describes the all-white Summerton High School as "modern, safe, sanitary, well equipped, lighted and healthy." It describes the Black schools as "inadequate…unhealthy…old and overcrowded and in a dilapidated condition," which means falling apart.

The Reverend Joseph A. DeLaine, a minister and teacher, brought the NAACP together with the Pearson brothers to start the case. Six white men later attacked DeLaine. He got away by pretending he had a gun in his pocket and jumping into the passing truck of another man who had signed.

The petition went round for signatures on 11 November 1949, and the parents' lawyers filed the lawsuit on 16 May 1950. It is named for Harry Briggs, whose name was first on the list. A National Park Service page records what happened to the signers. They were "fired from their jobs, denied service when trying to purchase supplies for their farms, or being turned down for loans on their properties."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Barbara Rose Johns

> **Who:** A sixteen-year-old who led her whole school out on strike, three years before *Brown*.
> **When and where:** Farmville, Prince Edward County, Virginia, 23 April 1951.

Barbara Rose Johns was sixteen when she led the students of her all-Black high school out on strike against their building. A strike is when people stop work, or here stop going to class, as a protest. The strike led to a lawsuit that became one of the five cases the justices of the Supreme Court decided as *Brown v. Board of Education*.

She was born in New York City on 6 March 1935. By 1951 she was a student at Robert Russa Moton High School in Farmville, the high school for Black students in Prince Edward County, Virginia. The building had been made for 180 students, and it held between 360 and 450. Classes met in the auditorium, in school buses and in three wooden sheds wrapped in tar paper, which people compared to chicken coops. The school had no cafeteria, no science laboratory and no gym. One day a half-empty school bus for white children passed her without stopping, and she decided to act.

On 23 April 1951 a student named John Watson telephoned the principal's office and disguised his voice. He said Moton students were making trouble at the bus station downtown. The principal, M. Boyd Jones, left to see. While he was gone, the strike Johns led began. The students stayed out for two weeks.

She wrote to two NAACP lawyers in Richmond, Oliver W. Hill and Spottswood William Robinson III. They agreed to take the case on one condition. The parents had to sue to end segregation itself, rather than ask for a better Black school. On 3 May 1951 the Reverend L. Francis Griffin called the parents to a meeting at the First Baptist Church in Farmville to hear the offer. They agreed. On 23 May 1951, 74 parents filed the lawsuit, *Davis v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, for 118 Moton students.

Her parents sent her out of the county. She finished her last year of high school in Montgomery, Alabama, living with her uncle, the Reverend Vernon Johns, and his wife, Altona. Her family's house in Prince Edward County later burned down, and the family believed someone had set the fire. Barbara Rose Johns died of bone cancer in Philadelphia on 25 September 1991, aged 56.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-johns" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Brown v. Board of Education, 17 May 1954" -->
On 17 May 1954, in *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka*, all nine justices of the Supreme Court ruled that separating children by race in public schools broke the 14th Amendment. The 14th Amendment, part of the Constitution since 1868, says that no state may deny any person "the equal protection of the laws." Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote, "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." Inherently means by their very nature. With that ruling, the justices rejected the reasoning of *Plessy v. Ferguson* for public schools, 58 years after that case. In *Plessy*, in 1896, the justices had allowed a Louisiana law that kept Black and white train passengers apart in "equal but separate" cars.

*Brown* was five lawsuits that the justices bundled together. They were *Brown v. Board of Education* from Topeka, Kansas, *Briggs v. Elliott* from Clarendon County, South Carolina, *Davis v. County School Board* from Prince Edward County, Virginia, *Belton v. Gebhart* from Delaware, and *Bolling v. Sharpe* from Washington, D.C.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rosa-parks-mlk" name="Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr." movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr.

> **Who:** The NAACP branch secretary whose arrest set off the Montgomery bus boycott, and the 26-year-old minister the boycotters chose to lead it.
> **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, December 1955 to December 1956.

Rosa Parks had been the secretary of the Montgomery NAACP for twelve years on the day she was arrested for keeping her bus seat. Black women in Montgomery had been organizing about the buses for six years. They turned her arrest into a boycott that lasted more than a year. A boycott is a refusal to use or buy something, as a protest.

She was born in Tuskegee, Alabama, on 4 February 1913, and married Raymond Parks in 1932. In the early 1940s she started the Montgomery NAACP's Youth Council, and she was the branch's secretary from 1943 to 1956. As secretary of the NAACP's Alabama State Conference, she traveled the state interviewing victims of discrimination and witnesses to lynchings. She had broken the bus seating rules many times before, and a driver had once put her off a bus for it.

Nine months before Parks's arrest, on 2 March 1955, a fifteen-year-old student named Claudette Colvin refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus when white passengers got on. She told police she had a right under the Constitution to sit anywhere on the bus, as white passengers did. The officers swore at her, hit her with a nightstick and dragged her off the bus. E. D. Nixon, a Montgomery organizer, and others decided not to build a campaign around her case. Nixon thought she was too young. When she became pregnant later that year, he looked for someone who fit the strict customs of the time. Colvin believed the choice also had to do with her family's standing outside Montgomery's Black middle class.

Police arrested Rosa Parks on 1 December 1955 for refusing to give up her bus seat. The Women's Political Council, a group of Black professional women formed in 1949, had already been pressing white city officials about the buses. Jo Ann Robinson of the council had proposed a boycott more than a year before. After Parks's arrest, council members printed 50,000 leaflets calling for a one-day boycott on Monday, 5 December.

After that Monday, Black riders stayed off the buses. The same day, Montgomery's Black ministers and organizers formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and elected Martin Luther King Jr. as its president. He was 26, the pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, and still finishing his doctorate at Boston University. About 40,000 Black people lived in Montgomery. For a year, most of them walked to work or school, shared cars, took taxis or hitchhiked.

City leaders had nearly 100 of the boycott's leaders charged with conspiracy, the crime of planning together to break the law. On 30 January 1956, someone bombed King's house while his wife, Coretta Scott King, their seven-week-old daughter, Yolanda, and a neighbor were inside. No one was hurt, and no one was ever charged with a crime for the bombing. On 1 February a bomb went off in E. D. Nixon's yard.

The lawsuit that ended bus segregation in Montgomery was brought by four other women. On 1 February 1956 the lawyer Fred Gray filed *Browder v. Gayle* for four women: Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Mary Louise Smith and Claudette Colvin, then sixteen. In June 1956 federal judges in Montgomery struck down Alabama's bus segregation laws, and in November the justices of the Supreme Court agreed. Black riders went back on the buses on 21 December 1956. Colvin later worked as a nurse's aide in New York for fifty years. A judge erased her 1955 arrest record on 16 December 2022, and she died on 13 January 2026, aged 86.

Rosa Parks lost her job as a tailor and received death threats. She and Raymond moved to Detroit in 1957. She worked in the office of Representative John Conyers from 1965 to 1988, and she died in Detroit on 24 October 2005.

King led the movement for twelve more years. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, at 35. In 1968 he went to Memphis, Tennessee, twice to support 1,300 Black sanitation workers on strike. On 1 February 1968, two of them, Echol Cole and Robert Walker, had been crushed to death in the compactor of their garbage truck. The strikers carried signs that read "I AM A MAN." King was shot in Memphis on 4 April 1968 and died at 7:05 that evening at St. Joseph's Hospital. He was 39. After his death, city officials recognized the workers' union.

James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to King's murder in March 1969, and a judge sentenced him to 99 years. For the rest of his life Ray tried to take back his plea, until he died in April 1998. In 1999 the King family brought a civil case, a lawsuit rather than a criminal trial, against a Memphis man named Jowers. The jury found that Jowers and "others, including government agencies" had plotted the killing. In 2000 Justice Department investigators reported that they found those claims not believable.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rosa-parks-mlk" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="After Brown: Little Rock, Prince Edward County and New Orleans, 1957 to 1964" -->
After *Brown*, Black families still had to force their way into white schools one district at a time, and white officials fought them with new city rules and by shutting down public schools.

In Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1957, nine Black students entered Central High School. Daisy Bates organized them. She was president of the Arkansas State Conference of the NAACP, and with her husband, L. C. Bates, she published a newspaper, the *Arkansas State Press*. The nine students met at her house every school day and left for Central High from there. In August 1957 a rock came through her living room window with a note tied to it: "STONE THIS TIME. DYNAMITE NEXT." Twice, people burned crosses in her front yard, and someone fired shots into the house from a passing car. The National Park Service's page on the Bates home does not name the people who did it.

Then members of the city councils of Little Rock and North Little Rock passed rules that made every organization hand its list of members to the city clerk, to be made public. Daisy Bates kept the records of the Little Rock branch of the NAACP. She refused to hand over the list, and she was found guilty in court for refusing. On 23 February 1960 the justices of the Supreme Court threw out that verdict. Justice Potter Stewart wrote that keeping a group's members private "may in many circumstances be indispensable to preservation of freedom of association, particularly where a group espouses dissident beliefs." He meant that people have a right to join a group without officials forcing the group to name its members. Dissident beliefs are beliefs that go against those of the people in power.

Prince Edward County, Virginia, was home to the Moton students' case. On 26 June 1959 members of the county's board of supervisors voted to stop paying for public schools. The county had no public schools until 8 September 1964. White children went to a private school, Prince Edward Academy. About 2,000 Black children went without formal schooling for five years. In 1963 Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy said "the only places on earth not to provide free public education are Communist China, North Vietnam, Sarawak, Singapore, British Honduras." Then he added, "and Prince Edward County, Virginia." On 25 May 1964 the justices of the Supreme Court decided a case brought by Black schoolchildren of the county, the first of them named Cocheyse J. Griffin. They ruled that closing the county's schools, while every other county in Virginia kept its schools open, denied the children "the equal protection of the laws." They said a federal judge could make the supervisors raise taxes to reopen the schools.

In New Orleans, a federal judge, J. Skelly Wright, ordered the city's school officials to start letting Black children into white schools in the spring of 1960. On 14 November 1960, four Black six-year-old girls went into two white schools. Ruby Bridges went alone into William Frantz Elementary. Leona Tate, Tessie Prevost and Gail Etienne went together into McDonogh 19, in the Lower Ninth Ward, with federal marshals, officers who work for the federal courts, walking them in. White parents took their children out. By the end of the month, fewer than ten white children attended the two schools combined. From January to May 1961, Ruby Bridges was the only pupil at William Frantz.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Levittown, 1957: a family moves in" -->
In August 1957 Daisy and William Myers became the first Black family to live in Levittown, Pennsylvania, and white mobs gathered outside their home for nearly three months. William Myers was an Army veteran of the Second World War, and Daisy Myers was a teacher. They had three young children.

In the first Levittown, in New York, the standard lease had barred anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race" from living in a house, except white servants. Caucasian meant white. In 1948 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled in *Shelley v. Kraemer* that no court could enforce clauses like that, which means no judge could order anyone to obey them. The builder, William Levitt, dropped the written clause and kept selling only to white buyers.

When the Myers family moved in, mobs gathered outside the house. People threw rocks through the windows, burned crosses and made death threats by telephone. Delivery men stopped bringing milk, bread and heating oil. State police guarded the house. A documentary, *Crisis in Levittown*, was filmed while it was happening, in 1957. Daisy Myers later wrote a book about it, *Sticks 'n Stones*, published in 2005.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sit-ins and Freedom Rides, 1960 and 1961" -->
In 1960 and 1961, Black and white students used their own bodies to test segregation, first at lunch counters and then on buses. On 1 February 1960, four freshmen from the Agricultural and Technical College of North Carolina walked into the Woolworth store in Greensboro. Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil, David Richmond and Ezell Blair Jr. sat at the lunch counter, which was for white customers only, ordered coffee and waited. The waitress and the manager ignored them. A police officer walked up and down behind them. Some white customers shouted at them, and others encouraged them.

The next day 19 students came. The day after that 85 came, from several colleges. Before the week was out there were 400, taking turns so they could still go to class. Students started sit-ins in seven other North Carolina cities and in Hampton, Virginia, and Nashville, Tennessee. Woolworth's managers opened the Greensboro counter to Black customers on 25 July 1960. By that summer, restaurants and lunch counters in 33 Southern cities served Black customers, and a year later those in 126 cities did.

In 1960, in *Boynton v. Virginia*, the justices of the Supreme Court struck down Virginia laws that required segregation in travel between states. Leaders of the Congress of Racial Equality, known as CORE, organized riders to find out whether the people running bus stations in the South would obey. On 4 May 1961, the first thirteen Freedom Riders, Black and white, left Washington, D.C., in two groups, one on a Greyhound bus and one on a Trailways bus. A National Park Service page gives the number as eleven.

On 14 May 1961, at Anniston, Alabama, a white mob that included members of the Ku Klux Klan attacked the Greyhound bus. They slashed its tires, followed it out of town and firebombed it. The riders were trapped on the burning bus. They got out after an explosion, and then the mob beat them. The Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth organized a line of cars and got them to Birmingham within a few hours. A photographer, Joe Postiglione, took pictures of the bus as it burned. Charles Person, one of the Freedom Riders, later wrote that the photograph appeared "on front pages of newspapers across the country and the world." Accounts name different men as the mob's leaders. No record shows that anyone went to prison for burning the bus.

The same day, young white men climbed onto the second bus, a Trailways bus, at Anniston and beat the riders until they moved into segregated seats. One of the riders they beat was Walter Bergman, a white man. The Encyclopedia of Alabama states that he suffered permanent brain damage.

When that bus reached Birmingham, a crowd armed with pipes, chains and baseball bats met it, and many of the men in it belonged to the Klan. Bull Connor, Birmingham's public safety commissioner, had told Klan leaders that his police would hold back from the station for 15 minutes, and he encouraged the Klan to attack the riders. With no police there, Klansmen beat the riders in the waiting room and in a dark hallway. They beat James Peck, a white rider, until he was unconscious, with heavy wounds to his face and head. Staff at a hospital for white people only refused to treat him, and doctors at Jefferson Hillman Hospital closed his wounds with 53 stitches. In the historian Raymond Arsenault's account, Bergman was beaten for the second time that day. Over the summer of 1961 the number of Freedom Riders grew to more than 400.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fannie-lou-hamer" name="Fannie Lou Hamer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fannie Lou Hamer

> **Who:** A plantation timekeeper in Mississippi who tried to register to vote, lost her job the same day, was beaten in a jail for her organizing, and told the country about it on national television.
> **When and where:** Ruleville, Sunflower County, Mississippi, 1962 and 1963. Atlantic City, New Jersey, 22 August 1964.

Fannie Lou Hamer was fired for trying to register to vote, and beaten in a Mississippi jail for helping others do it. In 1964 she described the beating on national television, to the Democratic Party's leaders, while they decided whether to seat her delegation.

She was born in Montgomery County, Mississippi, on 6 October 1917, the youngest of the twenty children of Ella and James Lee Townsend. She began working in the cotton fields at six, weeding first and then picking. In 1944 she married Perry Hamer, whom people called Pap. For eighteen years she worked on the W. D. Marlow plantation outside Ruleville, keeping the time records for the other workers.

In 1961 she went into a hospital, and a doctor performed a hysterectomy on her without asking her or telling her. A hysterectomy is an operation that cuts out the womb, the organ in which a baby grows. After it, a woman can never become pregnant, and it cannot be undone. An appendectomy is an ordinary operation to take out the appendix, a small part of the gut. Hamer's name for operations like hers, a "Mississippi appendectomy," came into use for them. The Mississippi Department of Archives and History's account of her life does not name the surgeon.

On 31 August 1962 she and seventeen others went 26 miles to the Sunflower County courthouse in Indianola to register to vote. Police let only two of them in to take the literacy test. Registrars, the officials who signed people up to vote, used that test to turn Black voters away. That evening the plantation owner told her, "If you don't go down and withdraw your registration, you will have to leave." She answered, "I didn't try to register for you. I tried to register for myself." She lost her job that day. On 10 September someone fired sixteen bullets into the Tucker family's house in Ruleville, and other houses were shot at the same night.

On 9 June 1963 she was one of ten people coming home by Continental Trailways bus from a voter registration workshop. At the bus station in Winona, Mississippi, some of the group tried to use the restaurant and the washroom. Winona's police chief, Thomas Herrod, ordered them arrested. In the jail, state highway patrolmen made two Black prisoners, Roosevelt Knox and Sol Poe, beat her with a blackjack. The patrolmen ordered her to lie face down on a bunk. The first prisoner beat her until he was exhausted. Then the patrolman gave the blackjack to the second prisoner and made the first one sit on her feet so she could not move them. "I began to scream and one white man got up and began to beat me in my head and tell me to hush," she said. The officers also had June Johnson, who was fifteen, and Annell Ponder beaten.

Hamer lost the sight of one eye and had kidney damage for the rest of her life. "After I got out of jail, half dead, I found that Medgar Evers had been shot down in his own yard," she said.

In December 1963, federal prosecutors tried five white Winona and Montgomery County officers, among them Chief Herrod, for violating the prisoners' civil rights. An all-white, all-male jury found them not guilty on every count.

Mississippi's delegation to the 1964 Democratic National Convention was all white. A delegation is the group of people a state party sends to its national party's meeting to vote. Hamer and others founded the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, and in August 1964 they went to the convention in Atlantic City to challenge the all-white delegation and ask to be seated, or given its places, instead. She was the group's vice chair. On 22 August she told the convention's credentials committee, the group that decided which delegates could take their seats, what had been done to her. Television carried it across the country. A National Archives page records that her testimony "was interrupted by President Lyndon Johnson, in an effort to suppress the truth and stabilize the Democratic Party." She ended, "All of this is on account of we want to register, to become first-class citizens. And if the Freedom Democratic Party is not seated now, I question America. Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave, where we have to sleep with our telephones off the hooks because our lives be threatened daily, because we want to live as decent human beings, in America?"

In 1969 she started the Freedom Farm to grow food and give families in Sunflower County a living. She used to say, "Nobody's free until everybody's free." She died on 14 March 1977, at 59, of heart failure brought on by cancer, diabetes and high blood pressure. Writers at the Equal Justice Initiative state that the kidney damage from the beating helped cause her death.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Birmingham, 1963: the children and the church" -->
In May 1963 Birmingham, Alabama, police arrested hundreds of Black children for marching against segregation, and firemen turned hoses on them. That September, members of the Ku Klux Klan killed four girls with a bomb at the church the marches had started from.

On 2 May 1963, students aged eight to eighteen gathered at the 16th Street Baptist Church and marched downtown to ask the mayor to end segregation. On the orders of Bull Connor, the public safety commissioner, police arrested them. National Park Service pages give two counts for that day. One page says 600 children were arrested, some as young as six. Another says 1,200. By the next day at least 1,000 children were in jail. Over the following days Connor's police jailed more than 3,000 demonstrators.

On 3 May Connor ordered fire hoses and police dogs turned on the marchers. A National Park Service page describes it: "Firemen blasted the protestors, including children, with high-pressure fire hoses, and police used their nightsticks indiscriminately. Police K-9 units loosed their dogs into crowds of peaceful demonstrators, pinning down one woman and severely wounding three teenagers." Indiscriminately means without caring whom they hit. These were the hoses firemen used on burning buildings, and the water came out hard enough to knock people off their feet.

On Sunday, 15 September 1963, just before 11 in the morning, a bomb went off under the steps of the 16th Street Baptist Church. It killed four girls: Addie Mae Collins, Carole Robertson and Cynthia Wesley, who were fourteen, and Denise McNair, who was eleven. A few minutes before, they had been together in the basement women's room in their Sunday clothes, ready to sing and to show people to their seats at the adult service, talking about the new school year. Addie Mae's sister, Sarah, survived and lost her right eye.

The bombers were members of the Ku Klux Klan. FBI agents named Robert Chambliss as their main suspect. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover closed the investigation in 1968 without charging anyone and ordered the evidence sealed. Investigators reopened the case after Hoover died in 1972. Juries later found three of the bombers guilty: Chambliss on 14 November 1977, fourteen years after the bombing, Thomas Blanton on 1 May 2001, and Bobby Frank Cherry in 2002, 39 years after it. Chambliss died in prison in 1985. Blanton was given four life sentences and died in prison in 2020. A fourth Klansman, Herman Cash, died in 1994 without being tried.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organizers killed in Mississippi, 1963 and 1964" -->
White men in Mississippi killed civil rights organizers in 1963 and 1964, and for decades no jury in the state found anyone guilty of murder for it.

Medgar Evers was born at Decatur, Mississippi, on 2 July 1925, and fought in Germany and France in the Second World War. From 1954 he was the NAACP's field secretary for the state of Mississippi. He led marches, prayer vigils, boycotts and drives to register voters. He worked to get James Meredith admitted to the University of Mississippi in 1962.

In the early hours of 12 June 1963, Byron De La Beckwith, a member of the White Citizens' Council, shot Evers in the back in the driveway of his home in Jackson. The Citizens' Council was a group founded in Mississippi in 1954, after *Brown*, to keep segregation in place. Evers's wife, Myrlie, and their children had waited up for him and were watching television. When they heard the shot, the children did what they had practiced: they dropped to the floor and crawled to the bathroom. Myrlie found her husband collapsed and bleeding, still holding the T-shirts he had carried in from the car. He died at University Hospital from his wounds and loss of blood. Beckwith was tried twice in 1964, and both juries failed to agree on whether he was guilty. On 5 February 1994, 31 years and three trials after the killing, a jury found him guilty, and the judge sentenced him to life in prison. He died on 21 January 2001, aged 80.

In the summer of 1964, volunteers came to Mississippi to register Black voters and run summer schools of their own, called Freedom Schools. It was called Freedom Summer. On 21 June 1964, three of the workers went to look at a Black church that had been burned after a civil rights meeting was held there. James Chaney, 21, was a Black college student from Mississippi. Andrew Goodman, 20, and Michael Schwerner, 24, were white New Yorkers working with CORE. Local police arrested the three for "speeding" and held them in jail. The National Archives records that they were "released after dark into the hands of the Ku Klux Klan."

Searchers found their bodies on 4 August 1964, near Old Jolly Farm in Neshoba County. Goodman and Schwerner had each been shot once in the head. Chaney had two broken arms, a broken jaw, a crushed right shoulder and injuries to his groin.

Twenty-one men were arrested. On 20 October 1967 a federal jury found seven of the eighteen men on trial guilty of violating the three workers' civil rights. They included Cecil Price, the Neshoba County deputy sheriff, and Samuel Bowers, the imperial wizard, or top leader, of the White Knights of the Ku Klux Klan of Mississippi. Their sentences ran from three to ten years. One juror would not vote to find Edgar Ray Killen guilty. Killen was a former preacher and Klan leader who had organized the killings. On 21 June 2005, 41 years to the day after the killings, a Mississippi jury found Killen guilty of manslaughter. He was sentenced to 60 years and died in the state prison at Parchman on 11 January 2018.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The March on Washington, 28 August 1963" -->
About 250,000 people came to Washington on 28 August 1963 for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. A National Park Service page counts about 190,000 of them as Black and about 60,000 as white. A. Philip Randolph, who had threatened a march on Washington in 1941 and called it off, led this one. Bayard Rustin organized it in less than three months.

The march's sponsors were all men. Besides Randolph, five of them led civil rights groups. They were Roy Wilkins of the NAACP, Whitney Young of the National Urban League, Martin Luther King Jr. of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, James Farmer of CORE and John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. The others were the union leader Walter Reuther, Joachim Prinz, Eugene Carson Blake and Matthew Ahmann. The sponsors issued ten demands, among them fair wages, fair hiring and desegregated schools. No woman gave a formal speech. Dorothy Height of the National Council of Negro Women helped plan the march, and the Park Service page states that she "operated in the background of this male dominated, leadership group."

The same page records that John Lewis's speech was "toned down...at the request of white liberals and moderate black allies," which means people on his own side asked him to say it more softly. What he said included, "we do not want our freedom gradually but we want to be free now."

### Bayard Rustin

> **Who:** The organizer who planned the March on Washington in under three months, and who was found guilty in a California court in 1953 for having sex with men.
> **When and where:** Born at West Chester, Pennsylvania, 17 March 1912. Los Angeles, 1953. Washington, D.C., 1963.

Bayard Rustin planned the 1963 March on Washington. In 1953 he was found guilty in court for having sex with men. His political opponents used that record to try to weaken him, the people he worked with and the civil rights movement. California's governor pardoned him in 2020, 33 years after he died. A pardon is an official forgiveness of a crime.

He was born in West Chester, Pennsylvania, on 17 March 1912. He was raised a Quaker, a member of a Christian group that teaches peace, in a family already doing civil rights work. He was a pacifist all his life, a person who refuses to take part in war or violence. In 1942, in California, he visited Japanese Americans held at the Manzanar camp and reported on the conditions there.

In 1947 he and George Houser organized the Journey of Reconciliation, a bus trip to test a Supreme Court ruling against racial discrimination in travel between states. A National Park Service page calls it the first of the Freedom Rides. Police arrested Rustin for breaking a state law on segregated seating and served 22 days on a chain gang, a line of prisoners chained at the ankles and made to work outdoors.

On 22 January 1953, in the Los Angeles County Superior Court, he was found guilty of vagrancy, a crime that means wandering with no job or home. What he had done was have sex with other men who agreed to it. The judge sentenced him to 60 days in jail and ordered him to register as a sex offender, which put his name on California's list of people found guilty of sex crimes. California's governor wrote in 2020 that "Mr. Rustin was sentenced pursuant to a charge commonly used to punish gay men for engaging in consensual adult sexual conduct." That means the charge was often used to punish gay men for sex that both adults agreed to. The same document says his conviction caused "an injustice that was compounded by his political opponents' use of the record of this case to try to undermine him, his associates, and the civil rights movement."

He taught Martin Luther King Jr. how to practice nonviolent resistance, and he worked for years with A. Philip Randolph. Randolph made him deputy director of the March on Washington, and Rustin planned the whole of it.

He died in New York City on 24 August 1987, of a burst appendix, aged 75. On 8 August 2013 President Barack Obama awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom and called him "an unyielding activist for civil rights, dignity, and equality for all." On 4 February 2020 Governor Gavin Newsom of California pardoned him. In the same order, Newsom told his office to find and pardon other people who had been arrested and charged for sex between adults who agreed to it.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two men climb the Gateway Arch, 1964" -->
On 14 July 1964, Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed 125 feet up the unfinished north leg of the Gateway Arch in St. Louis, Missouri. They were protesting the fact that Black workers were kept out of the jobs building it. Green belonged to CORE and later founded a group called ACTION.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selma and the Voting Rights Act, 1965" -->
In 1965 marchers in Selma, Alabama, campaigned for the right to vote, and state troopers, other officers and Klansmen attacked them. The 15th Amendment had barred states from denying the vote because of race since 1870. In much of the South, officials still used literacy tests to keep Black citizens from registering.

On 18 February 1965, during a march for voting rights in Marion, Alabama, State Trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Jimmie Lee Jackson in the stomach inside a cafe. Jackson was 26 and had been protecting his mother and grandfather. He died of an infection from the wound on 26 February. In May 2007 Fowler was charged with murder. In 2010 he pleaded guilty to misdemeanor manslaughter, a minor charge, and served five months of a six-month sentence.

On Sunday, 7 March 1965, about 600 people, most of them Black, walked out of Brown Chapel in Selma to march to Montgomery, the state capital. At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, Major John Cloud of the Alabama State Troopers gave them two minutes to turn back. Then state troopers and other officers attacked them "with nightsticks and teargas," in the National Park Service's words. At least 50 marchers needed hospital care. The day is called Bloody Sunday.

On 9 March about 2,000 people marched again, led by King. The marchers turned back at the bridge, and the day is called Turnaround Tuesday. That evening four men attacked three ministers outside a restaurant in Selma. One of the attackers hit the Reverend James Reeb of Boston in the head with a club, and Reeb died two days later. Elmer L. Cook, William Stanley Hoggle and Namon O'Neil Hoggle were tried for his murder, and on 10 December 1965 the jury found all three not guilty. The prosecutor had allowed a man whose brother was a suspect in the attack to sit on the jury.

The march set out again on 21 March 1965 with more than 8,000 people. By the time it reached the capitol in Montgomery on 25 March, the crowd had grown to nearly 25,000. That night, Viola Liuzzo was driving on Highway 80 between Selma and Montgomery. She was a 39-year-old white mother of five from Detroit who had come to help. In Lowndes County, four members of the Ku Klux Klan fired into her car and killed her. Her passenger, Leroy Moton, a 19-year-old Black volunteer, survived. The four men were Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., William Orville Eaton, Eugene Thomas and Gary Thomas Rowe. Rowe was a paid FBI informant, a person who secretly passed information to the FBI, and prosecutors promised not to charge him. Alabama juries did not find the other three guilty. A federal jury found them guilty of violating her civil rights, and the judge sentenced each to ten years. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover called Liuzzo an "outside agitator" and spread rumors about her to the press. Her family sued the FBI in 1979, and a federal judge dismissed the suit in 1983.

President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965. Under it, literacy tests were banned, and federal officials sent examiners into the places that had used them, with the power to sign up voters themselves. States and counties on the act's list had to get approval from federal officials in Washington before changing any voting rule. By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had registered, a third of them through the federal examiners. By the end of 1966, only four of the thirteen Southern states still had fewer than half of their Black citizens registered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chicano movement, 1954 to 1970" -->
Mexican Americans ran their own rights campaigns from the 1950s. Many called themselves Chicanos, and their campaigns the Chicano movement.

In 1954 they won a case at the Supreme Court. A jury in Jackson County, Texas, found a Mexican American man, Pete Hernandez, guilty of murder, and he was sentenced to life in prison. About 14 of every 100 people in the county had Mexican or Latin American last names. In 25 years, no one with such a name had served on a jury commission, the board that chose jurors. More than 6,000 jurors had been called in that time, and none had such a name. On the courthouse grounds, one of the two men's toilets was marked "Colored Men" and "Hombres Aqui," which means "Men Here." On 3 May 1954, in *Hernandez v. Texas*, all nine justices ruled that shutting Mexican Americans out of juries broke the 14th Amendment and its promise of equal protection. The ruling came two weeks before *Brown*.

From 1 to 8 March 1968, about 15,000 students walked out of seven schools in East Los Angeles. About 60 of every 100 Mexican American students there left high school without finishing. Staff forbade students to speak Spanish and steered them into trade classes instead of classes that prepared them for college. The students asked for teaching in both languages, changes to what they were taught, and Mexican American principals. Members of the Board of Education turned them down, saying the changes would cost too much. On 31 March police arrested thirteen organizers on charges of felony conspiracy, which means planning together to commit a serious crime. One of them, the teacher Sal Castro, was fired. He got his job back after months of sit-ins at the board's offices. On 2 October police arrested 35 people there.

On 29 August 1970, between 20,000 and 30,000 people marched through East Los Angeles in the National Chicano Moratorium, a protest against the war. They were protesting how many Mexican Americans were being drafted, wounded and killed in the Vietnam War. About 1,500 police officers came to Laguna Park, fired tear gas into the crowd and beat protesters. Four people died that day, among them Rubén Salazar, and 150 were arrested. Los Angeles County Sheriff's Deputy Thomas Wilson fired a tear gas projectile into the Silver Dollar Bar on Whittier Boulevard. It hit Rubén Salazar, a reporter for the *Los Angeles Times*, in the head and killed him. The coroner, the county official who decides how a person died, ruled his death a homicide, a killing by another person. District Attorney Evelle Younger decided not to charge Wilson. City officials later renamed Laguna Park for Salazar.

In the same years, farmworkers organized under Larry Itliong, Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta. On 8 September 1965 about 1,500 mostly Filipino American grape workers led by Itliong walked out at Delano, California. Eight days later, members of the union of Chavez and Huerta, the National Farm Workers Association, voted to join them.

In March 2026 the *New York Times* reported that Chavez had sexually abused two girls in the 1970s. The two women, Debra Rojas and Ana Murguia, are daughters of union organizers. They said the abuse began when they were 12 and 13, and Rojas said Chavez raped her in a motel room when she was 15. Dolores Huerta then said publicly that Chavez had pressured her into sex once and had raped her in a car in 1966, and that she became pregnant both times. She said she had kept the secret for 60 years because she believed the truth would hurt the farmworker movement. Chavez died in 1993, so no court has ever heard these accusations. The United Farm Workers canceled its events for Cesar Chavez Day. On 26 March 2026 California's lawmakers voted to rename the state holiday Farmworkers Day, and Governor Gavin Newsom signed the change into law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The women's movement and the Equal Rights Amendment, 1966 to 1982" -->
Women organized in the late 1960s for equal treatment at work and under the law, and they won some laws and lost a constitutional amendment by three states.

Since 1964, federal law had banned sex discrimination at work. On 30 June 1966, at a government conference on women in Washington, some delegates wanted the conference to demand that a federal office enforce that ban. The office was the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Conference officials ruled the demand out of order, against the meeting's rules. They said a government commission could not act against another government office. That night 28 people met in the hotel room of Betty Friedan, whose 1963 book *The Feminine Mystique* had been a best-seller, and founded the National Organization for Women, known as NOW. They set it up "to take action to bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society NOW."

Alice Paul wrote the first version of the Equal Rights Amendment, and it was first brought before Congress in 1923. The first section of the version members of Congress voted on in the 1970s reads: "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Representative Martha Griffiths of Michigan pushed it through the House in 1971. Senators passed it on 22 March 1972, by 84 votes to 8. Three-fourths of the states, 38 of them, had to ratify it within seven years. Hawaii ratified first, and 35 states had ratified by 1977.

Phyllis Schlafly led the campaign against it, called STOP ERA, from 1972. She argued that under the amendment women could be drafted into the military. She also argued that widows would lose Social Security money paid to them as wives, and that mothers would lose their edge in court fights over who keeps the children. Lawmakers in Nebraska, Tennessee, Idaho, Kentucky and South Dakota voted to take back their states' approval. Members of Congress extended the deadline to 1982, and no more states ratified before it ran out. When the deadline passed, the amendment was three states short of the 38 it needed.

In October 1974 members of Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. Under it, no lender could legally refuse a woman credit, a loan or the right to buy now and pay later, because of her sex or because she was married or single.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Title IX, 1972" -->
Title IX became law on 23 June 1972. Its main sentence reads: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." The sentence covers every school or college program paid for even in part with federal money, and school sports teams are among those programs.

Representative Patsy Takemoto Mink of Hawaii, Representative Edith Green of Oregon and Senator Birch Bayh of Indiana worked together to pass it. Green, who had taught school for 11 years, led the hearings in the House in 1970. After Mink died on 28 September 2002, members of Congress renamed the law the Patsy T. Mink Equal Opportunity in Education Act.

Before Title IX, fewer than 300,000 girls played high school sports. By 2012 more than 3 million did. That year the White House reported that American high schools still offered girls 1.3 million fewer chances to play sports than boys. In the 2013-14 school year, the Government Accountability Office found that girls were 49 of every 100 students at public high schools that offered sports, and 43 of every 100 players.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gay Americans organize, 1953 to 1979" -->
From the 1950s, gay Americans organized openly against being fired, arrested and photographed for who they were. On 27 April 1953 President Dwight Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450. The order listed "sexual perversion," the government's term then for being gay, among the reasons to remove a person from a federal job. The historian David K. Johnson estimates that "somewhere between 5,000 and 10,000 people lost their jobs" under it. Frank Kameny, who worked at the Army Map Service, was fired in 1957 and fought his firing as a civil rights case. On 21 May 1965 protesters picketed the White House against the firings. In 1975 the members of the Civil Service Commission, which set the rules for federal jobs, announced that federal workers would no longer be fired for being gay.

In New York in the 1960s, state rules forbade bars to serve alcohol to gay people. Police raided gay bars, arrested the customers and photographed them. In the early hours of 28 June 1969, police led by Deputy Inspector Seymour Pine raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in Greenwich Village. This time the crowd outside fought back. People threw coins, bottles, bricks, garbage cans and burning trash. The police shut themselves inside the bar until the Tactical Patrol Force, a police unit trained to break up riots, arrived. The protests went on for six nights. Police arrested 13 people the first night. A National Park Service page counts fifty to sixty gay groups in the country before Stonewall and at least 1,500 a year later.

On 28 June 1970, the first anniversary of the raid, thousands of people marched from Greenwich Village to Central Park in the Christopher Street Liberation Day March. Craig Rodwell led the organizing.

Harvey Milk was one of the first openly gay people elected to public office in the United States, as a member of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors. On 27 November 1978, Dan White, a former supervisor, shot Mayor George Moscone and then shot Milk five times at City Hall. In May 1979 a jury found White guilty of voluntary manslaughter, a lesser crime than murder, and he was sentenced to seven years and eight months. He served about five. Crowds rioted at City Hall after the verdict.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native activists hold their ground, 1969 to 1974" -->
Native activists occupied land, and some broke fishing laws on purpose to be arrested. They held Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay from 20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971. In 1973 they held the village of Wounded Knee, on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota, the Oglala Lakota's land, from 27 February to 8 May.

In Washington State, Native fishermen fished where their treaties said they could, and got arrested for it on purpose. A treaty is a signed agreement, here between a Native nation and the United States. Billy Frank Jr. of the Nisqually Nation was arrested more than 50 times, starting at age fourteen, at Frank's Landing. On 12 February 1974, in *United States v. Washington*, Judge Boldt ruled that the tribes were entitled to half of the salmon that could be caught. The justices of the Supreme Court agreed with the ruling in 1979. President Barack Obama awarded Frank the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015, after his death.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Done to children without asking them, 1965 to 1973" -->
Between 1965 and 1973, surgeons sterilized two poor girls in Alabama after nurses from a clinic paid for with federal money told their mother they would get shots, and staff at a New York state school beat and sexually abused disabled children. In June 1973, in Montgomery, Alabama, nurses told Minnie Relf that her daughters would get birth control "shots." The nurses worked at the family planning clinic of Montgomery Community Action, which was paid for with federal money. Minnie Relf could not read or write, and she signed the form with an "X." Surgeons then sterilized the two girls, who were 12 and 14, by tubal ligation. In that operation a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so that the girls could never become pregnant. Their father, Lonnie Relf, saw the surgical scars when they came home from three days in the hospital. The records do not name the surgeons. Lawyers for the Southern Poverty Law Center sued for the family. The judge in the case found that an estimated 100,000 to 150,000 poor people were being sterilized each year under programs paid for with federal money. The judge barred federal money for sterilizations done against people's will.

Willowbrook State School, on Staten Island in New York City, was a place run by the state for children with intellectual disabilities. It was built for 4,000 people and held 6,000 by 1965. Senator Robert F. Kennedy toured it that year and said the residents were "living in filth and dirt, their clothing in rags." In 1972 the television reporter Geraldo Rivera showed crowded wards, filthy bathrooms, and staff beating and sexually abusing residents. On 17 March 1972, parents of 5,000 residents sued the State of New York. New York officials agreed to a court order in 1975, and the last children left Willowbrook on 17 September 1987.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Parents sue to get their children into school, 1971 to 1975" -->
Parents of disabled children sued in 1971 and 1972 to get their children into public schools, and they won. Pennsylvania's school law let a school board "refuse to accept or retain beginners who have not attained a mental age of five years." Attained means reached. A child's mental age was a test score given as the age of an ordinary child who would score the same. It also allowed the "temporary or permanent exclusion from the public school of children who are found to be uneducable and untrainable." Uneducable meant judged unable to be taught. A child who scored under 30 on an intelligence test was called uneducable, and officials handed the child to the state welfare department for placement and perhaps training, instead of school.

The parents' organization was the Pennsylvania Association for Retarded Children. "Retarded" was the word used then, in laws and in the case, for people with intellectual disabilities. On 7 January 1971 the association's lawyer, Thomas K. Gilhool of Philadelphia, sued the state for fourteen children. The association's leaders estimated that about 100,000 children in Pennsylvania were being denied a free education. By October 1971 the two sides had reached an agreement, and three federal judges issued orders on 8 October 1971 and 5 May 1972. Pennsylvania officials agreed to give every such child from 6 to 21 a free public education. Schools had to offer a hearing before labeling a child retarded, and had to test each labeled child again at least every two years. On 1 August 1972 a federal judge ruled the same way for seven children in Washington, D.C., in *Mills v. Board of Education*.

Parents were bringing cases like these in twenty states. The federal Bureau of Education for the Handicapped estimated that in 1975 about a million disabled children were still shut out of school completely. At least 300,000 more were getting schooling that did not meet their needs. On 29 November 1975 President Gerald Ford signed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act, which promised a free public education suited to each disabled child, in every state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Section 504, Gallaudet and the Americans with Disabilities Act, 1973 to 1990" -->
In 1977 disabled Americans occupied federal offices to force officials to enforce a law that had been passed four years before. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 was the first federal law protecting the civil rights of disabled people. It says that no qualified person with a disability "shall, solely by reason of his or her disability, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." That meant no one who ran a school, hospital or office paid with federal money could legally turn a person away only because of a disability.

Federal officials could not enforce the law until someone signed the rules for carrying it out. For four years no one did. Joseph A. Califano Jr., the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, refused to sign them after leaders of schools and hospitals objected to what the rules would cost.

On 5 April 1977 disabled people demonstrated at federal offices in eleven cities: San Francisco, Washington, Seattle, Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, Dallas, Atlanta, New York, Boston and Philadelphia. In San Francisco, about 100 of them occupied the department's offices at 50 UN Plaza and stayed. National Park Service pages give the length of the sit-in as 26 days in one account and 24 days in another. Members of the Black Panther Party, a Black political group, brought in one hot meal a day and food for two more. Leaders of a union, the International Association of Machinists, paid bills and paid to fly a group to Washington. The Brick Hut, a business owned and run together by a group of lesbian women, and Glide Memorial Church also helped. A lesbian is a woman who loves women. Judy Heumann, Kitty Cone and Dennis Billups led the occupation from inside. Califano signed the rules.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="judy-heumann" name="Judy Heumann" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Judy Heumann

> **Who:** A woman refused a place at school as a child and a teaching license as an adult, who sued, won, and then helped lead the 1977 sit-in that won the Section 504 rules.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1949 to 1970. San Francisco, April 1977.

As a child, Judy Heumann was turned away by New York City school officials, and as a teacher by the city's Board of Education, because she used a wheelchair. She sued for her license and won, and in 1977 she helped lead the San Francisco sit-in.

She was born in Philadelphia on 18 December 1947. At eighteen months old she got polio, which paralyzed her, and she used a wheelchair from early childhood. New York City school officials refused to take her. Her mother, Ilse, spent years pressing them. Judy got in at about fourth grade, into a separate program for disabled students called Health Conservation 21.

She finished college and applied for a license to teach. In 1970 New York Board of Education officials turned her down because she used a wheelchair. She asked the American Civil Liberties Union for help, and its lawyers refused, saying that turning someone down for a medical reason was not discrimination. That year the *New York Times* put her story on its front page. A lawyer, Roy Lucas, then offered to represent her. She sued the Board of Education at 22. The judge was Constance Baker Motley. Board officials settled before trial and gave her the license. She and her supporters then started a group called Disabled in Action.

In April 1977 she was one of about a hundred disabled people who occupied the federal offices at 50 UN Plaza in San Francisco to force federal officials to sign the Section 504 rules. She and Dennis Billups flew to Washington during the sit-in, with money from the machinists' union.

She spent the rest of her life on the same work. From 1993 to 2001 she was the federal official in charge of special education and rehabilitation. She advised the World Bank on disability from 2001 to 2005, and from 2010 to 2017 she was the federal government's special adviser on international disability rights. She died in Washington on 4 March 2023. She once said, "Some people say that what I did changed the world. But really, I simply refused to accept what I was told about who I could be. And I was willing to make a fuss about it."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaf President Now, 1988, and the Capitol Crawl" -->
Gallaudet University, in Washington, D.C., is the country's university for deaf students. In March 1988 members of its board of trustees, the group that runs the university, chose a hearing candidate, Elisabeth Zinser, as president over deaf candidates. Students marched, stopped going to classes and let the air out of the tires of school buses in front of the university's gates. They made four demands. Zinser had to go and a deaf president be chosen. The board's chair, Jane Spilman, had to resign. At least 51 of every 100 board members had to be deaf. No one who protested could be punished. After eight days the trustees gave in. I. King Jordan became Gallaudet's first deaf president. He said, "A Deaf person can do anything a hearing person can, except hear."

While members of Congress were considering the Americans with Disabilities Act, disabled protesters left their wheelchairs, walkers and crutches at the bottom of the steps of the Capitol and climbed the steps without them. The protest is called the Capitol Crawl. President George H. W. Bush signed the Americans with Disabilities Act on 26 July 1990. Under it, employers, state and city governments, businesses open to the public and public bus and train systems all had to give disabled people an equal chance at their jobs, services and goods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Busing, Detroit and Boston, 1974" -->
When Black parents in the North sued over segregated schools in the 1970s, they won in Boston and lost at the Supreme Court over Detroit's suburbs. On 25 July 1974, in *Milliken v. Bradley*, the justices ruled five to four against busing children across school district lines. A judge could not send city children to suburban schools, or suburban children to city schools, unless someone proved that one district had caused segregation in the other. Under the ruling, a judge's plan that would have covered Detroit and 53 suburban districts could not go ahead. Justice Thurgood Marshall, who had argued *Brown* before the Supreme Court as the NAACP's lawyer, was one of the four who dissented.

In Boston, Tallulah Morgan, a Black mother of three, and thirteen other families sued the Boston School Committee, the city's elected school board, in 1972. On 21 June 1974 Judge W. Arthur Garrity ruled that "the entire school system of Boston was unconstitutionally segregated," which means kept apart by race in a way the Constitution forbids. He found that school officials "had knowingly carried out a systematic program of segregation affecting all of the city's students, teachers and school facilities." Busing, carrying children by bus to schools outside their neighborhoods, began on 9 September 1974 and paired South Boston with Roxbury. Protesters threw rocks and bottles at the buses, broke the windows and shouted racial insults. Of the 800 Black students assigned to South Boston High that first day, 56 came. Garrity handed the job of watching over the schools to state officials in 1982 and issued his final order in 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Warren County residents lie down in the road, 1982" -->
In September 1982, residents of mostly Black Warren County, North Carolina, lay down in front of dump trucks to stop a landfill for poisoned soil. In 1978 someone illegally dumped chemicals called PCBs along North Carolina's roadsides, poisoning the soil. State officials chose Afton, in mostly Black, rural Warren County, as the place to bury it. After the courts failed them, residents and civil rights leaders, among them the Reverend Benjamin Chavis of the United Church of Christ's Commission for Racial Justice, blocked the trucks. Police arrested about 500 people over six weeks. State officials had the landfill built anyway. The protest is widely named as the start of the environmental justice movement, the campaign against placing pollution where poor and nonwhite people live. In 1987 members of the church's commission published a report, *Toxic Wastes and Race*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An apology and $20,000, 1980 to 1990" -->
Japanese Americans who had been held in the camps of the Second World War campaigned for an apology and payment, and in 1988 they won both. The Japanese American Citizens League and the National Council for Japanese American Redress led the campaign.

On 31 July 1980 President Jimmy Carter signed a law creating a commission to study the camps. In 1981 its members held 20 days of hearings in 11 places and heard more than 750 witnesses. Their report, in February 1983, found that the causes were "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership." In 1983 a federal judge also threw out the finding that Fred Korematsu had been guilty.

Senators Daniel Inouye and Spark Matsunaga and Representatives Norman Mineta and Robert Matsui led the bill through Congress. President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act on 10 August 1988. Under it, each person still living who had been held received an apology and $20,000. The first letters and checks went out on 1 October 1990.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="rights-movements" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Same-sex couples, two men or two women, won the right to marry in every state in 2015. Twelve years earlier, police in some states could still arrest two men or two women for having sex at home. In the same years, judges and officials took back rights that earlier movements had won. Members of Congress passed the first federal law against lynching in 2022, after about 200 earlier bills had failed. The justices of the Supreme Court cut back the Voting Rights Act of 1965 in 2013 and again in 2026. The justices overruled the 1973 ruling that protected abortion in 2022, which means they declared it wrong and ended it, and a year later they stopped colleges from considering an applicant's race. Their rulings of 2025 and 2026 allowed states to ban some medical care for transgender young people and to keep transgender girls off girls' school teams. A transgender person is someone whose own sense of being male or female, called gender identity, differs from the sex recorded at birth.

Organizers used new tools alongside the old ones of meetings, lawsuits and marches. In 2020 a seventeen-year-old recorded a Minneapolis police officer kneeling on George Floyd, and after people saw her video they protested in cities around the world. Two words posted on Twitter in October 2017, "me too," were used more than 19 million times within a year. Police officers and federal agents also killed people in these years, among them George Floyd in 2020, and Renée Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis in 2026. As of September 2026 no one had been charged with killing Good or Pretti, and the fights over voting maps, DACA and the Equal Rights Amendment were still going on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The justices strike down the Voting Rights Act's list, 2013" -->
On 25 June 2013 the justices of the Supreme Court struck down the list of places that had to get federal permission before changing a voting rule. That permission is called preclearance. Since 1965, officials in the listed states and counties had needed it from federal officials in Washington.

Nine states were on the list in full. Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Virginia were covered from 1 November 1964. Alaska, Arizona and Texas were covered from 1 November 1972. Named counties in California, Florida, New York, North Carolina and South Dakota were covered too, and so were two townships in Michigan.

The case was *Shelby County v. Holder*, and the justices split five to four. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority that "the Act imposes current burdens and must be justified by current needs." The majority struck down Section 4(b), the formula that decided which places went on the list. They left the preclearance rule itself standing, with no list of places it applied to. "We issue no holding on §5 itself, only on the coverage formula," he wrote, and "Congress may draft another formula based on current conditions." Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Stephen Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan.

A Justice Department web page states the result. The places that had been on the list "no longer need to seek preclearance for the new voting changes," unless a separate court order covers them.

Members of Congress tried to write a new list. The John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act was named for John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, one of the speakers at the 1963 March on Washington. It would have set new rules for which places needed preclearance. Under one version, a state with 15 or more voting rights violations in the previous 25 years would be covered for 10 years. On 19 January 2022 senators voted 49 to 51 on ending debate over the bill. Ending debate needed 60 votes, so the bill never came to a final vote. Later that night senators voted 52 to 48 to keep the 60-vote rule in place. By September 2026 members of Congress had passed no new list.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Proving intent: Louisiana v. Callais, 2026" -->
Since a ruling of 29 April 2026, anyone suing under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act must show that state officials meant to discriminate by race. Section 2 lets people go to court over a voting rule or a map of election districts. It covers a rule or map that gives a racial group less chance to elect the candidates it wants. In 1982 members of Congress had rewritten Section 2 so that a person suing would not have to prove what the officials intended.

The case was *Louisiana v. Callais*. After the 2020 census, Louisiana's lawmakers drew a map of six congressional districts with one majority-Black district. A federal judge held that the map likely broke Section 2. The lawmakers then drew a second majority-Black district. Other voters sued, saying the new map was a racial gerrymander, a set of districts drawn by race in a way the Constitution forbids.

Justice Samuel Alito wrote for six justices that the Voting Rights Act had not required the second district, so the map broke the Constitution. He set a new rule for every future case. Section 2 "imposes liability only when the evidence supports a strong inference that the State intentionally drew its districts to afford minority voters less opportunity because of their race." That means a person suing must now show that officials meant to treat voters unfairly because of race. The Congressional Research Service, which writes explanations of the law for members of Congress, put it this way on 14 May 2026. A person suing "now must show that a state drew a redistricting map based on racial, and not political, considerations and present evidence of current-day, intentional voting discrimination that is based on race."

Justice Elena Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Ketanji Brown Jackson. She described a round district that is 90 percent Black inside a state that is otherwise 90 percent white. Lawmakers slice the district "into six pie pieces" and spread its Black voters across six mostly white districts. "Election after election, Black citizens' votes are, by every practical measure, wasted," she wrote. She said "'cracked' is the usual term" for this. She wrote that the majority had made "a nullity of Section 2," meaning a rule with no force left in it.

Louisiana's Republican lawmakers passed a new map in May 2026. It removed one of the state's two majority-Black districts and was expected to elect five Republicans and one Democrat. About a third of Louisiana's people are Black. State Senator Jay Morris wrote the bill. State Representative Beau Beaullieu, a Republican, said, "We focused on the Democrat numbers, not the racial numbers when drawing." State Representative Kyle Green Jr., a Democrat, said the map took Black Louisianians "from 33% of the population to 16% of the representation." Louisiana officials put off the House primary elections set for 16 May 2026. To use the new map, they threw out about 40,000 votes that people had already cast.

On 2 June 2026 the justices let Alabama keep using a 2023 congressional map with one majority-Black district. They stopped a lower court's order against it, saying Alabama was "likely to succeed on the merits as to both claims," which means Alabama would probably win. In May 2026 the Congressional Research Service reported that "some state legislatures are considering or have already enacted modifications to their redistricting maps that eliminate majority-minority districts for the upcoming 2026 congressional elections." That means lawmakers in some states were drawing new maps without districts where most voters were Black or Latino. In September 2026 members of Congress had passed no new rule for Section 2.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Separate schools, counted in 2021 and 2022" -->
Since 1954 it has been against the law for school officials to send children to separate schools because of their race. In the 2020s, federal and university researchers still found many schools where three-quarters or more of the students were of one race.

The federal Government Accountability Office, which checks on government work for Congress, published a report on 16 June 2022 about the school year 2020-21. Its researchers found that about 18.5 million children, more than a third of American public school students, "attended schools where 75 percent or more students were of a single race or ethnicity." Ethnicity means a group's shared background, such as Hispanic. Fourteen percent of students attended schools where 90 percent or more were of one race or ethnicity. The report includes the school years from 2014-15 to 2020-21.

Researchers at the Civil Rights Project at UCLA, a university research center, count a longer stretch using the federal government's school data. In a report of April 2024, Gary Orfield and Ryan Pfleger gave the share of public schools that were 90 to 100 percent non-white. It was 7.4 percent in 1988 and 19.8 percent in 2021, or about one school in five. In those schools in 2021, 78 of every 100 students were poor.

The same report follows Black students in the South. The share who attended a school where most students were white reached a peak of about 43 percent in the mid-1980s. It was about 16 percent in 2021. The authors tie the turn to a 1991 Supreme Court ruling, *Board of Education of Oklahoma City v. Dowell*, which let judges end court orders to desegregate. After it, the share fell for nearly 30 years in a row.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Race in college admissions, 2014 to 2023" -->
In 2023 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that colleges may not give applicants a boost because of their race. In Justice Sonia Sotomayor's count, the justices had let colleges consider race "in a limited way" for 45 years before that, to gain the benefits of racial diversity. The practice is often called affirmative action.

Students for Fair Admissions, a nonprofit group founded in 2014, sued Harvard College and the University of North Carolina in November 2014. Its first board had three members, Edward Blum, Abigail Fisher and Richard Fisher. At Harvard, readers and committees considered race at several steps of the process. For a significant share of the Black and Hispanic students admitted, race tipped the decision. At the University of North Carolina, readers could give an applicant a "plus" because of race.

On 29 June 2023 Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for six justices that both programs broke the 14th Amendment's promise of equal protection, equal treatment under the law. "The guarantee of equal protection cannot mean one thing when applied to one individual and something else when applied to a person of another color," he wrote. A college may still read an applicant's own account of "how race affected the applicant's life," he added, if it is tied to the applicant's character or abilities. The justices left open the question for the military academies.

Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justice Elena Kagan, and by Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson in the North Carolina case. Jackson took no part in the Harvard case. "The Court long ago concluded that this guarantee can be enforced through race-conscious means in a society that is not, and has never been, colorblind," Sotomayor wrote. Colorblind, here, means taking no notice of race at all. "Today, this Court stands in the way and rolls back decades of precedent and momentous progress." Precedent means the Court's own earlier rulings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From arrest to marriage, 2003 to 2015" -->
In 2003 two men or two women in some states could still be arrested for having sex at home. Twelve years later the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that same-sex couples could marry in every state.

On 26 June 2003, in *Lawrence v. Texas*, the justices voted six to three to strike down a Texas law that made it a crime for two people of the same sex to have sex in private. Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote the opinion. The justices overruled a 1986 ruling that had allowed such a law: "*Bowers* was not correct when it was decided, is not correct today, and is hereby overruled."

On 18 November 2003 the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts, the state's highest court, ruled that the state constitution guaranteed same-sex couples the right to marry. The judges held back their own order for 180 days "to permit the Legislature to take such action" as it chose. The Legislature meant the state's lawmakers. On 17 May 2004 Massachusetts became the first state where same-sex couples could marry.

Edith Windsor and Thea Spyer married in Ontario, Canada, in 2007 and lived in New York. Spyer died in February 2009 and left everything she owned to Windsor. The federal Defense of Marriage Act defined marriage as one man and one woman for more than 1,000 federal laws and rules. So federal officials counted Windsor as unmarried, and she paid $363,053 in estate taxes, a tax on what a person leaves at death. She sued for the money back. On 26 June 2013, in *United States v. Windsor*, the justices struck down that part of the law, five to four.

By 2015 voters and lawmakers had changed their marriage laws to include same-sex couples in eleven states and the District of Columbia, by the justices' count. On 26 June 2015, in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, the justices ruled five to four that same-sex couples may marry in every state. Kennedy wrote the opinion. Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Antonin Scalia, Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito each wrote a dissent. Within moments of the ruling, LGBT people gathered in Christopher Park in New York. LGBT is short for lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender. On 24 June 2016 President Barack Obama made that park, a triangle of about 0.12 acres, the Stonewall National Monument, in memory of the 1969 uprising at the Stonewall Inn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jim-obergefell" name="Jim Obergefell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jim Obergefell

> **Who:** A widower who sued Ohio to have his name put on his husband's death certificate, and won the case that made same-sex marriage legal in every state.
> **When and where:** Ohio, and Baltimore, Maryland, 2011 to 2015.

Jim Obergefell sued the state of Ohio over a death certificate. He wanted to be named on it as the husband of John Arthur, and Ohio law would not allow it.

The justices set out his story in their written opinion. In it, "petitioner" and "plaintiff" both mean a person who brought the case. "Petitioner James Obergefell, a plaintiff in the Ohio case, met John Arthur over two decades ago. They fell in love and started a life together, establishing a lasting, committed relation. In 2011, however, Arthur was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS."

ALS destroys the nerve cells that carry orders from the brain to the muscles. The muscles stop getting those orders, waste away and stop working. It takes the hands and legs first, then swallowing, then breathing. The Court called the disease "progressive, with no known cure."

The two men decided to marry before Arthur died. Ohio did not allow same-sex couples to marry, and Maryland did. The Court went on. "It was difficult for Arthur to move, and so the couple were wed inside a medical transport plane as it remained on the tarmac in Baltimore. Three months later, Arthur died. Ohio law does not permit Obergefell to be listed as the surviving spouse on Arthur's death certificate." The wedding was on 11 July 2013, and Arthur died on 22 October 2013.

Obergefell sued. His case was joined with cases from Michigan, Kentucky and Tennessee. Together they were brought by 14 same-sex couples and two men whose partners had died. The justices decided them together on 26 June 2015. The last lines of Justice Kennedy's opinion read: "They ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jim-obergefell" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Respect for Marriage Act, 2022, and Kim Davis" -->
In 2022 members of Congress wrote the protection for same-sex marriages into federal law. The Respect for Marriage Act became law on 13 December 2022. It does not require any state to issue marriage licenses. It forbids any state official to refuse to recognize a marriage from another state because of the couple's sex, race, ethnicity or national origin. The Attorney General, or the person harmed, may sue anyone who breaks it.

Senators passed it on 29 November 2022 by 61 votes to 36. Twelve Republican senators voted yes. The House passed it on 8 December 2022 by 258 votes to 169, with 39 Republicans voting yes. The law opens with a sentence from Kennedy's *Obergefell* opinion: "No union is more profound than marriage, for it embodies the highest ideals of love, fidelity, devotion, sacrifice, and family."

Kim Davis was a county clerk in Kentucky. After the 2015 ruling she refused, on religious grounds, to issue a marriage license to a gay couple, David Ermold and David Moore. She spent six days in jail. A jury later ordered her to pay the couple $100,000 for the harm done to them and $260,000 for their lawyers' fees. In 2025 she asked the justices of the Supreme Court to overturn *Obergefell*. Her request was the first since 2015 to ask them to do so. On 10 November 2025 the justices refused to hear it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Lives Matter and Ferguson, 2012 to 2016" -->
Black Lives Matter began in 2013, after a Florida jury found the man who killed Trayvon Martin not guilty. On 26 February 2012, in Sanford, Florida, George Zimmerman, a neighborhood-watch volunteer, shot and killed Martin, a 17-year-old Black teenager who was walking back from a convenience store. Martin was unarmed. On 12 March 2012 the Sanford police chief said no charge could be filed. After demonstrations across the country, Governor Rick Scott appointed a special prosecutor, a lawyer chosen to handle this one case, in April 2012. The special prosecutor charged Zimmerman with second-degree murder, a murder that was not planned ahead. On 13 July 2013 the jury found him not guilty. In 2015 Justice Department officials announced that they would not bring federal hate-crime charges.

After the verdict, three Black organizers, Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors and Opal Tometi, started a project to build a movement and called it #BlackLivesMatter. The mark # turns a phrase into a hashtag, a label that people can search for on social media.

On 9 August 2014, at about noon, Officer Darren Wilson of the Ferguson Police Department in Missouri shot and killed Michael Brown, an unarmed 18-year-old. On 4 March 2015 Justice Department officials announced that the evidence did not support charging Wilson with a federal crime. Their report states that Wilson did not willfully, meaning knowingly and on purpose, violate Brown's right to be free from unreasonable force.

The same day, lawyers in the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division published a report on the whole Ferguson police department. Its investigators had interviewed half the department's officers and read more than 35,000 pages of police records. Together they spent about 100 days of work in the town. They found "a pattern or practice of unlawful conduct within the Ferguson Police Department that violates the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution." That means Ferguson's police broke the Constitution again and again, as a regular way of working.

The police department's own numbers for 2012 to 2014 showed who the police stopped. Black people were 67 percent of Ferguson's population. They were 85 percent of the drivers stopped, 90 percent of the people given tickets and 93 percent of the people arrested. Police searched Black drivers more than twice as often as white drivers, and found something illegal on them 26 percent less often. The investigators found that "nearly 90% of documented force used by FPD officers was used against African Americans." In every dog-bite case where the race was recorded, the person bitten was Black. The report states that "Ferguson's law enforcement practices are shaped by the City's focus on revenue rather than by public safety needs." In March 2010 the city's finance director wrote to the police chief that "unless ticket writing ramps up significantly before the end of the year, it will be hard to significantly raise collections next year."

The investigators also found that officers punished people for recording them and for protesting. One officer saw a couple filming him. He arrested the father, then pulled the mother over and arrested her too, saying "no more mercy, since she wanted to videotape," and "nobody videotapes me." He took the phone from their daughter. When the parents got out of jail on bond, money paid so a person can wait for trial at home, the video had been deleted.

In November 2014 a federal judge ordered Ferguson officers to stop interfering with people "lawfully and peacefully" recording the police. On 9 February 2015, six months after Michael Brown's death, people protested outside the police station. Officers pushed a man who was recording the arrests to the ground and handcuffed him. The report states that his video shows he "was neither interfering nor resisting." Officers also arrested a protester in a wheelchair who was streaming the protest live. The report names none of these people.

Some athletes joined the protests against police killings. In the 2016 preseason, Colin Kaepernick, the quarterback of the San Francisco 49ers football team, sat and then knelt during the national anthem "in protest of police brutality and racial inequality." He chose to leave his contract in March 2017, and no team signed him. In October 2017 he accused the owners of the National Football League of secretly agreeing to keep him out. He settled with the league on 15 February 2019, and the terms were kept secret.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Minneapolis, 25 May 2020" -->
On 25 May 2020 a Minneapolis police officer, Derek Chauvin, knelt on George Floyd for about ten minutes while Floyd lay handcuffed and face down in the street. Floyd died, and a jury later found Chauvin guilty of murdering him. Chauvin later admitted these facts in writing, in a plea agreement he signed in federal court on 15 December 2021. A plea agreement is a statement in which a person accused of a crime admits to it, under a deal with prosecutors.

Chauvin and his partner, Officer Tou Thao, answered a call about a counterfeit bill, a fake banknote. Officers J. Alexander Kueng and Thomas Lane were already there. They tried to put Floyd in a squad car. Then Chauvin, Kueng and Lane moved Floyd, who was handcuffed, out of the car and face down on the street. He "remained restrained, prone and handcuffed on the ground for approximately ten minutes." Prone means lying face down. Chauvin "held his left knee on Mr. Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder area and his right knee on Mr. Floyd's left arm and upper back" the whole time.

Floyd stopped resisting soon after the officers first held him down, and Chauvin went on kneeling on him. He admitted that he knew he had no legal reason to continue, "because he was aware that Mr. Floyd not only stopped resisting, but also stopped talking, stopped moving, stopped breathing, and lost consciousness and a pulse." By his own account he "heard Mr. Floyd repeatedly explain that he could not breathe, was in pain, and wanted help." Bystanders asked him again and again to check for a pulse and to get off Floyd. Kueng checked for a pulse at least twice and said twice that he could not find one. Chauvin admitted he did not see Thao or Kueng do or say anything to get him off Floyd. Lane twice asked whether Floyd should be rolled onto his side.

Darnella Frazier was seventeen. She was walking with her nine-year-old cousin, and she recorded what the officers did on her phone. In 2021 the Pulitzer Prize Board gave her a special citation. News reports of the announcement quote it: "for courageously recording the murder of George Floyd, a video that spurred protests against police brutality around the world, highlighting the crucial role of citizens in journalists' quest for truth and justice." She testified at Chauvin's state trial.

On 20 April 2021 a Hennepin County jury found Chauvin guilty of two kinds of murder, second-degree and third-degree, and of second-degree manslaughter. Manslaughter means killing a person without planning to. Judge Peter Cahill sentenced him to 22 and a half years. In federal court Judge Paul Magnuson sentenced him to 21 years, to be served at the same time. Thao, Kueng and Lane got federal sentences of between two and a half and three and a half years.

In the same plea agreement, Chauvin admitted to an attack four years earlier. On 4 September 2017 he answered a call from a mother who said her 14-year-old son had hit her. Chauvin held the boy by the throat and hit him in the head with a flashlight, cutting him near the left ear. Then he knelt on the boy's neck, shoulders and upper back "for between fifteen and sixteen minutes" while the boy lay face down, handcuffed and not resisting, and "at times, cried from pain." Chauvin left all of it out of his report that night. The plea agreement does not name the boy.

People protested in cities and towns across the country that summer. Researchers at the Pew Research Center, an organization that runs surveys, asked 9,654 American adults about the protests between 4 and 10 June 2020. Six of every 100 said they had gone to a rally or protest on these issues in the past month. Two-thirds said they supported the Black Lives Matter movement, and 38 of every 100 said they strongly supported it. The Crowd Counting Consortium, a project run by researchers at Harvard and the University of Connecticut, counts protests. Its data supported a *New York Times* analysis that the 2020 protests were the largest and broadest in American history up to then. The same data showed the protests were "overwhelmingly peaceful, especially relative to the heavy-handed police response," in the words of Harvard's Ash Center.

Members of the House of Representatives passed the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act on 3 March 2021, by 220 votes to 212. Senators never voted on it. Under President Joe Biden, Justice Department lawyers sued Minneapolis and Louisville to force changes in how their police worked, through consent decrees. A consent decree is an agreement that a judge enforces. On 21 May 2025, under President Donald Trump, Harmeet Dhillon, the head of the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division, dropped both lawsuits. She said she was ending "the Biden Civil Rights Division's failed experiment of handcuffing local leaders and police departments with factually unjustified consent decrees."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Emmett Till Antilynching Act, 2022" -->
On 29 March 2022 the Emmett Till Antilynching Act became law. It was the first federal law against lynching. Members of the House passed it on 28 February 2022, and senators passed it on 7 March 2022.

Members of Congress had introduced about 200 anti-lynching bills before it, and none had become law. The House Judiciary Committee's own 2022 report on the bill states two different starting dates. One section states the first bill came from Congressman George Henry White in 1900, when he was the only Black member of Congress. Another section states that the first came from Congressman Leonidas Dyer of Missouri in 1918. Both sections agree that the House passed three anti-lynching bills between 1920 and 1940, and that none of them became law.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of Roe v. Wade, 2022" -->
In 2022 the justices of the Supreme Court overruled *Roe v. Wade*, and with it ended the right to an abortion that the Court had protected since 1973. An abortion is a medical procedure that ends a pregnancy. In 1973, in *Roe v. Wade*, the justices had ruled that the Constitution protected a woman's right to one. Mississippi's lawmakers banned abortion after fifteen weeks of pregnancy, except in a medical emergency or when the fetus had a serious defect. Fetus is the medical word for the unborn offspring growing in the womb after the first weeks of pregnancy. On 24 June 2022, in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, Justice Samuel Alito wrote for five justices: "We therefore hold that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion. Roe and Casey must be overruled, and the authority to regulate abortion must be returned to the people and their elected representatives." *Casey* was a 1992 ruling, *Planned Parenthood v. Casey*, that had kept *Roe* in place. Chief Justice John Roberts agreed that the Mississippi law could stand but did not join in overruling *Roe*. Overruling a case means declaring it wrong and ending it. Justices Stephen Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan dissented together.

After *Dobbs*, lawmakers in each state set their own rules. KFF, a health research group, counted them on 10 August 2026. Thirteen states banned abortion in almost every case. Seven more banned it after six to twelve weeks of pregnancy. Nine states and the District of Columbia set no limit by stage of pregnancy.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Disability: narrowed by judges, restored by Congress, dropped in 2026" -->
After 1990, disabled Americans fought over who the Americans with Disabilities Act covered and whether they could live outside institutions. On a single day in 1999 the justices of the Supreme Court narrowed who counted as disabled and ruled that keeping disabled people in institutions without need was discrimination. Members of Congress restored the wider meaning of disability in 2008. In 2026 a federal judge struck down the federal rule that carried out the 1999 ruling on institutions.

The first fight was over who counted as disabled. Twin sisters applied to fly for United Air Lines. Without glasses their eyesight was 20/200 or worse, and with glasses it was 20/20 or better. Perfect ordinary eyesight is called 20/20, and a bigger second number means worse sight. United's hiring rule required 20/100 or better without glasses and turned the sisters down. On 22 June 1999, in *Sutton v. United Air Lines*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that whether a person is disabled must be judged with glasses or other aids in use. So the airline could refuse the sisters because of their eyes. They could not sue, because with their glasses on they did not count as disabled. A second ruling in 2002 narrowed the definition again. Members of Congress later wrote that lower courts had then "incorrectly found in individual cases that people with a range of substantially limiting impairments are not people with disabilities."

On 25 September 2008 President George W. Bush signed a law to reverse those rulings. Its title reads: "To restore the intent and protections of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990." In its text, members of Congress named the rulings and said they had "narrowed the broad scope of protection intended to be afforded by the ADA." Under the new law, an illness that comes and goes still counts as a disability. Under the law, whether a person is disabled is decided without counting the help of aids. The aids include medicine, artificial limbs, hearing aids, cochlear implants (devices placed in the ear that help deaf people hear), wheelchairs and other aids for getting around, or oxygen equipment. Ordinary glasses and contact lenses are the one kind of aid the law leaves off that list.

The second fight was over where disabled people live. Lois Curtis and Elaine Wilson were two Georgia women with mental illness and developmental disabilities. They agreed to go into the psychiatric unit, the ward for mental illness, of Georgia Regional Hospital, a state hospital. Mental health workers said each of them was ready to move to a program in the community, and yet each of them remained locked in the state hospital for several years more. They sued under the ADA. On 22 June 1999, in *Olmstead v. L.C.*, the justices ruled that keeping a disabled person in an institution when the person could live in the community is discrimination. They wrote that "confinement in an institution severely diminishes the everyday life activities of individuals, including family relations, social contacts, work options, economic independence, educational advancement, and cultural enrichment."

Justice Department lawyers went on enforcing that ruling. On 3 March 2022 they wrote to Governor Jared Polis that Colorado was breaking the ADA by keeping people with physical disabilities in nursing homes "unnecessarily." More than 15,000 Coloradans received care in the state's 200-plus nursing facilities in a year, and "some individuals have remained in nursing facilities for many years with no movement toward discharge."

In 2026 Justice Department lawyers changed sides. In September 2024 officials of seventeen states, Texas first among them, sued over the federal rules for Section 504. That 1973 law bars disability discrimination by programs paid with federal money. By 2026 five states were left in the case: Texas, Alaska, Louisiana, Montana and Florida. They asked the judge to strike the rule that disabled people get services in "the most integrated setting," meaning at home or in their community rather than in an institution. On 18 June 2026 the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel wrote that federal law "does not include an integration mandate." That means, in the lawyers' reading, the law does not require officials to offer disabled people services at home or in their community instead of in an institution. On 31 August 2026 Justice Department lawyers joined the five states in asking the judge to strike the rule. On 23 September 2026 the judge struck it for the whole country. The Supreme Court's 1999 ruling and the laws Congress passed still stand.

Census Bureau officials reported on 22 July 2026 that 45.8 million Americans, 13.7 percent, had a disability in 2024. That figure counts the "civilian noninstitutionalized population," everyone except people who live in institutions. The people the *Olmstead* fight is about are left out of it.

### Alice Wong

> **Who:** A disabled organizer who collected disabled people's own accounts of their lives, and who served on the federal council that advises the President on disability.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, California, 2002 to 2025.

Alice Wong founded the Disability Visibility Project in 2014 to collect disabled people's accounts of their own lives, in their own words. She used a wheelchair and breathed through a tracheostomy.

She was born in 1974. A tracheostomy is a hole cut through the front of the neck into the windpipe, with a short tube held in it. Her tube was connected to a ventilator, a machine that pushed air through the tube into her lungs so that she kept breathing. Neither the University of California, San Francisco, where she studied and worked, nor the National Council on Disability names the condition behind it.

In 2002, as a student at the university, she founded its Disability Interest Group. She earned a master's degree in medical sociology, the study of health and illness in society, there in 2004. She worked at three of its research centers: the National Center for Personal Assistance, the Disability Statistics Center and the Community Living Policy Center.

On 25 January 2013 President Barack Obama appointed her to the National Council on Disability, a federal body whose members advise the President and Congress. She served until 2015. On 20 July 2015 the White House held a reception in the Blue Room for the 25th anniversary of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Wong could not travel to it, so she took part through a robot, a screen on wheels that carried her face and voice into the room. The White House photographer Pete Souza took a picture of it. Its caption reads: "President Barack Obama greets Alice Wong, Disability Visibility Project Founder and Project Coordinator via robot."

She edited *Disability Visibility: First-Person Stories from the Twenty-First Century* (2020), a collection of essays by disabled writers, and wrote her own life story, *Year of the Tiger: An Activist's Life* (2022). "Disability is part of the greater continuum of diversity, and we need to allow for variation and not get so focused on what is the norm," she said. She died on 14 November 2025, aged 51.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Transgender Americans and the law, 2020 to 2026" -->
Between 2020 and 2026 transgender Americans won one Supreme Court case over jobs and lost cases over medical care and school sports. The justices also let a military ban and a passport rule take effect while the lawsuits over them went on.

On 15 June 2020, in *Bostock v. Clayton County*, the justices ruled six to three that "an employer who fires an individual merely for being gay or transgender violates Title VII." Title VII is the part of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that bans job discrimination because of sex. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion. Three fired workers had brought the cases. Gerald Bostock had worked for Clayton County, Georgia, for ten years and was fired soon after he joined a gay softball league. Donald Zarda, a skydiving instructor in New York, was fired days after he told his employer he was gay. Aimee Stephens worked at a funeral home in Michigan. She had been hired presenting as a man, and six years later she told her employer she meant to "live and work full-time as a woman." Her employer fired her, saying the arrangement "is not going to work out."

Tennessee's lawmakers passed a law that stops doctors from giving puberty blockers or hormones to anyone under 18 for two purposes. One is to help the young person live as a gender other than their sex at birth. The other is to treat distress about the mismatch between the two. A puberty blocker is a drug that pauses the hormones that start puberty, so the body's changes do not begin while the drug is taken. Hormone treatment gives a person the hormone of the sex they identify as, which changes the body in that direction. Doctors in Tennessee may still give the same drugs to young people for other reasons, such as puberty that starts very early. On 18 June 2025, in *United States v. Skrmetti*, the justices ruled that the law could stand. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the Court's opinion. Justice Sonia Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson and in part by Justice Elena Kagan, and Kagan also wrote her own dissent.

Idaho's lawmakers passed the Fairness in Women's Sports Act in 2020, and West Virginia's passed the Save Women's Sports Act in 2021. Both laws say that girls' and women's teams are for students the laws define as female by biology. On 30 June 2026 Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote for six justices that Title IX "allows schools to provide separate women's and men's sports teams defined by biological sex." He wrote that the two laws did not break the promise of equal protection. The majority gave "the inherent physical differences between the sexes" as a reason, along with the safety and fairness of the competition. It also wrote that "27 States and various sports-governing bodies have all drawn the same line."

The West Virginia student is named in the record only as B.P.J., and the case was brought by her mother, Heather Jackson. Justice Sotomayor described her in dissent: "Respondent B. P. J. is a transgender girl who wants to live her life consistent with her gender identity. When B. P. J. was 11 years old, she sought the opportunity to do what she and so many other children love to do: play sports." She wanted to run on the cross-country and track teams. Sotomayor wrote that "B. P. J. is the only transgender girl publicly identified in the State who has sought to play sports with other girls" in the five years the ban had been in place. She agreed that the Title IX claim failed. On the equal protection question, she wrote that the trial court should have been allowed to find the facts first.

President Donald Trump acted on his first day back in office. On 20 January 2025 he signed an executive order titled "Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government." On 27 January he signed an order on the military, and on 5 February 2025 he signed one titled "Keeping Men Out of Women's Sports." In February 2025 National Park Service officials took the words "transgender" and "queer" off the Stonewall National Monument's website. On 26 February 2025 Defense Department officials issued a military ban. It disqualifies "anyone who either has gender dysphoria or has undergone medical interventions to treat gender dysphoria." Gender dysphoria is the medical term for the distress a person feels when the sex recorded at their birth does not match their own sense of their gender. Commander Emily Shilling, a Navy pilot for nearly twenty years, led a lawsuit by transgender service members, and District Judge Benjamin Settle blocked the ban. On 6 May 2025 the justices let the ban take effect while the case went on. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson would have kept it blocked.

On 6 November 2025 the justices let the government's new passport rule take effect. Under it, every new passport shows the holder's sex at birth. "Displaying passport holders' sex at birth no more offends equal protection principles than displaying their country of birth," the order reads. Justice Jackson dissented, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan.

On 31 March 2026, in *Chiles v. Salazar*, the justices ruled for Kaley Chiles, a licensed counselor in Colorado who uses only talk therapy. A 2019 Colorado law barred licensed counselors from "conversion therapy" with young people, meaning any practice that tries to change a person's sexual orientation or gender identity. Justice Gorsuch wrote for eight justices that the law, as used against her talk therapy, regulated her speech based on her point of view. The case went back to the lower courts. Justice Jackson dissented alone.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standing Rock: the right to protest, 2016 to 2026" -->
In 2016 members of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe and their supporters protested the Dakota Access oil pipeline. Police officers answered with tear gas, rubber bullets and a fire hose. The route chosen by Energy Transfer, the company building the pipeline, ran under Lake Oahe, less than a mile north of the Standing Rock Reservation. In treaties signed in the 1800s, officials of the United States agreed that the land the route crosses belonged to the Sioux Nation, and the Sioux never gave that land up.

On 19 August 2016 North Dakota's governor, Jack Dalrymple, declared an emergency. About 1,500 people were at the protest then, and 28 had been arrested. He organized a joint police command. Three police forces took part: the Morton County Sheriff's Office under Sheriff Kyle Kirchmeier, the Stutsman County Sheriff's Office under Sheriff Chad Kaiser, and the Mandan police under Chief Jason Ziegler. Each chief left decisions about using force to his individual officers.

On the night of 20 November 2016, officers had blocked the Highway 1806 bridge with two dump trucks chained to concrete barriers and barbed wire. Protesters used a semi-trailer truck to drag one dump truck away, and the crowd grew to several hundred. The officers fired tear gas canisters, rubber bullets and bags filled with lead into the crowd. They sprayed protesters with a fire hose from a Mandan fire truck. A lawsuit for the protesters says more than 200 people were hurt.

Sophia Wilansky was hit that night. In 2026 the judges of a federal appeals court set out her account. They accepted it as true for the appeal. Officers Dvorak, Arndt and Hanson fired rounds at her as she ducked behind a metal sheet near the remaining truck, and one hit her arm. She ran. About 30 yards away, as she bent to pick up a piece of plywood, Officer Jonathan Moll fired a round called an aerial warning munition. "It tore the flesh off her arm, and she fell to the ground," the judges wrote. A footnote in their ruling describes that round as "a flashbang embedded in a less-lethal bullet," a bullet made to hurt rather than kill. A flash-bang is an explosive device that makes a loud noise and a bright flash of light, and aerial ones are made to be fired above a crowd. Protesters carried her away. Her account says officers laughed and congratulated Moll, and the officers dispute that. "Her left hand and forearm are permanently and severely injured," the judges wrote.

No officer was held responsible. In 2023, and again on 24 June 2026 in Wilansky's case, judges of the federal appeals court ruled against the injured protesters. They said that in 2016 the law had not clearly established that firing at a crowd to drive it off was a "seizure" under the Constitution. A seizure means a way of stopping or holding a person by force. So the officers were protected by qualified immunity, a rule that shields government officials from being sued unless the right they broke was already clearly established. Police made hundreds of arrests at the protests. A Morton County judge, Allan Schmalenberger, threw out many trespass cases, charges of going onto private land without permission, because no one had told the protesters to leave.

Federal officials changed their decision more than once. On 4 December 2016 the Army's Assistant Secretary for Civil Works refused permission to cross under the lake. On 24 January 2017 President Donald Trump ordered the Army to approve the pipeline "in an expedited manner," which means quickly. Army officials gave permission on 8 February 2017, and oil has flowed since June 2017. In 2020 a federal judge, James Boasberg, ruled that Army officials had broken the law. They had approved the crossing without a full study of its effects. The pipeline kept running for about five years while the Army Corps of Engineers did the study. Corps officials finished it in December 2025, and in May 2026 Brigadier General William C. Hannan signed a decision to grant permission again. In June 2026 tribal leaders asked the judges of a federal appeals court to drop the tribe's own appeal, and the judges did. Standing Rock Chairman Steve Sitting Bear said in December 2025: "We will continue to fight for our homelands and the promises the United States made to our Tribe in Treaty."

Energy Transfer's lawyers sued the environmental group Greenpeace over the protests. On 19 March 2025 a jury in Morton County ordered Greenpeace to pay about $667 million. Judge James Gion cut the amount to $345 million. Greenpeace officials said the group had six employees at the camps and called the lawsuit an attempt to punish the group for supporting the protests. In September 2026 Nola Taken Alive, a member of the Standing Rock tribal council, said, "The oil is still flowing. The pipeline hasn't been shut down."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The DREAM Act and DACA, 2001 to 2026, and two killings in Minneapolis" -->
Young people who had been brought into the United States as children, without legal permission to live there, organized from 2010. They wanted a way to become citizens, and members of Congress never passed one. In 2001 Senator Richard Durbin of Illinois and Representative Howard Berman of California first introduced the DREAM Act. It would have let these young people become citizens after two years of college or military service.

On 1 January 2010 four students from Miami Dade College, Gaby Pacheco, Carlos Roa, Felipe Matos and Juan Rodriguez, set out to walk 1,500 miles from Miami to Washington. Three of them were in the country without legal permission. They said so in public all along the way, which put them at risk of arrest and deportation, being sent out of the country. They gathered 30,000 signatures. On 28 April 2010 a Secret Service agent refused to take their petition, and White House officials refused them a meeting with President Barack Obama. They walked the last four miles to the White House on 1 May 2010. On 18 December 2010 the DREAM Act came up in the Senate. Fifty-five senators voted to end debate and 41 voted against. Sixty votes were needed, so the bill failed.

On 15 June 2012 Janet Napolitano, the Secretary of Homeland Security, announced a program called Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, or DACA. It made no one a citizen. People who had arrived as children could apply if they met rules about age, schooling and criminal records. Those accepted could ask the government to put off deporting them for two years at a time, and could get permission to work. About 700,000 people signed up, by the Supreme Court's count. In September 2017 Attorney General Jeff Sessions wrote that the program should end, and the acting Secretary of Homeland Security, Elaine Duke, ended it. DACA holders, universities and states sued. On 18 June 2020 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled five to four that federal officials had ended the program unlawfully, so DACA stayed.

Officials of Texas and other states also sued. In January 2025 a federal appeals court ruled that DACA's work permits were unlawful in Texas. By July 2026 the judge in Texas, Andrew Hanen, had not issued the order that would carry that out. About 455,000 people held DACA on 31 March 2026, by government figures reported by the National Immigration Forum, an immigration advocacy group. Federal immigration officials were renewing permits and not approving new ones.

In January 2026 federal immigration agents shot and killed two American citizens in Minneapolis, Renée Macklin Good on 7 January and Alex Pretti on 24 January. The agents were there for a large federal immigration operation called Operation Metro Surge. The *Star Tribune* named ICE officer Jonathan Ross as the man who shot Good. ProPublica named Border Patrol agent Jesus Ochoa and Customs and Border Protection officer Raymundo Gutierrez as the agents who shot Pretti. Federal officials said Good had used her car as a weapon, and local officials said video contradicted that. Federal officials called Pretti a domestic terrorist, and NPR reported that video contradicted their account of his death.

At the Pretti shooting, federal officials physically blocked state investigators from the scene. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty and Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison sued for the evidence in March 2026 and received it in June and July. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche said there would be no civil rights investigation into Good's killing. As of September 2026 no one had been charged in either death.
