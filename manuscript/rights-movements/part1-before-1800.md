## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 25 of 37 · Rights and Movements · 10 eras · about 275 minutes**

**Most of the rights Americans have today were won by people who were first refused them, who organized and kept asking for years, often at the cost of their jobs, their freedom or their lives, and some of those rights were later taken away again.**

Four men at Germantown, in Pennsylvania, signed a paper against owning enslaved people on April 18, 1688, and sent it to their Quaker church. The members of four church meetings read it, one after another, and passed it on without doing anything. Then the Quakers forgot it. People working to end slavery found it again in 1844, and it went missing a second time. In March 2005 it turned up in a vault in a Philadelphia meetinghouse. Haverford College, in Pennsylvania, keeps it now.

This chapter follows people who were told no: women, Black Americans, Native nations, Chinese and Mexican Americans, Japanese Americans, gay and transgender Americans, and disabled Americans. It goes era by era, from the clan mothers of the Haudenosaunee to the courtrooms of 2026. Watch how the methods grow. The first tools are a petition and a printed booklet. Later come the planned arrest, the boycott, the march, the sit-in, and the lawsuit brought on purpose to test a law. Watch, too, what each step costs, and how often a right that was won had to be won a second time.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did people without power get the people in power to listen?
- What did organizing cost the people who did it?
- Can a right that has been won be taken away again?

**Five minutes?** Read "Sit-ins and Freedom Rides, 1960 and 1961" in 1950 to 2000.
**Can skip:** "Two men climb the Gateway Arch, 1964", "Suffragists remember the clan mothers, 1891 and 1893", "Equal pay for the women's soccer team, 2016 to 2022". The chapter still makes sense without them.

**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 3 minutes**

No record shows a rights movement in this land before 1500, but in at least one Native nation women held real power in government. A rights movement is a group of people who organize together to change a rule that denies them something.

About six hundred Native nations lived north of the Rio Grande, the river along part of today's border with Mexico, in 1492. Each one governed itself in its own way. The clearest record of women's power comes from the Haudenosaunee, a union of five nations in what is now New York, where women chose the chiefs and could remove them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The clan mothers of the Haudenosaunee" -->
The Haudenosaunee Confederacy has five founding nations: the Seneca, the Cayuga, the Onondaga, the Oneida and the Mohawk. The Onondaga Nation's own account places the founding on the shores of Onondaga Lake, in what is now central New York. The Onondaga still describe how their women choose leaders.

Each clan, a group of related families, has a clan mother. She chooses the man who speaks for her clan in council, the meeting where the chiefs talk and decide together. His title is Hoyane, which means chief. In the Onondaga account, a figure called the Peacemaker placed deer antlers on the headdress of every Hoyane to show that the man was a chief.

**Taking the antlers.** The clan mother can also start the removal of a chief. If he stops speaking for his clan, she warns him that he is not following the ways of the Great Peace, the laws the five nations agreed to live by when they joined together. If he still does not change, the clan removes him in council and takes his antlers off. The Onondaga say he has been "de-horned."

Haudenosaunee women held other powers too. An article by the National Park Service, the federal office that runs the national parks and historic sites, states that the women grew the food and shared it out, and through that work "controlled the economy" of their nations. They also held "the final authority over land transfers and decisions about engaging in war." They had the last word on giving land to anyone else and on going to war.

When a husband and wife separated, the wife took with her all the property she had brought into the home. The children went with her too.

**When the union began.** No one can prove when the five nations agreed to the Great Law of Peace. The Haudenosaunee keep their own account in spoken history and in wampum, belts and strings of shell beads that record agreements. By that account the date falls between about the year 1000 and 1400. Scholars working from written records mostly date it around 1450, the encyclopedia Britannica reports, and some date it to the late 1100s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the records can and cannot show" -->
Almost everything written down about how these nations governed themselves was written after Europeans arrived, by Europeans. The nations kept their own records in spoken accounts and in wampum, cut from the white and purple parts of clam shells and woven into strings and belts.

The Onondaga Nation describes how a belt works. A speaker puts the words of an agreement into the wampum while the belt is woven. Every speaker after that uses the belt to remember the agreement and what has happened since. The Onondaga have kept the Confederacy's wampum since its founding.

**Not known:** No record from before 1500 in this land names a single person who claimed a right that others denied.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 5 minutes**

Under Spanish law in the 1500s, settlers forced Native people to work for them. Nobody here organized to change that law. The loudest objection came from a Spanish priest, Bartolomé de Las Casas, who argued in Spain that Native people had rights.

People also fought back. Enslaved Africans at San Miguel de Gualdape, a Spanish settlement on the coast of what is now Georgia or the Carolinas, rose up and escaped in October 1526. It is the first rebellion of enslaved Africans on record in what became the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was the encomienda?" -->
The encomienda was a grant from the Spanish king that gave a settler the labor of a named group of Native people. It was one of the Spanish systems for forcing Native people to work.

On paper, the settler had to protect the people and teach them Christianity. In practice, he forced them to work for him and took the food and goods they produced. The historians who wrote the open textbook *The American Yawp* call it "an exploitive feudal arrangement in which Spain tied Indigenous laborers to vast estates." It used Native workers for the settlers' gain and bound them to huge farms, the way farm workers in old Europe were bound to a lord's land.

The same historians write that the settlers who held these grants "brutalized their laborers," beating and hurting the people who worked for them. Las Casas reached the Caribbean in 1502. He later wrote that he saw Spaniards "cut off the Hands, Noses, and Ears, both of Indians and Indianesses," meaning Native men and women. They did it, he wrote, "for no other reason, but only to gratify their bloody mindedness," for the pleasure of hurting people.

**Hispaniola.** On the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, Spaniards enslaved the Taíno, the Native people of the islands, and made them dig for gold. When the gold ran out, the Spaniards forced them to work on the encomienda farms. The Native population of Hispaniola fell from 60,000 in 1508 to fewer than 20,000 ten years later. Fewer than one person in three was left.

The authors of *The American Yawp* write of the Spaniards' "casual violence and dehumanizing exploitation," treating people as if they were not human. Within a few generations, the same authors write, the whole island had been emptied of its Native people.

King Charles I of Spain ended the encomienda in 1542 with a set of rules called the New Laws. Spanish officials replaced it with another forced-labor system, the repartimiento, and Spanish settlers went on forcing Native people to work for them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bartolomé de Las Casas" -->
Las Casas watched Spanish soldiers kill a whole town in Cuba, and he spent the rest of his life arguing for Native people. At first he also asked for enslaved Africans to take their place.

He went to Cuba in 1513 as a chaplain, a priest who travels with soldiers, with the Spanish forces who were taking the island from the Taíno. At the town of Caonao, the Taíno had made cassava bread and fish for the soldiers under Captain Narváez. About two thousand people sat in the town square, and more than five hundred were in a large house nearby.

In his *History of the Indies*, Las Casas wrote what happened next. One Spaniard drew his sword, and then the whole hundred soldiers drew theirs. They began "to rip open the bellies, to cut and kill" the men, women, children and old people, who were all sitting down. They went into the large house and killed the people there too. Some escaped by climbing to the top of the house. Las Casas had stepped away to another part of the town shortly before.

**Giving up his grant.** For serving with the soldiers, Las Casas received land and an encomienda. In 1514 he told the people of his church that the whole encomienda system was wrong. He freed the Native people he held and began arguing for them to the Spanish officials on the island.

**The request he later regretted.** Two years later, in 1516, Las Casas asked the Spanish king to bring enslaved Africans to the Indies, Spain's islands and lands across the Atlantic, to take the place of Native workers. He believed Africans were better suited to hard physical labor. Slave traders had already been carrying enslaved Africans across the Atlantic for about sixteen years.

King Charles I of Spain, later Emperor Charles V, gave a man named Lorenzo de Gorrevod a charter, a written permission, to ship enslaved Africans across the Atlantic. The date was August 18, 1518.

Las Casas also wrote a short book about what Spanish settlers did to Native people, *A Brief Account of the Devastation of the Indies*. Its Spanish title is *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias*. Soon after he wrote it, King Charles issued the New Laws of 1542.

**The debate at Valladolid.** Charles V called Las Casas and a scholar named Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda to the town of Valladolid, in Spain, in 1550. The question was whether the Spanish had the right to make war on Native people and rule them by force. Sepúlveda argued that they did. Las Casas argued that they did not.

The two men spoke in front of a panel of churchmen and royal officials, among them the church scholars Domingo Soto and Melchor Cano. On the first day Sepúlveda spoke for three hours. The panel held its final meeting in 1551. Andrea Daher, a scholar writing for an encyclopedia of Sorbonne University in Paris, records no ruling by the panel.

Later in life, Las Casas changed his mind about enslaving Africans. In his *History of the Indies* he wrote at length against the African slave trade and the slave markets of Spain and Portugal. He did not publish that book while he was alive, so his change of mind was not public while the trade grew.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 10 minutes**

In the English colonies of the 1600s, the men in charge punished people who spoke against them. The records name the people they punished. English colonists also brought English law with them, and under one of its rules a married woman did not count in law as a person separate from her husband. Nobody organized against that rule in this century.

The colony's leaders banished Anne Hutchinson from Massachusetts in November 1637 for what she taught in her own house. Massachusetts officials hanged Mary Dyer on Boston Common in 1660 for coming back to the colony as a Quaker, a member of a new religion that the colony's leaders had outlawed. In 1688 four men in Pennsylvania signed a protest against slavery and sent it to their church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coverture: what marriage did to a woman in law" -->
The rule was called coverture. In law, a married woman was "covered" by her husband. The legal name for her was *feme covert*, a covered woman, and she had no legal standing of her own.

She did not control property, even property she had brought into the marriage. Her husband had full rights to any money or wages she earned. She could not sign a contract, a written deal that a court can make people keep. Nobody could take her to court, and she could not bring a case to court in her own name.

An adult woman who had never married, or whose husband had died, was a *feme sole*, a woman on her own. She could buy and sell property, sign contracts and go to court in her own name. Under English law, a woman lost those rights on the day she married.

**Daily life.** Wives ran businesses beside their husbands, and for husbands who were away. Some married women traded in their own names under a legal exception, and the people of their town held everyone to the deal. Under the law, whatever a wife held, she held because her husband allowed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-rights-movements" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** A Boston woman whom the colony's leaders tried and banished for what she taught at religious meetings in her own house.
> **When and where:** Boston and Newtown, Massachusetts Bay Colony. Meetings from 1636, trial in November 1637, put out of her church in March 1638.

**"You have power over my body," she told the men judging her.**

Anne Hutchinson came to Boston, an English town built on Massachusett land, with her husband, William, and their children in 1634. From 1636 she held a weekly meeting in her house to go over the Sunday sermons. At first only women came. Then men came too, including some of the most important men in the colony.

Like the colony's leaders, she was a Puritan, one of the English Protestants who had come to Massachusetts to worship God in their own way. Puritans used the word grace for God's gift of being saved, of going to heaven after death. Hutchinson taught that God saved people by grace alone, and not because they behaved well. She also said she could tell which ministers and which neighbors had received it.

**The trial.** In November 1637 the colony's lawmakers, called the General Court, tried her for heresy, teaching against the church's beliefs. They sat as judges at Newtown, the town now called Cambridge, and the trial lasted two days. Governor John Winthrop told her she was "called here as one of those that have troubled the peace of the commonwealth," meaning the colony. He called her meetings "a thing not tolerable nor comely in the sight of God nor fitting for your sex."

She answered his Bible verses with Bible verses of her own. At the end she said God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation," and that God would curse the Puritans and their families for generations if they harmed her. The judges called that blasphemy, speaking against God. She told them, "You have power over my body, but the Lord Jesus hath power over my body and soul."

The trial record states the sentence: "Mrs. Hutchinson, the sentence of the court you hear is that you are banished from out of our jurisdiction as being a woman not fit for our society." A jurisdiction is the area a government rules, and banished meant forced out of it and forbidden to return. She asked, "I desire to know wherefore," or why, "I am banished?"

**The second trial.** Colony officials held her through the winter. In March 1638 the leaders of the Boston church tried her again and put her out of the church. Their charge said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer." It went on, "and a Magistrate than a Subject." A magistrate was an official with the power to judge cases.

That spring the family moved to Aquidneck Island, in what became Rhode Island, on land the minister Roger Williams helped them buy from the Narragansett. One of the first written rules of their new town, Portsmouth, said no person should be punished or "called into question on matter of religion ... so long as he keeps the peace."

**Her death.** After her husband died, she moved in 1642 to Dutch-held land on Long Island Sound, the country of the Siwanoy, a Native people. The Dutch governor, Willem Kieft, had started a war with the Native people there.

Armed Dutch settlers at Corlaer's Hook, on Manhattan, and Dutch soldiers at Pavonia, across the Hudson River, murdered at least eighty Munsee people, and perhaps as many as 120, on the night of February 25, 1643. Kieft and his council had approved both attacks. In August or September 1643, Siwanoy warriors killed Anne Hutchinson, her servants and all but one of the children who were with her. One daughter survived.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-rights-movements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts leaders outlaw Quakers, then hang four of them" -->
Massachusetts officials hanged a woman named Mary Dyer in 1660 because she kept coming back to the colony as a Quaker. The Quakers followed George Fox, an Englishman who taught that men and women were equal in God's eyes. Quakers refused to take their hats off to people of higher rank, because they believed all people were equal. They also refused to swear oaths, solemn promises made in God's name, to any government. Massachusetts Governor John Endecott called them a "pernicious sect," a harmful religious group.

**The laws.** The men of the General Court passed a series of laws against Quakers. In late 1656 and 1657 they outlawed "the cursed sect of heretics commonly called Quakers." They fined ship captains who brought Quakers to the colony and people who owned Quaker books.

In October 1657 they set punishments for Quakers who came back after being sent away. A man who came back once was to have one ear cut off with a blade, and a man who came back a second time was to lose the other ear. For a third return, the law stated, "they shall have their tongues bored through with a hot iron," a hole burned through the tongue with a heated iron rod. For women who came back, the law set whipping on the bare back.

**Three men's ears.** The colony's leaders used that law in 1658 against a Quaker named Christopher Holder and two other Quaker men. One of the two was John Rous. Governor Endecott sent Rous to prison, and on September 7, 1658, Rous was sentenced to have his right ear cut off. A British reference book, the *Dictionary of National Biography*, states that the cutting was done inside the prison, although the law called for it to be done in public.

A chronology by the law professor Douglas Linder gives a different date for the cutting of the three men's ears, June 3, 1658. Linder adds that the men were also whipped, and then beaten twice a week for nine weeks. Neither account names the judges or the men who did the cutting, the whipping and the beating.

On October 19, 1658, the men of the General Court voted 12 to 11 to banish Quakers "upon pain of death." Under that law, a Quaker who came back after being sent away could be hanged.

**Mary Dyer.** Dyer had lived in Boston in the 1630s and was a close friend of Anne Hutchinson. When church leaders put Hutchinson out in March 1638, Dyer walked out of the church beside her. Around 1650 she met George Fox in England, and she came back to Boston as a Quaker in 1657. Officials jailed her as soon as she landed. She spent two and a half months in a dark cell with its windows boarded up.

Her husband got her out by promising she would not preach, and the family moved to Rhode Island. She kept going back to Massachusetts to stand by Quakers who were in prison there.

Governor Endecott sentenced Mary Dyer and two Quaker men to death on October 20, 1659. The men were William Robinson, a merchant from London, and Marmaduke Stephenson, a farm worker from Yorkshire. Dyer's son, William Dyer, asked the governor to spare her.

On October 26 Endecott signed an order giving her 48 hours to leave the colony. First, the order said, she must be taken to the gallows, the wooden frame used for hangings, and stand there with a rope around her neck while the two men were hanged. Officials hanged Robinson and Stephenson in front of her on October 27, 1659. Then they took her back to prison and sent her out of the colony.

She came back to Massachusetts in 1660. Her husband asked for her life, and she refused to say she was sorry. On June 1 officials hanged Mary Dyer near the Frog Pond on Boston Common, on the sentence of Governor Endecott and the General Court. About 200 soldiers stood around the gallows with guns, swords, long spears called pikes, and flags.

**The count.** Officials hanged a fourth Quaker in Boston in 1661, William Leddra, a cloth maker and Quaker minister from Cornwall, in England. An English Quaker named Edward Burrough counted what had been done to Quakers in Massachusetts between 1656 and 1660. His count lists 64 jailings and 25 banishments. King Charles II of England ordered the punishments stopped on September 9, 1661, and said accused Quakers must be sent to England instead. By 1677, Quakers could meet openly in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four men sign a protest against slavery, 1688" -->
Four men at Germantown, in Pennsylvania, signed a paper against owning enslaved people on April 18, 1688. Their names were Garret Hendericks, Francis Daniel Pastorius, Derick op de Graeff and Abraham op de Graeff. They sent it up through the meetings of their Quaker church. A National Park Service page calls it the first protest against African slavery made by a religious body in the English colonies.

Germantown stood on the land of the Lenape, the Native people of the Philadelphia region. Pastorius had bought the land in 1683 from William Penn, a Quaker to whom King Charles II had given a charter for Pennsylvania in 1681. The surviving records of that sale do not name the Lenape families who lived on it.

The protest rested on the Golden Rule, the teaching that people should treat others as they want to be treated. In the paper's own spelling, the rule was "that we shall doe to all men licke as we will be done ourselves."

**Up through the meetings.** A Quaker meeting is a group of Quakers who worship and make decisions together. A monthly meeting is a local group, a quarterly meeting joins several local groups, and a yearly meeting joins the Quakers of a whole region. The members of four meetings, one after another, read the paper and passed it on without acting. The first three were Germantown Monthly Meeting, Dublin Monthly Meeting and Philadelphia Quarterly Meeting.

The last was Burlington Yearly Meeting, in New Jersey, and its members voted to take no stand either way. Nothing changed because of the protest.

**Lost and found.** The Quakers put the paper aside and forgot it. Abolitionists, people working to end slavery, found it again in 1844. Then it went missing a second time. In March 2005 it turned up in a vault at the Arch Street Meetinghouse in Philadelphia. It is now kept at Haverford College, in Pennsylvania.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 5 minutes**

Single men, each writing on his own, printed the arguments against slavery in these fifty years. No organized group yet worked for anyone's rights, and the Quakers began to argue about slavery among themselves. Quaker leaders punished the members who raised the question first. By 1776 the Quakers of Philadelphia had changed their own rules anyway and banned slaveholding among their members.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Boston merchant writes a pamphlet against slavery, 1700" -->
Samuel Sewall, a Boston merchant and magistrate, wrote a three-page pamphlet called *The Selling of Joseph* in 1700. A pamphlet is a short printed booklet. A Massachusetts Historical Society page calls it the first antislavery pamphlet published in America. Bartholomew Green and John Allen printed it in Boston.

Sewall argued that taking people and selling them is the crime the Bible calls man stealing, and he pointed to the verse Exodus 21:16. Then he went through the usual arguments in favor of slavery and answered each one.

**Adam's case.** Sewall wrote it because of a case in Boston. That year a petition, a written request signed by many people, went around Boston asking for the freedom of an enslaved man named Adam and his wife. The petition said they were "unjustly held in Bondage" by a merchant named John Saffin. Saffin had promised Adam his freedom after seven years of good behavior, and when the seven years were up, he would not free him.

Saffin printed an answer to Sewall in 1701, defending slavery and his own treatment of Adam. Adam went to court, and in 1703 he won his freedom. Sewall answered Saffin again in 1705 by reprinting an English attack on the slave trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quakers who spoke first, and what their church did to them" -->
Among the Quakers, an argument about slavery kept going in these years, and their own church leaders spoke out against the first Quakers who pressed it. Ralph Sandiford published *A Brief Examination of the Practice of the Times* in Philadelphia in 1729. It argued against owning slaves, and Quaker leaders spoke out against it in public.

**Benjamin Lay in Barbados.** Benjamin Lay was born in Essex, in England, in 1682. From 1718 he and his wife spent about eighteen months in Barbados, an island in the Caribbean where enslaved Africans already outnumbered the white settlers by 1660, about 27,000 to 26,000. He later wrote that there he "saw and heard of such very great Barbarity," or cruelty, "used toward the Slaves, Night and Day."

A man named Richard Parrot whipped the people he enslaved "very severely" every Monday morning. One of them, a barrel maker, told Lay he would not be whipped on Monday morning, and he killed himself on a Sunday. Hungry enslaved workers came into the Lays' shop to buy, to beg, and sometimes to steal food. Lay wrote that when he caught them he sometimes whipped them, "but I have been sorry for it many times."

**Near Philadelphia.** Lay then settled near Philadelphia. He was just over 4 feet tall, with a hunched back and thin arms and legs. For a time he lived in a cave with a library of about 200 books. His wife, Sarah Lay, was also small and had a curved spine. She was a recorded Quaker minister, a speaker her meeting officially recognized.

Lay published a book against slaveholding in 1737 with a long title: *All Slave-Keepers That Keep the Innocent in Bondage, Apostates*. An apostate is a person who has abandoned their faith. Benjamin Franklin printed it and left his own name off it.

Quaker leaders spoke out against the book, and the Quakers of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting disowned Lay. Disowning was a formal statement that a member no longer shared an essential part of the faith. The member's name came off the list, and he could no longer hold certain offices, though he could still come to worship. Sarah Lay stayed a member all her life.

**The sword and the book.** At the yearly meeting's gathering in Burlington, New Jersey, in 1738, Lay stood up in front of the Quakers there. His words began, "Oh, all you negro masters, who are contentedly holding your fellow-creatures in a state of slavery during life," using the word of the time for Black people. He threw off his plain Quaker cloak. Underneath he wore a soldier's uniform, in front of people who were committed to peace.

Holding people as slaves, he said, was as right, or "as justifiable," as "if you should thrust a sword through their hearts, as I do through this." Then he drove a sword into a hollowed-out book. Inside it he had hidden an animal bladder filled with pokeberry juice, and the red juice sprayed the people near him.

The Quakers carried him out and laid him on the doorstep. He lay there for the rest of the day, so everyone leaving had to step over him. Lay died in 1759.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Quaker meetings change their rules, 1754 to 1778" -->
Quakers changed their rules about slavery step by step after 1750. The Quakers of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting published a paper in 1754 that called slavery a sin, and sent it to other meetings. Four years later the members of the yearly meeting spoke out against buying and selling people, but still allowed members to keep the people they already held.

**The ban.** Philadelphia Yearly Meeting's members banned slaveholding among themselves in 1776, 88 years after the four men at Germantown signed their protest. A Quaker who held people in slavery could now be disowned, and Philadelphia Quakers disowned the first members for it in 1778.

Quakers in other regions made the same change in those years. The members of New England Yearly Meeting made slaveholding a reason to disown a member in 1773. New York Quakers spoke out against slaveholding in 1774, and North Carolina Quakers made it a reason to disown a member in 1776.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 12 minutes**

People left out of the Revolution's promises answered with the Revolution's own words. The delegates to the Continental Congress voted on July 4, 1776, to adopt the Declaration of Independence, which states that "all men are created equal." The men who wrote the new state constitutions of 1776 kept women from voting, except in New Jersey.

Black taxpayers in Massachusetts asked why they paid taxes without a vote. In New Jersey, some women and some free Black men voted for thirty-one years, until the state's lawmakers took the vote away in 1807. In Philadelphia, a group of men formed a society in 1775 to help free people who had been seized and held as slaves against the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dinah Nevil and the first antislavery society, 1775" -->
The Philadelphia antislavery society of 1775 started because one woman said she was free. Her name was Dinah Nevil, and the records call her an Indian woman, meaning a Native American. A Virginia man named Benjamin Bannarman bought Nevil and her four children from Nathaniel Lowry of New Jersey in 1773. When they were delivered in Philadelphia, Nevil said in public that she and her children were "free people."

The mayor of Philadelphia sent Nevil and her children to the city's Work House, a building where the city held people, to wait for a hearing. A group of Quakers led by Israel Pemberton brought a lawsuit for her. The case ran for two years. At the end, the judges ruled that Dinah Nevil and her children were slaves. The society's records do not name the judges.

**The society.** After that ruling and others like it, a group of men met at the Rising Sun Tavern in Philadelphia on April 14, 1775. They formed the Society for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage. Sixteen of the 24 first members were Quakers. Thomas Harrison was the secretary, and Anthony Benezet was a member.

A Historical Society of Pennsylvania page calls it the first abolition society in the western world. Its first work was helping free people of color, people who were not white, who had been seized and held as slaves. The members stopped meeting in November 1775. Most of them were Quakers, who refused to fight in the war with Britain, and they believed that refusal would turn people against the society.

**How Nevil got free.** Dinah Nevil never won her case. Thomas Harrison, who had been caring for her and her children, used his own money in 1781 to buy the family from the man who claimed them. Then he freed them. Judges had kept rejecting her claim, and buying her was the only way left. Eight years had passed since she first said she was free.

**The society grows.** The society began meeting again on April 23, 1787, and let in members who were not Quakers. Its members elected Benjamin Franklin president. Later members included Benjamin Rush, Thomas Paine and John Jay. People in other states set up societies like it. New York had one by 1785, two years before the Philadelphia society met again. In 1787 Richard Allen, Absalom Jones and other Black Philadelphians formed their own group, the Free African Society.

The Philadelphia members got a charter in 1789, an official paper from the Pennsylvania government that recognized the society. They also took a longer name: the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage, and for Improving the Condition of the African Race. By 1790 Delaware, Maryland and Connecticut had societies too.

### Abigail Adams

> **Who:** A Massachusetts woman who asked her husband, while he helped lead a new country, to "Remember the Ladies" in its laws.
> **When and where:** Braintree, Massachusetts. Letters of March 31, April 14 and May 7, 1776.
> **Movie:** John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries). The series is about John Adams and his family. Abigail is a lead character, not the subject.

**"Remember the Ladies," she wrote. "I cannot but laugh," he answered.**

John Adams had been away at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia since 1774, and that summer he helped draft the Declaration of Independence. Abigail Adams stayed at home in Braintree, the town now called Quincy, Massachusetts, and ran the family farm and its money. The couple wrote to each other often. The Massachusetts Historical Society keeps their letters.

On March 31, 1776, she wrote, in her own spelling, "I long to hear that you have declared an independency." She went on: "and by the way in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors."

"Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands," she wrote. "Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could." Tyrants are rulers who use their power cruelly. "If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation." To foment a rebellion is to stir one up.

**What she was asking about.** In 1776 coverture still governed marriage in the colonies, and a husband controlled everything his wife owned and earned. An English lawyer, William Blackstone, had written the rule down a few years earlier in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, published from 1765 to 1769. "By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law," he wrote, "that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage." While she was married, a wife did not exist in law as her own person.

Blackstone did not invent the rule. It was already centuries old. Her last sentence used the colonists' own complaint against Britain. That July the delegates accused the British king of "imposing Taxes on us without our Consent." They called "the right of Representation in the Legislature," a say in the body that makes the laws, "a right inestimable to them," too precious to measure.

**His answer.** John Adams wrote back on April 14, 1776: "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh." He wrote that the war had "loosened the bands of Government every where," He listed who had grown bold: "That Children and Apprentices were disobedient," "that schools and Colledges were grown turbulent," meaning rowdy, and "that Indians slighted their Guardians and Negroes grew insolent to their Masters."

Slighted means treated without respect, and insolent means rude to someone in charge. By "Negroes" and "their Masters" he meant enslaved Black people and the men who enslaved them. He would not give up the men's power, he wrote, because that "would compleatly subject Us to the Despotism of the Peticoat." Despotism is rule by a tyrant, and a petticoat was a woman's underskirt. He meant rule by women.

**Her reply.** She wrote back on May 7, 1776: "I can not say that I think you very generous to the Ladies, for whilst you are proclaiming peace and good will to Men, Emancipating all Nations, you insist upon retaining an absolute power over Wives," emancipating meaning setting free. She added that "Arbitary power is like most other things which are very hard, very liable to be broken," arbitrary power being power used however the ruler likes, with no rules to limit it.

No lawmakers in any state changed a law for women because of these letters, which were private. Her grandson Charles Francis Adams published a book of her letters, *Letters of Mrs. Adams, the Wife of John Adams*, in 1840, more than twenty years after she died.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Some women and free Black men vote in New Jersey, 1776 to 1807" -->
Unmarried women and free Black men who owned enough property could vote in New Jersey from 1776 to 1807. The state's constitution of 1776 gave the vote to "all inhabitants of this colony, of full age, who are worth fifty pounds ... and have resided within the county ... for twelve months." It did not say male, and it did not say white. The other states' constitutions of 1776 limited the vote to a "freeman," a "man" or a white man, and every one of them left women out.

**Who could pass the test.** Because of coverture, only unmarried women and widows could meet New Jersey's property test. A married woman could not own fifty pounds of anything, because in law everything she had belonged to her husband. Members of the state assembly said so in 1800: "Our Constitution gives this right to maids or widows black or white," maids meaning unmarried women. Some losing candidates later complained that married women had voted anyway.

**The names on the lists.** The state's election laws of 1790 and 1797 described the voter as "he or she." Researchers at the Museum of the American Revolution have found 163 women's names on nine lists of voters from 1800 to 1807. That count is the lowest possible number, because only nine lists survive.

Sarah Eoff, Margaret McDonald and Eleanor Boylan are on the 1800 list for Bedminster Township. Eleanor Boylan was a widow of about 51 when she voted. Four women of one family, Rebecca, Ann, Catherine and Sarah VanDike, voted together in October 1801. The 1801 list for Montgomery Township holds at least 46 women and at least four free Black men, among them Caesar Trent of Princeton.

**The vote taken away.** The men who made New Jersey's laws took the vote away in November 1807. Earlier that year, in February, Essex County held a vote on whether to build a new courthouse in Newark or in Elizabeth. People on both sides broke the rules and brought in voters who had no right to vote. In Elizabeth the votes counted came to 279 percent of the people allowed to vote, nearly three votes for every voter.

The new law limited the vote to "free, white, male citizens." It took the vote from women, from free Black men and from recent immigrants. In its opening paragraph the lawmakers wrote of "doubts" about letting "aliens, persons of color, or negroes" vote.

Aliens meant people from other countries who were not citizens. An exhibit by the Museum of the American Revolution says the lawmakers blamed women, people of color and newcomers for all the cheating, which the museum calls scapegoating, putting the blame on people who were not at fault.

**Why they did it.** Writers for the National Park Service, the New Jersey Historical Commission and the Museum of the American Revolution all say the lawmakers acted for party reasons, not out of a debate about women's rights. The museum's historians also link the law to the cheating in the courthouse vote. The two parties of the time were the Federalists and the Democratic-Republicans.

The three disagree about which party women's votes had helped. A National Park Service page says women often voted for the Federalists, and that the law helped the rival Democratic-Republicans before the 1808 presidential election. A New Jersey Historical Commission teaching sheet says Federalists had come to believe that women's votes helped the Democratic-Republicans.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="No vote, no taxes: Paul and John Cuffe, 1780" -->
Seven free Black men of Dartmouth, Massachusetts, told the state's lawmakers in February 1780 that they should not be taxed if they could not vote. They were John Cuffe, Adventur Child, Paul Cuffe, Samuel Gray, Pero Howland, Pero Russell and Pero Coggeshall. John and Paul Cuffe were brothers. Gray, Howland and Russell signed with a mark instead of a written name.

The petition said that "by reason of long bondage and hard slavery, we have been deprived of enjoying the profits of our labor or the advantage of inheriting estates from our parents." They were taxed all the same, although "we are not allowed the privilege of freemen of the State, having no vote or influence in the election of those that tax us." It also said that "many of our colour (as is well known) have cheerfully entered the field of battle in the defence of the common cause," meaning they had fought in the Revolution.

The new Massachusetts constitution of March 1780 contained nothing that answered their complaint. The Cuffe brothers wrote to the county court and to the town's leaders, calling themselves both "Indian men" and "free Negroes." They refused to pay their taxes, and Dartmouth's town officials put them in jail for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prince Hall asks for schools, 1787" -->
Prince Hall and other Black Bostonians asked the Massachusetts lawmakers for schools for their children in 1787. No law barred Black children from Boston's free schools, but the National Park Service records that Black students there "found themselves at the mercy of hostile teachers and fellow students." The petition said they "receive no benefit from the free schools in the town of Boston, which we think is a great grievance," a wrong worth complaining about, "as by woful experience we now feel the want of a common education."

The lawmakers turned the petition down. They turned down a second petition in 1796. In 1798 the families opened their own school, the African School. It met in the home of Primus Hall, Prince Hall's son.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Judith Sargent Murray argues that women's minds are equal, 1790" -->
Judith Sargent Murray published an essay called "On the Equality of the Sexes" in 1790. She was born on May 1, 1751, in Gloucester, Massachusetts. The essay ran in the *Massachusetts Magazine* under the pen name "Constantia," a made-up name for signing her writing.

She argued that men and women have equal minds, and that women fell behind only because girls did not get the education that boys got. One line of the essay asks "in what the minds of females are so notoriously deficient, or unequal." Unlike Abigail Adams's private letters, the essay was printed where anyone could read it.
