## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A rights movement is a group of people who organize together to change a rule that denies them something. No record shows a movement of that kind in this land before 1500. In 1492 about six hundred Native nations lived in North America north of the Rio Grande, the river that now runs along part of the border with Mexico. Each nation governed itself in its own way. In at least one of them, the Haudenosaunee, a union of five nations in what is now New York, women held power in government. They chose the leaders. When a leader did not do his job, his clan mother warned him, and his clan could then remove him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The clan mothers of the Haudenosaunee" -->
The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is a union of nations. Its five founding nations are the Seneca, the Cayuga, the Onondaga, the Oneida and the Mohawk. The Onondaga Nation's own account states that the founding took place on the shores of Onondaga Lake, in what is now central New York. The Onondaga still describe how their women choose leaders.

A clan is a group of related families. Each clan has a clan mother. She chooses the man who speaks for her clan in council, the meeting where the chiefs talk and decide together. His title is Hoyane, which means chief. In the Onondaga founding account, a figure called the Peacemaker placed deer antlers on the headdress of every Hoyane. The antlers show that the man is a chief.

The clan mother can also start the removal of a chief. If the chief stops speaking for his clan, she warns him that he is not following the ways of the Great Peace and must change. The Great Peace is the set of laws that joined the five nations, described below. If he still does not change, the clan removes him in council. The clan takes his antlers off, and the Onondaga say that he has been "de-horned."

The National Park Service is the federal agency that runs the country's national parks and historic sites. Its historians record more of the women's powers. Haudenosaunee women grew the food and shared it out. Through that work, the Park Service historians write, the women "controlled the economy" of their nations, meaning the food and goods their people lived on. They also held "the final authority over land transfers and decisions about engaging in war." That means the women had the last word on whether land was handed to anyone else and on whether their nation went to war. When a husband and wife separated, the wife took with her all the property she had brought into the home, and the children went with her.

The Great Law of Peace is the set of laws that the people of the five nations agreed to live by when they joined together. Nobody can prove when they agreed to it. Haudenosaunee tradition and the wampum records, belts and strings of shell beads that hold agreements, give a date between about the year 1000 and 1400. Accounts based on written records have mostly put it around 1450, and some put it in the late 1100s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the records can and cannot show" -->
Almost everything written down about how these nations governed themselves was written after Europeans arrived, by Europeans. The nations kept their own records in spoken accounts and in wampum. Wampum is beads cut from the white and purple parts of clam shells and woven into strings and belts. The Onondaga Nation describes how it works: a speaker puts the words of an agreement into the wampum while the belt is woven. Every speaker after that uses the belt to remember the agreement and what has happened since. The Onondaga have been the wampum keepers of the Confederacy since its founding.

No record from before 1500 in this land names a single person who claimed a right that others denied.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish settlers in the 1500s forced Native people to work for them under Spanish law. No group organized to change that law. In Spain, a Spanish priest named Bartolomé de Las Casas argued that Native people had rights. People also fought back in this century. In October 1526, enslaved Africans at San Miguel de Gualdape, a Spanish settlement on the coast of what is now Georgia or the Carolinas, rose up and escaped. It is the first rebellion of enslaved Africans on record in what became the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The encomienda" -->
One Spanish system for using Native people's work was the encomienda. It was a grant from the Spanish king that gave a settler the labor of a named group of Native people. On paper, the settler had to protect them and teach them Christianity. In practice, he took their work and the food and goods they produced. The historians who wrote the open textbook *The American Yawp* call it "an exploitive feudal arrangement in which Spain tied Indigenous laborers to vast estates." "Feudal" refers to the old system in Europe in which farm workers were bound to a lord's land. "Indigenous" means Native. In plain words, under these grants Spanish settlers held Native workers on their large estates. The same historians write that the settlers who held these grants "brutalized their laborers." Las Casas, who came to the Caribbean in 1502, wrote that he saw Spaniards "cut off the Hands, Noses, and Ears, both of Indians and Indianesses," meaning Native men and women, "for no other reason, but only to gratify their bloody mindedness," meaning for the pleasure of hurting them.

The Caribbean island of Hispaniola was one of the places where Spanish settlers forced Native people to work this way. The Native people of the islands were the Taíno. Spaniards enslaved them and made them dig for gold. When the gold ran out, the Spaniards forced them to work on the encomienda farms. The Native population of Hispaniola fell from 60,000 in 1508 to fewer than 20,000 ten years later.

In 1542 Spanish officials ended the encomienda in a set of rules called the New Laws. They replaced it with another labor system, the repartimiento, and Spanish settlers went on forcing Native people to work for them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bartolomé de Las Casas" -->
In 1513 Las Casas went to Cuba as a chaplain, a priest who travels with soldiers. He served with the Spanish forces who were taking the island from the Taíno. In his *History of the Indies*, he later wrote what he saw at the town of Caonao. The Taíno there had made cassava bread and fish for the Spaniards. About two thousand people sat in the town square, and more than five hundred were in a large house nearby. One Spaniard drew his sword. Then the whole hundred Spanish soldiers drew theirs and cut open and killed the men, women, children and old people where they sat. They went into the large house and killed the people there too. Some escaped by climbing to the top of the house. Las Casas had stepped away to another part of the town shortly before.

For serving with the soldiers, Las Casas received land and an encomienda. In 1514 he told the people of his church that the whole encomienda system was wrong. He freed the Native people he held and began arguing for them to the Spanish officials on the island.

In 1516 he asked the Spanish king to bring enslaved Africans to the Indies, the Spanish name for its islands and lands across the Atlantic, to take the place of Native workers. He believed Africans were better suited to hard physical labor. Slave traders had already been carrying enslaved Africans across the Atlantic for about sixteen years. On 18 August 1518, King Charles I of Spain, later Emperor Charles V, gave a man named Lorenzo de Gorrevod a charter, a written permission, to ship enslaved Africans across the Atlantic.

Las Casas wrote a short book about what Spanish settlers did to Native people. Its Spanish title is *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias*, in English *A Brief Account of the Devastation of the Indies*. After he wrote it, Spanish officials passed the New Laws of 1542.

In 1550, Charles V called Las Casas and a scholar named Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda to the town of Valladolid, in Spain. The question was whether the Spanish had the right to make war on Native people and rule them by force. Sepúlveda argued that they did. Las Casas argued that they did not. They spoke in front of a panel of churchmen and royal officials, among them the church scholars Domingo Soto and Melchor Cano. On the first day Sepúlveda spoke for three hours. The panel held its final meeting in 1551, and no record of a ruling survives.

Later in life, Las Casas changed his mind about enslaving Africans. In his *History of the Indies* he wrote at length against the African slave trade and the slave markets of Spain and Portugal. He did not publish that book while he was alive, so his change of mind was not public while the trade grew.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists brought English law with them. Under one of its rules, a married woman did not count in law as a person separate from her husband. Nobody organized against that rule in this century. Until 1688, people who spoke against the men in charge did it one at a time, and the colony's leaders punished them by name. In November 1637 the men who ran Massachusetts, sitting as a court, banished Anne Hutchinson, which means they ordered her out of the colony for good. They did it because of what she taught at religious meetings in her house. In 1660 Massachusetts officials hanged Mary Dyer on Boston Common for coming back to the colony as a Quaker. In 1688 four men in Pennsylvania signed a protest against slavery together and sent it to their church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coverture: what marriage did to a woman in law" -->
The rule was called coverture. In law, a married woman was "covered" by her husband. The legal name for her was *feme covert*, which means a covered woman. She had no legal standing of her own.

In law, that meant she did not control property, even property she had brought into the marriage. Her husband had full rights to any money or wages she earned. She could not sign a contract, a written deal that the law makes people keep. Nobody could take her to court, and she could not bring a case to court in her own name.

An adult woman who had never married, or whose husband had died, was a *feme sole*, a woman on her own. She could buy and sell property, sign contracts and go to court in her own name. Under English law, a woman lost those rights on the day she married.

In daily life, wives ran businesses beside their husbands, and for husbands who were away. Some married women traded in their own names under a legal exception, and the people of their town held everyone to the deal. Under the law, whatever a wife held, she held because her husband allowed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** A Boston woman put on trial for what she taught at religious meetings in her own house, and banished from Massachusetts for it.
> **When and where:** Boston and Newtown, Massachusetts Bay Colony. Meetings from 1636, trial in November 1637, put out of her church in March 1638.

In November 1637 the leaders of Massachusetts put Anne Hutchinson on trial for heresy, which means teaching against the church's beliefs. They banished her, which means they forced her to leave the colony and forbade her to return. The charge grew out of the religious meetings she held in her home for men and women, and out of what she taught there. The colony's governor told her that such meetings were not fitting for a woman.

She was born about 1591 at Alford, in England. In the fall of 1634 she crossed the Atlantic on the ship *Griffin* with her husband, William, and their children. They followed their minister, John Cotton, to Boston, a town the English had built on Massachusett land.

From 1636 she held a weekly meeting in her house to go over the Sunday sermons. At first only women came. Then men came too, including some of the most important men in the colony. Like the colony's leaders, she was a Puritan, one of the English Protestants who had come to Massachusetts to worship God in their own way. In Puritan teaching, grace meant God's gift of being saved, which means going to heaven after death. She said she could tell which ministers and which neighbors had received God's grace. She taught that God saved people by grace alone, and not because they behaved well.

In November 1637 the colony's lawmakers, sitting as judges, tried her at Newtown, the town now called Cambridge. These men were called the General Court. They made the colony's laws, and they also judged cases. The trial lasted two days.

Governor John Winthrop opened it by telling her she was "called here as one of those that have troubled the peace of the commonwealth." The commonwealth meant the colony. He called her meetings "a thing not tolerable nor comely in the sight of God nor fitting for your sex." She answered his Bible verses with Bible verses of her own. At the end of the trial she said God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate revelation," a message straight from God. She said God would curse the Puritans and their children and grandchildren if they harmed her. The men judging her called that blasphemy, which means speaking against God.

She told the judges, "You have power over my body, but the Lord Jesus hath power over my body and soul."

Winthrop read out her punishment: "Mrs. Hutchinson, the sentence of the court you hear is that you are banished from out of our jurisdiction as being a woman not fit for our society." A jurisdiction is the area that a government rules. She asked, "I desire to know wherefore I am banished?" Wherefore means why.

Colony officials held her through the winter. In March 1638 the leaders of the Boston church tried her a second time and put her out of the church. Their charge said she had "rather been a Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer." The charge went on, "and a Magistrate than a Subject." A magistrate is an official who makes and enforces rules.

In the spring of 1638 the family moved to Aquidneck Island, in what became Rhode Island. The minister Roger Williams helped them buy land there from the Narragansett. One of the first written rules of their new town, Portsmouth, said no person should be punished or "called into question on matter of religion ... so long as he keeps the peace."

After her husband died, she moved in 1642 to Dutch-held land on Long Island Sound. It was the country of the Siwanoy, a Native people. The Dutch governor, Willem Kieft, had started a war with the Native people there. On the night of 25 February 1643, armed Dutch settlers at Corlaer's Hook, on Manhattan, and Dutch soldiers at Pavonia, across the Hudson River, murdered at least eighty Munsee people, and perhaps as many as 120. The Munsee were a Native people of that region. Kieft and his council had approved both attacks. In August or September 1643, Siwanoy warriors killed Anne Hutchinson, her servants and all but one of the children who were with her. One daughter survived.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-rights-movements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts leaders outlaw Quakers, then hang four of them" -->
In 1660, Massachusetts officials hanged a woman named Mary Dyer because she kept coming back to the colony as a Quaker. The Quakers followed George Fox, an Englishman who had founded a new religion. Fox taught that men and women were equal in God's eyes. Quakers refused to take their hats off to people of higher rank, because they believed all people were equal, and they refused to swear oaths, solemn promises made in God's name, to any government. Massachusetts Governor John Endecott called them a "pernicious sect," meaning a harmful religious group.

The men of the General Court passed a series of laws against Quakers. In late 1656 and 1657 they outlawed "the cursed sect of heretics commonly called Quakers." They fined ship captains who brought Quakers to the colony and people who owned Quaker books. In October 1657 they set punishments for Quakers who came back after being sent away. A man lost one ear for the first return, cut off with a blade. He lost the second ear for a second return. For a third, "they shall have their tongues bored through with a hot iron," which means a hole was burned through the tongue with a heated iron rod. Women were to be whipped, which means struck on the bare back with a whip.

On 3 June 1658, under that law, colony officers cut off the ears of a Quaker named Christopher Holder and of two other men. Then they whipped the three men and beat them twice a week for nine weeks. The records do not name the officers. On 19 October 1658 the men of the General Court voted 12 to 11 to banish Quakers "upon pain of death." Under that law, a Quaker who came back after being sent away could be hanged.

Mary Dyer had lived in Boston in the 1630s. She was a close friend of Anne Hutchinson, and when leaders of the Boston church put Hutchinson out in March 1638, Dyer walked out of the church beside her. Around 1650 she met George Fox in England. She came back to Boston as a Quaker in 1657. Officials jailed her as soon as she landed, and she spent two and a half months in a dark cell with its windows boarded up. Her husband got her out by promising she would not preach, and the family moved to Rhode Island. She kept going back to Massachusetts to stand by Quakers who were in prison there.

On 20 October 1659, Governor Endecott sentenced Mary Dyer and two Quaker men to death. The men were William Robinson, a merchant from London, and Marmaduke Stephenson, a farm worker from Yorkshire. Dyer's son, William Dyer, asked the governor to spare her. On 26 October Endecott signed an order giving her 48 hours to leave the colony. The same order said she must first be taken to the gallows, the wooden frame used for hangings, and stand there with a rope around her neck while the two men were hanged. On 27 October 1659, officials hanged Robinson and Stephenson in front of her. Then they took her back to prison and sent her out of the colony.

She came back to Massachusetts in 1660, and on 1 June officials hanged Mary Dyer near the Frog Pond on Boston Common, on the sentence of Governor Endecott and the General Court. About 200 soldiers stood around the gallows with guns, swords, long spears called pikes, and flags. Her husband had asked for her life. She had refused to say she was sorry.

In 1661 officials hanged a fourth Quaker in Boston, William Leddra, a cloth maker and Quaker minister from Cornwall, in England. An English Quaker named Edward Burrough counted what had been done to Quakers in Massachusetts between 1656 and 1660. His count lists 64 jailings and 25 banishments. On 9 September 1661, King Charles II of England ordered the punishments stopped and said accused Quakers must be sent to England instead. By 1677, Quakers could meet openly in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four men sign a protest against slavery, 1688" -->
On 18 April 1688, four men at Germantown, in Pennsylvania, signed a paper against owning enslaved people. Their names were Garret Hendericks, Francis Daniel Pastorius, Derick op de Graeff and Abraham op de Graeff. They sent it up through the meetings of their Quaker church. A National Park Service page calls it the first protest against African slavery made by a religious body in the English colonies.

Germantown stood on the land of the Lenape, the Native people of the Philadelphia region. Pastorius had bought the land in 1683 from William Penn, a Quaker to whom King Charles II had given a charter for Pennsylvania in 1681. The surviving records of that sale do not name the Lenape families who lived on it.

The protest rested on the Golden Rule, the teaching that people should treat others as they want to be treated. In the paper's own spelling, the rule was "that we shall doe to all men licke as we will be done ourselves."

A Quaker meeting is a group of Quakers who worship together and make decisions together. A monthly meeting is a local group. A quarterly meeting joins several local groups, and a yearly meeting joins the Quakers of a whole region. The members of four meetings, one after another, read the paper and passed it on without acting. The first was Germantown Monthly Meeting, then Dublin Monthly Meeting, then Philadelphia Quarterly Meeting. The last was Burlington Yearly Meeting, in New Jersey, and its members voted to take no stand either way. Nothing changed because of the protest.

The Quakers put the paper aside and forgot it. Abolitionists, people working to end slavery, found it again in 1844. Then it went missing a second time. In March 2005 it turned up in a vault at the Arch Street Meetinghouse in Philadelphia. It is now kept at Haverford College, in Pennsylvania.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, single men printed arguments against slavery, and the Quakers began to argue about it among themselves. There was still no organized group working for anyone's rights. The Quaker leaders punished the members who raised the question first. By 1776, the Quakers of Philadelphia had changed their own rules anyway and banned slaveholding among their members.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Boston merchant writes a pamphlet against slavery, 1700" -->
In 1700 Samuel Sewall, a Boston merchant and magistrate, an official who enforced the colony's laws, wrote a three-page pamphlet called *The Selling of Joseph*. A pamphlet is a short printed booklet. A Massachusetts Historical Society page calls it the first antislavery pamphlet published in America. Bartholomew Green and John Allen printed it in Boston.

Sewall argued that taking people and selling them is the crime the Bible calls man stealing, and he pointed to the verse Exodus 21:16. Then he went through the usual arguments in favor of slavery and answered each one.

Sewall wrote it because of a real case. In 1700 a petition, a written request signed by people, went around Boston asking for the freedom of an enslaved man named Adam and his wife, who were "unjustly held in Bondage" by a merchant named John Saffin. Saffin had promised Adam his freedom after seven years of good behavior. When the seven years were up, Saffin would not free him.

Saffin printed an answer to Sewall in 1701, defending slavery and his own treatment of Adam. Adam went to court, and in 1703 he won his freedom. Sewall answered Saffin again in 1705 by reprinting an English attack on the slave trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quakers who spoke first, and what their church did to them" -->
Among the Quakers, an argument about slavery kept going in these years. Their own church leaders spoke out against the first Quakers who pressed it.

Ralph Sandiford published *A Brief Examination of the Practice of the Times* in Philadelphia in 1729. It argued against owning slaves. Quaker leaders spoke out against it in public.

Benjamin Lay was born in Essex, in England, in 1682. From 1718 he and his wife spent about eighteen months in Barbados, an island in the Caribbean where most of the people were enslaved. He later wrote that there he "saw and heard of such very great Barbarity used toward the Slaves, Night and Day." Barbarity means cruelty. A man named Richard Parrot whipped the people he enslaved "very severely" every Monday morning. One of them, a barrel maker, told Lay he would not be whipped on Monday morning, and he killed himself on a Sunday. Hungry enslaved workers came into the Lays' shop to buy, to beg, and sometimes to steal food. Lay wrote that when he caught them he sometimes whipped them, "but I have been sorry for it many times."

Then Lay settled near Philadelphia. He was just over 4 feet tall, with a hunched back and thin arms and legs. For a time he lived in a cave with a library of about 200 books. His wife, Sarah Lay, was also small and had a curved spine. She was a recorded Quaker minister, a speaker her meeting officially recognized.

In 1737 Lay published a book against slaveholding with a long title: *All Slave-Keepers That Keep the Innocent in Bondage, Apostates*. An apostate is a person who has abandoned their faith. Benjamin Franklin printed it and left his own name off it. Quaker leaders spoke out against the book, and the Quakers of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting disowned Lay. Disowning was a formal statement that a member no longer shared an essential part of the faith. The member's name came off the membership list, and he could no longer hold certain offices, though he could still come to worship. Sarah Lay stayed a member all her life.

In 1738, at the yearly meeting's gathering in Burlington, New Jersey, Lay stood up in front of the Quakers there. His words began, "Oh, all you negro masters, who are contentedly holding your fellow-creatures in a state of slavery during life." He threw off his plain Quaker cloak. Underneath he wore a soldier's uniform, in front of people who were committed to peace. He said that holding people as slaves would be as right, or "as justifiable," as "if you should thrust a sword through their hearts, as I do through this." Then he drove a sword into a hollowed-out book. Inside it he had hidden an animal bladder filled with pokeberry juice, and the red juice sprayed the people near him. The Quakers carried him out and laid him on the doorstep. He lay there for the rest of the day, so everyone leaving had to step over him. Lay died in 1759.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Quaker meetings change their rules, 1754 to 1778" -->
The Quaker meetings changed their rules step by step after 1750. In 1754 the Quakers of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting published a paper that called slavery a sin, and sent it to other meetings. In 1758 the members of the yearly meeting spoke out against buying and selling people, but still allowed members to keep the people they already held.

In 1776 Philadelphia Yearly Meeting banned slaveholding among its members, 88 years after the four men at Germantown signed their protest. A Quaker who held people in slavery could now be disowned. The meetings disowned the first members for it in 1778. Quakers in other regions made the same change in those years. New England Yearly Meeting made slaveholding a reason to disown a member in 1773. New York Yearly Meeting spoke out against slaveholding in 1774, and North Carolina Yearly Meeting made it a reason to disown a member in 1776.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="rights-movements" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1775 a group of men in Philadelphia formed the first society in the English colonies that worked to free people held as slaves. On 4 July 1776 the delegates to the Continental Congress voted to adopt the Declaration of Independence, which states that "all men are created equal." The men who wrote the other new state constitutions of 1776 kept women from voting. New Jersey's did not. Some of the people left out of those rights answered with the Revolution's own words. Black taxpayers in Massachusetts asked why they paid taxes without a vote. In New Jersey, some women and some free Black men could vote for thirty-one years, until the state's lawmakers took the vote away in 1807.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dinah Nevil and the first antislavery society, 1775" -->
The first antislavery society in the English colonies started because of one woman's claim that she was free. Her name was Dinah Nevil, and the records call her an Indian woman, meaning a Native American. In 1773 a Virginia man named Benjamin Bannarman bought Nevil and her four children from Nathaniel Lowry of New Jersey. When they were delivered in Philadelphia, Nevil said in public that she and her children were "free people."

The mayor of Philadelphia sent Nevil and her children to the city's Work House to wait for a hearing. A group of Quakers led by Israel Pemberton brought a lawsuit for her. The case ran for two years. At the end, the judges ruled that Dinah Nevil and her children were slaves. The society's records do not name the judges.

On 14 April 1775, after that ruling and others like it, a group of men met at the Rising Sun Tavern in Philadelphia. They formed the Society for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage. Sixteen of the 24 first members were Quakers. Thomas Harrison was the secretary, and Anthony Benezet was a member. A Historical Society of Pennsylvania page calls it the first abolition society in the western world. Its first work was helping free people of color, people who were not white, who had been seized and held as slaves.

The members stopped meeting in November 1775. Most of them were Quakers, who refused to fight in the war with Britain, and they believed that refusal would turn people against the society.

Dinah Nevil did not win her case. In 1781 Thomas Harrison, who had been caring for her and her children, used his own money to buy the family from the man who claimed them. Then he freed them. Judges had kept rejecting her claim, and buying her was the only way left. Eight years had passed since she first said she was free.

The society began meeting again on 23 April 1787 and let in members who were not Quakers. Its members elected Benjamin Franklin president. Later members included Benjamin Rush, Thomas Paine and John Jay. In 1789 the members got a charter from the state of Pennsylvania, an official paper that recognized the society. They also took a longer name: the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage, and for Improving the Condition of the African Race. People in other states copied it. New York had a society by 1785, and Delaware, Maryland and Connecticut had them by 1790. In 1787 Richard Allen, Absalom Jones and other Black Philadelphians formed their own group, the Free African Society.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="abigail-adams" name="Abigail Adams" movie="John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abigail Adams

> **Who:** A Massachusetts woman who wrote to her husband while he was helping lead a new country, and asked him to "Remember the Ladies" in its laws.
> **When and where:** Braintree, Massachusetts. Letters of 31 March, 14 April and 7 May 1776.
> **Movie:** John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries). The series is about John Adams and his family. Abigail is a lead character, not the subject.

In the spring of 1776, Abigail Adams asked her husband, John, to limit a husband's power over his wife in the new country's laws. He wrote back that he could only laugh at the idea.

John Adams had been away at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia since 1774. That summer he helped draft the Declaration of Independence. Abigail Adams stayed at home in Braintree, the town now called Quincy, Massachusetts. She ran the family farm and its money. The couple wrote to each other often, and their letters are kept at the Massachusetts Historical Society.

On 31 March 1776 she wrote, in her own spelling, "I long to hear that you have declared an independency." She went on: "and by the way in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could. If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation." To foment a rebellion means to stir one up. Tyrants are rulers who use their power cruelly.

The law that put "unlimited power" into husbands' hands was coverture, the rule that gave a husband control of everything his wife owned and earned. An English lawyer, William Blackstone, had written the rule down a few years earlier, in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, published from 1765 to 1769. "By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law," he wrote, "that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage, or at least is incorporated and consolidated into that of the husband." In plain words, while she was married a wife did not exist in law as her own person, or her legal self was folded into her husband's. Blackstone did not invent the rule. It was already centuries old.

Her last sentence turned against men a complaint that the colonists were making against Britain. In the Declaration of Independence that July, the delegates accused the British king of "imposing Taxes on us without our Consent." They also called "the right of Representation in the Legislature," the right to have a say in the body that makes the laws, "a right inestimable to them," meaning too precious to measure.

John Adams answered on 14 April 1776: "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh." He wrote, "We have been told that our Struggle has loosened the bands of Government every where." He listed who had grown bold: "That Children and Apprentices were disobedient," and "that schools and Colledges were grown turbulent," meaning rowdy. He added "that Indians slighted their Guardians and Negroes grew insolent to their Masters." Slighted means treated without respect. By "Negroes" and "their Masters" he meant enslaved Black people and the men who enslaved them. Insolent means rude to someone in charge. He would not give up the men's power, he wrote, because that "would compleatly subject Us to the Despotism of the Peticoat." Despotism means rule by a tyrant, and a petticoat was a woman's underskirt. He meant rule by women.

She wrote back on 7 May 1776: "I can not say that I think you very generous to the Ladies, for whilst you are proclaiming peace and good will to Men, Emancipating all Nations, you insist upon retaining an absolute power over Wives." She added that "Arbitary power is like most other things which are very hard, very liable to be broken." Emancipating means setting free. Arbitrary power is power used however the ruler likes, with no rules to limit it.

No lawmakers in any state changed a law for women because of these letters. The letters were private. In 1840, more than twenty years after she died, her grandson Charles Francis Adams published a book of her letters called *Letters of Mrs. Adams, the Wife of John Adams*.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Some women and free Black men vote in New Jersey, 1776 to 1807" -->
From 1776 to 1807, unmarried women and free Black men who owned enough property could vote in New Jersey. New Jersey's constitution of 1776 gave the vote to "all inhabitants of this colony, of full age, who are worth fifty pounds ... and have resided within the county ... for twelve months." It did not say male, and it did not say white. The other states' constitutions of 1776 limited the vote to a "freeman," a "man" or a white man, and every one of them left women out.

Because of coverture, only unmarried women and widows could meet New Jersey's property test. A married woman could not own fifty pounds of anything, because in law everything she had belonged to her husband. So married women could not vote, and unmarried women and widows who met the property test could vote. In 1800 members of the state assembly said so plainly: "Our Constitution gives this right to maids or widows black or white." Some losing candidates later complained that married women had voted anyway.

The state's election laws of 1790 and 1797 described the voter as "he or she." Researchers at the Museum of the American Revolution have found 163 women's names on nine lists of voters from 1800 to 1807. That count is the lowest possible number, because only nine lists survive. Sarah Eoff, Margaret McDonald and Eleanor Boylan are on the 1800 list for Bedminster Township. Eleanor Boylan was a widow of about 51 when she voted. In October 1801, four women of one family, Rebecca, Ann, Catherine and Sarah VanDike, voted together. The 1801 list for Montgomery Township holds at least 46 women and at least four free Black men, among them Caesar Trent of Princeton.

The men who made New Jersey's laws took the vote away in November 1807. Earlier that year, in February, Essex County held a vote on whether to build a new courthouse in Newark or in Elizabeth. People on both sides broke the rules and brought in voters who had no right to vote. In Elizabeth the number of votes counted came to 279 percent of the people allowed to vote, nearly three votes for every person allowed to vote. The new law limited the vote to "free, white, male citizens." Under it, the lawmakers took the vote away from women, from free Black men and from recent immigrants. In the law's opening paragraph, the lawmakers wrote that there were "doubts" about letting "aliens, persons of color, or negroes" vote. Aliens meant people from other countries who were not citizens. An exhibit by the Museum of the American Revolution says that in this way the lawmakers blamed women, people of color and newcomers for all the cheating. The museum calls it scapegoating, which means putting the blame on people who were not at fault.

Writers for the National Park Service, the New Jersey Historical Commission and the Museum of the American Revolution all say the lawmakers acted for party reasons, not out of a debate about women's rights. The museum's historians also link the law to the cheating in the courthouse vote. The three disagree about which party women's votes had helped. The two parties of the time were the Federalists and the Democratic-Republicans, two groups that ran candidates against each other. A National Park Service page says women often voted for the Federalist Party, and the law helped the rival Democratic-Republicans before the 1808 presidential election. A New Jersey Historical Commission teaching sheet says Federalists had come to believe that women's votes helped the Democratic-Republicans.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="No vote, no taxes: Paul and John Cuffe, 1780" -->
In February 1780, seven free Black men of Dartmouth, Massachusetts, told the state's lawmakers that they should not be taxed if they could not vote. They were John Cuffe, Adventur Child, Paul Cuffe, Samuel Gray, Pero Howland, Pero Russell and Pero Coggeshall. John and Paul Cuffe were brothers. Gray, Howland and Russell signed with a mark instead of a written name.

The petition said that "by reason of long bondage and hard slavery, we have been deprived of enjoying the profits of our labor or the advantage of inheriting estates from our parents." They were taxed all the same, although "we are not allowed the privilege of freemen of the State, having no vote or influence in the election of those that tax us." It pointed out that "many of our colour (as is well known) have cheerfully entered the field of battle in the defence of the common cause," meaning they had fought in the Revolution.

The new Massachusetts constitution of March 1780 contained nothing that answered their complaint. The Cuffe brothers wrote to the county court and to the town's leaders, calling themselves both "Indian men" and "free Negroes." They refused to pay their taxes, and Dartmouth's town officials put them in jail for it.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prince Hall asks for schools, 1787" -->
In 1787 Prince Hall and other Black Bostonians asked the Massachusetts lawmakers for schools for their children. No law barred Black children from Boston's free schools, but the National Park Service records that Black students there "found themselves at the mercy of hostile teachers and fellow students." The petition said they "receive no benefit from the free schools in the town of Boston, which we think is a great grievance, as by woful experience we now feel the want of a common education." A grievance is a wrong that people complain about.

The lawmakers turned the petition down. They turned down a second petition in 1796. In 1798 the families opened their own school, the African School. It met in the home of Primus Hall, Prince Hall's son.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Judith Sargent Murray argues that women's minds are equal, 1790" -->
In 1790 Judith Sargent Murray published an essay called "On the Equality of the Sexes." She was born on 1 May 1751 in Gloucester, Massachusetts. The essay ran in the *Massachusetts Magazine* under the pen name "Constantia," a made-up name for signing her writing. She argued that men and women have equal minds, and that women were held back because they had been denied an education. One line of the essay asks "in what the minds of females are so notoriously deficient, or unequal." Unlike Abigail Adams's private letters, the essay was printed where anyone could read it.
