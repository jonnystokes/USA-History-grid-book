## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 14 minutes**

Chain stores spread across the country, and the biggest grocery chain, the A&P, grew from about 200 stores in 1900 to about 15,000 by 1930. Grocers began letting customers take goods off the shelves themselves, and families bought cars and radios on monthly payments. Sellers began paying radio and television stations to air their advertisements. Coal companies paid miners in their own money, which only the company store accepted at full value. During the Second World War, shoppers needed ration stamps as well as money to buy many foods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The chain store at full size" -->
The A&P had 1,600 stores by 1915. By 1930 it had about 15,000 stores and sold $2.9 billion worth of goods a year. That made it the world's largest retailer, a business that sells goods to the people who use them. The A&P was the largest grocery seller in the United States from 1915 to 1975.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-woolworth" name="F. W. Woolworth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### F. W. Woolworth

> **Who:** The storekeeper who built a national chain of five-cent and ten-cent stores and paid cash for the tallest building in the world.
> **When and where:** Utica and Lancaster, 1879, and New York, 1911 to 1913.

**$13.5 million for the world's tallest building, paid in cash.**

Frank Winfield Woolworth turned his five-cent stores of 1879 into the largest store company in the world. On November 12, 1911, he signed agreements to join his chain with four rival chains, the Knox, Kirby, Charlton and C. S. Woolworth chains.

The new F. W. Woolworth Company started with 596 stores and $65 million in capital, the money put into a business to run it. The company was formally set up in 1912, so some histories list that year as its start.

**The building.** The Woolworth Building in New York was finished in 1913. At 792 feet, it was the tallest building in the world at the time. It cost $13.5 million, and Woolworth paid for it himself, in cash.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-woolworth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who first let shoppers serve themselves?" -->
Clarence Saunders started self-service shopping when he opened the first Piggly Wiggly store at 79 Jefferson Street in Memphis, Tennessee, on September 6, 1916, by most accounts. His customers picked goods off the shelves themselves instead of asking a clerk to fetch them.

**The turnstile.** Customers came in through a turnstile, a gate with turning arms that lets one person through at a time. Then they had to follow a single fenced path past every shelf in the store before they reached the place where they paid.

Saunders asked the government for a patent, a paper that lets only the inventor use an invention for a number of years. He applied for one on his "Self-Serving Store" on October 21, 1916, and received Patent 1,242,872 on October 9, 1917.

Michael J. Cullen opened King Kullen in Jamaica, in Queens, New York, on August 4, 1930. He had worked for the Kroger grocery company and had sent its managers a plan for a supermarket, which they ignored.

By the account of the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, King Kullen was the first supermarket. It met the museum's five tests: separate departments, self-service, low prices, many stores under one name and buying goods in huge amounts. Cullen's slogan, a short phrase repeated in advertising, was "Pile it high. Sell it low."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clarence-saunders" name="Clarence Saunders" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clarence Saunders

> **Who:** The Memphis grocer who started self-service shopping and then lost his company fighting traders on Wall Street.
> **When and where:** Memphis, Tennessee, 1916 to 1923, and later stores. He died in 1953.

**Orders for 196,000 of the 200,000 shares, and he still lost.**

Clarence Saunders built Piggly Wiggly into a chain of 1,268 stores and then lost it in a fight over its stock. By 1923 the stores sold $100 million worth of groceries a year, and Piggly Wiggly was the third-largest grocery business in the country. Saunders had put the company's stock up for sale on the New York Stock Exchange, a market where people buy and sell shares, small pieces of ownership in a company.

Writers on the history of money and business describe the fight this way. In early 1923, some Piggly Wiggly stores in New York that other owners ran under the Piggly Wiggly name failed. Traders then began selling Piggly Wiggly stock short. Selling short is a bet that a stock's price will fall. The trader sells shares he has borrowed, and later he must buy the same number of shares to give back. If the price has fallen by then, he buys them for less than he sold them for.

Saunders fought back. He borrowed about $10 million from bankers in the South, added his own money and kept buying shares. In the end he held orders for 196,000 of the 200,000 shares that existed.

**Why he still lost.** Many of the shares Saunders bought came from the short sellers, who now had to hand them over. Saunders held almost every share, so if they had to buy from him, he could name the price.

Then the men who ran the Stock Exchange stopped trading in the stock. They gave the short sellers five days to hand over the shares, instead of the usual one day. In those five days, shares poured in from distant places, and the short sellers used them to pay what they owed.

Saunders still owed the bankers, and a payment of $2.5 million on his loan was due on September 1, 1923. He did not have the money. In August he gave up the company and handed what he owned to the people he owed. He was bankrupt, which means he could not pay his debts.

He started again with a new chain called the "Clarence Saunders, Sole Owner of My Name" stores. They did well until the 1930s, when they failed during the Great Depression, the years of hard times that began in 1929. Later he built the Keedoozle, a grocery store that worked like a vending machine. It never made a profit. Saunders died on October 15, 1953.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarence-saunders" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The company store" -->
Many coal companies in Appalachia owned the store in their mining towns and paid their miners mostly in scrip, the company's own money, made as metal coins or paper. A miner could draw scrip against pay he had already earned. The company store accepted it at full value, but a miner could not turn it in for cash.

**Scrip at other stores.** Other merchants nearby took scrip only at a discount of 10 to 30 percent. A miner who spent $1 of scrip at another store got only 70 to 90 cents' worth of goods.

Researchers for the US Coal Commission, a government group that studied the coal industry, checked prices in coal towns in 1922. Food cost 4.2 percent more at company stores than at independent stores in southern West Virginia, and 7 percent more in Alabama. Later, Price Fishback, an economist who studies how money, work and business fit together, looked at these numbers. He found that at most companies a miner's store debt ran no longer than two weeks.

**Two views.** A page by the West Virginia Mine Wars Museum describes scrip differently. It describes the system as debt bondage, being forced to keep working to pay off a debt. In its words, scrip "created a kind of debt bondage that kept coal miners and their families tied to their employer for almost everything they needed to survive." Fishback found small price gaps and short debts.

West Virginia lawmakers passed a law in 1891 under which companies could give out scrip only as pay for work already done or as an advance on wages. In 1925 they made it against the law to pass scrip from one person to another. Scrip disappeared when the coal industry collapsed in the 1950s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The catalog under Jim Crow" -->
Jim Crow is the name for the laws and customs in the South under which white lawmakers and officials kept Black people apart from white people in schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, courtrooms and cemeteries, and at water fountains. White mobs enforced it with murder. Between 1877 and 1950, white mobs lynched more than 4,400 Black people, killing them without any trial, by the count of the Equal Justice Initiative.

**Hyman's argument.** Louis Hyman, a historian at Cornell University, argued in 2018 that mail-order catalogs helped Black farm families in the South get around Jim Crow at the store. NPR, the *Washington Post* and America's Black Holocaust Museum reported his argument.

According to Hyman, many Black farm families had to buy on credit at a white-owned country store, often owned by the landlord whose land they farmed. The storekeeper set the prices and made Black customers wait until white customers were served. A catalog order cost the same national price for everyone, and no clerk decided who was served first. Instead of the landlord's credit, the family could use the mail-order company's.

Hyman says that some Southern storekeepers held bonfires to burn catalogs, and told white customers that Sears was owned by Black people to keep them from buying there. These are Hyman's claims.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Woolworth's lunch counter, 1960" -->
Woolworth's stores had lunch counters, where customers sat and ate. In the South, the people who ran these counters served white customers only, and the rule was still in force in 1960. On February 1 of that year, four Black students from North Carolina A&T, a college in Greensboro, North Carolina, sat down at the Woolworth counter there and asked to be served. They were Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond. The counter staff refused to serve them.

Hundreds of people joined their protest over the next six months. On July 25, 1960, the staff of the Greensboro counter began serving Black customers. By the account of Geneva Tisdale, who worked in the store's kitchen, the manager of the lunch counter picked her and two other Black kitchen workers to be the first. He told them to get dressed, walk around the store like customers and then sit at the counter. Tisdale ordered an egg-salad sandwich. Chapter 25, Rights Movements, tells the sit-ins in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on time" -->
Americans began buying cars, radios, furniture and household machines on installments in the 1920s, paying a little at a time, as buyers of sewing machines had since the 1850s. By the end of the 1920s, about 60 percent of cars and about 80 percent of radios were bought on installments, according to the reference site Encyclopedia.com. The number of cars registered in the United States grew from about 9 million in 1920 to nearly 27 million by 1929, nearly three times as many.

Members of the National Association of Finance Companies, a group of lending companies, agreed on standard terms for car buyers in 1924. A buyer paid one-third of the price at the start for a new car and two-fifths for a used car. The buyer then had no more than twelve months to pay the rest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising on radio and television" -->
The first paid radio advertisement went on the air on August 28, 1922, on the station WEAF in New York. The Queensboro Corporation paid $50 for about ten minutes of air time to promote the Hawthorne Court apartments in Jackson Heights, Queens.

No television station could sell advertising until officials of the Federal Communications Commission, the government office that licenses stations, gave licenses to 10 stations on May 2, 1941. Under those licenses, the stations could sell advertising from July 1, 1941. Two years earlier, people at NBC had shown test advertisements for three companies.

**Nine dollars for ten seconds.** The first commercial under the new licenses ran on WNBT in New York on July 1, 1941, before a Brooklyn Dodgers baseball game against the Philadelphia Phillies. For about ten seconds it showed the face of a Bulova watch over a map of the United States, while a voice said, "America runs on Bulova time." It cost Bulova $9: $4 for the air time and $5 in station charges. There were about 4,000 television sets in the New York area at the time.

Mary Frances Gerety wrote advertisements at N. W. Ayer & Son, the advertising agency. For the diamond company De Beers, she wrote the slogan "A Diamond Is Forever" in the late 1940s. In 1999 the editors of the magazine *Ad Age* named it the most memorable slogan of the 1900s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shopping under rationing" -->
Officials of the US government's Office of Price Administration rationed many goods during the Second World War, limiting how much of each one a person could buy. Sugar, meat, cheese, fats, shoes, gasoline and processed foods, foods that were canned, dried or frozen, were among them. At the start, each person could buy 26 pounds of sugar a year, about 8 ounces a week. Every person in the country, babies included, got a ration book full of stamps.

War Ration Book Two came into use on March 1, 1943. Its blue stamps bought processed foods, and its red stamps bought meat, cheese, fats and oils. Each stamp was worth a number of points, such as 8, 5, 2 or 1, and every food had a price in points as well as in money. In a cartoon the US government made to teach the system, a shopper's string beans cost 3 blue points.

Each person got 48 blue points and 64 red points a month, so a family of four had 192 blue points a month for processed food. Every cut of almost every kind of meat had its own point value.

At the store a shopper paid with stamps and with money together. The price could not go above the ceiling price, the highest price government officials allowed. Point values changed often. Newspaper editors printed them, radio announcers read them out and members of local ration boards posted them. By the end of the war, more than 100,000 volunteers were running the program through about 5,600 local boards.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilia-lundberg" name="Emilia Ruth Palmquist Lundberg" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emilia Ruth Palmquist Lundberg

> **Who:** A widowed mother of six in Jersey City who shopped for her family with ration books during the Second World War.
> **When and where:** Jersey City, New Jersey, about 1942 to 1943.

**Six children, $100 a month and a ration book for each.**

Emilia Lundberg, who was born in Göteborg, Sweden, in 1894, fed six children on a small, fixed income, and during the war she had to count ration stamps as well. In 1930 her husband was killed in an explosion at the dry-cleaning plant where he was in charge. As her son Donald remembered it, his father left her with six children, no relatives to turn to and no money.

She raised the children on workmen's compensation, money paid to a worker's family after the worker is hurt or killed on the job, of $100 a month. The rent was $35 a month. Her oldest daughter, 16, moved to night school and took a job keeping accounts for $12 a week. Donald packed and delivered groceries at a store after school and on Saturdays for $5 a week.

**At the store.** Rationing changed little at the family's table. Before the war, they ate a roast on Sunday after church and potatoes and macaroni the rest of the week. Under rationing, they still bought meat only for Sunday and ate macaroni, spaghetti and mashed potatoes the rest of the week.

Everyone in the family had a ration book with stamps for meat and other goods, and they took the books to the store and bought what the stamps allowed. The ration was counted for each person, so a large family got more in all than a small one. Shoes were rationed too. Each child had one good pair for school and one pair of sneakers for play.

Emilia had a 1933 Plymouth car. Donald remembered the family's gasoline ration as about a gallon and a half, but he did not say whether that was for a week or a month. When they told the ration board they were driving to visit his older brother, a soldier at Camp Kilmer in New Jersey, they got a coupon for about two more gallons. Donald described all of this in an interview for the Rutgers Oral History Archives in 2008, about 65 years later.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilia-lundberg" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 10 minutes**

Americans began shopping at enclosed malls and discount stores after 1950, and paying with plastic cards. The first fully enclosed shopping mall in the United States opened in Minnesota in 1956. Kmart, Target and Wal-Mart all opened their first stores in 1962. In 1974 a cashier in Troy, Ohio, made the first sale with a scanner that read a printed code on a package. By 2000, people could shop at home on television shopping channels, and from 1995 on a computer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The enclosed mall" -->
Southdale Center opened in Edina, Minnesota, on October 8, 1956. It was the first fully enclosed shopping mall in the United States with its own heating and cooling. It had 72 stores on two levels around a brightly lit central court. Its two biggest stores were the department stores Dayton's and Donaldson's.

The Dayton family owned department stores in Minneapolis. In 1952 they hired the architect Victor Gruen and his firm, Victor Gruen & Associates, to design the mall. Building began in 1954. Southdale cost $20 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="victor-gruen" name="Victor Gruen" movie="The Gruen Effect (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Victor Gruen

> **Who:** The architect who designed the first enclosed American mall and later rejected what developers built from his idea.
> **When and where:** Vienna, then New York and Minnesota. Southdale opened October 8, 1956.
> **Movie:** *The Gruen Effect: Victor Gruen and the Shopping Mall* (2009), a documentary by Anette Baldauf and Katharina Weingartner, about Gruen and the malls he started.

**He arrived in New York with eight dollars and no English.**

Victor Gruen designed Southdale as the center of a new neighborhood, and developers copied only the mall. Gruen was a Jewish architect from Vienna, in Austria.

**Leaving Vienna.** In March 1938 the leaders of Nazi Germany, the Germany ruled by Adolf Hitler's Nazi Party, took over Austria and made it part of Germany, a takeover called the Anschluss. In the weeks after, Austrian Nazis and others beat, attacked and humiliated Jews. In Vienna, Nazis forced Jews to scrub the city's streets while crowds jeered at them. Soon Nazi officials put Austria's Jews under the same laws that German officials already used to take away Jewish rights in Germany.

Gruen fled Vienna that year. He reached New York on the ship *Statendam* with an architect's degree, eight dollars and no English. One of his early jobs was a shop front on Fifth Avenue in New York, for Ludwig Lederer's leather-goods shop. Gruen designed its entrance as an arcade, a covered walkway, meant as a "customer trap" to draw people inside.

For Southdale, the Dayton company bought about 500 acres of land in Edina. Gruen's plan for the land around the mall had apartment buildings, houses, schools, a medical center, a park and a lake. He said he wanted "the Minneapolis downtown you would get if you started over and corrected all the mistakes." The medical center opened in 1965. The Daytons sold the rest of the land for housing.

In a speech in London in 1978, Gruen rejected the malls that American developers had built. He said he refused to pay alimony, the money a person pays a former husband or wife after a divorce, for those developments, and he used a rude word to describe them. He went back to Vienna and objected to a shopping center built there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-gruen" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic at the counter" -->
With a credit card, a shopper could take goods home from any store that took the card and pay the bank later. Chapter 17, Money, describes how the cards began: the cardboard Diners Club card of 1950, which holders had to pay off in full every month, and the BankAmericards that officers of Bank of America mailed to people in Fresno, California, in September 1958.

**At the counter.** The Fresno cards came ready to use, though nobody had asked for them. Each of the 60,000 or so cardholders got $300 to $500 of credit at once, good in any store that took the card, and each store that took it paid the bank 6 percent of every sale. The bank's officers later licensed the card across the country, and in 1976 it was renamed Visa. In 1970 members of Congress banned mailing credit cards to people who had not asked for them.

**Women and credit.** Before 1974, lenders often turned women down for credit because they were women, or because they were married or single. Mortgage lenders, who lend money to buy houses, often counted only part of a married woman's pay, especially if she was young enough to have children. Lawmakers in Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in October 1974. Under it, lenders could no longer treat people differently because of their sex or whether they were married.

In March 1976 the lawmakers added more reasons lenders could not use: race, color, religion, national origin, which means the country a person or their family came from, age, and getting money from government aid programs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Four discount chains in one year" -->
Four discount chains, selling goods at prices below what other stores charged, began in 1962. The first Kmart store opened in Garden City, Michigan, on March 1, 1962, and reports stated that 4,000 customers waited in line on the first day. Target's first store followed on May 1, in Roseville, Minnesota. On July 2, Sam Walton opened the first Wal-Mart in Rogers, Arkansas. Woolco's first store opened that year too.

Kmart grew out of the S. S. Kresge chain of five-and-dime stores, which sold small goods for 5 and 10 cents, and Woolco out of F. W. Woolworth's company. Target grew out of the Dayton Company's department stores, run by the same Dayton family that had hired Victor Gruen to design Southdale.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sam-walton" name="Sam Walton" movie="Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sam Walton

> **Who:** The Arkansas storekeeper who put discount stores in small towns and built the largest retailer in the United States.
> **When and where:** Rogers, Arkansas, from July 2, 1962.
> **Movie:** *Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price* (2005), a documentary by Robert Greenwald. It argues against the company and is not a life story of Walton.

**He put discount stores in towns too small for the big chains.**

Sam Walton built Walmart, spelled Wal-Mart at first, by opening discount stores in small towns. The heads of the large chains had judged those towns too small to bother with. Before 1962 Walton ran Ben Franklin variety stores in Arkansas, which sold many kinds of small, cheap goods. He bought goods in huge amounts and priced them below the stores nearby. Walmart became the largest retailer in the United States and the largest private employer, which means it had more workers than any other company.

Economists have measured what happened to shoppers and to towns when Walmart stores opened. Two economists, one at MIT and one at the US Department of Agriculture, reported in 2007 that shoppers gained a great deal from Walmart's low prices, most of all households with lower incomes.

**Jobs and sales.** Three other economists reported in 2008 that when a Walmart opened, the county around it lost about 150 retail jobs, or 2.7 percent. By their count, each Walmart worker replaced about 1.4 workers at other stores.

**Small towns.** Kenneth Stone, an economist at Iowa State University, has studied Walmart in Iowa since 1988. In a 2012 study, he and a co-author followed 28 Iowa towns of 3,000 to 20,000 people for 15 years after each got a Walmart. Total retail sales in those towns rose, but most of the gain went to Walmart, and other stores in town that sold the same things lost sales.

None of the study's Walmart towns had fewer than 3,000 people. In Iowa's smallest towns, under 2,500 people, retail sales fell 30 percent, about $1.5 billion, in the years after Walmart came to the state. In those years, shopping in Iowa moved to the bigger cities and to the Walmart towns, where people from nearby towns now came to shop.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sam-walton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The franchise" -->
Roy Allen bought a recipe for root beer in 1919. In 1924, by most accounts, he began selling A&W franchises: the right for other people to run a business under the A&W name, following his rules. Writers on the history of franchising often call A&W the first successful food franchise. J. Willard Marriott was one of its early franchise owners.

The Howard Johnson's restaurant business sold its first franchise to Reginald Sprague in 1935. By the end of 1936 there were 41 Howard Johnson's restaurants. By 1939 there were 107, with about $10.5 million in sales.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The barcode" -->
The Universal Product Code, or UPC, is the barcode printed on packages in stores, read by a scanner at the checkout.

The people who planned the first scan chose a small pack of gum on purpose. They wanted to show that the code could be printed clearly on a package that size. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History keeps one of the first ten scanners, a Spectra-Physics Model A.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sharon-buchanan" name="Sharon Buchanan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sharon Buchanan

> **Who:** The supermarket cashier who made the first sale ever scanned with a barcode.
> **When and where:** Marsh Supermarket, Troy, Ohio, 8:01 a.m., June 26, 1974.

**A ten-pack of Juicy Fruit gum, 67 cents, at 8:01 in the morning.**

Sharon Buchanan was the cashier at the first sale ever made with a barcode scanner. At 8:01 in the morning on June 26, 1974, she was working at the Marsh Supermarket in Troy, Ohio. The customer was Clyde Dawson, who headed research and development, the work of trying out new ideas, at Marsh. He bought a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum. Buchanan passed it over the scanner. The price was 67 cents.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sharon-buchanan" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stamps, television selling and the end of the big catalog" -->
**Green Stamps.** About 80 percent of American households collected S&H Green Stamps in the 1960s and 1970s. These trading stamps were small stamps that shoppers got with purchases at grocery stores, gas stations and other stores. Shoppers pasted them into books and traded full books for goods at a Green Stamps store or from a catalog. In the 1960s more S&H stamps were printed than US postage stamps, and about 35 million S&H catalogs went out each year.

**Shopping on television.** The Home Shopping Network began with a radio station. In 1977 a company that advertised on Lowell "Bud" Paxson's radio station paid him in electric can openers instead of money. The station's host, Bob Circosta, sold them on the air for $9.95 each. In 1982 Paxson and Roy Speer started the Home Shopping Club on cable television in Pinellas County, Florida, and it went national on July 1, 1985.

Joe Segel founded QVC, another shopping channel, in 1986. It first went on the air that November, and the first thing it sold was a radio made to be used in the shower.

**The last Sears catalog.** Sears officials announced on January 25, 1993, that they were closing the company's catalog business, cutting 50,000 jobs and closing 113 stores. The last general catalog went out that spring. The catalog had run for about a hundred years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on a computer begins" -->
Jeff Bezos filed papers to start a company called Cadabra, Inc., on July 5, 1994, working from a rented house with a garage in Bellevue, Washington. The company became Amazon. Amazon.com opened for orders on July 16, 1995. The first book it sold was *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies*, by Douglas Hofstadter. In its first week it took in about $12,000 in book orders.

Pierre Omidyar started a website called AuctionWeb, where people sold goods at auction to whoever offered the most money. He started it over Labor Day weekend, on September 3, 1995. It later became eBay. The first thing sold on it went for $14.83. By eBay's account, it was a broken laser pointer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 12 minutes**

About one dollar in six of all American retail spending went to online sales from April to June 2026, by the count of the US Census Bureau. Since 2005, members of Amazon Prime have paid a fee for two-day shipping. Drivers carry the orders to people's doors, including on days of summer heat. Sellers now buy the right to show advertisements to one person at a time, chosen from records of what that person has done online.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much shopping is online?" -->
Online sales were 17.1 percent of all retail sales in the United States from April through June 2026, by the count that workers at the US Census Bureau publish every three months. In all of 2025 they were 16.4 percent. The April-to-June figure came to $340.2 billion, 12.2 percent more than in the same months of 2025. The bureau's staff released those numbers on August 18, 2026. By those numbers, more than four dollars in five were still spent in stores.

Researchers at the Federal Reserve, which acts as a bank for other banks, ask people to keep diaries of how they pay. In 2024, credit cards were the most-used way to pay in the United States, used for 35 percent of all payments. Cash was used for 14 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeff-bezos" name="Jeff Bezos" movie="Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos (Frontline, 2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jeff Bezos

> **Who:** The founder of Amazon, which began as an online bookstore and grew into a store selling books, music, movies and many other kinds of goods.
> **When and where:** Bellevue and Seattle, Washington, from July 1994.
> **Movie:** *Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos* (2020), a documentary by James Jacoby and Anya Bourg in the PBS series *Frontline*. It is an investigation that argues a case against Amazon's power.

**Web use was growing 2,300 percent a year, so he quit.**

Jeff Bezos was a vice president at D. E. Shaw, an investment company in New York, when he read in 1994 that use of the web had been growing by 2,300 percent a year that spring. He quit his job to sell goods online.

He and his wife, MacKenzie Bezos, moved from Manhattan to the Seattle area. They chose it because software engineers lived and worked there, at Microsoft and the University of Washington, and because of its taxes. Bezos studied which product would sell best online, and he chose books. Music and DVDs were the next goods Amazon added.

**Prime.** When Amazon Prime began on February 2, 2005, members paid $79 a year and got two-day shipping on their orders at no extra charge, according to Amazon's announcement. By 2021, more than 200 million people around the world were members. In 2026 Prime in the United States cost $139 for members who paid once a year, or $14.99 a month for members who paid monthly, about $180 over a year. Those prices had not changed since February 2022, according to the shopping news site dealnews.

**The workers.** Senate committee staff, drivers and union representatives have complained about how Amazon treats the people who pack and deliver its orders. A Senate committee's staff counted more injuries in Amazon's warehouses than in other warehouses. In 2023 Amazon ended its contract with one delivery company soon after the company's drivers joined a union, a group that workers form to bargain together with their employer. Amazon's representatives said the contract ended for other reasons.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Malls empty out" -->
As shoppers moved online or to newer outdoor shopping centers, the anchor department stores of many malls closed. Anchor stores are the big stores that draw shoppers to the smaller shops around them, and the smaller shops that had depended on the department stores' crowds lost customers. Mall owners could not find new stores to fill the empty space. Owners and builders have turned some empty malls into apartments, clinics, colleges and warehouses.

**How empty.** Nobody keeps an official government count of malls, and the private counts disagree, because people define a mall in different ways. In 2017, Green Street Advisors, a company that grades malls for investors, sorted them by how well they were doing. The more than 300 malls it gave its lowest grades were only about 72 percent full, while the best malls were about 95 percent full. In the weakest malls, more than a quarter of the space stood empty.

Across the whole country, researchers at Coresight Research, a company that studies stores, counted about 3,000 more stores closing than opening in 2025.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising aimed at one person" -->
When a person visits a website, the company that runs the site can save small files and codes on the person's device that record what the person looked at. Companies called data brokers collect records like these and combine them with records from outside the internet, such as store purchases, warranty cards and property records. Then they sell what they have gathered, including the chance to show an advertisement to a chosen group of people.

The staff of the Federal Trade Commission, the government office that protects shoppers, published a report in May 2014 called *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability*. The commission's staff found that data brokers held "billions of data elements," meaning billions of separate facts, about nearly every American shopper. They gathered most of it without people knowing.

The report describes how brokers attached offline records to the files in a person's web browser, so the person could be followed from site to site and shown advertisements chosen for them. The commissioners asked Congress to pass a law under which people could see the information held about them.

Website and app owners use money from this advertising to keep their services free to use, and the advertisements can show a person something they want to buy. But companies the person has never heard of buy and sell records of what the person reads and buys. As of 2026, members of Congress had passed no general federal privacy law. By June 29, 2026, lawmakers in 24 states had passed their own, according to the law firm Husch Blackwell.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling to children inside a game" -->
Fortnite is a video game made by Epic Games that came out in 2017. It is free to play. Epic sells V-Bucks, game money that players buy with real money to get costumes and dance moves for their characters. By 2022, the game had more than 400 million players around the world, according to the Federal Trade Commission.

**The $520 million.** Officials of the Federal Trade Commission announced on December 19, 2022, that Epic had agreed to pay $520 million. Of that, $275 million was a penalty under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, a law that requires a company to get a parent's permission before it collects personal information from a child under 13. The officials said Epic had collected personal information from children under 13 who played Fortnite without telling their parents or getting their permission.

The other $245 million went back to customers as refunds. The officials said Epic had used "dark patterns," confusing buttons and screens that led players into charges they did not mean to make. They said children could buy V-Bucks just by pressing buttons, with no parent or card owner agreeing, and that the game could charge a player who was only waking it from sleep mode.

The officials cited "hundreds of millions of dollars in unauthorized charges," meaning charges that players had not agreed to. Lina Khan was the head of the commission at the time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Payday loans" -->
A payday loan is a small loan from a storefront lender that the borrower is supposed to pay back on the next payday. Researchers at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a government office that watches over lenders, published a study of these loans in April 2013. They found that a borrower who took the typical $350 loan, with a fee of $15 for each $100, had to find more than $400 in 14 days.

Nearly half of payday borrowers took out more than 10 loans in a year, and the typical borrower was in debt 199 days of the year, more than half of it. Chapter 17, Money, describes the rest of the study, including how a fee like that comes to 391 percent a year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying secondhand clothes" -->
The staff of ThredUp, a company that sells used clothes online, publish a yearly report on the market for secondhand clothes. Because ThredUp sells used clothes, its report is a company's estimate, not a government count.

By ThredUp's 2025 report, the market for used clothes in the United States grew 14 percent in 2024, and online sales of used clothes grew 23 percent. In 2024, 58 percent of shoppers bought something secondhand, up from 52 percent in 2023. The firm GlobalData did the research for ThredUp's 2026 report, which states that in 2025 used clothing sales grew almost four times as fast as all clothing sales. GlobalData's researchers forecast that the US secondhand market will reach $78.8 billion by 2030.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last step: heat in the delivery truck" -->
The drivers who make fast delivery possible keep delivering in summer heat. Injury records kept by OSHA, the government's workplace safety office, list at least 40 UPS drivers taken to the hospital since 2015 for heat illness, which happens when the body gets too hot and cannot cool itself down. Danielle Kaye of NPR gave that count in July 2023.

José Cruz Rodriguez was 23. He died of heat illness during a UPS delivery shift in Waco, Texas, in August 2021, according to OSHA. His family sued UPS for causing his death, and the case was settled.

Esteban Chavez Jr., a UPS driver for four years, died after he was found unconscious in his truck. It was June 25, 2022, the day after his 24th birthday, and he was on his route in Pasadena, California. The temperature that day reached the upper 90s. UPS trucks had no air conditioning then. His family said at least 20 minutes went by before anyone noticed him and called for help.

The medical examiner, the doctor whose job is to find out why a person died, found that his heart stopped working. His family believes the heat caused it.

UPS managers and leaders of the Teamsters union, which represents UPS drivers, agreed in June 2023 that every small delivery truck UPS bought after January 1, 2024, would have air conditioning, heat shields and fans.

**Inside the warehouses.** Warehouse workers pack the orders before drivers carry them. In December 2024, Senator Bernie Sanders released a report by staff of the Senate committee he led, staff members who work for the party that holds the most seats in the Senate. They found that in 2023 Amazon recorded more than 30 percent more injuries at its warehouses than the average for the warehouse industry. For seven years in a row, Amazon warehouse workers were nearly twice as likely to be hurt as workers at other warehouses.

The report states that Amazon had plans to lower injuries and did not use them, because the plans would slow down work.

### Renica Turner

> **Who:** A driver who delivered Amazon packages in the California desert for a small delivery company, and who joined a union to push for protection from the heat.
> **When and where:** Palmdale and Victorville, California, about 2022 to 2023.

**110 degrees, a broken air conditioner and 300 packages to go.**

Renica Turner delivered Amazon packages for a company called Battle Tested Strategies. It was one of about 3,000 small companies under contract to deliver Amazon's packages. Its vans were leased from Amazon, and its drivers worked out of an Amazon building in Palmdale, California.

**The hot day.** In a July 2023 story by the NPR reporter Danielle Kaye, Turner described a day in Victorville, California, when the temperature reached 110 degrees. The van's air conditioning and fans were not working, she said. When she rolled the windows down, hot air came in. Her body began to tingle, and she thought she might pass out.

She told the Amazon dispatcher, the worker who sends drivers out and keeps track of them, and the dispatcher gave her a 20-minute break. In her words, "They never sent no one out to help me with the rest of the route." The route was 300 packages, and she was expected to deliver 25 every hour. She finished it herself, feeling woozy and numb.

Amazon's representatives told NPR that its vans had working air conditioning and that any van without it was taken off the road at once. The delivery companies were in charge of fixing the vans, they said, and Amazon had changed some routes the year before so drivers could take more breaks to cool down. The owner of Battle Tested Strategies, Johnathon Ervin, said Amazon regularly failed to fix broken air conditioning, sometimes for weeks or months.

**The union.** Turner and 83 other drivers at the company joined the Teamsters union in April 2023 and bargained a contract with Battle Tested Strategies. NPR reported that they joined the union in large part to push for protection from the heat.

Amazon ended its contract with the company in June 2023. Teamsters representatives and the company's owner said Amazon was punishing the drivers for joining a union. Amazon's representatives said Amazon ended the contract over safety violations and missed insurance payments by the company. The drivers went on strike, stopping work as a protest.

**What the drivers got.** Lawyers for the National Labor Relations Board, the government office that enforces labor law, brought a case against Amazon. They argued that Amazon was the drivers' employer too, even though another company had hired them. In April 2026 they settled the case with Amazon, agreeing on terms that ended it. Turner and the other drivers, about 84 of them, were each to get two weeks of pay, about $250,000 in all.

Under the settlement, Amazon did not admit that it had been the drivers' employer. A judge approved the settlement in May 2026, and the Teamsters asked to appeal. The news stories about the case do not say what Turner did after the strike.

**The three questions, answered**
- How did colonists pay for goods when they had almost no coins? They took goods home on credit and paid later in crops, other goods, work and wampum, while the shopkeeper wrote each debt in a ledger.
- Where were people bought and sold like goods? At the market New York's aldermen set up on Wall Street in 1711, in newspaper advertisements such as the 277 or more in Franklin's *Pennsylvania Gazette*, and at a racetrack near Savannah, where Pierce Butler sold between 429 and 436 people in 1859.
- How much of what Americans buy today is bought online? About one dollar in six: online sales were 17.1 percent of all retail sales from April to June 2026, by the US Census Bureau's count.

**What changed:** Before 1500, goods passed hand to hand at trading places like the one on the Columbia River, and by 2026 one dollar in six of American retail spending went to online orders carried to the door.

**Five dates to keep**
- 1653: John Stewart owes John Pynchon 30 pounds for his freedom.
- 1711: New York's aldermen set up a market for selling enslaved people.
- 1872: Montgomery Ward mails his first catalog.
- 1916: The first Piggly Wiggly lets shoppers serve themselves.
- 1995: Amazon.com opens for orders.

**Not known:** No one counted how many children the morphine in Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup killed.

**Where to go:** A sign at Wall Street and Water Street, in New York City, marks where the city's market for enslaved people stood from 1711 to 1762.
