## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Chain stores spread to thousands of towns between 1900 and 1950, and by 1930 one grocery chain, the A&P, ran about 15,000 stores. Grocers began letting customers take goods off the shelves themselves. Families bought cars and radios on monthly payments, and sellers began paying radio and television stations to carry their advertisements into people's homes. In coal towns, miners were paid in company money that only the company's own store took at full value. During the Second World War, shoppers needed ration stamps from the government as well as money to buy many foods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The chain store at full size" -->
The A&P had 1,600 stores by 1915. By 1930 it had about 15,000 stores and sold $2.9 billion worth of goods a year. That made it the largest retailer in the world. A retailer is a seller who sells goods to the people who use them. The A&P was the largest grocery seller in the United States from 1915 to 1975.

Woolworth's stores had lunch counters, where customers sat on stools and ate. In the South, the people who ran these counters served white customers only. Later, on February 1, 1960, four Black college students sat down at the Woolworth counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and asked to be served. They were Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond. The counter staff refused to serve them. Hundreds of people joined their protest, and the counter was opened to Black customers on July 25, 1960.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-woolworth" name="F. W. Woolworth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### F. W. Woolworth

> **Who:** The storekeeper who built a national chain of five-cent and ten-cent stores and paid cash for the tallest building in the world.
> **When and where:** Utica and Lancaster, 1879, and New York, 1911 to 1913.

Frank Winfield Woolworth turned his five-cent stores of 1879 into the largest store company in the world. On November 12, 1911, he signed agreements to join his chain with four rival chains. They were the Knox, Kirby, Charlton and C. S. Woolworth chains. The new F. W. Woolworth Company started with 596 stores and $65 million in capital, which is the money put into a business to run it. Some sources give the start as 1912, the year the new company was formally set up.

In 1913 the Woolworth Building was finished in New York. At 792 feet, it was the tallest building in the world at the time. It cost $13.5 million, and Woolworth paid for it himself, in cash.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-woolworth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Self-service" -->
In a self-service store, customers pick goods off the shelves themselves instead of asking a clerk to fetch them. Clarence Saunders opened the first Piggly Wiggly store at 79 Jefferson Street in Memphis, Tennessee, on September 6, 1916. Some sources give the date as September 11. A turnstile let customers in one at a time. A single path, fenced on both sides, led them past every shelf in the store before they reached the place where they paid.

Saunders asked the government for a patent on his "Self-Serving Store" on October 21, 1916. A patent is a government paper that lets only the inventor use an invention for a number of years. He received Patent 1,242,872 on October 9, 1917.

King Kullen opened in Jamaica, in Queens, New York, on August 4, 1930. Michael J. Cullen started it. He had worked for the Kroger grocery company and had sent its managers a plan for a supermarket, which they ignored. The Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, calls King Kullen the first supermarket. By the museum's account, King Kullen met five tests: separate departments, self-service, low prices, many stores under one name and buying goods in huge amounts. Cullen's slogan was "Pile it high. Sell it low." Piggly Wiggly started self-service, and King Kullen was the first supermarket by the museum's tests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clarence-saunders" name="Clarence Saunders" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clarence Saunders

> **Who:** The Memphis grocer who started self-service shopping and then lost his company fighting traders on Wall Street.
> **When and where:** Memphis, Tennessee, 1916 to 1923, and later stores until his death in 1953.

Clarence Saunders built Piggly Wiggly into a chain of 1,268 stores and then lost it in a fight over its stock. By 1923 the stores sold $100 million worth of groceries a year. It was the third-largest grocery business in the country. Saunders had put the company's stock up for sale on the New York Stock Exchange. A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company that people can buy and sell.

In early 1923, some Piggly Wiggly stores in New York that other owners ran under the Piggly Wiggly name failed. Traders then began selling Piggly Wiggly stock short. Selling short is a bet that a stock's price will fall. The trader sells borrowed shares and plans to buy them back later at a lower price.

Saunders fought back. He borrowed about $10 million from bankers in the South, added his own money and kept buying shares. In the end he held orders for 196,000 of the 200,000 shares that existed. Then the men who ran the Stock Exchange stopped trading in the stock and gave the short sellers more time to pay. Within months Saunders was bankrupt, which means he could not pay his debts, and he was out of the company.

He started again with a new chain called the "Clarence Saunders, Sole Owner of My Name" stores. They did well until the 1930s, when they failed during the Great Depression. Later he built the Keedoozle, a grocery store that worked like a vending machine. It never made a profit. Saunders died on October 15, 1953.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarence-saunders" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The company store" -->
Many coal companies in Appalachia owned the store in their mining towns and paid their miners mostly in scrip instead of dollars. Scrip was the company's own money, made as metal coins or paper. A miner could draw scrip against pay he had already earned. The company store took it at full value, but the company would not trade it for cash. Other merchants nearby took scrip only at a discount of 10 to 30 percent. That meant a miner who spent $1 of scrip at another store got only 70 to 90 cents' worth of goods.

In 1922 the US Coal Commission surveyed prices in coal towns. In southern West Virginia, company stores charged 4.2 percent more for food than independent stores. In Alabama they charged 7 percent more. The economist Price Fishback later studied these numbers. He found that most companies did not let a miner's store debt run longer than two weeks.

The West Virginia Mine Wars Museum describes scrip differently. It says scrip "created a kind of debt bondage that kept coal miners and their families tied to their employer for almost everything they needed to survive." Debt bondage means being forced to keep working to pay off a debt. The price studies and the museum's account disagree about how hard the system was on miners.

West Virginia lawmakers limited scrip in 1891. In 1925 they passed a law that kept scrip from being passed from one person to another. Scrip disappeared when the coal industry collapsed in the 1950s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The catalog under Jim Crow" -->
Jim Crow is the name for the laws and customs that kept Black and white people apart in the South and treated Black people unequally. Louis Hyman, a historian at Cornell University, argued in 2018 that mail-order catalogs helped Black farm families in the South get around Jim Crow at the store. His argument was reported by NPR, the *Washington Post* and America's Black Holocaust Museum.

According to Hyman, many Black farm families had to buy on credit at a white-owned country store, often owned by the landlord whose land they farmed. The storekeeper set the prices and made Black customers wait until white customers were served. A catalog order cost the same national price for everyone, and no clerk decided who was served first. The family could also buy on the mail-order company's credit instead of the landlord's.

Hyman also says that some Southern storekeepers held bonfires to burn catalogs, and told white customers that Sears was owned by Black people to keep them from buying there. These are Hyman's claims.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on time" -->
In the 1920s, buying on installments spread from sewing machines to cars, radios, record players, furniture and household machines. By the end of the 1920s, about 60 percent of cars and about 80 percent of radios were bought on installments, according to the reference site Encyclopedia.com. The number of cars registered in the United States grew from about 9 million in 1920 to nearly 27 million by 1929.

In 1924 members of the National Association of Finance Companies, a group of lending companies, adopted standard terms for car buyers. A buyer paid one-third of the price at the start for a new car and two-fifths for a used car. The buyer then had no more than twelve months to pay the rest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising comes into the house" -->
The first paid radio advertisement went on the air on August 28, 1922, on the station WEAF in New York. The Queensboro Corporation paid $50 for about ten minutes of air time to promote the Hawthorne Court apartments in Jackson Heights, Queens.

The first legal television commercial ran on the station WNBT in New York on July 1, 1941, before a Brooklyn Dodgers baseball game against the Philadelphia Phillies. For about ten seconds it showed the face of a Bulova watch over a map of the United States. It cost Bulova $9: $4 for the air time and $5 in station charges.

Mary Frances Gerety wrote advertisements at N. W. Ayer & Son, the advertising agency. For the diamond company De Beers, she wrote the slogan "A Diamond Is Forever." A slogan is a short phrase repeated in advertising. Sources date it to 1947 or to 1949. In 1999 the magazine *Ad Age* named it the most memorable slogan of the 1900s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shopping under rationing" -->
During the Second World War, officials of the federal Office of Price Administration ran rationing. To ration means to limit how much of something each person may buy. Sugar, meat, cheese, fats, canned and other processed foods, shoes and gasoline were among the rationed goods. At the start, each person could buy 26 pounds of sugar a year, about 8 ounces a week. Every person in the country, babies included, got a ration book full of stamps.

War Ration Book Two came into use on March 1, 1943. Its blue stamps bought processed foods, which are canned, dried or frozen foods. Its red stamps bought meat, cheese, fats and oils. Each person got 48 blue points and 64 red points a month, so a family of four had 192 blue points a month for processed food. Every cut of almost every kind of meat had its own point value.

At the store a shopper paid with stamps and with money together. The price could not go above the ceiling price, the highest price government officials allowed. Point values changed often. Newspapers and radio stations announced them, and local ration boards posted them. By the end of the war, more than 100,000 volunteers were running the program through about 5,600 local boards.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilia-lundberg" name="Emilia Ruth Palmquist Lundberg" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emilia Ruth Palmquist Lundberg

> **Who:** A widowed mother of six in Jersey City who shopped for her family with ration books during the Second World War.
> **When and where:** Jersey City, New Jersey, about 1942 to 1943.

Emilia Lundberg fed six children on a small, fixed income, and during the war she also had to count ration stamps. She was born in Göteborg, Sweden, in 1894. In 1930 her husband was killed in an explosion at the dry-cleaning plant where he was in charge. He left her with six children and no family or money to fall back on.

She raised the children on workmen's compensation of $100 a month. Workmen's compensation is money paid to a worker's family after the worker is hurt or killed on the job. The rent was $35 a month. Her oldest daughter, 16, moved to night school and took a job keeping accounts for $12 a week. Her son Donald packed and delivered groceries at a store after school and on Saturdays for $5 a week.

Before the war, the family ate a roast on Sunday after church and potatoes and macaroni the rest of the week. Under rationing, everyone in the family had a ration book with stamps for meat and other goods. They took the books to the store and bought what the stamps allowed. The ration was counted for each person, so a large family got more in all than a small one. They bought meat for Sunday and ate macaroni, spaghetti and mashed potatoes the rest of the week. Shoes were rationed too. Each child had one good pair for school and one pair of sneakers for play.

Emilia drove a 1933 Plymouth. Donald remembered the family's gasoline ration as about a gallon and a half. When they told the ration board they were driving to visit his older brother, a soldier at Camp Kilmer in New Jersey, they got a coupon for about two more gallons. Donald described all of this in an interview for the Rutgers Oral History Archives in 2008, about 65 years later.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilia-lundberg" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first fully enclosed shopping mall in the United States opened in Minnesota in 1956. Plastic cards issued by banks let shoppers buy on credit in any store that took the card. Kmart, Target and Wal-Mart all opened their first stores in 1962. By 1974 a scanner at the checkout could read a printed code on each package. The century ended with two new ways to shop at home, first through television and then, from 1995, through a computer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The enclosed mall" -->
A mall is a group of stores under one roof or around one walkway. Southdale Center opened in Edina, Minnesota, on October 8, 1956. It was the first fully enclosed shopping mall in the United States with its own heating and cooling. It had 72 stores on two levels around a brightly lit central court. Two department stores, Dayton's and Donaldson's, stood at its ends.

The Dayton family owned department stores in Minneapolis. In 1952 they hired the architect Victor Gruen and his firm, Victor Gruen & Associates, to design the mall. Building began in 1954. Southdale cost $20 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="victor-gruen" name="Victor Gruen" movie="The Gruen Effect (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Victor Gruen

> **Who:** The architect who designed the first enclosed American mall and later rejected what developers built from his idea.
> **When and where:** Vienna, then New York and Minnesota. Southdale opened October 8, 1956.
> **Movie:** *The Gruen Effect: Victor Gruen and the Shopping Mall* (2009), a documentary by Anette Baldauf and Katharina Weingartner, about Gruen and the malls he started.

Victor Gruen designed Southdale as the center of a whole new town, and developers copied only the mall. Gruen was a Jewish architect from Vienna, in Austria. In 1938 Austria became part of Nazi Germany in the German annexation called the Anschluss, and hatred of Jews was rising. Gruen left Vienna that year. He reached New York on the ship *Statendam* with an architect's degree, eight dollars and no English.

One of his early jobs was a shop front on Fifth Avenue in New York, for Ludwig Lederer's leather-goods shop. Gruen designed its entrance as an arcade, a covered walkway, meant as a "customer trap" to draw people inside.

For Southdale, the Dayton company bought about 500 acres of land in Edina. Gruen planned the mall as the center of a new neighborhood, with apartment buildings, houses, schools, a medical center, a park and a lake. He said he wanted "the Minneapolis downtown you would get if you started over and corrected all the mistakes." The medical center opened in 1965. The Daytons sold the rest of the land for housing.

In a speech in London in 1978, Gruen rejected the malls that American developers had built. He said he refused to pay alimony for those developments, and he used a rude word to describe them. Alimony is money that a person pays to a former husband or wife after a divorce. He meant that he would not take responsibility for them. He went back to Vienna, and there he objected to a shopping center built at the edge of the city.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-gruen" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic at the counter" -->
A credit card lets a person buy now and pay the bank or the card company later. Diners Club, started in February 1950, was the first charge card that many different businesses accepted. It was made of cardboard. The first place it was used was Major's Cabin Grill, a restaurant in New York. It was a charge card, which means cardholders had to pay the whole bill every month. The company's first publicity man, Matty Simmons, later admitted that the famous story of how the card began, with a founder who forgot his wallet, was made up for publicity.

In September 1958, officers of Bank of America mailed about 60,000 BankAmericards to people in Fresno, California, who had not asked for them. The cards were ready to use. Each cardholder got $300 to $500 of credit at once. Stores that took the card paid the bank 6 percent of each sale. The bank had expected about 4 percent of cardholders to fall behind on paying. About 22 percent did. The bank's officers later licensed the card across the country, and in 1976 it was renamed Visa.

In 1970, members of Congress passed a law that banned mailing credit cards to people who had not asked for them.

Before 1974, lenders could turn a woman down for credit because she was a woman, or because she was married or single. Mortgage lenders, who lend money to buy houses, often counted only part of a married woman's pay, especially if she was young enough to have children. They were also more likely to turn down single women. In October 1974 members of Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. It banned lenders from treating people differently because of their sex or whether they were married. In March 1976 members of Congress added race, color, religion, national origin, age and receiving public assistance to the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1962: the discount stores" -->
A discount store is a store that sells goods at prices below what other stores charge. Four discount chains began in 1962. The first Kmart store opened in Garden City, Michigan, on March 1, 1962. Reports said 4,000 customers waited in line on the first day. The first Target store opened in Roseville, Minnesota, on May 1. Sam Walton opened the first Wal-Mart in Rogers, Arkansas, on July 2. The first Woolco store also opened that year.

Kmart came from the S. S. Kresge chain of five-and-dime stores. Target came from the Dayton Company's department stores, the same family business that paid for Southdale. Woolco came from F. W. Woolworth's company.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sam-walton" name="Sam Walton" movie="Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sam Walton

> **Who:** The Arkansas storekeeper who put big discount stores in small towns and built the largest retailer in the United States.
> **When and where:** Rogers, Arkansas, from July 2, 1962.
> **Movie:** *Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price* (2005), a documentary by Robert Greenwald. It argues against the company and is not a life story of Walton.

Sam Walton built Walmart by opening big discount stores in small towns. The large chains had judged those towns too small to bother with. Before 1962 Walton ran Ben Franklin variety stores in Arkansas. He bought goods in huge amounts and priced them below the stores nearby. Walmart became the largest retailer in the United States and the largest private employer, which means it had more workers than any other company.

Economists have measured what Walmart did to shoppers and to towns. Jerry Hausman of MIT and Ephraim Leibtag of the US Department of Agriculture reported in 2007 that shoppers gained a great deal from Walmart's low prices, most of all households with lower incomes.

David Neumark, Junfu Zhang and Stephen Ciccarella reported in 2008 that when a Walmart opened, the county around it lost about 150 retail jobs, or 2.7 percent. By their count, each Walmart worker replaced about 1.4 workers at other stores. Kenneth Stone, an economist at Iowa State University, has studied Walmart in Iowa since 1988. In a 2012 study, Stone and a co-author found that retail sales in Iowa towns with fewer than 2,500 people fell 30 percent in the years after Walmart arrived. That was about $1.5 billion. Towns of 3,000 to 20,000 people that got a Walmart saw their total retail sales rise.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sam-walton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The franchise" -->
Franchising began before 1950 and spread after it. A franchise is a deal in which the owner of a business name and its way of working sells other people the right to run a business under that name, following the owner's rules. Roy Allen bought a recipe for root beer in 1919. He began selling A&W franchises in 1924, or in 1925 by some accounts. J. Willard Marriott was one of his early franchise owners.

Howard Johnson gave his first restaurant franchise to Reginald Sprague in 1935. By the end of 1936 there were 41 Howard Johnson's restaurants. By 1939 there were 107, taking in about $10.5 million a year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The barcode" -->
A barcode is a pattern of printed stripes that a scanner at the checkout can read. The Universal Product Code, or UPC, is the barcode printed on packages in stores.

The people who planned the first scan chose a small pack of gum on purpose. They wanted to show that the code could be printed clearly on a package that size. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History keeps one of the first ten scanners, a Spectra-Physics Model A.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sharon-buchanan" name="Sharon Buchanan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sharon Buchanan

> **Who:** The supermarket cashier who made the first sale ever scanned with a barcode.
> **When and where:** Marsh Supermarket, Troy, Ohio, 8:01 a.m., June 26, 1974.

Sharon Buchanan was the cashier at the first sale ever made with a barcode scanner. At 8:01 in the morning on June 26, 1974, she was working at the Marsh Supermarket in Troy, Ohio. The customer was Clyde Dawson, the head of research at Marsh. He bought a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum. Buchanan passed it over the scanner. The price was 67 cents.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sharon-buchanan" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stamps, television selling and the end of the big catalog" -->
Trading stamps were small stamps that grocers and gas stations gave out with each purchase. Shoppers pasted them into books and traded full books for goods from a catalog. In the 1960s and 1970s, about 80 percent of American households collected S&H Green Stamps. In the 1960s more S&H stamps were printed than US postage stamps, and about 35 million S&H catalogs went out each year.

The Home Shopping Network began with a radio station. In 1977 a company that advertised on Lowell "Bud" Paxson's radio station paid him in electric can openers instead of money. The station's host, Bob Circosta, sold them on the air for $9.95 each. In 1982 Paxson and Roy Speer started the Home Shopping Club on cable television in Pinellas County, Florida. It went national on July 1, 1985. Joe Segel founded QVC, another shopping channel, in 1986. It first went on the air that November, and the first thing it sold was a radio made to be used in the shower.

On January 25, 1993, the heads of Sears announced that they were closing the company's catalog business. They also announced 50,000 job cuts and the closing of 113 stores. The last general catalog went out that spring. News reports said the catalog was 97 years old. Other accounts say 101 or 105 years, because they count from different starting dates.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buying on a computer begins" -->
On July 5, 1994, Jeff Bezos filed papers to start a company called Cadabra, Inc. He worked from a rented house with a garage in Bellevue, Washington. The company became Amazon. Amazon.com opened for orders on July 16, 1995. The first book it sold was *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies*, by Douglas Hofstadter. In its first week it took in about $12,000 in book orders.

Pierre Omidyar started an auction website called AuctionWeb over Labor Day weekend, on September 3, 1995. It later became eBay. The first thing sold on it went for $14.83. According to eBay, it was a broken laser pointer. Some accounts say it was a broken printer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="marketplace" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, more and more buying has moved from stores to screens. From April to June 2026, about one dollar in six of all American retail spending went to online sales, by the count of the US Census Bureau. Orders arrive in a day or two, carried by drivers who work outdoors in all weather. Sellers now buy the right to show advertisements to one person at a time, chosen from records of what that person has done online.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big online shopping is" -->
Every three months, the US Census Bureau publishes a count of online sales. In 2025, online sales were 16.4 percent of all retail sales in the United States. In the second quarter of 2026, from April through June, they were 17.1 percent. That came to $340.2 billion in three months. It was 12.2 percent more than in the same months of 2025. The Census Bureau released those numbers on August 18, 2026. By those numbers, more than four dollars in five were still spent in stores.

The way people pay at the counter has changed too. Researchers at the Federal Reserve, the country's central bank, ask people to keep diaries of how they pay. In 2024, credit cards were used for 35 percent of all payments, and cash for 14 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeff-bezos" name="Jeff Bezos" movie="Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos (Frontline, 2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jeff Bezos

> **Who:** The founder of Amazon, which began as an online bookstore and grew into a store selling almost everything.
> **When and where:** Bellevue and Seattle, Washington, from July 1994.
> **Movie:** *Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos* (2020), a documentary by James Jacoby and Anya Bourg in the PBS series *Frontline*. It is an investigation that argues a case against Amazon's power.

Jeff Bezos left a job in New York in 1994 to sell books online. He was a vice president at D. E. Shaw, an investment company. In 1994 he read that use of the web had been growing by 2,300 percent a year that spring, and he quit. He and his wife, MacKenzie Bezos, moved from Manhattan to the Seattle area. They chose it because software engineers lived and worked there, at Microsoft and the University of Washington, and because of its taxes. Bezos studied which product would sell best online, and he chose books. Music and DVDs were the next goods Amazon added.

Amazon Prime began on February 2, 2005. According to Amazon's announcement, members paid $79 a year and got two-day shipping on their orders at no extra charge. By 2021, more than 200 million people around the world were members, according to Amazon. In 2026 a year of Prime in the United States cost $139, or $14.99 a month, the same as since February 2022, according to the shopping news site dealnews.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeff-bezos" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Malls empty out" -->
Nobody keeps an official government count of malls, and the private counts disagree, because people define a mall in different ways. One industry count gives about 700 enclosed malls in 2023. A broader count, which includes open-air shopping centers, gives about 1,150 for 2024.

Store closings are counted more carefully. Researchers at Coresight Research, a company that studies stores, counted 7,325 store closings in the United States in 2024 and later raised that count to 8,825. They had predicted about 15,000 closings for 2025. Their count for 2025, published in January 2026, was 8,270 closings and 5,270 openings.

As shoppers moved online or to newer outdoor shopping centers, the big department stores at the ends of many malls closed. The smaller shops that had depended on the department stores' crowds lost customers. Mall owners could not find new stores to fill the empty space. Some empty malls have been rebuilt as apartments, clinics, colleges and warehouses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising aimed at one person" -->
When a person visits a website, the site can save small files and codes on the person's device that record what the person looked at. Companies called data brokers collect records like these and combine them with records from outside the internet, such as store purchases, warranty cards and property records. Then they sell what they have gathered, including the chance to show an advertisement to a chosen group of people.

In May 2014 the Federal Trade Commission, the federal agency that protects shoppers, published a report called *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability*. The commission's staff found that data brokers held "billions of data elements" about nearly every American shopper. They gathered most of it without people knowing. The report describes how brokers attached offline records to the files in a person's web browser. That way the person could be followed from site to site and shown advertisements chosen for them. The commissioners asked Congress to pass a law letting people see and control this information.

Money from this advertising pays for websites and apps that people use for free. The advertisements can also show a person something they want to buy. The same system means that records of what a person reads and buys are bought and sold by companies the person has never heard of. As of 2026, members of Congress had passed no general federal privacy law. By June 29, 2026, lawmakers in 24 states had passed their own, according to the law firm Husch Blackwell.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling to children inside a game" -->
Fortnite is a video game made by Epic Games that came out in 2017. It is free to play. Epic sells V-Bucks, game money that players buy with real money to get costumes and dance moves for their characters. By 2022, the game had more than 400 million players around the world, according to the Federal Trade Commission.

On December 19, 2022, the Federal Trade Commission announced that Epic had agreed to pay $520 million. Of that, $275 million was a penalty under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. That law requires a company to get a parent's permission before it collects personal information from a child under 13. The other $245 million went back to customers as refunds. The commission said Epic had used "dark patterns," confusing buttons and screens that charged players money. Players could be charged, for example, while they were waking the game from sleep mode. The commission counted "hundreds of millions of dollars in unauthorized charges," meaning charges that players had not agreed to. Lina Khan was the head of the commission at the time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Payday loans" -->
A payday loan is a small loan from a storefront lender that the borrower is supposed to pay back on the next payday. In April 2013, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a federal agency that watches over lenders, published a study of these loans. The bureau's researchers found that storefront payday lenders charged $10 to $20 for every $100 borrowed. On a $350 loan with a $15 fee, that works out to 391 percent a year.

They also found that nearly half of payday borrowers took out more than 10 loans in a year. Fourteen percent took out 20 or more. The typical borrower was in debt 199 days of the year, more than half of it.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Secondhand goes mainstream" -->
ThredUp, a company that sells used clothes online, publishes a yearly report on the market for secondhand clothes. Because ThredUp sells used clothes, its report is a company's estimate, not a government count.

In its 2025 report, ThredUp said the market for used clothes in the United States grew 14 percent in 2024. It said online sales of used clothes grew 23 percent that year, and 58 percent of shoppers bought something secondhand in 2024, up from 52 percent in 2023. In its 2026 report, researched by the firm GlobalData, ThredUp said that in 2025 used clothing sales grew almost four times as fast as all clothing sales. GlobalData's researchers predict that the US secondhand market will reach $78.8 billion by 2030. That number is a forecast.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last step: heat in the delivery truck" -->
Fast delivery depends on drivers who work outside all day, including in summer heat. In July 2023, NPR reported that federal injury records from OSHA, the government's workplace safety agency, showed at least 40 UPS drivers taken to the hospital for heat illness since 2015.

José Cruz Rodriguez was 23. He died of heat-related illness during a UPS delivery shift in Waco, Texas, in August 2021, according to OSHA. His family sued UPS for causing his death, and the case was settled.

Esteban Chavez Jr. had worked for UPS for four years. On June 25, 2022, the day after his 24th birthday, he was found unconscious in his UPS truck on his route in Pasadena, California. The temperature that day reached the upper 90s. UPS trucks had no air conditioning then. His family said at least 20 minutes went by before anyone noticed him and called for help. The medical examiner found that his heart stopped working. His family believes the heat caused it.

In June 2023, UPS managers and the Teamsters union, which represents UPS drivers, agreed that every small delivery truck UPS bought after January 1, 2024, would have air conditioning. The trucks would also get heat shields and fans.

The packages are packed in warehouses first. In December 2024, Senator Bernie Sanders released a report written by the majority staff of the Senate committee he led, who work for the members of the party in control. The staff found that Amazon warehouses recorded more than 30 percent more injuries than the average warehouse in 2023. For seven years in a row, Amazon warehouse workers were nearly twice as likely to be hurt as workers at other warehouses.

### Renica Turner

> **Who:** A driver who delivered Amazon packages in the California desert for a small delivery company, and who joined a union to push for protection from the heat.
> **When and where:** Palmdale and Victorville, California, about 2022 to 2023.

Renica Turner delivered Amazon packages for a company called Battle Tested Strategies. It was one of about 3,000 small companies under contract to deliver Amazon's packages. The company leased its vans from Amazon and worked out of an Amazon building in Palmdale, California.

In a story NPR published in July 2023, Turner described a day in Victorville, California, when the temperature reached 110 degrees. The van's air conditioning and fans were not working, she said. When she rolled the windows down, hot air came in. Her body began to tingle, and she thought she might pass out. She told the Amazon dispatcher, who gave her a 20-minute break. Nobody came to take the rest of her route. The route was 300 packages, and she was expected to deliver 25 every hour. She finished it herself, feeling woozy and numb.

Amazon's representatives told NPR that its vans had working air conditioning and that any van without it was taken off the road at once. The owner of Battle Tested Strategies, Johnathon Ervin, said Amazon's managers often failed to fix broken air conditioning, sometimes for weeks or months.

In April 2023, Turner and 83 other drivers at the company joined the Teamsters union and signed a contract with Battle Tested Strategies that included protection from heat. In June 2023, Amazon's managers ended their contract with the company. The union and the company's owner said Amazon was punishing the drivers for joining a union. Amazon's representatives said Amazon ended the contract over safety violations and missed insurance payments by the company. The drivers went on strike.

In April 2026, lawyers for the National Labor Relations Board, the federal agency that enforces labor law, settled the case with Amazon. Each of the roughly 84 drivers who worked there from April to June 2023 would get two weeks of pay, about $250,000 in all. Under the settlement, Amazon did not admit that it was the drivers' employer. In late May 2026, G. Rebekah Ramirez, a judge who hears labor cases for the board, approved the settlement. The Teamsters objected and asked to appeal.
