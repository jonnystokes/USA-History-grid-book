## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 18 of 37 · Marketplace and Consumer Life · 10 eras · about 75 minutes**

**Americans have taken goods home and paid later since the 1600s, and new ways of selling, from the peddler's trunk to the online store, kept changing who could buy and what they paid.**

In Springfield, Massachusetts, the merchant John Pynchon kept a page in his account book for John Stewart, a Scottish soldier captured in battle in 1650 and "sold for eight years service." On December 29, 1653, Pynchon wrote that Stewart owed him 30 pounds in English money "for releasing him from my Service." Stewart had bought his freedom on credit. For 36 years his page filled with iron, boots, pepper and sugar. He paid in shell beads, blacksmith's work, land and a day of haymaking, almost never in coins.

This chapter describes buying and selling from the trading place on the Columbia River, long before there were shops, to buying online. The peddler at the door, the department store with a price tag on every item, the mail-order catalog, the supermarket, the mall and the credit card come in between. Watch how people paid: in shells and crops, in work, on the shopkeeper's book, a little each month, and at last with plastic. Watch, too, who was turned away and who was sold. Printers ran advertisements offering enslaved people for sale in the same newspapers as everything else, and in the South the people who ran Woolworth's lunch counters served white customers only.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did colonists pay for goods when they had almost no coins?
- Where were people bought and sold like goods?
- How much of what Americans buy today is bought online?

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 3 minutes**

People in this land traded copper, seashells and stone blades across hundreds of miles long before there were any stores. They traded one thing for another, and goods passed from hand to hand. At a few great trading places, people from many nations gathered to fish and to trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where was the biggest market in the Northwest?" -->
The biggest trading place in the Northwest was a ten-mile stretch of the Columbia River. It ran from Celilo Falls down to a place called The Dalles, on the border of what are now Oregon and Washington. People fished and lived there. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past from what people left behind, date the first human use of the site to at least 10,000 years ago. Some newspaper and museum writers give 15,000 years.

Traders came from as far north as what is now British Columbia and as far south as California. Some accounts, cited by the Oregon Historical Society, state that traders came from as far east as the Great Lakes.

Northern traders brought coiled baskets, skins and berries. Traders from the south carried obsidian, a black volcanic glass that makes sharp blades, along with bows and captives, people seized by force and traded away with the other goods. The accounts of the trade do not identify who seized them. From the east came buffalo hides and camas, a root that people dug up and ate. From the coast came whale oil, dentalium shells and wapato, another root people ate.

**Dentalium shells.** These are the shells of a small sea animal. Each one is shaped like a tiny elephant tusk and can grow a few inches long. Most of them came from the sea off Vancouver Island, and people passed them from hand to hand over a wide area. So many people accepted them that traders used them to measure what other goods were worth.

Horses came later, after 1500. Spanish colonists had brought horses to the Americas. In the early 1700s, Native traders passed horses north to this part of the river.

Much later, in October 1805, two American explorers, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, reached this stretch of river. President Thomas Jefferson had sent them west to look for a water route to the Pacific Ocean. In his journal, Clark called the place a "mart," an old word for a market: the "Great Mart of all this Country." People had been trading there long before his visit.

**Under the lake.** The trading place is now under water. Workers for the US Army Corps of Engineers, the part of the army that builds dams and other large works, built The Dalles Dam eight miles downstream. At 10 o'clock on the morning of March 10, 1957, the dam's floodgates were closed.

Four and a half hours later, Celilo Falls lay under the lake behind the dam. The villages of Wyam, on the Oregon side, and S'kin, on the Washington side, went under too. US government officials later paid the Warm Springs, Yakama, Umatilla and Nez Perce nations $26.8 million for the fishing sites they lost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Goods that traveled without a store" -->
Archaeologists have found copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf of Mexico, obsidian from the West and turquoise from the Southwest hundreds of miles from where each was dug up or gathered. People carried and traded these goods over those distances, one exchange at a time.

Cahokia was a city near what is now St. Louis. It was at its largest from about 1050 to 1150. Around 1100, about 10,000 to 20,000 people lived there. People at Cahokia controlled the trade in hoe blades made from Mill Creek chert, a hard stone that holds a sharp edge. Archaeologists have found those blades more than 400 miles away. People that far from Cahokia got the blades by trading for them.

No trader from before 1500 is known by name.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

Native traders chose which European goods to take, and copper and brass kettles were among the things they wanted most. They traded for them with the crews of European whaling and trading ships off what is now eastern Canada. There were still no stores. Archaeologists can see the traders' choices in the kettles they cut up and made into new things, and in the metal they passed far inland, as far as the Great Lakes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Native customers chose" -->
Native buyers in the Northeast and around the Great Lakes traded for copper and brass kettles in the late 1500s. Basque, French and Dutch traders carried the kettles across the ocean because those buyers asked for them. Crews from the Basque Country, a mountain region on the border of Spain and France, crossed the ocean to hunt whales. They traded with the Beothuk of Newfoundland, the Innu of Labrador and the Mi'kmaq.

Many of these kettles were not used for cooking. Archaeologists dig them up cut into flat sheets. Native craftspeople turned the sheets into beads, tubes, arrow points and pendants, which are ornaments that hang from a cord. For a long time, people in these nations had made the same shapes from copper found as pure metal in the ground. Buyers like these used a kettle as a supply of metal.

Pieces of Basque copper kettles from the late 1500s and early 1600s turn up from the Canadian Maritimes, on the Atlantic coast, to the lower Great Lakes. Native traders passed the metal along among themselves, far inland. Basque kettles and axes reached the lands of the Huron and their neighbors in what is now Ontario.

Native traders chose carefully what they took in trade, and they kept doing so as the trade grew. In the fur trade of later years, they were skilled bargainers who asked for useful tools and for fine goods alike, such as iron knives, wool cloth and glass beads.

**Good guns.** One fur-trading company, the Hudson's Bay Company, began in 1670. Its Native customers demanded good guns, so the men running the company sent gunsmiths to its trading posts to check the guns and repair them.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

Colonial shoppers in the 1600s took their goods home first and paid later, mostly in crops, other goods and work. In Springfield, Massachusetts, John Pynchon wrote down in his account books what each customer owed and let the debts run for months. In the same century, public markets opened in the colonial towns, and colonists built trading posts to trade with Native people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Market days" -->
A market day was a set day when farmers brought meat, milk, butter, cheese, fruit and vegetables into town to sell. Town people could not travel out to the farms for these foods, so the farmers came to them.

Governor John Winthrop of Massachusetts ordered a market for Boston in 1634. It is the first public market of the European kind recorded by officials in the English colonies. Hartford, in Connecticut, had a market in 1643. New York had one by 1686, and Philadelphia had one in 1693.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trading posts" -->
A trading post was a building where colonists traded goods with Native people and with other traders. In 1627, the colonists of Plymouth Colony built one at Aptucxet, on the Manomet River on Cape Cod. It was about 20 miles from Plymouth. The post stood on land of the Wampanoag people, who used the place as summer fishing grounds. The name Aptucxet meant "little trap in the river."

At Aptucxet the Plymouth colonists traded with the Wampanoag for corn and beans, and with Dutch traders. That same year, Isaack de Rasieres, the secretary of the Dutch colony, brought wampum, small beads made from shells and strung together, into Plymouth's trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ledger" -->
A colonial shopkeeper kept two books. The first, the journal, listed every sale and every purchase in the order it happened. The second, the ledger, gave each customer a page of their own. The customer's purchases went on the left side of the page and the customer's payments on the right.

Customers bought on credit, taking the goods home and paying for them later. A shopkeeper usually let a customer's debt run for up to a year. Customers paid in crops, in other goods, in work and sometimes in coins.

John Pynchon kept a shop in Springfield, Massachusetts. The town and the shop stood on land on both sides of the Connecticut River that his father, William Pynchon, bought from the Agawam people on July 15, 1636. William Pynchon paid for the land in goods, with no coins: 18 fathoms of wampum, strings of beads six feet long, and 18 coats, 18 hatchets, 18 hoes and 18 knives. The Agawam kept their planted fields and the right to fish, hunt deer and gather nuts there.

Seven of John Pynchon's account books survive, with entries from 1651 to about 1702. Almost every adult in Springfield bought goods at his shop. The editors who printed the books found only a few payments in coins. Books like these record what ordinary colonists bought, item by item.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-stewart-marketplace" name="John Stewart" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Stewart

> **Who:** A Scottish soldier, captured in war and sold into forced labor, who became the blacksmith of Springfield and bought on credit at John Pynchon's shop for more than thirty years.
> **When and where:** Springfield, Massachusetts, 1653 to 1689.

**A prisoner of war who owed 30 pounds for his freedom.**

Soldiers of Oliver Cromwell's army captured John Stewart, a Scottish soldier, at the Battle of Dunbar, in Scotland, in 1650. The men on the council that ruled England ordered the governor at Newcastle to hand over 150 of the Dunbar prisoners to the agents of two men who had put money into an ironworks in Massachusetts. In his own recorded words, Stewart was "sold for eight years service." He was forced to work for whoever owned his service until the eight years ran out.

Stewart and the other prisoners crossed the ocean on a ship called the *Unity*. He reached New England in December 1650 and worked at the ironworks at Lynn, in Massachusetts. Then the merchant John Pynchon bought his service and took him to Springfield.

**Buying his freedom.** Pynchon wrote in his account book on December 29, 1653, that Stewart owed him 30 pounds, the main English unit of money, "for releasing him from my Service." While Pynchon waited for the money, Stewart agreed to pay an extra 12 shillings, a smaller unit, each year for three years. He paid that extra in blacksmith's work, "in mending or making of Iron worke."

Stewart became the Springfield blacksmith, and his page in Pynchon's ledger lists what he bought, item by item. For his forge, the fire where a blacksmith heats iron to shape it, he bought 241 pounds of iron by weight in one purchase, then more bars of iron every few weeks, bars of steel and a coal basket. Pynchon charged him for using Pynchon's tools at the shop and for an old bellows, the air pump that blows on a forge fire.

He bought a sword and belt, a grindstone, a pair of boots, wool cloth, silk and buttons, a bushel of apples, soap, a comb, pepper and ginger. Almost none of it was paid in coins. By December 23, 1654, he had paid in two lots of wampum and in smith's work, including work on Pynchon's mill, and neighbors had paid wheat to Pynchon for him. He still owed 13 pounds 6 shillings. Later he paid in land and in a day of haymaking.

The editors of the account books explain that he was fined in 1660 for striking a man named William Morgan and breaking his nose, and in 1662 for playing cards at his house.

**Ten years on.** By 1664 he was still buying on the book: sugar again and again, salt, gunpowder, scissors, pins, a knife for "the Boy," and iron for the horseshoes of the troopers, soldiers on horseback. On February 20, 1666, he owed 11 pounds 17 shillings and 1 penny.

**His wife.** By then Stewart was married. The books charge him for church seats for himself and his wife, but they never give her name. His page also records a charge of 1 pound, a fine for the two of them, for "unseasonable night meetings" (meetings at an improper hour of the night).

Pynchon's books list his purchases until 1689. They do not say what became of his wife. Stewart died at Springfield on April 21, 1691. The Scottish Prisoners of War Society, a group that traces the Dunbar prisoners, records that he left "no family, so far as is known."

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 4 minutes**

Goods made in Britain became cheap enough in the 1700s for colonial families to buy them in large amounts. Shops in the port towns sold these goods, and in Philadelphia Benjamin Franklin made advertisements pay for much of his newspaper. Colonists bought and sold enslaved people in the same towns. In New York, city officials set up a market for those sales, and printers, Franklin among them, ran advertisements offering people for sale.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The consumer revolution" -->
Colonists began buying many of the clothes, tools and kitchen things they had once made for themselves, mostly from Britain. Historians call this big change in buying by consumers, the people who buy goods to use them, the "consumer revolution."

In Britain, makers found better ways to make goods. Merchants got better at shipping goods across the ocean and at selling them on credit. Prices fell. At the same time, colonial families earned more. Goods that only rich families had owned became ordinary.

Families who drank tea bought a whole set of goods to go with it: a teapot, cups, sugar and a table to set them on. Buying goods like these became a sign that a family was respectable.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A market where people were sold" -->
In New York, colonists bought and sold enslaved people at a market created by the men who ran the city, the aldermen of the Common Council. They set it up by law on November 30, 1711, at the bottom end of Wall Street, near Pearl Street and Water Street.

Slaveholders sold enslaved Africans at this market and rented them out to others as workers. They sold and rented out Native people held in slavery there too. Under the law, owners who rented out enslaved people as workers by the day had to do it at this market. Buyers inspected the people for sale there, looking them over before buying. The accounts of the market do not describe how the inspections were done.

**The building.** The market was a wooden building with a roof and open sides. Slaveholders used it for 51 years, until 1762.

New York had about 5,000 people in 1700, and at least 750 of them, about one person in seven, were enslaved. Jim O'Grady, a reporter for the New York radio station WNYC, wrote in 2015 that thousands of people were sold at the market: men, women, shiploads of children and captured Native people. The accounts of the market do not identify the men who bought and sold people there.

A sign now marks the place. Jumaane Williams, a member of the New York City Council, asked for it in 2015, and in June of that year Mayor Bill de Blasio officially opened it at Wall Street and Water Street.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspaper advertising begins to pay" -->
Benjamin Franklin bought a newspaper in Philadelphia, the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, from a printer named Samuel Keimer on October 2, 1729. It had about 90 subscribers, the people who paid to get every copy. Under Franklin, the money that sellers paid to print their advertisements became one of the main ways the paper made money.

Franklin changed the way the advertisements looked. He put more empty space around them and used letters of different sizes. He made the *Gazette* four pages instead of two, partly to make room for more advertisements. By 1748 it had more than 1,500 subscribers, and it was the most widely read newspaper in the colonies.

**The partnership.** Franklin stepped back from printing in 1748, when he was 42. He made a partnership with a printer named David Hall, who ran the shop while Franklin kept a share of the profits. Much of the money that let Franklin stop working at his trade so early came from the paper's advertising.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-marketplace" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** The Philadelphia printer who ran advertisements offering enslaved people for sale in his newspaper, and took the fees.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from October 2, 1729.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It covers Franklin's whole life.

**At least 277 of his paper's advertisements listed enslaved people for sale.**

Benjamin Franklin published the *Gazette* for 37 years, counting the years when he was David Hall's partner in the paper. In those 37 years, the *Gazette* contained at least 277 advertisements offering at least 308 enslaved people for sale. Franklin ran these notices and took the fees for them. He also acted as the go-between for the buyers and the sellers.

**The runaways.** Slaveholders paid Franklin to print notices about enslaved people who had run away from them. He printed notices about runaway servants too: workers bound to a master for a set number of years, who had left before their years were up.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-marketplace" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shops and shopkeepers in the port towns" -->
Shopkeepers in the port towns ordered cloth and other goods from London and sold them. Some sold haberdashery, small sewing goods such as thread, ribbons and buttons. Colonists who bought on credit could end up in debt to a local shopkeeper or to a merchant in London.

Women ran some of these shops. Elizabeth Murray came to Boston from Scotland in 1749, when she was 23. She opened a shop that sold cloth and other goods from Britain. Her brother's business friends helped her get started. She traveled to London to choose the goods for her shop, and she earned enough to support herself. Later she helped other women open shops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 7 minutes**

Many colonists refused to buy British goods from 1767 to 1770, as a protest against British taxes. Merchants promised not to bring the goods in, and families made their own cloth instead of buying it. Members of merchants' committees warned the shopkeepers who kept selling British goods. In Boston, Ebenezer Richardson fired from his house during a protest at one of those shops, next door, and killed a boy named Christopher Seider. Away from the ports, a single storekeeper sold a whole farming neighborhood its goods on credit until the next harvest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boycott of British goods" -->
Members of Parliament, the lawmakers in London, passed a tax law called the Townshend Revenue Act in 1767. Under it, customs officials collected duties, taxes on goods brought into the colonies from Britain. Colonists who opposed the duties answered with a boycott, refusing to buy British goods as a protest.

The seniors at Harvard College in Massachusetts voted in the autumn of 1767 not to drink tea or wear clothes from Britain at their graduation, as a protest against the Townshend duties. At the next year's graduation, the seniors' theses, a printed sheet of the ideas they were ready to argue for in public at the ceremony, were on paper made in New England.

**The agreement.** About 60 Boston merchants and traders signed the Boston Non-Importation Agreement on August 1, 1768. They promised not to bring in most British goods from January 1, 1769, to January 1, 1770. Within two weeks, all but 16 of the town's merchants had joined.

The value of British goods brought into Boston fell by about half, from about 420,000 pounds in 1768 to about 208,000 pounds in 1769. In 1770, members of Parliament took back most of the Townshend taxes. The agreement in Boston ended that year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spinning instead of buying" -->
Families who did not buy British cloth had to make their own, called homespun. To make it, women first spun wool or flax, a plant whose stems give the threads for linen, into yarn on a spinning wheel. Then the yarn was woven into cloth.

Women in New England held at least 46 spinning meetings between 1768 and 1770, where they spun together in public. In all, 1,644 women took part. Ministers held 30 of the meetings, and 94 out of every 100 spinners came to those 30.

**One day in Brookfield.** Women in Brookfield, Massachusetts, met at the minister's house from 5 o'clock in the morning one day in August 1769. The young woman who did best on the linen wheel spun 70 knots, measured lengths of yarn. One married woman did her morning work at home and made cheese first. Then she rode more than two miles carrying her wheel, spun from 9 in the morning to 7 at night, and finished 53 knots. After that she went home to do the milking.

Members of a society in Hartford, Connecticut, offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of woolen cloth in a year. Printers ran newspaper stories about the meetings. Only six of those stories called the spinners "Daughters of Liberty," a name for women on the side of the protest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cuming-sisters" name="Ame and Elizabeth Cuming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ame and Elizabeth Cuming

> **Who:** Two sisters who kept a shop in Boston, refused to join the boycott and were named in the newspaper and the town record for it.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1765 to 1776, then Halifax, Nova Scotia.

**A shipment worth 300 pounds, and a warning of "consequences."**

Ame and Elizabeth Cuming kept a shop in Boston and would not stop selling British goods when the town's merchants told them to. The sisters were in their middle thirties and not married, and by 1765 they earned their own living. They sold fashionable goods from London, taught embroidery, sewing pictures and patterns with colored thread, and took in young women who paid to live with them. Elizabeth Murray had helped them start their business.

**The warning.** A shipment of silk and haberdashery worth 300 pounds reached them from London in late 1769. In the middle of November, men from the merchants' committee came to the shop and warned the sisters of "consequences" if they sold the goods. The sisters refused. They said they had never signed any agreement to stop bringing in goods. They called themselves "two industrious Girls" (two hard-working girls) who were trying "in an honest way" to earn their bread.

A notice in the *Boston Gazette* described them as "enemies to their country" who "preferred their own little private Advantage to the Welfare of America." On March 19, 1770, the men at Boston's town meeting voted to write the sisters' names into the town record. The wording of the vote states that this was so that "POSTERITY" (the people who live later) "may know" they had "opposed their Country."

The sisters left Boston in March 1776 with the Loyalists, the colonists who sided with Britain in the Revolutionary War, and went to Halifax, Nova Scotia. By 1780 they were "more prosperous than ever they were in Boston."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cuming-sisters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A killing at a shop door, 1770" -->
A Boston man named Ebenezer Richardson shot and killed a boy, Christopher Seider, during a protest at a shop door in February 1770. Theophilus Lillie kept the shop, in Boston's North End, and he went on selling British goods during the boycott.

A carved head on a long pole went up in front of Lillie's shop door on February 22, 1770. According to some accounts, it was a sign that read "Importer." Thomas Hutchinson, the acting governor of Massachusetts, wrote about that day in a letter. Hutchinson was running the colony for the king while the governor was away in England. A crowd of boys and young men gathered and let no one go into the shop.

Richardson lived next door to Lillie and worked as a customs informer. Customs officials collected the taxes on goods brought in by ship, and they paid informers like him to report smugglers. When Richardson tried to pull the pole down, the crowd threw stones and sticks at him, chased him into his house and broke his windows. From inside, he fired a musket at the boys. The shot hit Christopher Seider in the chest, and he died soon after.

Hutchinson wrote that Richardson "killed one & wounded another." The second boy was hit in the thigh. Christopher was 10 and a half years old in Hutchinson's letters, and 11 in most other accounts.

**The funeral.** Christopher Seider's funeral was on February 26, 1770. Hutchinson wrote that people said the procession, the line of people walking behind the coffin, "reached from the Town House to Liberty Tree," two places in Boston. The modern editors of his letters call it the largest procession ever seen in Boston, as people reported it at the time.

Richardson was tried for murder on April 20, 1770, and the next day the jury found him guilty. The judges believed he was innocent, and they put off his sentence. Richardson stayed in jail for almost two years. On March 10, 1772, the news came that the king had pardoned him, and he went free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The country store and the crop ledger" -->
Away from the port towns, one country storekeeper sold goods to a whole farming neighborhood. Customers paid in crops, in work and in promises to pay later. The storekeeper wrote each family's debt in a ledger. A farm family bought goods all through the year and paid after the harvest. When a harvest failed, the family could not pay, and the debt carried into the next year.

**A shop near the city.** Not every store worked this way. Robert Townsend kept a shop in Oyster Bay, on Long Island, close to New York City, and kept account books there and in New York. His surviving books record sales from 1773 to 1785. Staff at the East Hampton Library have copied them out and published them.

One book records sales from April 1, 1773, to November 15, 1779. In 1773 it lists tea at 4 shillings 8 pence a pound, stockings, calico, a cotton cloth, linen cloth, gloves, packs of pins, molasses, a dark sweet syrup, and large barrels of spirits, a strong liquor.

Townsend sold on credit too, and he let a customer's debt run for months, but usually not for more than a year. Unlike the farm families above, most of his customers paid in cash. Library staff counted the payments in his book from 1773 to 1779. In their count, 73 out of every 100 were cash, 22 were farm goods and 5 were notes, written promises to pay. Some customers paid with their work, as a washerwoman, a doctor, a barber or a tailor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->
