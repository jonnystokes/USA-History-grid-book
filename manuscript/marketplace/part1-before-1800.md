## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People in this land got goods from far away long before 1500, with no stores, coins or advertising. They traded one thing for another, face to face. Much of this trade happened at gathering places, where people from many nations met to fish, trade and hold ceremonies. Goods passed from one person to the next along rivers and trails, sometimes across hundreds of miles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dalles and Celilo Falls" -->
The biggest trading place in the Northwest was a ten-mile stretch of the Columbia River. It ran from Celilo Falls down to a place called The Dalles, on the border of what are now Oregon and Washington. People fished and lived there. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past from what people left behind, date the first human use of the site to at least 10,000 years ago. Some estimates say 15,000 years.

Traders came from as far north as what is now British Columbia and as far south as California. Some accounts say traders came from as far east as the Great Lakes. From the north they brought coiled baskets, skins and berries. From the south they brought obsidian, a black volcanic glass that makes sharp blades, and bows. They also brought captives from the south. Captives were people taken by force, and they were traded like the other goods. From the east came horses, camas root and buffalo hides. From the coast came wapato root, whale oil and dentalium shells.

Dentalium shells are long, thin, white shells. People passed them along the coast and far inland. So many people accepted them that traders used them to measure what other goods were worth.

In October 1805, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark reached this stretch of river. Clark wrote in his journal that it was the "Great Mart of all this Country." A mart is a market. The trading was thousands of years older than his visit. His journal is the first written record of it.

The trading place is now under water. Workers for the US Army Corps of Engineers built The Dalles Dam eight miles downstream. At 10 o'clock on the morning of March 10, 1957, the dam's floodgates were closed. The written account of that day does not name who gave the order. Four and a half hours later, Celilo Falls lay under the lake behind the dam. The villages of Wyam, on the Oregon side, and S'kin, on the Washington side, went under too. Federal officials later paid the Warm Springs, Yakama, Umatilla and Nez Perce nations $26.8 million for the fishing sites they lost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Goods that traveled without a store" -->
Archaeologists have found copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf of Mexico, obsidian from the West and turquoise from the Southwest hundreds of miles from where each was dug up or gathered. People carried and traded these goods, one exchange at a time, along rivers and trails.

Cahokia was a city near what is now St. Louis. It was at its largest from about 1050 to 1150. Thousands of people gathered in its open plazas. People at Cahokia controlled the trade in hoe blades made from Mill Creek chert, a hard stone that holds a sharp edge. Archaeologists have found those blades more than 400 miles away. People that far from Cahokia got the blades with no shop, no price tag and no coins.

No trader from before 1500 is known today by name.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Native people along the northern coasts began trading with the crews of European fishing and trading ships. There were still no stores. Native traders chose which European goods they wanted, and archaeologists can see those choices in what the traders kept, cut up and made into new things.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What Native customers chose" -->
In the second half of the 1500s, copper and brass kettles were among the European goods that Native buyers in the Northeast and around the Great Lakes wanted most. Basque, French and Dutch traders carried the kettles across the ocean because those buyers asked for them. The Basques come from the Basque Country, a region in the mountains on the border of Spain and France. Basque ship crews crossed the ocean to hunt whales. They traded with the Beothuk of Newfoundland, the Innu of Labrador and the Mi'kmaq.

Many of these kettles were never used for cooking. Archaeologists dig them up cut into flat sheets. Native metalworkers turned the sheets into beads, pendants, tubes and arrow points. For a long time, people in these nations had made the same shapes from copper found as pure metal in the ground. The buyers wanted the metal, and many of them had no use for the pot.

Pieces of Basque copper kettles from the late 1500s and early 1600s turn up from the Canadian Maritimes, on the Atlantic coast, to the lower Great Lakes. Native traders passed the metal along among themselves, far inland. Basque kettles and axes reached the lands of the Huron and other Iroquoian nations in what is now Ontario.

Native customers also traded for wool cloth, linen shirts, glass beads, iron knives, awls, axes, fish hooks, blankets, guns and gunpowder. An awl is a pointed tool for punching holes in leather. Historians describe the Native traders as hard bargainers who asked for useful tools and for fine goods alike. Records from the next century show the same pattern. The Hudson's Bay Company, an English fur-trading company, began in 1670. Native customers demanded guns that worked, so the men running the company sent gunsmiths to its trading posts to check the guns and repair them.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists in the 1600s bought and sold with very few coins. Most of the time they paid with crops, with other goods or with work. A shopkeeper wrote down what each customer owed and let the debt run for months. The first fixed places to buy also opened in this century: market days in the towns and trading posts on the rivers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Market days" -->
A market day was a set day when farmers brought meat, milk, butter, cheese, fruit and vegetables into town to sell. Town people could not travel out to the farms for these foods, so the farmers came to them.

In 1634, Governor John Winthrop of Massachusetts ordered a market for Boston. It is the first public market of the European kind recorded by officials in the English colonies. Hartford, in Connecticut, had a market by 1643. New York had one by 1686, and Philadelphia had one in 1693.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trading posts" -->
A trading post was a building where colonists traded goods with Native people and with traders from other colonies. In 1627, the colonists of Plymouth Colony built one at Aptucxet, on the Manomet River on Cape Cod. It was about 20 miles from their town. The post stood on land of the Wampanoag people, who used the place as summer fishing grounds. The name Aptucxet meant "little trap in the river."

At Aptucxet the Plymouth colonists traded with the Wampanoag for corn and beans. They also traded with Dutch traders from New Amsterdam. That same year, Isaack de Rasieres, the secretary of the Dutch colony, brought wampum into Plymouth's trade. Wampum is beads made from shells.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ledger" -->
A colonial shopkeeper kept two books. The first, the journal, listed every sale and every purchase in the order it happened. The second, the ledger, gave each customer a page of their own. What the customer bought went on the left side. What the customer paid went on the right side.

Buying without paying at once is called buying on credit. The amount still owed is the debt. A shopkeeper usually let a customer's debt run for up to a year, often until the harvest came in. Customers paid in crops, in other goods, in work and only sometimes in coins.

John Pynchon kept a shop in Springfield, Massachusetts. Springfield stood on land of the Agawam people. On July 15, 1636, John's father, William Pynchon, took a deed from the Agawam for land on both sides of the Connecticut River. The deed names Commucke and Matanchan, "ancient Indians of Agaam," acting for the community, and Cuttonus as "the right owner." Pynchon paid 18 fathoms of wampum, 18 coats, 18 hatchets, 18 hoes and 18 knives. A fathom is six feet, here a length of strung beads. The deed let the Agawam keep their planted fields and their right to take fish, deer, ground nuts, walnuts and acorns.

Seven of John Pynchon's account books survive, covering 1651 to about 1702. Almost every adult in Springfield bought goods at his shop. The editors who printed the books found only a few payments in coins. From books like these, historians learn what ordinary colonists bought, item by item. The names in them belong to real customers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-stewart-marketplace" name="John Stewart" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Stewart

> **Who:** A Scottish soldier, taken prisoner in war and sold into servitude, who became the blacksmith of Springfield and bought on credit at John Pynchon's shop for more than thirty years.
> **When and where:** Springfield, Massachusetts, 1653 to 1689.

John Stewart bought his own freedom on credit. He was a Scottish soldier. Oliver Cromwell's English army captured him at the Battle of Dunbar, in Scotland, in 1650. In his own recorded words, he was "sold for eight years service." That meant he had to work for whoever bought his service until the eight years ran out. He was shipped to New England on a ship called the *Unity* and arrived in December 1650. He worked at the ironworks at Lynn, in Massachusetts. Then the merchant John Pynchon bought his service and took him to Springfield.

On December 29, 1653, Pynchon wrote in his account book that Stewart owed him £30 "for releasing him from my Service." The sign £ stands for pounds, the main British unit of money. Shillings and pence were smaller units. Stewart agreed to pay an extra 12 shillings a year for three years while Pynchon waited for the £30. He paid that extra in blacksmith's work, "in mending or making of Iron worke."

Stewart became the Springfield blacksmith. His page in Pynchon's ledger lists what he bought, item by item. For his forge he bought 241 pounds of iron at a time, more bars of iron every few weeks, bars of steel and a coal basket. He also bought a sword and belt, a grindstone, a pair of boots, wool cloth, silk and buttons, a bushel of apples, soap, a comb, pepper and ginger. Pynchon charged him for using Pynchon's tools at the shop and for an old bellows, the air pump that blows on a forge fire.

Almost none of it was paid in coins. Stewart paid in wheat, in two lots of wampum, in land and in a day of haymaking. He paid in smith's work, including work on Pynchon's mill. Neighbors also paid wheat to Pynchon for him. On December 23, 1654, he still owed £13 6s. The letter s stands for shillings.

Ten years later he was still buying on the book: sugar again and again, salt, gunpowder, scissors, pins, a knife for "the Boy," and iron for the troopers' horseshoes. On February 20, 1666, he owed £11 17s 1d. The d stands for pence. The same page charges him £1 as a fine for himself and his wife for "unseasonable night meetings." The editors of the account books explain that in 1662 he was fined for playing cards at his house at night. In 1660 he was also fined for striking a man named William Morgan and breaking his nose.

His account runs on in the books until 1689. He died at Springfield on April 21, 1691. No record shows that he left any family.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s, ordinary colonial families began buying British factory goods in large amounts. Cloth, dishes and furniture from Britain became cheap enough for them. Shops became a normal part of life in the port towns, and printers began paying for their newspapers with money from advertisements. People were also bought and sold in these markets. In New York, city officials set up a market where enslaved people were sold, and newspapers printed advertisements offering people for sale.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The consumer revolution" -->
A consumer is a person who buys goods to use them. Historians use the words "consumer revolution" for a change in the colonies during the 1700s. Colonists had made many of their own clothes, tools and kitchen things. Now they began buying them instead, mostly from Britain.

In Britain, workshops and factories got better at making goods, and ships and credit reached the colonies more easily. Cloth, furniture and dishes made of clay became cheaper. At the same time, colonial families earned more. Goods that only rich families had owned became ordinary.

Tea shows how this worked. Drinking tea in the proper way took a teapot, cups, sugar and a table to set them on. A family that owned these things and used them correctly showed its neighbors that it was respectable.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A market where people were sold" -->
In the colonies, people were bought and sold in the markets like goods. On November 30, 1711, the aldermen of New York City's Common Council passed a law that set up a market at the foot of Wall Street. It stood near Pearl Street and Water Street. The Common Council was the city's governing board. Its members were called aldermen.

At this market, enslaved Africans were sold, rented out and inspected. Native people held in slavery were sold and rented there too. The law required owners who rented out enslaved people as workers by the day to do it at this market. The market was a wooden shed with a roof and open sides. It held about 50 men. It was used for 51 years, until 1762.

In 1700, New York had about 5,000 people. At least 750 of them were enslaved. The city's aldermen chose the place for the market. The accounts of the market do not name the men who bought and sold people there.

In 2015, Jumaane Williams, a member of the New York City Council, sponsored a historical marker for the market. In June 2015, Mayor Bill de Blasio dedicated the marker at Wall Street and Water Street.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspaper advertising begins to pay" -->
An advertisement is a notice that a seller pays to have printed, so that people will buy what the seller offers. On October 2, 1729, Benjamin Franklin bought a newspaper in Philadelphia, the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, from a printer named Samuel Keimer. Under Franklin, the money that sellers paid for advertisements became one of the paper's main sources of income.

Franklin changed the way the advertisements looked. He put more empty space around them and used letters of different sizes. That made each notice stand out from the news around it. The *Gazette* grew from two pages to four, partly to make room for more advertisements.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-marketplace" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** The Philadelphia printer who made advertising pay for a newspaper, and whose paper printed advertisements selling enslaved people.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from October 2, 1729.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about Franklin's whole life, not only his printing business.

Benjamin Franklin made his fortune as a printer partly by selling space in his newspaper to advertisers. When he took over the *Pennsylvania Gazette* in 1729, it had about 90 subscribers, the people who paid to get every copy. By 1748 it had more than 1,500. It was the most popular newspaper in the colonies.

Some of the advertisements offered people for sale. In the 37 years that Franklin published the *Gazette*, it printed at least 277 advertisements offering at least 308 enslaved people for sale. Franklin's print shop also acted as the go-between for buyers and sellers. Franklin ran these notices and took the fees for them. The *Gazette* also printed notices about servants who had run away from the people they were bound to work for.

In 1748, when he was 42, Franklin stepped back from printing. He made a partnership with a printer named David Hall. Hall ran the shop, and Franklin kept a share of the profits. Historians give the paper's advertising money much of the credit for how early Franklin could stop working at his trade.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-marketplace" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shops and shopkeepers in the port towns" -->
Shops in the port towns sold cloth, dishes, tea and sugar brought in from overseas. They also sold small sewing goods such as ribbons, buttons and thread, which were called haberdashery. A shopkeeper ordered the goods from London on credit. Then the shopkeeper sold them on credit again to customers in town.

Women ran some of these shops. Elizabeth Murray came to Boston from Scotland in 1749, when she was 23. She opened a shop that sold cloth and other goods from Britain. Her brother's business friends helped her get started. She traveled to London to choose her own goods, and she earned her own living. Later she helped other women set up shops of their own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1767 to 1770, many colonists refused to buy British goods as a protest against British taxes. Merchants promised not to bring the goods in, and families made their own cloth instead of buying it. Merchants' committees warned the shopkeepers who kept selling British goods, and newspapers printed their names. One quarrel at a shop door ended with a boy shot dead. Away from the ports, a single country store sold a whole neighborhood its goods on credit until the next harvest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Non-importation" -->
In 1767, members of Parliament, the lawmaking body in London, passed a tax law called the Townshend Revenue Act. It set duties, which are taxes on goods brought into the colonies from Britain. Colonists who opposed the duties answered by refusing to buy British goods. Refusing to buy something as a protest is called a boycott.

In the autumn of 1767, the seniors at Harvard College in Massachusetts voted not to drink tea or wear imported British clothes at their graduation. It was a protest against the Townshend duties. Imported goods are goods brought in from another country. At the next year's graduation, the students' papers were printed on paper made in New England.

On August 1, 1768, about 60 Boston merchants and traders signed the Boston Non-Importation Agreement. They promised not to import most British goods from January 1, 1769, to January 1, 1770. To import means to bring goods in from another country to sell. Within two weeks, all but 16 of the town's merchants had joined.

The value of British goods brought into Boston fell from about £420,000 in 1768 to about £208,000 in 1769. That is about half. In 1770, members of Parliament took back most of the Townshend taxes, and the merchants ended the agreement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spinning instead of buying" -->
If families did not buy British cloth, they had to make their own. Cloth made at home was called homespun. To make it, women first spun wool or flax into yarn on a spinning wheel. Then the yarn was woven into cloth.

Between 1768 and 1770, women in New England held at least 46 spinning meetings, where they spun together in public. In all, 1,644 women took part. Ministers held 30 of the meetings, and those 30 drew 94 out of every 100 of the spinners.

In August 1769, women in Brookfield, Massachusetts, met at the minister's house from 5 o'clock in the morning. The young woman who spun the most on the linen wheel spun 70 knots. A knot was a measured length of yarn. One married woman did her morning work at home and made cheese first. Then she rode more than two miles carrying her wheel, spun from 9 in the morning to 7 at night, and finished 53 knots. After that she went home to milk the cows.

A society in Hartford, Connecticut, offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of woolen cloth in a year. Printers ran newspaper stories about the meetings. Only six of those stories called the spinners "Daughters of Liberty."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cuming-sisters" name="Ame and Elizabeth Cuming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ame and Elizabeth Cuming

> **Who:** Two sisters who kept a shop in Boston, refused to join the boycott and were named in the newspaper and the town record for it.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1765 to 1776, then Halifax, Nova Scotia.

Ame and Elizabeth Cuming sold imported goods in Boston and would not stop when the town's merchants told them to. The sisters were not married and were in their middle thirties. Their parents were from Scotland, and they grew up in Concord, Massachusetts. By 1765 they lived in Boston and earned their own living. They sold fashionable goods from London, taught embroidery, which is sewing pictures and patterns with colored thread, and took in young women as boarders. Elizabeth Murray had helped them start their business.

In late 1769, a shipment of silk and haberdashery worth £300 reached them from London. In the middle of November, men from the merchants' committee came to the shop. They warned the sisters of "consequences" if they sold the goods. The sisters refused. They said, "We have never [entered] into [any] agreement not to import." They called themselves "two industrious Girls who [were] Striving in an honest way to [get their] Bread."

The *Boston Gazette* called them "enemies to their country." On March 19, 1770, the men at Boston's town meeting voted to write the sisters' names into the town record. The vote said this was so that "POSTERITY may know" they had "opposed their Country." Posterity means the people who live later.

The Cuming sisters kept their shop open until March 1776. Then they left Boston with the Loyalists, the colonists who sided with Britain in the war, and went to Halifax, Nova Scotia. By 1780 they were "more prosperous than ever they were in Boston."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cuming-sisters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A killing at a shop door, 1770" -->
In February 1770, a Boston man named Ebenezer Richardson shot and killed a boy, Christopher Seider, during a protest at a shop door. Theophilus Lillie kept the shop, in Boston's North End. He went on selling imported goods during the boycott. On February 22, 1770, a carved head on a long pole was set up in front of his shop door. Some accounts say it was a sign that read "Importer." The accounts do not name who put it there. A crowd of boys and young men gathered and let no one go into the shop.

Richardson was Lillie's neighbor. He was a customs informer, a man paid by customs officials to report smugglers. Richardson tried to pull the pole down. The crowd threw stones and sticks at him, chased him into his house and broke his windows. From inside the house, Richardson fired a musket at the boys. The shot hit Christopher Seider in the chest, and he died soon after. Christopher was 10 and a half years old in the letters of Governor Thomas Hutchinson, and 11 in most other accounts. A second boy was shot in the thigh.

Christopher Seider's funeral procession went from the Town House to the Liberty Tree. It was reported as one of the largest ever seen in America. Richardson was tried for murder on April 20, 1770. The next day the jury found him guilty. The judges believed he was innocent, and they put off his sentence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The country store and the crop ledger" -->
Away from the port towns, one country store served a whole farming neighborhood. It sold salt, iron, cloth, sugar, tea, tools and rum. Customers paid in crops, in work and in promises to pay later.

The storekeeper kept the credit in a ledger. A farm family bought goods all through the year and paid after the harvest. When a harvest failed, the family's debt carried over into the next year, and the family still owed the storekeeper.

Many of these account books survive, and they name the customers. The books that Robert Townsend kept at Oyster Bay and in New York from 1773 to 1785 have been printed by the East Hampton Library.
