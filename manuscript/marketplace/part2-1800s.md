## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 6 minutes**

Peddlers carried goods from house to house in the first half of the 1800s, selling to farm families in the country. In the cities, P.T. Barnum sold tickets to his shows with posters and newspaper advertisements, and he lied in them. His first show was an enslaved woman, Joice Heth, whom he advertised as 161 years old. A. T. Stewart opened a store in New York in 1846 with several floors of goods and a set price on each one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The peddler's trade" -->
A peddler traveled a route through the countryside and sold goods at people's doors. Some went on foot. Others drove a wagon. They carried tinware, clocks, pins, needles, combs, books and patent medicines, bottled remedies sold under a brand name. The best known were the tin peddlers from Connecticut, who sold tinware: pots, pans, cups and other things made of thin sheets of metal coated with tin.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-guild" name="James Guild" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Guild

> **Who:** A Vermont farm boy who set out to peddle with a trunk of goods and wrote down what happened to him.
> **When and where:** Tunbridge, Vermont, and the roads beyond it, 1818 to 1824.

**Three days on the road, and 30 cents' worth of goods sold.**

James Guild grew up in Tunbridge, Vermont, bound out from the age of 9 to work on a farm, which meant he had to work for a farmer until he turned 21. In the opening pages of his journal, Guild does not say who bound him out. He turned 21 on July 9, 1818. He wrote that his "disposition," his nature, "would not allow me to work on a farm."

Guild held a note, a written promise that someone would pay him money. He could not turn the note into cash, so he traded it for "a trunk of goods." The first entry in his journal is headed "Tunbridge Oct, 5th 1818." Tunbridge is a town about thirty miles from Montpelier. He felt ashamed of the work and wondered why he "should stoop so low as to follow so mean a calling" (so low a trade).

At the houses he visited, he asked, in his own spelling, "do you wish to by some harecombs, needles, buttons, buttonmolds, sewing silk." Customers who wanted to buy "would want to banter" (haggle over the price) "until they could get it for nothing."

**On the road.** At first he sold very little. On the road toward the Dog River, he wrote, "I think I traveled 3 days and selling but 30 cent worth of goods." At poor houses that wanted "a needle or a few pins," he gave them away, and he believed he gave away more than he sold.

He killed a partridge with a stone and traded it for "a dish of bread and milk." A flood had carried off a bridge, so he crossed the river on a log and fell in with his trunk.

Over the next six years, Guild went from peddling to mending pots and pans as a tinker, then to teaching school, then to painting portraits. In the end he went to London. In 1937 an editor at the Vermont Historical Society printed his journal as *From Tunbridge, Vermont, to London, England*.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pt-barnum-marketplace" name="P.T. Barnum" movie="The Circus (American Experience, 2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### P.T. Barnum

> **Who:** A showman who sold tickets through advertising, and whose first paid show was an enslaved woman he put on show under a false claim.
> **When and where:** New York City and towns across the Northeast, from 1835.
> **Movie:** *The Circus* (2018), a two-part documentary in the *American Experience* series on PBS. It is about the American circus and tells a large part of Barnum's story.

**He advertised an elderly blind woman as 161 years old.**

Phineas Taylor Barnum started his career in show business by showing an enslaved woman named Joice Heth to paying crowds. In 1835 a man named R. W. Lindsay of Kentucky signed a contract selling Barnum possession of Heth, the right to hold her and show her for ten months. Heth was Black, elderly and blind. Barnum advertised her as George Washington's nurse and said she was 161 years old. He lied about her age.

**On show.** For seven months, Barnum and his partner, Levi Lyman, showed Heth to paying crowds in cities and towns across the Northeast. They put her on show in taverns and inns, places where people drank, ate and stayed the night, and in museums, railway houses and concert halls. To bring in the crowds, they used posters and advertisements in the *New York Sun*.

When fewer people came, Barnum wrote a letter to a Boston newspaper without signing his name. In it he claimed that Heth was a machine made of whalebone and old leather, and not a person. People came back to see for themselves.

**After her death.** Joice Heth died in February 1836. Barnum then sold tickets to a dissection of her body, the cutting open of a dead body to look at its insides. On February 25, 1836, at the City Saloon in New York, 1,500 people paid 50 cents each to watch.

Dr. David L. Rogers, a surgeon, cut open her body while doctors and medical students watched from the crowd. Rogers examined her heart, her lungs, her brain and the places where the bones of her skull join. He decided that she had been no more than 75 years old, or at the most 80.

Barnum called himself the "Prince of Humbug," from humbug, a trick or a false claim made to fool people. He wrote a book called *The Humbugs of the World*, dated 1865 on its copyright page and 1866 on its title page. In it he wrote that humbug did not include crimes or swindles, tricks to cheat people out of their money.

*The Greatest Showman*, a musical movie about Barnum, came out in 2017. Its story is mostly made up, and Joice Heth does not appear in it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pt-barnum-marketplace" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Barnum's American Museum" -->
Barnum bought John Scudder's American Museum, at Broadway and Ann Street in New York, in 1841. A day's ticket cost 25 cents.

Barnum showed the Feejee Mermaid there in 1842. His advertisements showed pictures of a beautiful creature, half woman and half fish. The object in the museum was the head and upper body of a monkey sewn to the tail of a fish. Large crowds came to see it in New York for about a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Marble Palace" -->
Alexander Turney Stewart opened a dry goods store, selling cloth, clothing and sewing supplies, at 280 Broadway in New York on September 21, 1846. The building was faced with Tuckahoe marble, and it became known as the Marble Palace.

Stewart sold goods to shoppers on several floors, and that was new. Before, shopkeepers sold to shoppers only on the ground floor and used the upper floors for wholesale, selling goods in bulk to other sellers, or as living space. Stewart set one standard price on his goods, so that customers did not haggle, and he held sales to bring in women customers.

Many writers call the Marble Palace the first American department store, a large store with many kinds of goods, each kind in its own section, called a department. Other writers name Macy's or Wanamaker's as the first, because definitions of a department store differ. The building still stands and is now called the Sun Building.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 13 minutes**

City shoppers got the department store between 1850 and 1900, and farm families got the mail-order catalog. Store owners such as John Wanamaker made price tags and money-back promises common. Edward Clark, a partner in the Singer sewing machine business, sold the machines across the whole country on monthly payments. Sellers used advertisements to sell medicines that harmed people. In 1859 a slave dealer advertised the largest recorded sale of people in the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paying a little each month" -->
Edward Clark began selling Singer sewing machines on installments in 1856, letting buyers take a machine home and pay for it in small amounts over time. Isaac Singer's company made the machines, and Clark, a lawyer, was Singer's business partner.

**The trade-in.** Clark announced in February 1856 that customers could trade in any old sewing machine, even a worthless one, for $50 off a new Singer. In September he began the installment sales. Furniture makers in New York and clock makers in New England had already sold goods this way. Peter Lyon, who wrote the history of Singer's machine for the magazine *American Heritage* in 1958, calls Clark's plan the first time a business selling across the whole country did it.

A writer for the magazine *Scientific American* said that a woman "would rather pay $100 for a machine in monthly installments of five dollars" than pay $50 all at once. A Singer machine that sold for $110 could cost as little as $23 to make.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The department store" -->
Rowland Hussey Macy opened a small dry goods store at Sixth Avenue and 14th Street in New York on October 28, 1858. It grew into Macy's department store. On the first day he took in $11.06, by most accounts, or $11.08 by others. In his first year he took in about $85,000.

John Wanamaker opened a store called the Grand Depot in Philadelphia in 1876. It stood at Thirteenth Street and Market Street, in an old freight station of the Pennsylvania Railroad, a building where trains loaded and unloaded goods.

Wanamaker gets the credit for making three practices common: one price for every customer, a printed price tag on the goods and a promise of money back on anything returned. He was not the first to use all of them. A. T. Stewart had set fixed prices in 1846, and histories of the Wanamaker stores describe him as "one of the first" to use the other two.

With a money-back promise, a customer who bought something poor could return it and get the money back. Montgomery Ward, a mail-order seller, made the same promise to customers who bought goods without seeing them first.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-wanamaker" name="John Wanamaker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Wanamaker

> **Who:** A Philadelphia merchant who gets the credit for spreading the price tag and the money-back guarantee in American stores.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1861, and the Grand Depot from 1876.

**A whole newspaper page for one advertisement, in 1879.**

John Wanamaker gets the credit for spreading the one-price rule: every customer paid the price marked on the goods. Before stores like his, customers bargained over prices, as James Guild's customers did when he peddled in Vermont. Wanamaker opened a store selling men's clothes in Philadelphia in 1861, with his brother-in-law Nathan Brown. In 1876 he turned a railroad freight building into the Grand Depot.

Wanamaker promised customers their money back on anything they returned. In 1879 he bought a whole page of a newspaper for one advertisement. It was the first full-page newspaper advertisement bought by an American department store.

**Postmaster General.** From 1889 to 1893, Wanamaker was the US Postmaster General, the official in charge of the national mail service. He pushed for rural free delivery, a service in which postal workers carried mail to farm families' homes. Later, mail carriers on those rural routes brought catalogs from mail-order companies, his competitors, to the same farms.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-wanamaker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="People sold at auction: Savannah, 1859" -->
Slave dealers advertised sales of people in the newspapers, the same way sellers advertised goods. Pierce Mease Butler held people in slavery on Georgia plantation land. On March 2 and 3, 1859, he sold between 429 and 436 of them, men, women and children, to pay his gambling debts. The sale took place at the Ten Broeck Race Course, a racetrack near Savannah, Georgia. It was the largest single sale of enslaved people recorded in the United States.

**The advertising.** Joseph Bryan, a slave dealer in Savannah, ran the sale. He advertised it for weeks in the Savannah newspapers and in papers in seven other Southern cities, from Richmond to New Orleans. The sale brought Butler $303,850. It rained hard on both days.

The terms of the sale said that people would be "sold in families," but the same terms sometimes kept a husband and wife from being bought together. Different buyers bought the husband and the wife, and each buyer took away the person he had paid for. Black Georgians remembered the two days as "the weeping time."

Mortimer Thomson, a reporter for the *New York Tribune*, went to the sale pretending to be a buyer. He wrote a long account of what he saw, and the *Tribune* printed it under the title "What Became of the Slaves on a Georgia Plantation?"
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chain stores and the five-cent store" -->
George Gilman started a business in New York selling tea and coffee in 1859. Most company histories identify George Huntington Hartford as his partner from the start. Other accounts state that Hartford began by working for Gilman and rose to run the business later.

**The A&P.** The owners named the business the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company in 1869, to mark the finishing of the first railroad across the country, and people called it the A&P. It grew into a chain store, one of many stores that belong to the same owners and sell the same kinds of goods. The A&P had 70 stores by 1878 and nearly 200 by 1900.

Frank Winfield Woolworth opened a store in Utica, New York, on February 22, 1879. Everything in it cost five cents. The store did well for about two weeks and then failed. On June 21, 1879, he opened another five-cent store in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, and that one succeeded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising agencies and patent medicines" -->
Francis Wayland Ayer was 21 when he started an advertising agency in Philadelphia in 1869. He planned advertisements for sellers and bought space for them in newspapers and magazines. His father, Nathan Wheeler Ayer, gave him $250, and Francis named the business after him: N. W. Ayer & Son. It is usually called the first advertising agency in the country.

Ayer showed his customers what each newspaper charged for space. That way the sellers knew he was working for them and not for the newspapers.

Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound, a patent medicine, went on sale in the middle of the 1870s. Its advertisements stated that it cured almost any "female complaint," meaning a health problem of women. Smithsonian museum records and other histories state that the mixture was 18 to 20 percent alcohol. Its label stated that the alcohol was there to dissolve and preserve the other ingredients.

Pinkham's sons put her picture in the newspaper advertisements. She asked customers to write to her personally, and she used their letters to sell more of the compound.

**A syrup for babies.** Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup was older. Jeremiah Curtis and his partner, Benjamin A. Perkins, began selling it in 1849, to mothers to give to babies who were teething, growing their first teeth. The syrup held alcohol and morphine, about 65 milligrams of morphine in each ounce. Morphine is a strong painkilling drug. It slows a person's breathing, and too much can kill, especially a child.

One teaspoon of the syrup held enough morphine to kill an average child. The makers advertised it on recipe cards, in newspapers, on calendars and on trade cards, small printed cards given out to customers.

Members of Congress passed a food and drug law in 1906. Under it, makers had to list the drugs in their medicines on the label. Some time after that law, the makers of Mrs. Winslow's syrup took out the morphine and dropped the word "soothing" from its name.

The American Medical Association, the national organization of doctors, printed a book in 1911 called *Nostrums and Quackery*, which means fake cures and the selling of fake medicine. The book listed Mrs. Winslow's syrup under the heading "Baby Killers." No one counted how many children the morphine in the syrup killed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mail order reaches the farm" -->
Aaron Montgomery Ward mailed his first catalog, a printed list of goods that customers could order by mail, from Chicago on August 18, 1872. It was one sheet of paper. It listed 163 items by the count of the Chicago History Museum, or about 150 by the count of the reference work Encyclopedia.com.

Leaders of the Illinois Grange, an organization of farmers, made Ward their buyer late in 1872. His catalog grew to 32 pages in 1874 and to 152 pages in 1876, with about 3,000 items.

Richard Warren Sears began selling watches in 1886, while he was the railroad worker in charge of a station in Minnesota. He moved his watch business to Chicago, where he worked with a watchmaker, Alvah Roebuck. In 1893 their company became Sears, Roebuck and Company, and that year its catalog ran to 196 pages.

**Mail to the farm.** In 1890 nearly 41 million Americans, about 65 out of every 100, lived in the countryside. Many city people had their mail brought to their homes for free from 1863, but farm families had to pick up their mail at the post office. A Postal Service history describes farmers who had to unhitch the horse from the plow and go five or six miles into town to get it.

Postal workers began bringing mail to farm families' homes in West Virginia on October 1, 1896, on three routes that started from Charles Town, Halltown and Uvilla. This service was called rural free delivery. Within a year, 44 routes were running in 29 states.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Aaron Montgomery Ward

> **Who:** A traveling salesman who worked out that a farm family could buy from a printed list instead of from a country store.
> **When and where:** Chicago, from August 18, 1872.

**A newspaper called his company a "swindling firm," then took it back.**

Aaron Montgomery Ward built his mail-order business on a complaint he heard from farmers about their country stores. He had worked as a clerk at the Chicago store that later became Marshall Field's, and then as a traveling salesman for a St. Louis company that sold goods in bulk to stores.

On the road, Ward heard the farmers' main complaint. Country general stores charged high prices and had few goods to choose from. The farmers blamed the middlemen, the sellers between the maker and the buyer, for taking too much profit. Ward's plan was to buy straight from the factories, pay cash and sell straight to customers by mail.

The Chicago Fire of 1871 destroyed Ward's savings. In 1872 he started over with $1,600 of his own and $800 from his brother-in-law, George R. Thorne.

**"Grangers, Beware!"** Editors at the *Chicago Tribune* printed an article with that heading on November 8, 1873. It described Montgomery Ward & Co. as a "swindling firm" that hid behind a post-office box. Ward sued the *Tribune* for libel, printing something false that harms a person's name, and asked for $20,000.

The editors printed a notice taking back the charge on December 24, 1873. In it they called Ward's company "a bona fide firm" (a real one), "composed of respectable persons, and doing a perfectly legitimate business."

Ward promised that customers could send goods back for their money. His 1875 catalog included the words "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back." The Encyclopedia.com entry on Ward records the promise a year earlier, in 1874. When he died in 1913, his company sold $40 million worth of goods a year and had 6,000 workers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aaron-montgomery-ward" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-sears" name="Richard Sears" movie="Mr. Sears' Catalogue (American Experience, 1989)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Richard Sears

> **Who:** A railroad station agent who started by selling watches and went on to build the Sears catalog.
> **When and where:** Minnesota in 1886, then Chicago.
> **Movie:** *Mr. Sears' Catalogue* (1989), a documentary in the *American Experience* series on PBS, about Richard Sears and his catalog.

**A box of watches a jeweler refused, sold for $14 each.**

Richard Sears was 22 when 1886 began, and he was the station agent for the Minneapolis and St. Paul Railroad at North Redwood, Minnesota. That is the station *Encyclopaedia Britannica* identifies. The Encyclopedia.com entry identifies Redwood Falls, the town next door, instead.

That year a jeweler refused a shipment of watches that came to Sears's station. Sears got permission to sell the watches himself, and he sold them to other station agents along the line for $14 each. Then he quit the railroad and started the R. W. Sears Watch Company in Minneapolis. He moved the business to Chicago, where he hired a watchmaker, Alvah C. Roebuck, to repair watches that customers sent back.

**The big book.** Sears wrote the words in the catalog himself. At its biggest, the Sears catalog ran to about 1,500 pages and listed about 100,000 items. The documentary *Mr. Sears' Catalogue* states that it went to about 20 million people at its height. The company's own counts and later accounts differ, so these figures are estimates.

Sears resigned as president of the company in 1908, by the date in *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, or in 1909, by Encyclopedia.com's. He died on September 28, 1914.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-sears" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="persis-albee" name="Persis Foster Eames Albee" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Persis Foster Eames Albee

> **Who:** The first woman to sell door to door for the company that later became Avon.
> **When and where:** Winchester, New Hampshire, and across the Northeast, from 1886.

**At 50, she took perfume from door to door.**

Persis Foster Eames Albee was 50 years old in 1886 when David McConnell hired her to sell perfume door to door. McConnell had sold books from house to house. He gave away perfume as a free gift with the books, and he found that customers liked the perfume more than the books. He started a perfume company, the California Perfume Company. It later became Avon.

Albee, who lived in Winchester, New Hampshire, became the company's first General Traveling Agent. Her product was the Little Dot Perfume Set, which held five scents: violet, white rose, hyacinth, lily of the valley and heliotrope, a small purple flower. She sold it from house to house in Cheshire County, New Hampshire, and then across the Northeast, and she found other women to sell for the company and trained them.

Most of what is known about Albee comes from Avon's own history of itself.

**Selling without a vote.** When Albee started selling, women could vote in only three western territories, lands that were not yet states: Wyoming, Utah and Washington. In 1887, lawmakers in Congress took the vote away from women in Utah, and judges on Washington's highest court struck down that territory's law.

A change called the Nineteenth Amendment became part of the Constitution in 1920, 34 years after Albee started selling. It states that no state and no federal official may keep a citizen from voting because of whether the citizen is a man or a woman.

- 1872: Montgomery Ward mails his first catalog, one sheet of paper.
