## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Peddlers carried goods from house to house in the first half of the 1800s, selling to farm families who lived far from any store. In New York, sellers found new ways to bring in customers. P. T. Barnum used newspaper advertising to sell tickets to shows, and many of his claims were false. His first show was an enslaved woman. In 1846, A. T. Stewart opened a store in New York with several floors of goods and a set price on each one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The peddler's trade" -->
A peddler traveled a route through the countryside and sold goods at people's doors. Some walked with a pack on their back. Others drove a wagon. They carried tinware, clocks, pins, needles, combs, books and patent medicines, which were bottled remedies sold under a brand name. The tin peddlers from Connecticut were the best known. Tinware means pots, pans, cups and other things made of thin sheets of metal coated with tin.

A farm family many miles from a store could buy these goods at its own door.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-guild" name="James Guild" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Guild

> **Who:** A Vermont farm boy who set out to peddle with a trunk of goods and wrote down what happened to him.
> **When and where:** Tunbridge, Vermont, and the roads beyond it, 1818 to 1824.

James Guild wrote one of the few accounts of peddling by a peddler himself. He was born in Vermont, and from the age of 9 he was bound out to work on a farm. Being bound out meant he had to work for a farmer until he turned 21. He turned 21 on July 9, 1818. In his own words, "My disposition would not allow me to work on a farm."

Guild held a note, a written promise that someone would pay him money. He could not turn the note into cash, so he traded it for "a trunk of goods." The first entry in his journal is headed "Tunbridge Oct, 5th 1818." Tunbridge is a town about thirty miles from Montpelier. He felt ashamed of the work. He wondered why he "should stoop so low as to follow so mean a calling." Here mean meant low, and a calling was a trade.

At each house he asked, in his own spelling, "do you wish to by some harecombs, needles, buttons, buttonmolds, sewing silk." Customers who wanted to buy "would want to banter until they could get it for nothing." To banter here meant to haggle, or argue over the price.

At first he sold very little. On the road toward the Dog River, he wrote, "I think I traveled 3 days and selling but 30 cent worth of goods." At poor houses that wanted "a needle or a few pins," he gave them away, and he believed he gave away more than he sold. He killed a partridge with a stone and traded it for "a dish of bread and milk." A flood had carried off a bridge, so he crossed the river on a log and fell in with his trunk.

Over the next six years, Guild went from peddling to mending pots and pans as a tinker, then to teaching school, then to painting portraits. In the end he went to London. In 1937 the Vermont Historical Society printed his journal as *From Tunbridge, Vermont, to London, England*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-guild" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pt-barnum-marketplace" name="P.T. Barnum" movie="The Circus (American Experience, 2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### P.T. Barnum

> **Who:** A showman who sold tickets through advertising, and whose first paid show was an enslaved woman he exhibited under a false claim.
> **When and where:** New York City and towns across the Northeast, from 1835.
> **Movie:** *The Circus* (2018), a two-part documentary in the *American Experience* series on PBS. It is about the American circus and tells a large part of Barnum's story.

Phineas Taylor Barnum made his first money in show business by exhibiting an enslaved woman named Joice Heth and lying about her age. In 1835 a man named R. W. Lindsay of Kentucky agreed by contract to sell Barnum possession of Heth, so that Barnum could put her on show. Heth was Black, elderly and blind. Barnum advertised her as George Washington's nurse and said she was 161 years old.

Barnum and his partner, Levi Lyman, showed Heth to paying crowds for seven months. They showed her in taverns, inns, museums, railway houses and concert halls in cities and towns across the Northeast. When fewer people came, Barnum spread a rumor in the newspapers that she was a machine and not a person. People came back to see for themselves.

Joice Heth died in February 1836. Barnum then sold tickets to a dissection of her body. A dissection is the cutting open of a dead body to look at its insides. On February 25, 1836, at the City Saloon in New York, 1,500 people paid 50 cents each to watch. Dr. David L. Rogers, a surgeon, cut open her body. Doctors and medical students were in the crowd. Rogers examined her heart, her lungs, her brain and the bones of her skull. He concluded that she had been no more than 75 years old, or at the most 80.

Barnum called what he did humbug. A humbug is a trick or a false claim made to fool people. He called himself the "Prince of Humbug." In 1865 he published a book called *The Humbugs of the World*. In it he described humbug in a way that left out crimes and outright swindles, which are tricks to cheat people out of their money.

In 2017 a musical movie about Barnum came out, called *The Greatest Showman*. Its story is mostly made up, and it leaves out Joice Heth.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pt-barnum-marketplace" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Barnum's American Museum" -->
In 1841 Barnum bought John Scudder's American Museum, at Broadway and Ann Street in New York. A day's ticket cost 25 cents.

In 1842 Barnum showed the Feejee Mermaid there. His advertisements carried pictures of a beautiful creature, half woman and half fish. The object in the museum was the head and upper body of a monkey sewn to the tail of a fish. It drew large crowds in New York for about a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Marble Palace" -->
Alexander Turney Stewart opened a dry goods store at 280 Broadway in New York on September 21, 1846. Dry goods means cloth, clothing and sewing supplies. The building was faced with marble from Tuckahoe, New York, and shoppers called it the Marble Palace.

Two things about it were new. Before, stores sold to shoppers only on the ground floor. The upper floors were used for wholesale, which is selling goods in bulk to other sellers, or as living space. Stewart sold goods on several floors. He also put a standard price on the goods, so customers paid the marked price and did not haggle. He held sales to bring in women customers.

Many writers call the Marble Palace the first American department store. A department store is a large store with many kinds of goods, each kind in its own section, called a department. Historians do not agree on what counts as a department store, so other writers name Macy's or Wanamaker's as the first. The Marble Palace building still stands. It is now called the Sun Building.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, city shoppers and farm families both got new ways to buy. Department store owners in the cities marked a price on every item and gave money back for goods that customers returned. Mail-order sellers mailed catalogs to farm families, who could then order goods by mail. Chain stores and advertising agencies began in these same fifty years, and a sewing machine maker began selling across the whole country on monthly payments. Advertisements also sold medicines that harmed people, and in 1859 a slave dealer advertised the largest recorded sale of people in the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paying a little each month" -->
Buying on installments means taking the goods home now and paying for them in small amounts over time. Isaac Singer's company made sewing machines. Edward Clark, a lawyer, was Singer's business partner.

In February 1856, Clark announced that customers could trade in any old sewing machine, even a worthless one, for $50 off a new Singer. In September 1856 he began selling the machines on installments. Furniture makers in New York and clock makers in New England had already sold goods this way. Singer's company was the first business selling across the whole country to do it. A writer for the magazine *Scientific American* said that a woman "would rather pay $100 for a machine in monthly installments of five dollars" than pay $50 all at once.

A Singer machine that sold for $110 could cost as little as $23 to make.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The department store" -->
Rowland Hussey Macy opened a dry goods store at Sixth Avenue and 14th Street in New York on October 28, 1858. On the first day he took in $11.06, according to most accounts, or $11.08, according to others. In his first year he took in about $85,000.

John Wanamaker opened a store called the Grand Depot in Philadelphia in 1876. The building had been a freight station of the Pennsylvania Railroad, at Thirteenth Street and Market Street.

Wanamaker gets the credit for making three practices common: one price for every customer, a printed price tag on the goods and a promise of money back on anything returned. He was not the first to use all of them. A. T. Stewart had set fixed prices in 1846, and sources call Wanamaker "one of the first" to use the others. He made them normal.

With a money-back promise, a customer who bought something poor could return it and get the money back. Mail-order sellers later made the same promise to customers who bought goods without seeing them first.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-wanamaker" name="John Wanamaker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Wanamaker

> **Who:** A Philadelphia merchant who made the price tag and the money-back guarantee normal in American stores.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1861, and the Grand Depot from 1876.

John Wanamaker marked a price on his goods so that every customer paid the same. Bargaining over prices had been normal before. He opened a store selling men's clothes in Philadelphia in 1861, with his brother-in-law Nathan Brown. In 1876 he turned a railroad freight building into the Grand Depot.

A guarantee is a promise. Wanamaker promised customers their money back on anything they returned. In 1879 he bought a whole page of a newspaper for one advertisement. It was the first full-page newspaper advertisement bought by an American department store.

From 1889 to 1893, Wanamaker was the US Postmaster General, the official in charge of the national mail service. He pushed for rural free delivery, which brought mail to farm families' own mailboxes. Later, rural free delivery carried catalogs from mail-order companies, his competitors, to those same farms.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-wanamaker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="People sold at auction: Savannah, 1859" -->
People were also sold as goods, in public sales that were advertised like any other. On March 2 and 3, 1859, Pierce Mease Butler, who held them in slavery, sold between 429 and 436 enslaved men, women and children to pay his gambling debts. The sale took place at the Ten Broeck Race Course, a racetrack near Savannah, Georgia. It was the largest single sale of enslaved people recorded in the United States.

Joseph Bryan, a slave dealer in Savannah, ran the sale. He advertised it for weeks in the Savannah newspapers and in papers in Charleston, Macon, Augusta, Mobile, New Orleans, Memphis and Richmond. The sale brought Butler $303,850. It rained hard on both days.

The rules of the sale said people would be sold in families as far as possible. Under those rules, some husbands and wives were still bought by different buyers. Black Georgians remembered the two days as "the weeping time."

Mortimer Thomson, a reporter for the *New York Tribune*, went to the sale pretending to be a buyer. He wrote a long account of what he saw, and the *Tribune* printed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chain stores and the five-cent store" -->
A chain store is one of many stores that belong to the same owners and sell the same kinds of goods. In 1859, George Gilman and George Huntington Hartford started a business in New York selling tea and coffee. It grew into the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, known as the A&P. The A&P had 70 stores by 1878 and nearly 200 by 1900.

Frank Winfield Woolworth opened a store in Utica, New York, on February 22, 1879. Everything in it cost five cents. The store did well for about two weeks and then failed. On June 21, 1879, he opened another five-cent store in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, and that one succeeded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertising agencies and patent medicines" -->
An advertising agency is a business that plans advertisements for sellers and buys space for them in newspapers and magazines. Francis Wayland Ayer started one in Philadelphia in 1869, when he was 21. His father, Nathan Wheeler Ayer, gave him $250, and Ayer named the business N. W. Ayer & Son after him. It is usually called the first advertising agency in the country. Ayer showed his customers what each newspaper charged for space. That way the sellers knew he was working for them and not for the newspapers.

Sellers of patent medicines also depended on advertising. Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound went on sale in the middle of the 1870s. Its advertisements said it cured almost any "female complaint," meaning a health problem of women. The mixture was 18 to 20 percent alcohol, and its label called the alcohol a way to dissolve and preserve the other ingredients. Pinkham's sons put her picture in the newspaper advertisements. She asked customers to write to her personally, and she used their letters to sell more of the compound.

Jeremiah Curtis and his partner, Benjamin A. Perkins, began selling Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup in 1849. It was sold to mothers for babies who were teething. Each ounce of the syrup held about 65 milligrams of morphine. Morphine is a strong painkilling drug. It slows a person's breathing, and too much can kill, especially a child. One teaspoon of the syrup held enough morphine to kill an average child. The makers advertised it on recipe cards, in newspapers, on calendars and on trade cards, which were small printed cards given out to customers.

In 1906, members of Congress passed a food and drug law that required makers to list the drugs in their medicines on the label. Some time after that law, the makers of Mrs. Winslow's syrup took out the morphine and dropped the word "soothing" from its name. In 1911, doctors writing for the American Medical Association, the national organization of doctors, denounced the syrup as a baby killer. No one counted the children who died after taking it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mail order reaches the farm" -->
Mail order means choosing goods from a printed list, called a catalog, and ordering them by mail. On August 18, 1872, Aaron Montgomery Ward mailed his first catalog from Chicago. It was one sheet of paper listing 163 items.

Late in 1872, leaders of the Illinois Grange, an organization of farmers, made Ward their buyer. With the Grange behind him, Ward had his first steady customers. His catalog grew to 32 pages in 1874 and to 152 pages in 1876, with about 3,000 items.

In 1886 Richard Warren Sears, a railroad station agent in Minnesota, began selling watches. He moved his watch business to Chicago. There he worked with a watchmaker, Alvah Roebuck. In 1893 their company became Sears, Roebuck and Company, and that year its catalog ran to 196 pages.

Before 1896, a farm family had to fetch its mail from the nearest post office, which could be miles away. On October 1, 1896, postal workers began bringing mail to farm families' own mailboxes on three routes, out of Charles Town, Halltown and Uvilla, in West Virginia. This service was called rural free delivery. Within a year, 44 routes were running in 29 states.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Aaron Montgomery Ward

> **Who:** A traveling salesman who worked out that a farm family could buy from a printed list instead of from a country store.
> **When and where:** Chicago, from August 18, 1872.

Aaron Montgomery Ward built his mail-order business on a complaint he heard from farmers about their country stores. From 1865 he worked as a clerk at the Chicago store that later became Marshall Field's. Then he worked for a wholesale dry goods company, Willis, Gregg and Brown. After that he traveled as a salesman for a St. Louis wholesaler, Walter M. Smith and Company.

On the road, Ward heard the farmers' main complaint. Country general stores charged high prices and had few goods to choose from. The farmers also blamed the middlemen, the sellers between the maker and the buyer, for taking too much profit. Ward's plan was to buy straight from the factories, pay cash and sell straight to customers by mail.

The Chicago Fire of 1871 destroyed Ward's savings. In 1872 he started over with $1,600 of his own and $800 from his brother-in-law, George R. Thorne.

On November 8, 1873, the *Chicago Tribune* printed an article headed "Grangers, Beware!" It called Montgomery Ward & Co. a "swindling firm" that hid behind a post-office box. Ward sued the *Tribune* for libel and asked for $20,000. Libel is printing something false that harms a person's name. On December 24, 1873, the editors of the *Tribune* printed a correction. It called Ward's company "a bona fide firm, composed of respectable persons, and doing a perfectly legitimate business." Bona fide means real.

Ward promised that customers could send goods back for their money. His 1875 catalog carried the words "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back." One source dates the promise a year earlier, to 1874. When he died in 1913, his company sold $40 million worth of goods a year and had 6,000 workers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aaron-montgomery-ward" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-sears" name="Richard Sears" movie="Mr. Sears' Catalogue (American Experience, 1989)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Richard Sears

> **Who:** A railroad station agent who started by selling watches and went on to build the Sears catalog.
> **When and where:** Minnesota in 1886, then Chicago.
> **Movie:** *Mr. Sears' Catalogue* (1989), a documentary in the *American Experience* series on PBS, about Richard Sears and his catalog.

Richard Sears began his business with a shipment of watches that someone else refused. He was born on December 7, 1863, in Stewartville, Minnesota. In 1886 he was a station agent for the Minneapolis and St. Paul Railroad, at North Redwood, Minnesota. Some sources name the station as Redwood Falls, the town next to North Redwood. He was 22 when the year began and 23 by its end.

That year a jeweler refused a shipment of watches that came to Sears's station. Sears got permission to sell the watches himself. He sold them to other station agents along the line for $14 each. Then he quit the railroad and started the R. W. Sears Watch Company in Minneapolis. He moved the business to Chicago, where he hired a watchmaker, Alvah C. Roebuck, to repair watches that customers sent back.

Sears wrote the words in the catalog himself. At its biggest, the Sears catalog ran to about 1,500 pages and listed about 100,000 items. The documentary *Mr. Sears' Catalogue* says it went to about 20 million people in those years. The company's own counts and later accounts give different numbers, so these figures are estimates.

Sears resigned as president of the company in 1908 or 1909. Sources give both years. He died on September 28, 1914.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-sears" -->

### Persis Foster Eames Albee

> **Who:** The first woman to sell door to door for the company that later became Avon.
> **When and where:** Winchester, New Hampshire, and across the Northeast, from 1886.

Persis Foster Eames Albee was 50 years old in 1886 when David McConnell hired her to sell perfume door to door. McConnell had sold books from house to house. He gave away perfume as a free gift with the books, and he found that customers liked the perfume more than the books. He started a perfume company, the California Perfume Company. It later became Avon.

Albee lived in Winchester, New Hampshire. She became the company's first General Traveling Agent. She sold the Little Dot Perfume Set, which held five scents: violet, white rose, hyacinth, lily of the valley and heliotrope. She sold it from house to house in Cheshire County, New Hampshire, and then across the Northeast. She also found other women to sell for the company and trained them.

In 1886, women in the United States could not vote. The Nineteenth Amendment to the Constitution gave them that right 34 years later. Most of what is known about Albee comes from Avon's own history of itself.
