## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The number of American newspapers grew from about 900 in the 1830s to 2,526 in 1850. The 1850 census, the government's count of the people, found half a billion copies printed that year, for a population of about 23 million. From 1833 a New York printer sold a daily paper for one cent, when most daily papers cost six cents. From 1844 news could travel along a telegraph wire, faster than any horse or train.

Free Black New Yorkers started the first Black-owned newspaper in 1827. Leaders of the Cherokee Nation started the first Native American newspaper in 1828. Men who defended slavery attacked antislavery editors. In 1835 men of the Georgia Guard, a militia of armed men from the state of Georgia, seized the Cherokee press. That same year Amos Kendall, the Postmaster General, who ran the post office, let Southern postmasters hold back antislavery papers. In Illinois in 1837, a mob killed an editor who was defending his press.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last Sedition Act trials, 1800" -->
The Sedition Act of 1798 made it a crime to print "false, scandalous and malicious" writing against the government, Congress or the President. It was still the law in 1800, and federal judges used it that year against two more writers. On May 24, 1800, in the federal court at Richmond, Virginia, a grand jury charged the writer James Callender with a crime. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof to put someone on trial. The charge was over his pamphlet, a short printed booklet, called *The Prospect Before Us*. In it Callender wrote words that harmed the good name of President John Adams. The trial began on June 3, and the jury found him guilty the same day. Justice Samuel Chase sentenced him to nine months in prison and a fine. The Federal Judicial Center's history of the trials states the fine as $400 in one place and $200 in another.

In Philadelphia the same year, Chase sentenced the writer Thomas Cooper to six months in prison. The same history gives Cooper's fine as $400 in one place and $500 plus court costs in another. The act ran out on March 3, 1801. After Thomas Jefferson became president in 1801, he pardoned Callender, which means he officially forgave him. Jefferson also paid $50 of his own money toward giving back Callender's fine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers by mail, and who could carry it" -->
Newspapers went to readers through the mail. Under the Post Office Act of 1792, which members of Congress passed, readers got newspapers by mail at very low rates. Editors mailed their papers to each other for free. A small-town editor could then reprint news from papers all over the country. Indiana had one newspaper in 1810 and 73 by 1840. In 1840 the United States had about 17 million people, and Europe had about 230 million. Even so, more copies of American newspapers went out each week than of all the newspapers in Europe.

By 1831, 76 out of every 100 people who worked for the federal government outside the army and navy worked for the post office. Postmasters outnumbered soldiers, 8,764 to 6,332.

Letters cost much more to mail than newspapers. From 1799 to 1815, one sheet of paper cost 8 cents to send 40 miles or less, and up to 25 cents to send more than 500 miles. A second sheet doubled the price. In 1845 postal officials began charging by weight instead, and in 1847 they began selling postage stamps.

Some enslaved men carried the mail in the South until 1802. That March, Postmaster General Gideon Granger warned a senator that enslaved post riders, "hourly mixing with people," would "acquire information." "They will learn that a man's rights do not depend on his color," he wrote. He feared that post riders would carry an enslaved leader's plan for a revolt "from town to town." Members of Congress then passed a law, and from 1802 to 1865 the postal laws said that mail carriers had to be "free white persons." A mail contractor, a person paid to carry the mail, who broke the rule had to pay a fine. Postal Service historians name no one who was fined. For 63 years, no Black American, free or enslaved, could legally carry the mail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom's Journal, the first Black-owned newspaper, 1827" -->
In March 1827 a group of free Black New Yorkers met to start a newspaper of their own. It was the first newspaper in the United States owned and run by Black Americans. Samuel Cornish and John Brown Russwurm edited it. Cornish was a Presbyterian minister, a preacher in one of the Protestant churches. Russwurm was one of the first Black Americans to graduate from a college in the United States.

*Freedom's Journal* came out on March 16, 1827, from 5 Varick Street in Manhattan. In the first issue the editors wrote: "We wish to plead our own cause. Too long have others spoken for us." Editors of other New York papers printed attacks on Black people, and Cornish and Russwurm answered them. They also wrote against slavery. A year of the paper cost $3. More than 800 copies went out to readers in 11 states and Washington, D.C., and copies also went to Canada, Europe and Haiti. A man named David Walker was one of the agents who sold subscriptions to it, which were orders to receive the paper for a year.

Russwurm came to support a plan called colonization, which meant sending free Black Americans to live in Africa. Cornish opposed the plan and quit in September 1827. The last issue of *Freedom's Journal* came out on March 28, 1829. Russwurm moved to Liberia, in Africa, and started a newspaper there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee Phoenix, 1828 to 1835" -->
The *Cherokee Phoenix* was the first newspaper printed by a Native American nation. In 1825 the members of the Cherokee Nation's council set aside $1,500 for a printing press and type. Samuel Worcester, a missionary, a Christian preacher sent to win people to his faith, helped the Cherokee set up a print shop at New Echota, their capital. New Echota was in land that the state of Georgia claimed. An iron press came by ship from Boston. Workers cast type for the Cherokee syllabary, the writing system that Sequoyah made. In a syllabary, each symbol stands for a syllable. Sequoyah's has 85 symbols.

The first issue came out on February 21, 1828. Columns in Cherokee ran beside columns in English. The Cherokee leaders who ran the paper opposed every plan to move the Cherokee west, and they used it to argue their side.

Georgia and federal officials made the paper hard to keep going. Colonel Charles Nelson commanded the Georgia Guard, a unit that Georgia officials set up to police the Cherokee land their state claimed. He threatened to shut the paper down and to beat its editor. President Andrew Jackson ordered the federal government's yearly payment to the Cherokee Nation paid to separate people instead of to the Nation. That cut off the paper's money. Georgia officials ordered the paper's white printer to leave. A local sheriff sued the editor, Elijah Hicks, for libel, which means printing false words that harm someone. The last issue came out in May 1834.

In 1835 Principal Chief John Ross planned to start the paper again. Men of the Georgia Guard went to New Echota, took the press and threw out the type. Two accounts say they also burned the building, and that Stand Watie, a Cherokee who supported removal, helped them. The records do not name the officer who led them.

In September 1844 a new Cherokee Nation newspaper, the *Cherokee Advocate*, began in Tahlequah, with William Potter Ross as its editor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Elias Boudinot

> **Who:** A Cherokee writer who edited the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper, and later signed the treaty that gave up the Cherokee homeland.
> **When and where:** New Echota, Cherokee Nation, in present-day Georgia, 1826 to 1835, and Park Hill, Indian Territory, 1837 to 1839.

Elias Boudinot helped raise the money for the Cherokee press and edited its paper. Later he signed away the Cherokee homeland. He was born in the Cherokee Nation, in what is now northwest Georgia. Records give his birth year as 1802, 1803 or about 1804. His Cherokee name was Gallegina, which means "the Buck."

He studied at a school run by missionaries in Cornwall, Connecticut. There he took the name of Elias Boudinot, the president of the American Bible Society. He became engaged to Harriet Gold, a white woman. Opponents of the marriage burned the couple in effigy. That means they set fire to dummies made to look like them. The two married in 1826.

That year Boudinot went on a speaking tour that included Philadelphia. He gave a speech called "An Address to the Whites" and collected money for a Cherokee press. He became the editor of the *Cherokee Phoenix* when it began in 1828. He printed reports of how the Georgia Guard treated missionaries in the Cherokee Nation. The Guard's commander, Charles Nelson, threatened to beat him.

In 1830 members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, the president could make treaties with Native nations to move them west. A treaty is a written agreement between nations. In 1832 Boudinot's cousin John Ridge began to speak openly for moving west, and Boudinot began writing in the paper in favor of it. Principal Chief John Ross opposed removal on any terms. Ross pressured Boudinot to resign, and he did on August 1, 1832. In December 1835 Boudinot and a small group of Cherokee signed the Treaty of New Echota. In it they handed all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi River to the United States for five million dollars, and they bound the Cherokee to move west to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma.

Boudinot moved west to Park Hill, in Indian Territory, in 1837. On June 22, 1839, a group of Cherokee who supported John Ross stabbed him to death. On the same day, other men killed his uncle, Major Ridge, and his cousin John Ridge, who had also signed the treaty. The killers were never identified or tried. In 1890 a man named Allen Ross described a secret meeting before the killings. The men at the meeting decided that the three should die under a Cherokee law. That law said anyone who agreed to sell the Nation's land "should forfeit their lives," which means they should be put to death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elias-boudinot" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers against slavery, and the men who attacked them, 1831 to 1835" -->
Abolitionists were people who worked to end slavery. On January 1, 1831, William Lloyd Garrison, a white abolitionist, began *The Liberator* in Boston. Garrison demanded an immediate end to slavery, with no payment to the enslavers. About 3,000 people paid for his paper. The drawing across the top of its front page showed whippings and the sale of children, set in Washington, D.C. Garrison believed that "slavery and freedom of the press cannot exist together." By 1835 members of the American Anti-Slavery Society were printing more than a million papers and pamphlets a year.

In the summer of 1835 members of the society mailed their papers to people in the South. On the night of July 29, 1835, a small group of men broke into the post office in Charleston, South Carolina. They took the bags of antislavery papers, and a mob burned them. Opening or holding back the mail was against federal law. Postmaster General Amos Kendall was a close ally of President Andrew Jackson. He gave Southern postmasters permission to refuse to deliver antislavery papers.

On October 21, 1835, a crowd gathered outside *The Liberator*'s office in Boston, where the Boston Female Anti-Slavery Society was meeting. A newspaper of the time put the crowd at 5,000. A member of the society later described the men as "gentlemen of wealth and standing." They forced the women out and seized Garrison. They tied a rope around his waist and dragged him through the streets. They nearly tarred and feathered him, which means pouring tar over a person's body and then covering it with feathers. The mayor stopped them and had Garrison held in the city jail overnight for his own safety. Garrison later wrote that "every mail" brought letters telling him he had "only so many days to live."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The penny press, 1833" -->
In 1833 Benjamin Day, a New York printer, started *The Sun* partly to bring more work to his print shop. Most daily papers cost six cents. Day sold *The Sun* for one cent a copy, a penny, so people with little money could buy it every day. Boys sold it on the street. Day printed crime stories, and he hired George Wisner to write short reports from the police office. Wisner was the first police reporter in the United States.

In 1835 Day printed in *The Sun* a made-up report that living creatures had been found on the Moon. Sales rose, and critics attacked Day when readers learned it was false. By 1839 *The Sun* sold 50,000 copies a day. Day paid for the paper mostly by selling advertisements. By 1842 New York City had nine daily papers that cost one or two cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Elijah Lovejoy

> **Who:** A white Presbyterian minister and newspaper editor who wrote against slavery and was shot dead by a mob while defending his press.
> **When and where:** St. Louis, Missouri, 1833 to 1836, and Alton, Illinois, 1836 to 1837.

On November 7, 1837, a mob in Alton, Illinois, shot and killed Elijah Lovejoy while he defended his printing press. He was born in Albion, Maine, on November 9, 1802. In 1833 he became the editor of the *St. Louis Observer*, a Presbyterian weekly paper in St. Louis, Missouri. Slavery was legal in Missouri. Lovejoy wrote against it. In 1835 a group of leading men in St. Louis signed a letter asking him to tone down what he wrote. He refused.

On April 28, 1836, a white mob in St. Louis chained Francis McIntosh, a free Black steamboat worker, to a tree and burned him alive. Judge Luke Lawless refused to convict anyone and blamed abolitionists. Lovejoy attacked the judge in the *Observer*. In May 1836 men in St. Louis threatened him with mob violence and ran him out of town.

He moved his paper across the Mississippi River to Alton, in the free state of Illinois. Men in Alton who supported slavery destroyed his press three times. They threw one press from a second-floor window. On November 6, 1837, a fourth press arrived by steamboat. Lovejoy and his supporters stored it in a stone warehouse owned by Benjamin Godfrey and Winthrop Gilman.

On the night of November 7, a mob surrounded the warehouse. Its members said they would burn the building and kill every man inside. About twenty men inside had guns. One of them shot and killed a man in the mob named Bishop. A man from the mob climbed a ladder to set fire to the roof. When Lovejoy stepped outside, men in the mob shot him five times. He was 34. The mob went into the warehouse, broke the press and threw it into the river.

In January 1838 Alton's prosecutors, joined by Illinois Attorney General Usher F. Linder, put twelve of the press's defenders on trial for riot. A jury found Gilman not guilty in ten minutes, and the city attorney dropped the charges against the other eleven. On January 19, 1838, prosecutors tried men from the mob for breaking into the warehouse and destroying the press. Among them were Frederick Drucher, William Carr, James M. Hock, David Butler, Horace Beall, Levi Palmer, and two men named Nutter and Jennings. Linder was now their lawyer. The jury found them not guilty. No one was ever charged with killing Lovejoy.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elijah-lovejoy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telegraph, 1844, and who owned it" -->
In 1843 members of Congress voted Samuel Morse $30,000 to build a test telegraph line from Washington to Baltimore. A telegraph sends messages along a wire as pulses of electricity. On May 24, 1844, Morse sent the first message over the line from the US Capitol: "What hath God wrought." Alfred Vail received it in Baltimore and sent it back.

Morse and his partners tried to sell the right to use his invention to the federal government and to groups of businessmen. No one bought it. Morse hired Amos Kendall, the former Postmaster General, to run the business. Kendall sold the right to use Morse's patent to separate companies, one region at a time. A patent is the legal right to be the only one to make or use an invention. In 1851 the census counted 75 telegraph companies with 21,147 miles of wire. Private companies owned the lines. From 1844 on, many newspapers printed a section headed "News by Telegraph."

In 1846 the war between the United States and Mexico began. Getting fast news of the war now cost much more than news had cost before. Moses Yale Beach owned *The Sun*. He got the owners of the leading New York papers, six counting his own, to share the cost. That group grew into the New York Associated Press.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-douglass-publisher" name="Frederick Douglass" movie="Becoming Frederick Douglass (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A man who escaped slavery in Maryland and started his own newspaper, the *North Star*.
> **When and where:** Rochester, New York, from 1847.
> **Movie:** *Becoming Frederick Douglass* (2022), a PBS documentary about his life, directed by Stanley Nelson.

Frederick Douglass started his own newspaper because he believed that Black Americans had to lead the fight for their own freedom. He was born enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. He escaped on September 3, 1838. In 1841 he spoke about his life in slavery at a meeting of the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society on Nantucket island, and the society's leaders asked him to travel and give speeches for pay. In 1845 he published the story of his life. He then gave speeches in Britain and Ireland, and the money from that tour paid to start his paper.

In the late 1840s Douglass broke with Garrison over whether politics could help end slavery. Douglass came to believe that political action could. On December 3, 1847, he published the first issue of the *North Star* in Rochester, New York. Its motto was "Right is of no sex, Truth is of no color, God is the Father of us all, and we are brethren." Brethren means brothers.

The *North Star* was four pages long and came out every week. It had more than 4,000 subscribers in the United States, Europe and the West Indies. On the front page Douglass wrote that the paper would "attack SLAVERY in all its forms and aspects." He also wrote that it would "promote the moral and intellectual improvement of the COLORED PEOPLE." Its first printer and publisher was William Cooper Nell, a Black man from Boston.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A telegraph wire crossed the continent in 1861, and by 1866 one company, Western Union, owned nearly all the telegraph lines in the country. During the Civil War, Northern papers sent dozens of reporters to the battlefields. Union generals and members of Lincoln's cabinet closed newspapers and had editors arrested without trials.

In 1863 Boston's postmaster hired the first Black American known to work for the federal government. In New York, Joseph Pulitzer and William Randolph Hearst competed for readers, and some of the stories they printed were false. In 1896 postal workers began carrying mail to farm families at their homes. White men drove Black editors out of Memphis in 1892 and out of Wilmington, North Carolina, in 1898. In Wilmington a white mob burned a Black-owned newspaper and murdered Black men in the streets.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pony Express and the wire across the continent, 1860 to 1861" -->
On April 3, 1860, William H. Russell and his partners, Alexander Majors and William B. Waddell, started the Pony Express. Riders carried mail on horseback nearly 2,000 miles, from St. Joseph, Missouri, to Sacramento, California. The route crossed land where mostly Native Americans lived. The riders changed horses every 10 to 15 miles and covered 75 to 100 miles a day. The first run took ten days.

In March 1861 the riders carried President Abraham Lincoln's inaugural address across in 7 days and 17 hours. An inaugural address is the speech a new president gives on taking office. The company charged as much as $5 for half an ounce of mail, when ordinary postage was ten cents or less. On October 24, 1861, workers finished the first telegraph line across the continent. Two days later the Pony Express shut down.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="News of the Civil War, 1861 to 1865" -->
The North had far more newspapers than the South when the Civil War began. In 1860 fewer than 10 of every 100 print shops in the country were in the South. The South had 70 of the country's 387 daily papers, and those 70 sold only about 10 of every 100 daily copies. In April 1861 soldiers of the Confederacy, the Southern states that had broken away from the United States, fired on Fort Sumter. The next day the *New York Herald* printed 135,000 copies, about as many as all the South's daily papers together. Only about 20 Southern daily papers were still printing when the war ended.

The *New York Herald* alone kept more than 40 reporters with the armies. At Gettysburg the telegraph lines had been cut, so A. Homer Byington of the *New York Tribune* repaired the wires near Hanover in return for the first use of them. After the Battle of Antietam in September 1862, George W. Smalley of the *New York Tribune* wrote his story on a train to New York. The *Tribune* had thousands of copies on the streets the second morning after the battle. The Associated Press was a group of New York papers that shared news, and Southern papers could no longer get its news. So Southern publishers started their own Press Association in 1862.

Photographs of the time could not show action or reach many readers quickly. Weekly papers such as *Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper* and *Harper's Weekly* sent artists to draw the battles. Engravers carved the drawings into blocks of wood for printing. Readers saw the pictures within about a week.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Editors arrested and papers closed, 1861 to 1864" -->
During the Civil War, Union officers and members of Lincoln's cabinet shut down newspapers and arrested editors, most of the time without any court case. The First Amendment states that Congress shall make no law "abridging the freedom ... of the press," which means cutting it back. From April 1861, Lincoln's officials censored reporters' telegraph messages to and from Washington. To censor means to control what people are allowed to send or print. Soldiers arrested editors and closed their presses. Military courts sent some editors south into the Confederacy.

Secretary of State William Seward ordered the arrest of an editor of the *Freeman's Journal*. Officials held the editor for 11 weeks and then let him go without a trial. Secretary of War Edwin Stanton allowed a military governor to destroy the office of the *Sunday Chronicle* in Washington. In Canton, Ohio, Archibald McGregor edited the *Stark County Democrat*. After he printed words that his opponents called treason, a mob destroyed his paper's office. The record does not name the men in the mob. A year later soldiers arrested him without saying what the charge was.

In 1863 General Ambrose Burnside closed the *Chicago Times*, a paper that backed the Democratic Party. Lincoln canceled Burnside's order. He said he was "embarrassed" that his general had failed to weigh the needs of the Union against "the Liberty of the Press."

In May 1864 two New York reporters printed a fake announcement signed with Lincoln's name. It called for a day of fasting and a much larger draft, the government's order for men to join the army. The two men did it to change the price of gold. The *New York World* had printed it. Lincoln ordered General John A. Dix to arrest the *World*'s editors and take over its offices. He canceled the order once it was clear the two reporters had forged the announcement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Black postal workers, 1863 to 1869" -->
From 1802 to 1865, federal law had barred anyone but "free white persons" from carrying the mail. In early 1863 John Palfrey, the postmaster of Boston, hired William Cooper Nell as a clerk. On March 3, 1865, members of Congress ended the rule. Their new law said that "no person, by reason of color, shall be disqualified from employment in carrying the mails."

In June 1869 James Christian began delivering mail in Richmond, Virginia. He was the first Black letter carrier known in the United States. Richmond's postmaster, Elizabeth Van Lew, hired him along with other Black clerks and letter carriers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-cooper-nell" name="William Cooper Nell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Cooper Nell

> **Who:** A Black writer and printer from Boston who printed Frederick Douglass's first newspaper and became the first Black American known to work for the federal government.
> **When and where:** Boston, and Rochester, New York, 1840s to 1874.

William Cooper Nell was born in Boston. In the 1840s he went to Rochester, New York, and worked for nearly two years with Frederick Douglass. He was the first printer and publisher of Douglass's newspaper, the *North Star*.

The members of the Boston School Committee, who ran the city's public schools, kept Black children apart from white children. In 1844 Nell and other Black Bostonians sent the committee a petition, a written request signed by many people, asking it to open the schools to their children. Eleven years later, in 1855, the governor of Massachusetts signed a law that banned schools from turning children away because of their race. The same year, Nell published a 396-page book, *The Colored Patriots of the American Revolution*, about Black Americans who fought in the Revolution.

In early 1863, during the Civil War, Boston's postmaster, John Palfrey, hired Nell as a clerk at the Boston Post Office. It was the first job in the federal government known to go to a Black American. At that time federal law still said that only white people could carry the mail. Nell worked at the post office until he died in 1874.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-cooper-nell" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One company owns the telegraph, 1866 to 1900" -->
In 1866 Western Union bought its last two big rivals and ended up with more than 90 percent of the country's telegraph business. Percent means out of every 100. Tomas Nonnenmacher of Allegheny College calls it the country's first nationwide industrial monopoly. A monopoly is a company that has no real competition, so it can set its own prices. In almost every session of Congress, members brought bills to control the company or to put the government into the telegraph business itself. Western Union's lobbyists, people paid to persuade lawmakers, blocked nearly all of them.

Between 1867 and 1900, the number of telegrams the company carried rose from 5.8 million a year to 63.2 million. The average price of a telegram fell from $1.09 to 30 cents. Of every dollar customers paid, about 30 to 40 cents was profit for the company.

By the late 1800s the New York Associated Press, run by the owners of a few New York papers, held what the American Antiquarian Society calls a news monopoly. Chicago newspaper owners formed a rival group, the Western Associated Press, and they broke the New York group's hold. In 1892 a new, national Associated Press began.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telephone, 1876 to 1900" -->
Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876. He offered the patent to Western Union for $100,000, and the company's leaders turned it down. In 1900 the United States had about 1.36 million telephones, about 18 for every 1,000 people, or fewer than 2 telephones for every 100 people. Of every 100 telephone calls, 97 were local calls within one town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers in Chinese, 1854 and 1855" -->
Before the Civil War there were already American newspapers in German, French, Spanish, Polish, Swedish, Norwegian, Hawaiian and other languages. Chinese was harder to print because it uses thousands of characters instead of an alphabet. The first Chinese-language paper in the country, *Golden Hill's News*, came out in San Francisco in 1854 and lasted a few months.

The second, *The Oriental*, began there on January 4, 1855. Its editor was William Speer, a Presbyterian minister. Its printers drew the characters on stone with a grease pencil and printed from the stone. The paper closed in 1857. No one tried another Chinese paper until the 1870s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nellie-bly" name="Nellie Bly" movie="Escaping the Madhouse: The Nellie Bly Story (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nellie Bly

> **Who:** A newspaper reporter who pretended to be mentally ill so that she could report from inside New York City's asylum for women.
> **When and where:** Pittsburgh, 1885 to 1887, and New York City, 1887 to 1895.
> **Movie:** *Escaping the Madhouse: The Nellie Bly Story* (2019), a Lifetime television film that its makers call a fictionalized account.

In 1887 the reporter Nellie Bly got herself locked in New York City's asylum for women so she could report what happened to the patients there. An asylum was a hospital where people with mental illness were kept. Nellie Bly was the pen name of Elizabeth Cochran. She was born in Cochran's Mills, Pennsylvania, on May 5, 1864.

In 1885 the *Pittsburgh Dispatch* printed an article called "What Girls Are Good For." She wrote an angry letter to the paper answering it, and its editor hired her. She wrote about working girls and poor neighborhoods in Pittsburgh.

In 1887 she went to work for Joseph Pulitzer's *New York World*. For her first big story she pretended to be insane until she was locked in the asylum on Blackwell's Island. She stayed ten days. When she arrived, a nurse, Miss Grupe, took her to a cold, wet bathroom. The nurses pulled off her clothes while a group of patients watched from the door. A patient scrubbed her with soft soap. Then three buckets of ice-cold water came down over her head, and Bly did not say who poured them.

Bly saw 52 women tied together by wide leather belts locked around their waists and fastened to one long rope. She watched a nurse, Miss Grady, tease a patient named Urena Little-Page until the young woman cried. Then Grady and the other nurses slapped her, knocked her head and choked her, and dragged her into a closet. The marks of their fingers stayed on her throat all day. Other patients told Bly that the nurses beat them if they complained.

Bly's reports ran in the *World* and then as a book, *Ten Days in a Mad-House*. A grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether someone should be charged with a crime, looked into the asylum. Bly wrote that New York City officials had set aside $1,000,000 a year more than ever before for the care of people with mental illness.

Bly then asked her editor to let her travel around the world faster than Phileas Fogg, the hero of Jules Verne's novel *Around the World in Eighty Days*. She set out in November 1889. In France she met Verne himself, who asked about her route. She did it in 72 days, 6 hours, 11 minutes and 14 seconds. The *World*'s contest to guess her time drew nearly a million entries. She died in New York on January 27, 1922.

### Ida B. Wells

> **Who:** A Black newspaper editor in Memphis who counted lynchings, printed what she found and was driven from her city for it.
> **When and where:** Memphis, Tennessee, 1892, and New York City, 1892 to 1895.

Ida B. Wells counted the lynchings in the South and printed the count, and white men in Memphis drove her out of the city for it. A lynching is a killing by a crowd of people, outside the law, with no trial. Wells was born into slavery in Holly Springs, Mississippi, on July 16, 1862. By 1892 she was an editor and part owner of the *Free Speech*, a Black newspaper in Memphis, Tennessee.

In the early morning of March 9, 1892, a white mob took three Black men out of their cells in the Memphis jail and shot them to death. They were Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and a third man whom the records name as Will Stewart or Henry Steward. Moss was part owner of the People's Grocery, a store run by Black people. It was taking business from a white-owned store nearby. Wells wrote that the white store's owner, a man named Barrett, led twelve officers to the grocery at night, and the grocery's defenders fired on them, wounding three. Officers then arrested 31 men, and the mob took the three from among them. Wells wrote that Black people in Memphis asked the authorities to bring the killers to justice, and "No attempt was made to do so."

Wells and the other editors of the *Free Speech* advised Black people to leave Memphis. Wells wrote that Black men "left the city by thousands." Those who stayed stopped riding the streetcars. The streetcar company lost so much money that its managers came to the *Free Speech* and asked the editors to tell people to ride again.

Wells began to study lynchings. She used the lists of lynchings that the *Chicago Tribune* printed each year. In 1892 she wrote that mobs had killed 728 Black people in the past eight years. Mobs and their defenders said again and again that the men they lynched had raped white women. Rape means forcing someone into sex. Wells found that only one-third of the 728 had even been accused of rape. Her list included "the boy Will Lewis," hanged in Tullahoma, Tennessee, "for being drunk and 'sassy' to white folks." It also included a fifteen-year-old girl hanged in Louisiana.

On May 21, 1892, Wells printed an editorial in the *Free Speech*. An editorial is an article that gives the paper's opinion. It listed eight Black men lynched since the last issue, and it described the claim that Black men raped white women as "the old thread-bare lie." Wells was in New York at the time. On May 24 white men in Memphis held a meeting at the Cotton Exchange Building. They sent a group to find the paper's editors. Wells wrote that these men made "open threats of lynching" and did not carry them out only because they could not find the editors. The business manager and half owner, J. L. Fleming, fled the city and was ordered never to return. Letters and telegrams warned Wells that she would be hurt if she came home.

People the paper owed money to then took over the office and sold "the outfit," meaning the printing equipment. Some later accounts say a mob burned or smashed the office. Wells, writing in 1892, described no fire.

Wells did not go back. On June 25, 1892, the *New York Age*, a Black newspaper, printed most of her account under the name "Exiled." On October 5, 1892, Black women in New York and Brooklyn held a meeting at Lyric Hall and raised the money to print it as a pamphlet, a short printed booklet. It was called *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases*. In 1895 she published *A Red Record*, 100 pages of tables of lynchings.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rural Free Delivery, 1896" -->
From 1863 people in cities had their mail brought to their homes for free. Farm families still had to travel to a post office to get their letters and newspapers. In 1890 about 41 million Americans, 65 of every 100, lived in the country. One farmer asked, "Why should the cities have fancy mail service and the old colonial system still prevail in the country districts?"

Postmaster General John Wanamaker argued that rural people, the people who lived in the country, needed the information in newspapers. In 1893 Congressman Tom Watson of Georgia won $10,000 to test home delivery in the country. The next Postmaster General, Wilson S. Bissell, did not use it. Members of Congress added $20,000 in 1895 and $10,000 in 1896.

On October 1, 1896, postal officials began Rural Free Delivery at Charles Town, Halltown and Uvilla, in West Virginia. Within a year 44 routes ran in 29 states. Rural families sent more than 10,000 petitions asking for routes. A petition is a written request signed by many people. Rural delivery became permanent on July 1, 1902, with about 8,500 rural carriers.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yellow journalism and the war with Spain, 1895 to 1898" -->
In New York, Joseph Pulitzer owned the *World* and William Randolph Hearst owned the *Journal*. In 1896 Hearst hired away Pulitzer's cartoonist, Richard F. Outcault, whose comic strip had a character called the Yellow Kid. Pulitzer hired another cartoonist to keep drawing the Kid. People began to call the two men's kind of reporting "yellow journalism." The State Department's historians define it as reporting that put exciting stories ahead of facts. Pulitzer and Hearst printed stories about Cuba's fight for independence from Spain, and some of those stories turned out to be false.

On the night of February 15, 1898, the US battleship *Maine* blew up and sank in the harbor of Havana, Cuba. Officials of Spain's government in Cuba wrote an early report that said the explosion started inside the ship. Hearst and Pulitzer printed rumors that someone had planned to sink it. A US Navy investigation later said a mine outside the ship caused the explosion. A mine is a bomb placed in the water. By early May the war between the United States and Spain had begun. The State Department's historians say the two papers helped make Americans ready for a war, but that the papers alone did not cause it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Black newspaper burned in Wilmington, 1898" -->
In 1898 a mob of white men in Wilmington, North Carolina, burned the city's Black newspaper, murdered Black men in the streets and forced the elected city government out. Alex Manly edited the paper, the *Daily Record*. That year he printed an editorial about relationships between Black people and white people. It challenged what white Southerners claimed about those relationships. Democratic Party leaders, with Furnifold Simmons at the head of their campaign, used the editorial to stir up white voters before the November election.

The day after the Democrats won, a meeting of white men in Wilmington passed a set of demands. They ordered Manly to close his paper and leave the city. They also demanded that the mayor and the chief of police resign. On November 10, 1898, Alfred Moore Waddell, a former Confederate officer and congressman, led a crowd of white men to the *Daily Record* office. About 500 men gathered at the start, and the crowd grew to as many as 2,000. They broke in and burned the building.

Later that morning, members of the white mob shot and killed Black men in the streets of Wilmington. Every person reported killed was Black. The shooting started where groups of Black and white men were arguing, and each side there said the other fired first. A white militia company, the Wilmington Light Infantry, had a machine gun mounted on a wagon, which white businessmen had bought before the election. A militia is a group of armed citizens who serve as part-time soldiers. The coroner, the official who looks into deaths, counted 14 men dead. In 2006 a state commission, a group of people chosen to study the killings, settled on as many as 60 men murdered. The records do not name the men who set the fire or the men who killed.

By late afternoon Waddell and his allies had forced the elected mayor and city council to resign, and Waddell was made mayor. They then banished people from the city, which means they forced them to leave and not come back. Manly came first, and he escaped. Black leaders, successful Black business owners and white Republicans came next. A federal investigation ended in 1900, and no one was charged.
