## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 31 of 37 · News and Communication · 10 eras · about 120 minutes**

**Each new way of carrying news in America, from a runner with a knotted cord to a phone, has come with a fight over who may carry it and what it may say.**

One copy of the first newspaper printed in the English colonies is known to survive, and it is in London. It is called *Publick Occurrences, Both Forreign and Domestick*. Benjamin Harris printed it in Boston on September 25, 1690. It has three printed pages and a blank fourth page, where readers could write more news before they passed the paper on. Four days later the governor and his council stopped the paper and ordered every copy handed back. There was never a second issue.

This chapter follows the news through ten eras. Watch two things as you go. The first is speed. In 1775 riders took five days and seven hours to carry the news of Lexington to Philadelphia. In 1860 the first Pony Express run took ten days to reach California. By the 1920s radio brought the news into homes as it happened. The second is who tried to stop the news, and who was kept from carrying it. Look for governors and judges, mobs that wrecked printing presses, a law that let only free white people carry the mail, and station managers who cut the news off the air.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Did the First Amendment stop the government from punishing people for what they printed?
- Who was shut out of carrying and printing the news?
- Why have so many local newspapers closed since 2005?

**Five minutes?** Read "The station that cut the news: WLBT, 1955 to 1971" in 1950 to 2000.
**Can skip:** "The telephone, 1876 to 1900", "Newspapers in Chinese, 1854 and 1855", "Pictures by wire, 1935", "Television begins, 1927 to 1941". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

News traveled on foot in this land before 1500. Runners carried messages from one nation to another, and nations that spoke different languages talked with their hands. What is known about how they did it comes from two places. One is the accounts that Native nations and Europeans gave later. The other is the old objects that archaeologists, scientists who study the past by digging, have found in the ground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Runners and wampum" -->
The Haudenosaunee have lived for a thousand years and more in what is now central New York. They are also called the Iroquois, or the Six Nations. Five nations joined together before Europeans came, and the Tuscarora became the sixth in 1722.

**The runners.** Haudenosaunee runners called the nations to council, a meeting of their leaders. They also carried news from nation to nation and warned of danger. A runner carried a string of wampum, small beads cut from the white and purple parts of a clam shell, to show that his message was official. His pouch held pounded, dried corn mixed with maple sugar.

The Onondaga Nation keeps the wampum records of the Haudenosaunee. Its people describe how a council is called. Each nation receives a string of wampum that stands for the topic the leaders will meet about. A wooden stick is tied to the end of the string. Its notches count the days until the meeting. As each day passes, someone cuts a notch off the stick.

**Older beads.** Shell beads as old as 4,500 years have been found in the Northeast. Early bead makers drilled them with stone drill bits. Drilling with stone was hard, so their beads were larger than later wampum, and there were fewer of them. After Europeans arrived, Native bead makers drilled with nails and with iron awls, thin pointed tools for making holes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hand signs and trade" -->
People in this land spoke many different languages. Near the Gulf coast and in what is now Texas, nations that could not understand each other's speech talked with their hands.

The first and fullest written accounts of this sign language come from the survivors of Cabeza de Vaca's Spanish expedition, which sailed in 1527. They noted which groups spoke the same language and which groups could understand each other only through signs. Scholars who study these accounts, among them Jeffrey E. Davis, think the sign language was in use across that region well before the Spanish came.

Trade goods moved hundreds of miles. Copper from Lake Superior and shells from the Gulf of Mexico have been found far from where they came from. No record survives of what news the traders passed along with the goods.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 5 minutes**

Newcomers from Spain, France and England sent their news home across the ocean in letters and printed books, and each side told its own version. A Spanish commander wrote to his king about the Frenchmen his soldiers had killed in Florida. A French survivor wrote a book about the same killings, and a printer in France published it. On the plains, Native people made themselves understood to the Spanish in hand signs. On Roanoke Island, English colonists left a message carved in a post.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A letter to a king and a book by a survivor, 1565 and 1566" -->
**The killings.** The Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his soldiers killed French Protestants, Christians who do not belong to the Catholic Church, at Fort Caroline in Florida in September 1565. The fort stood in the homeland of Timucua-speaking peoples. Menéndez's men then killed more of the French in two groups at Matanzas Inlet, south of the fort. Both groups had given up before they were killed.

The National Park Service counts 111 men killed at the inlet in late September and 134 more, with their leader Jean Ribault, on October 11 or 12. Matanzas is the Spanish word for "slaughters." Menéndez gave his reason himself: the Frenchmen were Protestants.

**The letter.** Menéndez reported the killings to King Philip II of Spain in a letter dated October 15, 1565. By his own count, his men cut the throats of 132 men at the fort. The next day they cut the throats of 10 more, prisoners they had caught in the woods. At the inlet, he wrote, the first group of Frenchmen gave up their weapons. "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife." He meant that his men killed them with knives. He spared sixteen.

**The book.** Nicolas Le Challeux, a carpenter who escaped from Fort Caroline, wrote the French account of the killings. A printer in Dieppe, France, published it in 1566. Its title calls the killings the cruelty of the Spaniards against the French king's subjects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hand signs on the plains, 1541" -->
Francisco Vázquez de Coronado and his Spanish expedition met a Native nation on the plains of what is now the Texas Panhandle in 1541. The Spaniards called them Querechos. Pedro de Castañeda, a member of the expedition who later wrote its history, recorded that "although they conversed by means of signs they made themselves understood so well that there was no need of an interpreter," someone to put one language into another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A message carved in a post, Roanoke, 1587 to 1590" -->
John White left 117 English colonists on Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, in 1587 and sailed to England for supplies. The island and the coast around it were the land of peoples who spoke Algonquian languages, a family of related languages. Among them were the Roanoke, the Secotan and the Croatoan.

**The signal.** Before White left, he and the colonists agreed on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of their new place on a tree or a doorpost. If they left because they were in danger, they would carve a cross above the name.

White could not sail back for three years. England's queen took every English ship for her navy in 1588, to fight the Spanish Armada, a great fleet of warships sent from Spain. When White landed on August 18, 1590, the houses were gone and a fence of posts stood around the empty site. The letters CROATOAN were carved on one post "in fayre Capitall letters," old spelling for neat capital letters. The letters CRO were cut into a tree. There was no cross.

White wrote that Croatoan was "the place where Manteo was borne," and he called the people of that island "our friends." Manteo was an Algonquian man who had learned English and interpreted for the English. A hurricane blew White's ship out to sea before he could search there. White never found the colonists.

**Books for Europe.** Readers in Europe learned about this coast from printed books. Thomas Harriot came to Roanoke Island with the English in 1585, and he and John White explored and mapped the area. Harriot published *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* in London in 1588. Two years later the engraver Theodor de Bry, who cut pictures into metal plates for printing, printed it again in four languages. De Bry made his pictures from John White's watercolor paintings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="manteo-news-communication" name="Manteo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Manteo

> **Who:** An Algonquian man, born on Croatoan by John White's account, who learned English and interpreted for the English.
> **When and where:** The Outer Banks of North Carolina, and London, 1584 to 1590.

**He crossed to London in 1584 and came home speaking English.**

The English captains Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe spent six weeks on the Outer Banks in 1584. When they sailed home, Wingina, a leader of the Algonquian people there, chose two men to go with them. They were Manteo and Wanchese.

Both men began to learn English from the sailors during the voyage. The ship reached England in mid-September. In London they met Sir Walter Raleigh, who sent the voyages. By the end of 1584 Manteo could speak enough English to interpret. He sailed home on April 9, 1585, on the *Tyger*, the lead ship of Raleigh's fleet.

**Back on Roanoke.** Manteo helped the colonists of 1587 set up a village near the old English fort on Roanoke Island. On August 13 that year the English baptized him into their church, a Christian ceremony with water that made him a member. They also named him Lord of "Dasamongueponke." When White came back in 1590, he found neither the colonists nor Manteo.

**Not known:** The National Park Service's account of his life says that what happened to Manteo is unknown.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 8 minutes**

The men who governed the English colonies in the 1600s decided who could print news, and they stopped printers they had not approved. Virginia's governor, Thomas Culpeper, and his council, a small group of men who advised him, ordered the printer William Nuthead to stop printing in 1683. Seven years later, in Massachusetts, Governor Simon Bradstreet and his council shut down the first newspaper four days after it appeared.

The colonies also got their first mail service, a rider who carried letters from New York to Boston. In New Mexico in 1680, Pueblo runners carried the plan for a revolt against Spanish rule from town to town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Letters by ship, by tavern and by planter" -->
Most letters in the early colonies crossed the ocean, between the colonies and the countries in Europe that ruled them. In 1639 the lawmakers of Massachusetts, who met as the General Court, named Richard Fairbanks's tavern in Boston as the place to leave and collect letters sent "beyond the seas." Inside the colonies, people sent letters with friends, with merchants and with Native messengers.

Virginia officials around the middle of the 1600s wrote "for public service" on the outside of government letters. Virginia's rulers required each planter to send such a letter on to the next plantation. A planter who failed to do it had to give up a hogshead, a large barrel, of tobacco as a fine. On the big plantations of the South, Postal Service historians write, the messengers were usually enslaved people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first post rider, 1673, and a mail service for all the colonies, 1692" -->
The first post rider, a man on horseback who carried the mail along a set route, left New York for Boston on January 22, 1673. The ride was about 250 miles, through New Haven, Hartford, Springfield and Worcester. Governor Francis Lovelace of New York set up the route with John Winthrop Jr. of Connecticut. The rider was to make the trip once a month. It took him about two weeks.

The monthly service did not last long. The rider's trail became known as the Boston Post Road, and part of it is now US Route 1. In 1683 William Penn set up the first post office in Pennsylvania.

**A mail service for all the colonies.** A man named Thomas Neale got the right in 1692 to run a mail service for all the colonies for 21 years. The right came from the English Crown, the king or queen of England and the royal government. Neale never came to America. Andrew Hamilton, the governor of New Jersey, ran the service for him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia: no printing allowed, 1671 to 1730" -->
William Berkeley was the governor of Virginia. In 1671 he wrote to the king's officials in London: "I thank God, there are no free schools nor printing, and I hope we shall not have these hundred years." He gave his reason. Printing, he wrote, had spread new religious groups, disobedience and attacks on the government.

**Printing stopped.** A merchant named John Buckner brought a printer, William Nuthead, to Virginia in 1682. By February 1683 Nuthead had printed the laws that Virginia's lawmakers had just passed. Governor Thomas Culpeper and his council ordered both men to stop printing, and Nuthead moved on to Maryland.

The next governor, Francis Howard, arrived in 1684 with orders from King Charles II that barred printing in Virginia. The first printing press that Virginia's rulers allowed arrived in 1730, when William Parks opened a print shop in Williamsburg.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first newspaper, 1690, closed after one issue" -->
Printers in Massachusetts had printed single sheets of news called broadsides since 1667, one at a time, with no plan for a next issue.

**Numb. 1.** Benjamin Harris printed *Publick Occurrences, Both Forreign and Domestick* in Boston on September 25, 1690. It was the first newspaper in the English colonies. Harris marked it "Numb. 1," number one, and planned to print it once a month. It had three printed pages and a blank fourth page. Harris's notice suggested that readers could write more news on the blank page and pass the paper on.

**Four days.** Governor Simon Bradstreet and the men of his council ordered the paper "Suppressed and called in" on September 29. That meant it was stopped, and every copy had to be handed back. They said Harris had printed it without their permission.

They also objected to what he had printed. Harris had accused Native allies of the English of "barbarous" cruelty to French prisoners. He had complained that colonial officials told the Mohawks, one of the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee, too much about the colonists' war plans. The paper also carried a rumor that King Louis XIV of France was having sex with his son's wife.

Bradstreet and his council then ordered that no one could print news in the colony without a license, written permission from the government. There was never a second issue of *Publick Occurrences*. One copy is known to survive, in London.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" name="Benjamin Harris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Harris

> **Who:** The London printer who printed the first newspaper in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** London, then Boston, 1679 to 1695.

**Three arrests in London came before his one Boston newspaper.**

In London in the 1670s and 1680s Harris printed pamphlets, short printed booklets, for Protestants who had broken away from the Church of England. He was a Baptist, a member of one of those Protestant groups, and he spoke out against King Charles II.

Harris printed a newspaper without a license in 1679, *The Domestick Intelligence*. English officials jailed him twice. After a third arrest in 1685 he fled to the Netherlands. A year later he sailed to Boston and opened a print shop.

**The paper.** His opening notice in *Publick Occurrences* said he wanted to stop the "many False Reports, maliciously made, and spread among us." The paper reported a fire that burned about 20 houses. It also reported an outbreak of smallpox, a disease that spreads from person to person and kills many of the people who catch it. When the governor and his council ordered every copy called in, they also forbade Harris to print any newspaper in the colony again.

Harris obeyed. Massachusetts officials named him the colony's official printer in 1692. He went back to England in 1695 and started a London paper, the *London Post*, in 1699. He died in 1720.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Knotted cords and the Pueblo Revolt, 1680" -->
Pueblo leaders in New Mexico planned a revolt in 1680 to drive out the Spanish, and runners carried the plan. Spanish soldiers and settlers had come in 1598 under Juan de Oñate to take Pueblo land, labor and food.

**The arrests of 1675.** The Spanish governor Juan Francisco Treviño ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo religious leaders in 1675 for "practicing sorcery," using magic. Spanish officials sentenced four of them to hang. They hanged three, and the fourth man killed himself. They whipped the others in public and put them in prison. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be let go, and the Spanish released them. One of the men released was Po'pay, a Tewa leader from Ohkay Owingeh.

**The knotted cords.** Po'pay planned the revolt with towns that spoke at least five different languages. All of them had to rise on the same day. To set that day, he sent runners with knotted cords. Each town untied one knot a day, and when the last knot was untied, the revolt would begin. The accounts differ on the day planned. The National Museum of the American Indian gives August 11, and the Indian Pueblo Cultural Center gives August 13.

The Spanish learned of the plan and caught two of the runners on August 9. The revolt began a day early, on August 10, 1680. Pueblo fighters killed about 400 people, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in New Mexico. Governor Antonio de Otermín led about 2,000 Spanish settlers out of Santa Fe on August 21 and south to El Paso. The Spanish did not return until 1692.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="catua-and-omtua" name="Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua

> **Who:** Two young runners from Tesuque Pueblo who carried the knotted cords of the Pueblo Revolt.
> **When and where:** New Mexico, August 8 and 9, 1680.

**Their cord had two knots, and the Spanish hanged them for it.**

Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua set out on the morning of August 8, 1680, with cords for the Tano towns. They came from Tesuque Pueblo, whose Tewa name means "village of the narrow place of the cottonwood trees." The first town they reached was Pecos.

After they left Pecos, Christian Pueblo people told Fernando de Velasco that two Tewa messengers had visited the war chief's house. Velasco was a leader of the Franciscan missionaries, Catholic priests sent to turn the Pueblo people Christian. Two Pueblo leaders from the Galisteo Basin who sided with the Spanish also sent word of a revolt to Governor Antonio de Otermín. Otermín ordered the messengers arrested, and the Spanish caught them on August 9.

**Tortured, then hanged.** Spanish officials tortured Catua and Omtua to make them explain the knotted cord. Torture means hurting a person's body on purpose to force him to talk. The surviving accounts do not say how the two young men were hurt.

The historian Charles W. Hackett worked from Otermín's own records. He wrote in 1911 that the two told Otermín they had been given a cord with two knots, to carry to the chiefs of the Tano towns, San Marcos and La Cienega. The two knots stood for the two days left before the revolt. Then the Spanish hanged them. The accounts do not name the men who tortured and hanged them.

When Po'pay heard that the runners had been caught, he ordered the revolt to start a day early. It began on August 10.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 10 minutes**

The first colonial newspaper that lasted began in Boston in 1704, and many of the men who printed the colonies' papers also ran the post office. Printers and postmasters started papers in Boston, Philadelphia, New York, Charleston and Williamsburg. Colonial governors and lawmakers still had printers jailed when the printers offended them. The owners made their money from advertisements, and some of those advertisements offered human beings for sale.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston News-Letter, 1704" -->
John Campbell, the postmaster of Boston, published the first issue of the *Boston News-Letter* on April 24, 1704. It was one small sheet printed on both sides, and it came out once a week. A printer named Bartholomew Green printed it for Campbell. The words "Published by Authority," meaning with the approval of the people in power, appeared in its title from 1704 to 1720, and again from 1723 to 1725.

Most of the first issue was copied from London newspapers of mid-December, more than four months old. Local news filled one column. The *News-Letter* came out every week until February 1776, the first newspaper in the colonies to keep going. Boston had no other newspaper until the *Boston Gazette* began on December 21, 1719. The *American Weekly Mercury* started in Philadelphia the next day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Courant: a paper the government did not approve, 1721 to 1726" -->
James Franklin started the *New-England Courant* in Boston on August 7, 1721, during an epidemic of smallpox, an outbreak that spread to many people at once. He soon used the paper to take a side in a fight over how to stop the disease.

**Inoculation.** The minister Cotton Mather and the doctor Zabdiel Boylston urged people to try inoculation. A doctor put a little pus from a sick person into a cut in the skin of a healthy person. The healthy person usually got a mild case of smallpox and soon got well. Mather had learned of the method from Onesimus, an African man he had owned as a slave since 1706, and from other enslaved Africans.

Almost every doctor in Boston except Boylston opposed inoculation. James Franklin printed attacks in the *Courant* on the people who supported it. Someone threw a lit bomb through a window of Mather's house on November 14, 1721. A note with it read: "Cotton Mather, You Dog, Dam You: I'll inoculate you with this, with a Pox to you." The fuse came off, and the bomb did not explode. No record names the person who threw it.

Nearly 6,000 people caught smallpox in the epidemic, and 844 of them died. Boylston inoculated 248 people, and 98 out of every 100 of them lived. Among people who caught smallpox the ordinary way, 85 out of every 100 lived.

**A month in jail.** James Franklin printed a short item in the *Courant* in June 1722. It said Massachusetts officials were fitting out a ship to chase pirates, which would sail "some time this Month, if Wind and Weather permit." Massachusetts officials read it as a hidden attack on them. The members of the General Court, the colony's lawmakers, had James Franklin jailed for a month. His younger brother Benjamin, then 16, ran the paper while James was locked up. James was let out on July 2, 1722.

The members of the General Court then ordered censorship, checking and cutting what a printer may print before it comes out. Early in 1723 they forbade James Franklin to print the *Courant* unless officials checked each issue first. James then put the paper in Benjamin's name, and it came out that way until it closed in 1726.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Weekly Journal and the Zenger trial, 1733 to 1736" -->
For years New York had one newspaper, the *New-York Gazette*. Its printer, William Bradford, printed for the government and supported Governor William Cosby. In 1733 Lewis Morris, the chief judge of New York, wrote a dissent, a judge's written disagreement with the other judges, in a court case of Cosby's called Cosby v. Van Dam. Cosby removed Morris from office.

**A second paper.** Morris and two lawyers, James Alexander and William Smith, then started the *New-York Weekly Journal* on November 5, 1733. Alexander edited it and used it to accuse Cosby and his officials of tyranny, cruel and unjust rule. John Peter Zenger, a printer born in Germany, printed it. Cosby had copies of the *Journal* burned by the hangman.

**Arrested for libel.** Officials arrested Zenger on November 17, 1734, for seditious libel. Under the law of that time, seditious libel meant printing blame of any official, of the law, or of anything the law had set up, without a lawful excuse. Whether the words were true did not matter.

The court set Zenger's bail, the money paid to a court so that a prisoner can go free until his trial, at £400. Zenger stayed in jail for more than eight months. His wife Anna kept the *Journal* coming out.

**Not guilty.** A jury found Zenger not guilty on August 4, 1735. The verdict changed no law. In New York, a printer could not defend himself by showing that his words were true until 1805, when the state's lawmakers passed a law that allowed it. An account of the trial came out as a pamphlet in 1736, *A Brief Narrative of the Case and Tryal of John Peter Zenger*. Printers reprinted it many times.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" name="Anna Catharina Zenger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anna Catharina Zenger

> **Who:** The printer's wife who kept the *New-York Weekly Journal* coming out while her husband was in jail, and who ran the print shop after he died.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1734 to 1748.

**Instructions came, the story goes, through a hole in the jail door.**

Anna Catharina Zenger was born Anna Catharina Maulin, and she married John Peter Zenger on September 11, 1722. When officials jailed him on November 17, 1734, she took charge of the shop. She kept the *New-York Weekly Journal* coming out for more than eight months while he was held. The writer Ann T. Keene reports the story that Zenger gave her his instructions "through the hole of the door of the prison."

**After the trial.** Zenger became New York's official printer in 1737. He died on July 28, 1746. Anna Zenger then ran the family print shop for more than two years, until their eldest son, John, took it over in December 1748.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Printers who were postmasters, and papers in German" -->
A printer who was also the postmaster got the letters and newspapers from other towns first, and so got the news first. He could also stop the post riders from carrying a rival's newspaper.

**Bradford and Franklin.** Andrew Bradford printed the *American Weekly Mercury* in Philadelphia and also kept the town's post office. He forbade the post riders to carry Benjamin Franklin's paper. Franklin wrote that he got his papers carried only by bribing the riders to take them in secret.

Alexander Spotswood, a former governor of Virginia, was in charge of the post office for the colonies. He was unhappy with how carelessly Bradford kept the post office's accounts, and in 1737 he took the job away from Bradford and gave it to Franklin, who was 31.

Franklin also set up partners as printers in other towns. He made Lewis Timothy the printer of the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston in November 1733. In Virginia, William Parks printed the first issue of the *Virginia Gazette* in Williamsburg on August 6, 1736. Virginia's lawmakers allowed only one printing office in the colony, and they wanted it to print what the government needed first.

**Papers in German.** Franklin tried a German-language paper, the *Philadelphische Zeitung*, in May and June 1732, and it failed. Christopher Sauer, a German printer in Germantown, started a German-language paper on August 20, 1739. His family printed it, under changing names, until 1777. Each month sixteen newspapers from Europe reached Sauer, and he took his news of Europe from them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elizabeth-timothy" name="Elizabeth Timothy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Elizabeth Timothy

> **Who:** A widow born in Holland who ran the *South-Carolina Gazette*. The *South Carolina Encyclopedia* names her the first woman in the American colonies to own and publish a newspaper.
> **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, 1738 to 1746.

**A widow with six children, she owned the paper within a year.**

Elizabeth Timothy's husband, Lewis Timothy, printed the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston under a six-year partnership with Franklin. He died in December 1738 after what was described at the time as "an unhappy Accident," meaning an unlucky one. He left her with six small children and a seventh about to be born. Franklin wrote that she was "born and bred in Holland."

Their eldest son, Peter, was too young to run the shop, so Elizabeth Timothy ran it herself. She printed the next issue on January 4, 1739, and promised to make the paper "as entertaining and correct as may be reasonably expected." By the end of 1739 she had bought out Franklin's share. Franklin praised how exactly she kept her accounts.

She handed the shop to Peter when he came of age in 1746 and opened a bookstore next to it. She died in 1757. In her will she left her children eight enslaved people, along with her other property.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-timothy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertisements for people as property" -->
Newspaper owners made much of their money from advertisements, and some of those advertisements were for human beings. Franklin printed advertisements in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* that slave owners and slave traders paid for. Some offered enslaved people for sale. Others were notices about enslaved people who had escaped, so that readers would help catch them. According to the International Printing Museum, by the 1750s these made up nearly a quarter of the paper's advertisements.

Franklin himself owned enslaved people. According to the Benjamin Franklin House museum in London, he and his household held enslaved people from about 1735 until 1790 and bought at least seven people. Their names were Joseph, Jemima, Peter, King, Othello, George and Bob. Peter and King went with Franklin to London.

Lawmakers in South Carolina made it a crime in 1740 to teach an enslaved person to write.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-news-communication" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Boston printer's apprentice who became a Philadelphia newspaper owner and the town's postmaster.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1718 to 1723, and Philadelphia, 1723 to 1753.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns about his life, shown on PBS.

**At 17 he ran away from his brother's print shop.**

Benjamin Franklin grew rich as a printer, and much of his money came from the advertisements in his newspaper. In 1718 he became an apprentice, a young worker bound to a master for years to learn a trade, to his older brother James, a printer in Boston. Benjamin carried copies of the *New-England Courant* through the streets to the people who paid for it. In 1722, at 16, he wrote letters to the paper under the made-up name "Silence Dogood." He later wrote that his brother "had often beaten me."

**Running away.** When James was jailed in 1722, Benjamin ran the paper. In 1723, at 17, he broke his apprenticeship and ran away. He landed at the Market Street wharf in Philadelphia on a Sunday morning.

**The Gazette.** Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from the printer Samuel Keimer on October 2, 1729. Advertising became the paper's main source of money, and Franklin grew the paper from two pages to four, partly to hold more advertisements.

He began *Poor Richard's Almanac*, a book that came out once a year with a calendar and other useful facts, in 1732. He wrote it under the made-up name Richard Saunders. He said that he sold "near ten thousand" copies a year, and that ordinary people "bought scarcely any other books."

**The post office.** Franklin became the postmaster of Philadelphia in 1737. He wrote that the post office "facilitated the correspondence that improv'd my newspaper." He meant that the job helped him get the letters and news that made his paper better, and with them more readers and more advertisements. British officials named him and William Hunter in 1753 to run the royal post office for all the colonies together.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="news-communication" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 16 minutes**

Printers, letter writers and post riders carried the news and the arguments that led to the American Revolution. Supporters of independence also wrecked the press of a New York printer who took Britain's side. Once the war was won, the new country's leaders fought over how free the press should be.

Lawmakers in three-fourths of the states approved the First Amendment to the Constitution in 1791, and it protects freedom of the press. Seven years later the Federalist members of Congress passed the Sedition Act. Federal judges then jailed newspaper editors who had criticized President John Adams.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Stamp Act and the printers, 1765" -->
The members of Parliament in London, Britain's lawmakers, passed the Stamp Act in March 1765. It took effect on November 1, 1765. Under it, colonists had to pay a tax on newspapers, pamphlets, almanacs and legal papers. Printers had to print on stamped paper made in London, which showed that the tax was paid.

**The tax.** Colonists paid a halfpenny, a small British coin, on each copy of a newspaper printed on half a sheet of paper, and a penny on each copy printed on a whole sheet. They paid two shillings, or 24 pennies, on each advertisement in a newspaper.

**Black borders.** The Philadelphia printer William Bradford printed his *Pennsylvania Journal* on October 31, 1765, the day before the tax began, with thick black borders, like a tombstone. In the place for the official stamp he printed a skull and crossbones. Across the top of the front page he printed that the paper was "EXPIRING," or dying, "In Hopes of a Resurrection to LIFE again."

This William Bradford was born in 1722 and was a different man from the New York printer of the same name. He never actually stopped printing. He put out the next issue on November 7.

John Hughes had the job of handing out the stamps in Philadelphia. Opponents of the tax threatened him with violence and with destroying his property, and he promised not to enforce the act. The members of Parliament voted in 1766 to repeal, or cancel, the Stamp Act.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Committees of correspondence, 1772 to 1774" -->
Samuel Adams proposed a committee of correspondence for Boston, a small group of men chosen to write letters to other towns and share news. Boston's town leaders voted on November 2, 1772, to set up a Committee of Correspondence of 21 men. They did it in answer to news that the colony's judges would now get their pay from the royal treasury, the king's own money.

The 21 men wrote a set of reports on the colonists' rights and on how Parliament had broken them. Printed as the Boston Pamphlet, the reports went to every town in Massachusetts.

**Virginia's committee.** Dabney Carr asked the members of Virginia's House of Burgesses, the colony's elected lawmakers, to set up an 11-man committee on March 12, 1773, and they did. Patrick Henry and Thomas Jefferson were two of its members.

The 11 men wrote to the lawmakers of the other colonies. They asked them to share news of laws from London and of what each colony's leaders planned to do about them. Within a year, lawmakers in eleven colonies had set up their own committees. North Carolina and Pennsylvania were the two that did not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The news of Lexington, April 1775" -->
British soldiers fired on the militia, ordinary men trained to fight when they were called, at Lexington, Massachusetts, on the morning of April 19, 1775. At about 10 o'clock that morning, in Watertown, Joseph Palmer wrote a letter "To all the Friends of American liberty." It said the soldiers had killed six men and wounded four.

Palmer was a member of the Massachusetts Committee of Safety. The colonists' own congress had set up this committee to gather supplies for a fight with the British army in Boston and to spread the alarm if the army marched.

**Copy by copy.** Palmer gave the letter to a rider, Isaac Bissell, to carry toward Connecticut. At Worcester the town clerk, Nathan Baldwin, copied the letter, wrote that the copy was true and signed it. Someone made another copy at Springfield. Other riders then carried copies on, and the alarm reached Philadelphia five days and seven hours after it left Watertown.

Palmer took care to make his letter believable. Seven months earlier, in September 1774, thousands of militiamen had turned out for nothing after false rumors of a British attack.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-bissell" name="Isaac Bissell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Bissell

> **Who:** A 26-year-old blacksmith from Suffield, Connecticut, who carried a written report of the killings at Lexington from Massachusetts toward Connecticut.
> **When and where:** From Watertown, Massachusetts, to Connecticut, April 19 and 20, 1775.

**His name was Isaac, not Israel, and he stopped in Connecticut.**

Isaac Bissell came from Suffield, a town on Connecticut's northern border. He was in Watertown, Massachusetts, on April 19, 1775, when Joseph Palmer handed him the letter about the killings at about 10 in the morning. The letter said that "the Bearer Mr. Isaac Bissell" was "charged to alarm the country quite to Connecticutt." His job was to warn people all the way to Connecticut. The letter asked people along the way to give him the horses he needed.

Bissell reached Worcester in the early afternoon, and the town clerk copied the letter there. On the morning of April 20 the letter reached Connecticut's leaders.

**Too fast to believe.** In Wethersfield, the home town of Governor Jonathan Trumbull, Silas Deane wrote in his journal that the news "was discredited," not believed, "from the suddeness of its arrival." After men from Massachusetts confirmed the news, Trumbull agreed to call Connecticut's lawmakers together.

**The unpaid bill.** Bissell then joined the siege of Boston, where colonial soldiers surrounded the British army in the town. He served as a private in Captain Elihu Kent's company from Suffield. In July 1775 the members of the Massachusetts committee of safety approved his bill as a post rider, two pounds and one shilling. The colony's treasurer then would not pay it without a signed approval from the colony's congress.

**The story that grew.** Many books call the rider "Israel Bissell" and say he rode all the way to Philadelphia. The historian J. L. Bell found that his name was Isaac, that he carried the letter only as far as Connecticut, and that the long ride to Philadelphia is a story that grew up later. Many riders, most of them unnamed, carried the news the rest of the way.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-bissell" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Common Sense and the Declaration in print, 1776" -->
The printer Robert Bell published *Common Sense* on January 10, 1776. It was a pamphlet by Thomas Paine that argued for independence from Britain. Printers printed it at least twenty-five times, sixteen of them in Philadelphia.

**The claims.** Paine claimed in April 1776 that 120,000 copies had sold, and later he claimed 150,000. Moncure Conway, who wrote a book about Paine's life, guessed half a million in 1892.

**Not known:** No sales records survive, so nobody knows the real number. The historian Trish Loughran put the highest possible number at 75,000, and she thinks the real number was much lower.

**The Declaration.** Members of the Continental Congress, the meeting of men sent by each colony to act for all of them, declared independence in Philadelphia on July 4, 1776. Over that night the Philadelphia printer John Dunlap printed about 200 copies of the Declaration of Independence as broadsides.

The members of Congress ordered the copies sent to the new states and to the soldiers, read aloud, and put up in public places. George Washington's soldiers in New York City heard it read aloud on July 9. Twenty-six of Dunlap's copies are known to survive.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-paine" name="Thomas Paine" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Paine

> **Who:** An English-born writer whose pamphlet *Common Sense* argued that the colonies should declare independence.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1774 to 1776, and with the army, 1776.

**He took no money from the sales of *Common Sense*.**

Thomas Paine met Benjamin Franklin in London, and Franklin gave him letters of introduction to people in America. Paine arrived in Philadelphia on November 30, 1774. He helped edit the *Pennsylvania Magazine* and wrote articles under pen names, made-up names that hid who the writer was.

**The slavery essay.** For a long time Paine was named as the writer of "African Slavery in America," an essay printed in March 1775 under the name "Justice and Humanity." The essay calls slavery unjust. Gary Berton of the Thomas Paine National Historical Association later concluded that a Rhode Island minister, Samuel Hopkins, most likely wrote it, so the accounts differ.

**Common Sense.** The pamphlet came out on January 10, 1776. Paine soon fell out with its first printer, Robert Bell. Paine later wrote that he gave the right to print it to every state.

Paine published *The American Crisis* on December 19, 1776. It began, "These are the times that try men's souls." Washington ordered it read to all his troops. Paine died in New York City on June 8, 1809.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-paine" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Loyalist printer's press wrecked, 1775" -->
Colonists who stayed loyal to Britain were called Loyalists, and supporters of independence attacked the printers among them. The New York printer James Rivington printed the *New-York Gazetteer*, one of the most widely read newspapers in the colonies, and he used it to back the British government.

**The press wrecked.** Isaac Sears led a group of Sons of Liberty, colonists who organized against British taxes and rule, from New Haven, Connecticut, to Rivington's shop in New York City in November 1775. The men wrecked Rivington's press and his type, the set of metal letters that a printer puts together by hand to print a page. Type was a printer's most valuable possession. Rivington left for England a few weeks later.

After British soldiers captured New York, Rivington came back with the title of King's Printer, the official printer for the British government there. He printed his paper on the British side until the British left in November 1783.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A postal service for the new country, 1774 to 1792" -->
British officials fired Benjamin Franklin from his job running the royal post office in the colonies in 1774. In the words of the Postal Service's own history, he was "too much of an American."

**A rival post.** The printer William Goddard set up a rival mail service, which he called the Constitutional Post. He warned that letters could be stopped and opened on the orders of the king's ministers, the men who ran Britain's government. What the letters said, he warned, could be twisted into proof of treason, the crime of betraying one's own government. By 1775 Goddard's service had nearly 50 post offices from Maine to Virginia.

The members of the Continental Congress made Franklin their postmaster general, the head of their post office, on July 26, 1775, at $1,000 a year.

**Post roads.** The Constitution, approved in 1788, lists among the powers of Congress the power "To establish Post Offices and post Roads." In 1789 the country had 75 post offices and about 2,400 miles of post roads, for almost four million people. The members of Congress passed a new law about the mail on February 20, 1792. Under it, printers could send newspapers through the mail at very low rates, some for free. The law also forbade postal officials to open people's letters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-katharine-goddard" name="Mary Katharine Goddard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Katharine Goddard

> **Who:** A printer and newspaper publisher who ran Baltimore's post office for 14 years and printed the first copies of the Declaration that listed the signers.
> **When and where:** Baltimore, Maryland, 1774 to 1789.

**Readers could pay for her newspaper in beeswax or pork.**

Mary Katharine Goddard learned printing in her brother William's shop in Providence, Rhode Island, in the 1760s. She moved to Baltimore in 1774. She ran his print shop and his newspaper, the *Maryland Journal*, during his many trips away, and she reprinted *Common Sense* in it.

**Postmaster.** She became the postmaster of Baltimore in 1775. Postal Service historians call her the first known woman postmaster in the colonies. Postmasters then earned a share of the postage instead of a salary. The members of Congress often failed to pay her, so she paid the post riders and the high rent of the office herself. In 1778 she let readers pay for the newspaper in beeswax, flour, lard, butter, beef or pork.

**The Declaration with names.** The Continental Congress was meeting in Baltimore in January 1777. Its members ordered new copies of the Declaration of Independence, and Goddard printed them on January 18. Hers were the first printed copies to show the signers' names. At the bottom she printed "Baltimore, in Maryland: Printed by Mary Katharine Goddard." Nine of her copies are known to survive.

**Pushed out.** Her brother William "apparently forced his sister out" of the *Maryland Journal* in January 1784, Smithsonian Magazine reports. The new postmaster general, Samuel Osgood, replaced her with a man in October 1789. Osgood's assistant, John Burrell, said the job might need "more travelling ... than a woman could undertake."

More than 200 people in Baltimore signed a letter asking Osgood to give her the job back. She wrote to President George Washington and to the Senate, and neither helped her. She ran a bookshop in Baltimore until about 1810, and she died there on August 12, 1816.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-katharine-goddard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The First Amendment and the party papers, 1789 to 1793" -->
The First Amendment, an addition to the Constitution, took effect on December 15, 1791. It states that "Congress shall make no law ... abridging," or cutting back, "the freedom of speech, or of the press."

**Party papers.** Newspaper editors took sides in the quarrels inside Washington's government. John Fenno used his *Gazette of the United States* to push the plans of Alexander Hamilton, the head of the Treasury. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson called Fenno's paper "a paper of pure Toryism," using the old name for colonists who had sided with Britain's king.

Jefferson hired Philip Freneau as a translating clerk at the State Department, paid $250 a year, even though Freneau knew only English and French. Freneau started the *National Gazette* on October 31, 1791. It came out twice a week and had about 1,700 subscribers, people who paid to get each issue. Freneau closed it on October 26, 1793.

Benjamin Franklin's grandson, Benjamin Franklin Bache, printed a paper in Philadelphia called the *Aurora*. In it he attacked Washington and John Adams.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sedition Act, 1798 to 1801" -->
American and French warships began fighting each other in the Caribbean Sea in 1798. President John Adams's party, the Federalists, held a majority in Congress. The Federalist members voted through the Sedition Act, named for sedition, the crime of stirring people up against their government. Adams signed it on July 14, 1798.

**What it banned.** Under the act, it was a crime to write or print "any false, scandalous and malicious writing" against the federal government, Congress or the President. The punishment was a fine of up to $2,000 and up to two years in prison. Unlike the law in the Zenger trial, the act let a person charged try to prove that his words were true, and the jury could decide what the law meant as well as what the facts were.

**Who was charged.** Federal prosecutors and judges used the act mostly against editors of newspapers that supported Jefferson's party, the Democratic-Republicans. Peter McNamara, writing in the First Amendment Encyclopedia, gives the count that scholars long used: 17 formal criminal charges under the act, and 10 people found guilty. The legal historian Wendell Bird later found 11 more court cases against 16 more people. Writers for the National Constitution Center, a museum about the Constitution, count more than two dozen people found guilty, most of them publishers who opposed Adams's party.

Among those charged were Congressman Matthew Lyon of Vermont, William Duane of the *Aurora*, Ann Greenleaf of New York's *Argus*, and Anthony Haswell of the *Vermont Gazette*. Luther Baldwin and Brown Clark of Newark, New Jersey, were charged for words they spoke against President Adams in a tavern.

**Bache.** Federalist supporters physically attacked Benjamin Franklin Bache in Philadelphia. Jonathan Dayton of New Jersey, a Federalist, was Speaker of the House of Representatives, the member chosen to run its meetings. Dayton barred Bache from the floor of the House, the room where its members met, so that he could not report its debates.

A federal judge in Philadelphia, Judge Peters, signed an order for Bache's arrest on June 24, 1798, more than two weeks before the Sedition Act became law. The charge was seditious libel against President Adams, the same crime that officials had charged Zenger with in 1734. Peters let Bache out on bail, and his trial was set for October.

Bache kept printing the *Aurora* through an epidemic of yellow fever, a disease carried from person to person by mosquitoes. He died of the fever on September 10, 1798, at 29, a month before his trial date. His widow kept the paper going, with William Duane as editor. The text of the Sedition Act stated that it would stay in force only until March 3, 1801.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-lyon" name="Matthew Lyon" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Lyon

> **Who:** An Irish-born congressman and newspaper owner, the first person tried under the Sedition Act.
> **When and where:** Vermont, 1798 to 1799.

**He ran for Congress from a jail cell and won.**

Matthew Lyon came to America from Ireland and fought in the Revolutionary War. He went to Congress for Vermont in 1797, and he also owned a newspaper. In 1798 he became the first person tried under the Sedition Act.

**His words.** In a letter that a newspaper printed, Lyon wrote of seeing the public good "swallowed up in a continual grasp for power, in an unbounded thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, or selfish avarice." Adulation is an old word for too much praise, and avarice for greed. At campaign meetings he also read aloud a letter by the poet Joel Barlow, which said Adams should be sent to a "madhouse."

**The trial.** A federal grand jury in Vermont, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to charge someone, charged Lyon with three crimes under the Sedition Act on October 5, 1798. A deputy marshal, an officer who made arrests for the federal courts, arrested him the next day. At his trial in Rutland on October 9, Lyon defended himself. He pleaded not guilty, and in a second plea he stated that the Sedition Act broke the Constitution.

Justice William Paterson of the Supreme Court told the jury that its job had "nothing whatever to do with the constitutionality or unconstitutionality of the sedition law." He meant that the jurors must not decide whether the law broke the Constitution. The jurors came from towns that supported Adams's party. They found Lyon guilty within an hour. Paterson sentenced him to four months in jail and a $1,000 fine.

Lyon was held in the jail at Vergennes. He ran for Congress again from his cell, and the voters of his district re-elected him. He was let out on February 9, 1799.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="matthew-lyon" -->
