## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, news in this land traveled with people. Runners carried messages from one nation to another. Nations that spoke different languages talked with their hands. Most of what is known about how they did it comes from two places. One is the accounts that Native nations and Europeans gave later. The other is the objects that archaeologists have dug up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Runners and wampum" -->
The Haudenosaunee live in what is now New York. They are also called the Six Nations, or the Iroquois. Their runners called the nations to council, carried news from nation to nation, and warned of danger. A runner carried a string of wampum to show that his message was official. Wampum is small beads cut from the white and purple parts of a clam shell. A runner also carried a pouch of pounded, dried corn mixed with maple sugar.

The Onondaga Nation keeps the wampum records of the Haudenosaunee. Its people describe how a council is called. A string of wampum tells each nation what the meeting is about. A wooden stick is tied to the end of the string, and it tells the nation when the meeting will be. As each day passes, someone cuts a notch off the stick.

Shell beads found in the Northeast are about 4,500 years old. The earliest bead makers drilled their beads with stone drill bits. Drilling with stone was slow, so their beads were larger than later wampum, and there were fewer of them. After Europeans arrived, Native bead makers drilled with iron awls and nails. An awl is a thin, pointed tool for making holes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hand signs and trade" -->
People in this land spoke many different languages. Near the Gulf coast and in what is now Texas, nations that could not understand each other's speech talked with their hands. Spanish travelers wrote about this sign language from 1527 on. The travelers noted which groups spoke the same language and which groups could understand each other only through signs. Scholars who study these accounts think the sign language was in use across that region well before the Spanish came.

Trade goods moved hundreds of miles. Copper from Lake Superior and shells from the Gulf of Mexico have been found far from where they came from. No record survives of what news the traders passed along with the goods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish, French and English newcomers could send news home only by ship. Commanders wrote reports to their kings. Survivors and explorers wrote books, and printers in Europe sold them to readers there. Native nations went on sending messages by runner and by hand sign.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A letter to a king and a book by a survivor, 1565 and 1566" -->
In September 1565 the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his soldiers killed French Protestants at Fort Caroline, in Florida. The fort stood in the homeland of Timucua-speaking peoples. Menéndez's men then killed more of the French at an inlet to the south. Menéndez gave his reason himself: the Frenchmen were Protestants.

On October 15, 1565, Menéndez reported the killings in a letter to King Philip II of Spain. By his own count, his men cut the throats of 132 men at the fort and of 10 more the next day. At the inlet, he wrote, the Frenchmen gave up their weapons. "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife."

The French side of the story reached Europe in print. Nicolas Le Challeux was a carpenter who escaped from Fort Caroline. He wrote an account of the killings, and a printer in Dieppe, France, published it in 1566. Its title called the killings the cruelty of the Spaniards against the French king's subjects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hand signs on the plains, 1541" -->
In 1541 the Spanish army of Francisco Vázquez de Coronado met a buffalo-hunting people on the plains of what is now the Texas Panhandle. The Spaniards called them Querechos. Pedro de Castañeda was a soldier who later wrote the army's history. He recorded that "although they conversed by means of signs they made themselves understood so well that there was no need of an interpreter." An interpreter is a person who puts one language into another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A message carved in a post, Roanoke, 1587 to 1590" -->
In 1587 John White left 117 English colonists on Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, and sailed to England for supplies. The island and the coast around it were the land of Algonquian-speaking peoples, among them the Roanoke, the Secotan and the Croatoan. Before White left, he and the colonists agreed on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of their new place on a tree or a doorpost. If they left because they were in danger, they would carve a cross above the name.

War between England and Spain kept White from sailing back until 1590. On August 18, 1590, he found the houses gone and a fence of posts standing around the empty site. The letters CROATOAN were carved on one post "in fayre Capitall letters," and the letters CRO were cut into a tree. There was no cross.

White wrote that Croatoan was the place where Manteo was born. Manteo was an Algonquian man who had learned English and interpreted for the English. He called the people of Croatoan "our friends." A storm drove White's ship out to sea before he could search there. No record shows that anyone from England ever found the colonists.

Readers in Europe learned about this coast from printed books. Thomas Harriot had been there in 1585 and 1586. In 1588 he published *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia*. In 1590 the engraver Theodor de Bry printed it again in four languages. An engraver cuts pictures into metal plates for printing. De Bry's pictures were copies of John White's paintings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="manteo-news-communication" name="Manteo" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Manteo

> **Who:** An Algonquian man, born on Croatoan by John White's account, who learned English and interpreted for the English.
> **When and where:** The Outer Banks of North Carolina, and London, 1584 to 1590.

Manteo crossed the ocean to England in 1584 and sailed home the next year able to speak English. In 1584 the English captains Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe spent six weeks on the Outer Banks. When they sailed home, Wingina, a leader of the Algonquian people there, chose two men to go with them. They were Manteo and Wanchese.

Both men began to learn English from the sailors during the voyage. The ship reached England in mid-September. In London they met Sir Walter Raleigh, who sent the voyages. By the end of 1584 Manteo could speak enough English to interpret. On April 9, 1585, he sailed home on the *Tyger*, the lead ship of Raleigh's fleet.

In 1587 Manteo helped the new colonists set up a village near the old English fort on Roanoke Island. On August 13, 1587, the English baptized him into their church and named him Lord of "Dasamongueponke." In 1590 White wrote that the colonists had gone to Croatoan, "the place where Manteo was borne." When White came back, he found neither the colonists nor Manteo. No record says what happened to him.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies got their first mail services and their first newspaper in the 1600s. The men who governed the colonies decided who could print news and who could not. In 1683 the governor and council of Virginia stopped all printing there. In 1690 the governor and council of Massachusetts shut down the first newspaper four days after it appeared. In New Mexico in 1680, Pueblo runners carried the plan for a revolt against Spanish rule from town to town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Letters by ship, by tavern and by planter" -->
Most letters in the early colonies crossed the ocean between the colonies and England. In 1639 the General Court of Massachusetts, the colony's lawmaking body, named Richard Fairbanks's tavern in Boston as the place to leave and collect letters sent "beyond the seas." Inside the colonies, people sent letters with friends, with merchants and with Native messengers.

In Virginia, around the middle of the 1600s, officials wrote "for public service" on the outside of government letters. Each planter then had to send such a letter on to the next plantation. A planter who failed to do it lost a hogshead of tobacco. A hogshead is a large barrel. On the big plantations of the South, the planters used enslaved people as their messengers, according to the Postal Service's own history.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first post rider, 1673, and a mail service for all the colonies, 1692" -->
A post rider is a man on horseback who carries the mail along a set route. On January 22, 1673, the first post rider left New York for Boston. The ride was about 250 miles, through New Haven, Hartford, Springfield and Worcester. Governor Francis Lovelace of New York set up the route with John Winthrop Jr. of Connecticut. The rider was to make the trip once a month, and each way took him about two weeks.

The monthly service did not last long. The rider's trail became known as the Boston Post Road, and part of it is now US Route 1. In 1683 William Penn set up the first post office in Pennsylvania.

In 1692 the English Crown gave a man named Thomas Neale the right to run a mail service for all the colonies for 21 years. The Crown means the king or queen of England and the royal government. Neale never came to America. Andrew Hamilton, the governor of New Jersey, ran the service for him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia: no printing allowed, 1671 to 1730" -->
William Berkeley was the governor of Virginia. In 1671 he wrote to the king's officials in London: "I thank God, there are no free schools nor printing, and I hope we shall not have these hundred years." He gave his reason. Printing, he wrote, had spread new religious groups, disobedience and attacks on the government.

In 1682 a merchant named John Buckner brought a printer, William Nuthead, to Virginia. By February 1683 Nuthead had printed the laws that the colony's assembly had just passed. Governor Thomas Culpeper and his council ordered both men to stop printing. The next governor, Francis Howard, arrived in 1684 with orders from King Charles II that barred printing in Virginia. The colony had no printer again until William Parks came, around 1730.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first newspaper, 1690, closed after one issue" -->
From 1667 on, printers in Massachusetts printed single sheets of news, each about one event. A single printed sheet like this is called a broadside. On September 25, 1690, Benjamin Harris printed *Publick Occurrences, Both Forreign and Domestick* in Boston. It was the first newspaper in the English colonies. Harris marked it "Numb. 1," which means number one, and planned to print it once a month. It had three printed pages and a blank fourth page. Harris's notice suggested that readers could write more news on the blank page and pass the paper on.

Four days later, on September 29, Governor Simon Bradstreet and the Massachusetts council ordered the paper "Suppressed and called in." They said Harris had printed it without their permission. They also objected to what it said. It accused Native allies of the English of "barbarous" cruelty to French prisoners. It complained that colonial officials told the Mohawks too much about English war plans. It also printed a rumor about the private life of King Louis XIV of France.

The council then ordered that no one could print news in the colony without a license from the government. A license is written permission. There was never a second issue of *Publick Occurrences*. One copy is known to survive, in London.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" name="Benjamin Harris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Harris

> **Who:** The London printer who printed the first newspaper in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** London, then Boston, 1679 to 1695.

English officials had jailed Benjamin Harris for his printing before he ever came to Boston. He was born about 1647. In London in the 1670s and 1680s he printed pamphlets for Protestants who had broken away from the Church of England. A pamphlet is a short printed booklet. Harris was a Baptist, and he spoke out against King Charles II.

In 1679 Harris printed a newspaper without a license, *The Domestick Intelligence*. English officials jailed him twice. After a third arrest in 1685 he fled to the Netherlands. A year later he sailed to Boston and opened a print shop.

On September 25, 1690, he printed *Publick Occurrences*. His opening notice said he wanted to stop the "many False Reports, maliciously made, and spread among us." The paper reported a fire that burned about 20 houses. It also reported smallpox spreading through Boston. On September 29 the governor and council ordered every copy called in and forbade Harris to print any newspaper in the colony again.

Harris obeyed. In 1692 the colony's government made him its official printer. He went back to England in 1695 and started a London paper, the *London Post*, in 1699. He died in 1720.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-harris-news-communication" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Knotted cords and the Pueblo Revolt, 1680" -->
In 1680 the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico planned to drive out the Spanish. Spanish soldiers and settlers had come in 1598 under Juan de Oñate and had taken Pueblo land, labor and food. In 1675 the Spanish governor Juan Francisco Treviño ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo religious leaders. Spanish officials sentenced four of them to hang. They hanged three, and the fourth man killed himself. They whipped the others in public and put them in prison. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be let go, and the Spanish released them. One of the men released was Po'pay, a Tewa leader from Ohkay Owingeh.

Po'pay planned the revolt with towns that spoke at least five different languages. All of them had to rise on the same day. To set that day, he sent runners with knotted cords. Each town untied one knot a day. When the last knot was untied, the revolt would begin. Sources differ on the day planned. One gives August 11 and another gives August 13.

The Spanish learned of the plan and caught two of the runners on August 9. The revolt began a day early, on August 10, 1680. Pueblo fighters killed about 400 people, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in New Mexico. On August 21 Governor Antonio de Otermín led about 2,000 Spanish settlers out of Santa Fe and south to El Paso. The Spanish did not return until 1692.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="catua-and-omtua" name="Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nicolás Catua and Pedro Omtua

> **Who:** Two young runners from Tesuque Pueblo who carried the knotted cords of the Pueblo Revolt.
> **When and where:** New Mexico, August 8 and 9, 1680.

Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua carried the knotted cords of the Pueblo Revolt, and Spanish officials hanged them for it. On the morning of August 8, 1680, the two young men set out from Tesuque Pueblo with cords for the Tano towns to the south. Tesuque is Tewa for "village of the narrow place of the cottonwood trees." The first town they reached was Pecos.

After they left Pecos, Christian Pueblo people told a Franciscan missionary, Fernando de Velasco, that two Tewa messengers had visited the war chief's house. Two Pueblo leaders from the Galisteo Basin who sided with the Spanish also sent word of a revolt to Governor Antonio de Otermín. On August 9 Otermín ordered the messengers arrested.

Spanish officials tortured Catua and Omtua to make them explain the knotted cord. Then they hanged them. The surviving accounts do not name the men who tortured and hanged them.

When Po'pay heard that the runners had been caught, he ordered the revolt to start a day early. It began on August 10.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="catua-and-omtua" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1704 and 1750, printers started weekly newspapers in Boston, Philadelphia, New York, Charleston and Williamsburg. Several of those printers were also postmasters, who ran the post office in their town. Colonial governors and assemblies still jailed printers who offended them. The newspaper owners made money from advertisements. Many of the advertisements offered enslaved people for sale, or offered rewards for the capture of people who had escaped from slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston News-Letter, 1704" -->
On April 24, 1704, John Campbell, the postmaster of Boston, published the first issue of the *Boston News-Letter*. It was one small sheet printed on both sides, and it came out once a week. The words "Published by Authority" appeared in its title from the first issue until 1720. Authority here meant the colony's government. A printer named Bartholomew Green printed the paper for Campbell.

Most of the first issue was copied from London newspapers of mid-December, more than four months old. Local news filled one column. The *News-Letter* came out every week until February 1776. It was the first newspaper in the colonies to keep going. Boston had no second newspaper until the *Boston Gazette* began on December 21, 1719. The *American Weekly Mercury* started in Philadelphia the next day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Courant: a paper the government did not approve, 1721 to 1726" -->
James Franklin started the *New-England Courant* in Boston in August 1721, during an epidemic of smallpox. An epidemic is a disease spreading to many people at once. The minister Cotton Mather and the doctor Zabdiel Boylston urged people to try inoculation. In inoculation, a doctor put a little pus from a sick person into a cut in the skin of a healthy person. The healthy person then caught a milder case. Mather had learned of the method from Onesimus, an African man he held in slavery, and from other enslaved Africans.

Almost every doctor in Boston except Boylston opposed inoculation. The *Courant* printed attacks on the people who supported it. On November 14, 1721, someone threw a lit bomb through a window of Mather's house. A note with it read: "Cotton Mather, You Dog, Dam You: I'll inoculate you with this, with a Pox to you." The fuse came off, and the bomb did not explode. No record names the person who threw it.

About 6,000 people in Boston caught smallpox that year, and 844 of them died. Boylston inoculated 248 people, and 98 out of every 100 of them lived. Among people who caught smallpox the ordinary way, 85 out of every 100 lived.

In June 1722 the *Courant* printed a short item. It hinted that the government of Massachusetts was slow to send a ship after pirates. The members of the General Court, the colony's assembly, had James Franklin jailed for a month. His younger brother Benjamin, then 16, ran the paper while James was locked up. James was let out on July 2, 1722.

Early in 1723 the General Court forbade James Franklin to print the *Courant* unless officials checked each issue first. Checking and cutting what a printer may print before it comes out is called censorship. James then put the paper in Benjamin's name. From then on it came out under Benjamin's name, until it closed in 1726.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Weekly Journal and the Zenger trial, 1733 to 1736" -->
For years New York had one newspaper, the *New-York Gazette*. Its printer, William Bradford, printed for the government and supported Governor William Cosby. In 1733 Lewis Morris, the chief judge of New York, wrote a dissent in a court case of Cosby's called Cosby v. Van Dam. A dissent is a judge's written disagreement with the other judges. Cosby removed Morris from office. Morris and two lawyers, James Alexander and William Smith, then started the *New-York Weekly Journal* on November 5, 1733. Alexander edited it. The paper accused Cosby's government of tyranny, which means cruel and unjust rule. John Peter Zenger, a printer born in Germany, printed it. Cosby had copies of the *Journal* burned in public by the hangman.

Officials arrested Zenger on November 17, 1734, for seditious libel. Under the law of that time, seditious libel meant printing blame of any official, of the law, or of anything the law had set up, without a lawful excuse. Whether the words were true did not matter. Zenger could not pay his bail of £400, so he stayed in jail for more than eight months. Bail is money paid to a court so that a prisoner can go free until his trial. His wife Anna kept the *Journal* coming out.

On August 4, 1735, a jury found Zenger not guilty. The verdict changed no law. In New York, a printer could not defend himself by showing that his words were true until 1805. That year the state's lawmakers passed a law that allowed it. In 1736 Zenger printed an account of his trial, *A Brief Narrative of the Case and Tryal of John Peter Zenger*. Printers reprinted it many times.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" name="Anna Catharina Zenger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anna Catharina Zenger

> **Who:** The printer's wife who kept the *New-York Weekly Journal* coming out while her husband was in jail, and who ran the print shop after he died.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1734 to 1748.

Anna Catharina Zenger ran the *New-York Weekly Journal* for more than eight months while officials held her husband in jail. She was born Anna Catharina Maulin. She married John Peter Zenger on September 11, 1722. When officials jailed him on November 17, 1734, she took charge of the shop and kept the *Journal* coming out every week. Zenger later wrote that he gave her his instructions "through the hole of the door of the prison."

After the trial, in 1737, Zenger became New York's official printer. He died on July 28, 1746. Anna Zenger then ran the family print shop for more than two years. In December 1748 their eldest son, John, took it over.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anna-zenger-news-communication" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Printers who were postmasters, and papers in German" -->
A printer who was also the postmaster got the letters and newspapers from other towns first, and so got the news first. He could also stop the post riders from carrying a rival's newspaper. Andrew Bradford printed the *American Weekly Mercury* in Philadelphia and also kept the town's post office. He did this to Benjamin Franklin's paper. Franklin wrote that he got his papers carried only by bribing the riders to take them in secret. In 1737 Alexander Spotswood, the deputy postmaster general for the colonies, took the post office away from Bradford and gave it to Franklin, who was 31.

Franklin also set up partners as printers in other towns. In November 1733 he made Lewis Timothy the printer of the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston. Virginia got its first newspaper, the *Virginia Gazette*, on August 6, 1736. William Parks printed it in Williamsburg.

Franklin tried a German-language paper, the *Philadelphische Zeitung*, in May and June 1732, and it failed. On August 20, 1739, Christopher Sauer, a German printer in Germantown, started a German-language paper. His family printed it, under changing names, until 1777. Each month sixteen newspapers from Europe reached Sauer, and he took his news of Europe from them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elizabeth-timothy" name="Elizabeth Timothy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Elizabeth Timothy

> **Who:** A widow born in Holland who ran the *South-Carolina Gazette* and became the first woman in the colonies to own and publish a newspaper.
> **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, 1738 to 1746.

The *South Carolina Encyclopedia* calls Elizabeth Timothy the first woman in the American colonies to own and publish a newspaper. Franklin wrote that she was "born and bred in Holland." Her husband, Lewis Timothy, printed the *South-Carolina Gazette* in Charleston under a six-year partnership with Franklin. He died in December 1738 after what the paper called "an unhappy Accident." He left her with six small children and a seventh about to be born.

The partnership said that their eldest son, Peter, would carry on the business. Peter was too young, so Elizabeth Timothy ran the shop herself. She printed the next issue on January 4, 1739. She promised to make the paper "as entertaining and correct as may be reasonably expected." By the end of 1739 she had bought out Franklin's share. Franklin praised how exactly she kept her accounts.

She handed the shop to Peter when he came of age in 1746, and she opened a bookstore next to it. She died in 1757. Her will left her children eight enslaved people, along with her other property.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-timothy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Advertisements for people as property" -->
Newspaper owners made much of their money from advertisements, and some of those advertisements were for human beings. Franklin's *Pennsylvania Gazette* printed advertisements paid for by slave owners and slave traders. Some offered enslaved men, women and children for sale. Others offered rewards for people who had escaped. Franklin was paid for printing them. According to the International Printing Museum, by the 1750s these made up nearly a quarter of the paper's advertisements.

Franklin's own household held enslaved people from about 1735 until 1790. Records show that he bought at least seven people. Their names were Joseph, Jemima, Peter, King, Othello, George and Bob.

In 1740 lawmakers in South Carolina made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to write.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-news-communication" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Boston printer's apprentice who became Philadelphia's leading newspaper owner and its postmaster.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1718 to 1723, and Philadelphia, 1723 to 1753.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns about his life, shown on PBS.

Benjamin Franklin grew rich as a printer, and historians trace much of his money to the advertisements in his newspaper. He was born in Boston in 1706. In 1718 he became an apprentice to his older brother James, a printer. An apprentice is a young worker bound to a master for years to learn a trade. Benjamin carried copies of the *New-England Courant* through the streets to the people who paid for it. In 1722, at 16, he wrote letters to the paper under the made-up name "Silence Dogood." He later wrote that his brother "had often beaten me." When James was jailed in 1722, Benjamin ran the paper. In 1723, at 17, he broke his apprenticeship and ran away to Philadelphia.

On October 2, 1729, Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from the printer Samuel Keimer. Advertising became the paper's main source of money. Franklin grew the paper from two pages to four, partly to hold more advertisements. In 1732 he began *Poor Richard's Almanack*, which he wrote under the name Richard Saunders. He wrote that he sold "near ten thousand" copies a year, and that ordinary people "bought scarcely any other books."

In 1737 Franklin became the postmaster of Philadelphia. He wrote that the post office "facilitated the correspondence that improv'd my newspaper." It brought him more readers and more advertisements. In 1753 the Crown made him and William Hunter the two deputy postmasters general for all the colonies.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1765 to 1800, printers, letter writers and post riders spread the arguments and the news that led to the American Revolution. Supporters of independence also wrecked the press of a New York printer who took Britain's side. In 1791 freedom of the press became part of the First Amendment to the Constitution. Seven years later, in 1798, the Federalist members of Congress passed the Sedition Act. Federal judges then jailed newspaper editors who had criticized President John Adams.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Stamp Act and the printers, 1765" -->
In March 1765 the members of Parliament in London passed the Stamp Act. It took effect on November 1, 1765. Under it, colonists had to pay a tax on newspapers, pamphlets, almanacs and legal papers. A newspaper printed on half a sheet of paper owed a halfpenny a copy, and one printed on a whole sheet owed a penny. Each advertisement in a newspaper owed two shillings. Printers had to print on stamped paper made in London, which showed that the tax was paid.

On October 31, 1765, the day before the tax began, William Bradford of Philadelphia printed his *Pennsylvania Journal* with thick black borders, like a tombstone. In the place for the official stamp he printed a skull and crossbones. His front page said the paper was "EXPIRING: In Hopes of a Resurrection to LIFE again." Bradford never actually stopped printing. He put out the next issue on November 7.

John Hughes was the man picked to hand out the stamps in Philadelphia. Opponents of the tax threatened him with violence and with destroying his property. He then promised not to enforce the act. In 1766 the members of Parliament repealed the Stamp Act, which means they canceled it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Committees of correspondence, 1772 to 1774" -->
A committee of correspondence was a small group of men chosen to write letters to other towns or colonies and share news. Samuel Adams proposed one for Boston. On November 2, 1772, Boston's town leaders voted to set up a Committee of Correspondence of 21 men. They set it up in answer to a British rule that paid the colony's judges from the royal treasury, the king's own funds. The committee wrote a report on the colonists' rights and on how Parliament had broken them. It was printed as the Boston Pamphlet and sent to every town in Massachusetts.

On March 12, 1773, Virginia's House of Burgesses, the colony's elected assembly, set up an 11-man committee. Patrick Henry and Thomas Jefferson were two of its members. The committee wrote to the assemblies of the other colonies. It asked them to share news of laws from London and of what each colony planned to do about them. Within a year, eleven colonial assemblies had set up their own committees. North Carolina and Pennsylvania were the two that did not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The news of Lexington, April 1775" -->
On the morning of April 19, 1775, British soldiers fired on the militia at Lexington, Massachusetts. A militia is a group of ordinary men trained to fight when they are called. At about 10 o'clock that morning, in Watertown, Joseph Palmer of the Massachusetts Committee of Safety wrote a letter "To all the Friends of American liberty." It said the soldiers had killed six men and wounded four.

Palmer gave the letter to a rider, Isaac Bissell, to carry toward Connecticut. At each town on the way, the local committee copied the letter. It added a line saying that the copy was true and signed it. Then it sent riders on with copies. Seven months earlier, in September 1774, false rumors of a British attack had sent thousands of militiamen out for nothing. Palmer took care to make his letter believable for that reason.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-bissell" name="Isaac Bissell" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Bissell

> **Who:** A 26-year-old blacksmith from Suffield, Connecticut, who carried a written report of the Lexington shooting from Massachusetts toward Connecticut.
> **When and where:** From Watertown, Massachusetts, to Connecticut, April 19 and 20, 1775.

Isaac Bissell carried a written report of the shooting at Lexington west from Watertown. He was a blacksmith from Suffield, a town on Connecticut's northern border. On April 19, 1775, he was in Watertown, Massachusetts. At about 10 in the morning, Joseph Palmer handed him the letter about the killings. The letter said that "the Bearer Mr. Isaac Bissell" was "charged to alarm the country quite to Connecticutt." It asked people along the way to give him fresh horses.

Bissell reached Worcester in the early afternoon. The town clerk, Nathan Baldwin, copied the letter and signed the copy. At Springfield someone made another copy. On the morning of April 20 the letter reached Connecticut's leaders.

In Wethersfield, the politician Silas Deane wrote in his journal that the news "was discredited, from the suddeness of its arrival." Discredited means people did not believe it. Governor Jonathan Trumbull believed it. He called Connecticut's assembly together to raise soldiers.

Bissell then marched to the siege of Boston as a private in the Suffield militia. In July 1775 the Massachusetts committee approved his bill as a post rider, two pounds and one shilling. The colony's treasurer then would not pay it without a signed approval from the colony's congress.

Many books call the rider "Israel Bissell" and say he rode all the way to Philadelphia. The historian J. L. Bell found that his name was Isaac, and that other riders carried copies of the letter beyond Connecticut.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-bissell" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Common Sense and the Declaration in print, 1776" -->
On January 10, 1776, the Philadelphia printer Robert Bell published *Common Sense*. It was a pamphlet by Thomas Paine that argued for independence from Britain. Printers printed it at least twenty-five times, sixteen of them in Philadelphia. Paine claimed that 120,000 copies sold in the first three months, and later he claimed 150,000. A writer of Paine's life guessed half a million in 1892.

No sales records survive, so nobody knows the real number. The historian Trish Loughran counted the known printings. She put the highest possible number at 75,000, and she thinks the real number was much lower.

Members of the Continental Congress approved the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776. The Continental Congress was the meeting of men sent by each colony to act for all of them. Over that night the Philadelphia printer John Dunlap printed about 200 copies as broadsides, to send to the states and to the army. On July 9 George Washington's soldiers in New York City heard it read aloud. Twenty-six of Dunlap's copies are known to survive.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-paine" name="Thomas Paine" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Paine

> **Who:** An English-born writer whose pamphlet *Common Sense* argued that the colonies should declare independence.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1774 to 1776, and with the army, 1776.

Thomas Paine was paid nothing for *Common Sense*. He was born in Thetford, England, on January 29, 1737. He met Benjamin Franklin in London, and Franklin gave him letters of introduction to people in America. Paine arrived in Philadelphia on November 30, 1774. He helped edit the *Pennsylvania Magazine* and wrote articles under false names. One of them, "African Slavery in America," attacked the slave trade.

*Common Sense* came out on January 10, 1776, without Paine's name on it. He soon fell out with its first printer, Robert Bell. Other printers then printed it without asking him. Paine later wrote that he gave the right to print it to every state.

By December 1776 Washington's army was close to breaking apart. On December 19 Paine published *The American Crisis*. It began, "These are the times that try men's souls." Washington ordered it read to all his troops. Paine died in New York City on June 8, 1809.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-paine" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Loyalist printer's press wrecked, 1775" -->
Supporters of independence also attacked printers who took Britain's side. A colonist who stayed loyal to Britain was called a Loyalist. James Rivington printed the *New-York Gazetteer*, one of the most widely read newspapers in the colonies, and it took the British government's side.

In November 1775 Isaac Sears led a group of Sons of Liberty from New Haven, Connecticut, to Rivington's shop in New York City. The Sons of Liberty were colonists who organized against British taxes and rule. The men wrecked Rivington's press and his type. Type is the set of metal letters that a printer puts together by hand to print a page, and it was a printer's most valuable possession. Rivington left for England a few weeks later.

After the British army took New York in 1776, Rivington came back with the title of King's Printer. He printed his paper on the British side until the British left in November 1783.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A postal service for the new country, 1774 to 1792" -->
In 1774 British officials dismissed Benjamin Franklin as deputy postmaster general. In the words of the Postal Service's history, he was "too much of an American." The printer William Goddard set up a rival mail service, which he called the Constitutional Post. He warned that officials of the British post could open letters and read them as proof of treason. Treason is the crime of betraying one's own government. By 1775 Goddard's service had nearly 50 post offices from Maine to Virginia.

On July 26, 1775, the members of the Continental Congress made Franklin their postmaster general. Goddard's post offices became the base of the new American postal system.

The Constitution, approved in 1788, gave Congress the power "To establish Post Offices and post Roads." In 1789 the country had 75 post offices and about 2,400 miles of post roads, for almost four million people. In 1792 Congress passed the Post Office Act. Under it, newspapers traveled by mail at very low rates, some for free. Postal officials who opened other people's letters committed a crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-katharine-goddard" name="Mary Katharine Goddard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Katharine Goddard

> **Who:** A printer and newspaper publisher who ran Baltimore's post office for 14 years and printed the first copies of the Declaration that listed the signers.
> **When and where:** Baltimore, Maryland, 1774 to 1789.

The first printed copies of the Declaration of Independence to show the signers' names came from Mary Katharine Goddard's press in Baltimore. Goddard was born on June 16, 1738, in Connecticut, in a family of printers and postmasters. In the 1760s she learned printing in her brother William's shop in Providence, Rhode Island. In 1774 she moved to Baltimore. She ran his print shop and his newspaper, the *Maryland Journal*, while he traveled to promote his Constitutional Post. She reprinted *Common Sense* in the *Journal*.

In 1775 she became the postmaster of Baltimore. She was the first woman known to be a postmaster under the new American government. Postmasters then earned a share of the postage instead of a salary, and prices rose fast during the war. When Congress did not pay her, she paid the post riders and the rent herself. In 1778 she let readers pay for the newspaper in beeswax, flour, lard, butter, beef or pork.

In January 1777 the Continental Congress was meeting in Baltimore. Its members ordered new copies of the Declaration of Independence, and Goddard printed them on January 18, 1777. Hers were the first printed copies to show the signers' names. At the bottom she printed "Baltimore, in Maryland: Printed by Mary Katharine Goddard." Nine of her copies are known to survive.

In January 1784 her brother William pushed her out of the *Maryland Journal*. In October 1789 the new postmaster general, Samuel Osgood, replaced her with a man. Osgood's assistant, John Burrell, said the job might need "more travelling ... than a woman could undertake." More than 200 people in Baltimore signed a letter asking Osgood to give her the job back. She wrote to President George Washington and to the Senate. Neither helped her. She ran a bookshop in Baltimore until about 1810, and she died there on August 12, 1816.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-katharine-goddard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The First Amendment and the party papers, 1789 to 1793" -->
The First Amendment took effect on December 15, 1791. An amendment is a change or addition to the Constitution. The First Amendment says that "Congress shall make no law ... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press." Abridging means cutting back.

Newspaper editors took sides in the quarrels inside Washington's government. John Fenno's *Gazette of the United States* supported Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson and Congressman James Madison wanted a paper on their side. Jefferson gave the poet Philip Freneau a part-time job at the State Department as a translating clerk, paid $250 a year. Freneau started the *National Gazette* on October 31, 1791. It attacked President George Washington's government and had about 1,700 subscribers. Freneau closed it on October 26, 1793.

Benjamin Franklin's grandson, Benjamin Franklin Bache, printed a paper in Philadelphia called the *Aurora*. In it he attacked Washington and, later, John Adams.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sedition Act, 1798 to 1801" -->
In 1798 the United States was close to war with France. President John Adams's party, the Federalists, held a majority in Congress. The Federalist majority passed the Sedition Act, and Adams signed it on July 14, 1798. Sedition means stirring people up against their government. Under the act, it was a crime to write or print "any false, scandalous and malicious writing" against the federal government, Congress or the President. The punishment was a fine of up to $2,000 and up to two years in prison.

The act had two differences from the law in the Zenger trial. A person charged under it could try to prove that his words were true. And the jury could decide what the law meant as well as what the facts were.

Federal prosecutors and judges used the act mostly against editors of newspapers that supported Jefferson's party, the Democratic-Republicans. Historians have long counted 17 indictments and 10 convictions under it. An indictment is a formal charge of a crime. The legal historian Wendell Bird later found 11 more prosecutions of 16 more people. The National Constitution Center counts more than two dozen people convicted, most of them publishers who opposed Adams's party.

Among those charged were Congressman Matthew Lyon of Vermont, William Duane of the *Aurora*, Ann Greenleaf of New York's *Argus*, and Anthony Haswell of the *Vermont Gazette*. Luther Baldwin and Brown Clark of Newark, New Jersey, were charged for words they spoke against President Adams in a tavern.

Federalist supporters physically attacked Benjamin Franklin Bache in Philadelphia. He was also barred from attending meetings of the House of Representatives. Officials arrested him in 1798 for libeling President Adams. He kept printing the *Aurora* through an epidemic of yellow fever. He died of the fever on September 10, 1798, at 29, a month before his trial date. His widow kept the paper going, with William Duane as editor. The Sedition Act ended on March 3, 1801, the date its authors had written into it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-lyon" name="Matthew Lyon" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Lyon

> **Who:** An Irish-born congressman and newspaper owner, the first person tried under the Sedition Act.
> **When and where:** Vermont, 1798 to 1799.

In 1798 Matthew Lyon became the first person tried under the Sedition Act. He came to America from Ireland and fought in the Revolutionary War. In 1797 he went to Congress for Vermont. He also owned a newspaper.

In a letter that a newspaper printed, Lyon wrote of seeing the public good "swallowed up in a continual grasp for power, in an unbounded thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, or selfish avarice." Adulation means too much praise, and avarice means greed. At campaign meetings he also read aloud a letter by the poet Joel Barlow. It said Adams should be sent to a "madhouse."

On October 5, 1798, a federal grand jury in Vermont charged Lyon with three crimes under the Sedition Act. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to charge someone. A deputy marshal arrested him the next day. At his trial in Rutland on October 9, Lyon defended himself. He argued that the act broke the Constitution.

Justice William Paterson of the Supreme Court told the jury that its job had "nothing whatever to do with the constitutionality or unconstitutionality of the sedition law." The jurors came from towns that supported Adams's party. They found Lyon guilty within an hour. Paterson sentenced him to four months in jail and a $1,000 fine.

Lyon was held in the jail at Vergennes. He ran for Congress again from his cell, and the voters of his district re-elected him. He was let out on February 9, 1799.
