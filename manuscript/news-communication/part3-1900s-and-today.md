## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1902, magazine reporters investigated companies and city governments and printed long reports about what they found. During World War I, federal officials ran the government's first large propaganda office. They also barred dozens of newspapers from the mail and filed more than 2,000 criminal cases against people for what they said or printed.

From 1920, radio brought news into homes as it happened. Companies that made radio equipment owned the first stations, and then networks paid for by advertisers took over. In World War II, government officials asked newspapers and radio stations to keep military secrets. Japanese Americans forced into camps printed their own newspapers, and camp officials checked what they printed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The muckrakers, 1902 to 1912" -->
Some reporters in the early 1900s dug into one subject and then wrote long reports about it. Editors at magazines such as *McClure's*, *Cosmopolitan* and *Collier's* printed them. The January 1903 issue of *McClure's* contained three such reports. One was part of Ida Tarbell's history of the Standard Oil Company, which by 1880 controlled the refining of 90 to 95 percent of the oil produced in the United States. Another was Lincoln Steffens's report on corruption in the city government of Minneapolis. Corruption means officials using their jobs to get money or favors illegally. The third was Ray Stannard Baker's report on coal miners.

Upton Sinclair spent about seven weeks undercover, hiding who he was, in the meatpacking plants of Chicago, the factories where animals were killed and the meat was cut up and packed. His novel *The Jungle* came out as a book in February 1906. He wanted readers to care about the workers, but readers were most upset about what went into their meat. "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach," Sinclair wrote. In a series that ran in *Collier's* in 1905 and 1906, Samuel Hopkins Adams reported on patent medicines, bottled cures sold with false claims. He wrote that Americans would "swallow huge quantities of alcohol, an appalling amount of opiates and narcotics" in them. On June 30, 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt signed two new laws. The Meat Inspection Act put federal inspectors in packing plants. The Pure Food and Drug Act banned food and medicine with false labels or harmful added ingredients from being sold across state lines.

David Graham Phillips attacked members of the US Senate in a series called "The Treason of the Senate." On April 14, 1906, Roosevelt answered with a speech. He compared these writers to the Man with the Muck Rake, a man in John Bunyan's religious book *The Pilgrim's Progress* "who could look no way but downward." Muck is mud and filth. Roosevelt meant it as an insult. The writers took his word "muckraker" as a name to be proud of. By about 1912 most of the magazines had stopped this work. One reason was that large companies stopped buying advertisements in them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-s-abbott" name="Robert S. Abbott" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert S. Abbott

> **Who:** The son of formerly enslaved parents who founded the *Chicago Defender* and sent it into the South, where it urged Black families to move north.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1905 to 1940.

Robert S. Abbott started the *Chicago Defender* with 300 copies in 1905 and by 1920 it outsold every other Black newspaper in the country. He was born on St. Simons Island, Georgia, on November 28, 1868. His parents, Thomas and Flora Abbott, had been enslaved. He learned printing at Hampton Institute in Virginia. After he graduated in 1896 he moved to Chicago, where he was shut out of printing jobs because he was Black. The record does not name the shops that turned him away. He earned a law degree in 1899. Because he was Black, he could not get work as a lawyer in Indiana and Kansas.

On May 5, 1905, Abbott printed the first issue of the *Chicago Defender* and sold the 300 copies himself. In his paper's headlines and articles he attacked lynching and white supremacy. Lynching is the killing of a person by a mob, with no trial. White supremacy is the belief that white people should rule over everyone else. One read, "When the Mob Comes and You Must Die Take at Least One with You." White papers such as the *Chicago Tribune* printed "colored" after the names of Black people. Abbott printed "(white)" after the names of white people.

At that time 90 of every 100 Black Americans lived in the South, and Abbott sent the paper there. He urged readers to leave the South. In the *Defender* he printed job listings, train schedules, and photos of what he presented as the best schools, parks and housing in Chicago. For four years starting in 1916, more than 10,000 Black Southerners left the South each month. Between 1916 and 1919 about 500,000 left.

Local police in Southern towns tried to stop the paper. They seized copies, and they arrested people who sold it under vagrancy laws, which made it a crime to have no job. Some police departments banned the sale of Black newspapers altogether. The accounts do not name the towns. Abbott arranged for Pullman porters, the Black workers who served passengers on railroad sleeping cars, to carry bundles of the paper south and sell them in secret.

By 1929 the *Defender* sold more than 250,000 copies a week, and Abbott was one of the first Black millionaires. He died in Chicago on February 29, 1940.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-s-abbott" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War I: the government's propaganda office, 1917 to 1919" -->
The United States entered World War I in April 1917. That month President Woodrow Wilson set up the Committee on Public Information and put George Creel, a former newspaper reporter, in charge. It was the federal government's first large propaganda office. Propaganda is information made and spread to push people toward one opinion.

Creel's staff sent articles to newspapers. Creel later estimated that their material filled 20,000 newspaper columns a week. The committee's staff printed their own paper, the *Official Bulletin*, and about 115,000 copies went out. Its artists made posters, and one showed a German soldier as a gorilla carrying a club. Staff in one division of the committee watched the hundreds of newspapers printed in other languages in the United States. After the war, many Americans concluded that Creel and his staff had pushed the war too hard, and that they had made it harder for people to speak against it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War I: papers banned from the mail and people jailed for words, 1917 to 1921" -->
Members of Congress passed the Espionage Act on June 15, 1917. Espionage means spying. The act made it a crime to get in the way of the draft, the government's call-up of men into the army, or to stir up disloyalty in the armed forces. Under the act, papers that urged treason could not go through the mail. Postmaster General Albert S. Burleson told local postmasters to report anything suspicious. By 1918 his officials had barred 74 newspapers from the mail. The New York City postmaster, Thomas G. Patten, stopped the magazine *The Masses*, and its November-December 1917 issue was its last.

On October 6, 1917, members of Congress passed the Trading with the Enemy Act. Under it, newspapers printed in German and other languages had to file English translations of their war news with the local postmaster.

On May 16, 1918, members of Congress added the Sedition Act to the Espionage Act. Sedition means stirring people up against their government. The new act made it a crime to speak or write anything "disloyal" about the government, the Constitution, the armed forces or the flag. The punishment was up to 20 years in prison and a $10,000 fine. Federal prosecutors filed more than 2,000 cases under the two laws, and more than 1,000 ended in convictions. The Sedition Act's main targets were pacifists, people who oppose all war, and anarchists and socialists. Anarchists want to do away with government. Socialists want the public, rather than private owners, to own big businesses.

On June 16, 1918, the Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs gave a speech against the war to about 1,200 people in Canton, Ohio. A judge sentenced him to 10 years in prison, and the justices of the Supreme Court upheld his conviction in 1919. President Warren G. Harding shortened his sentence, and Debs walked out of prison on Christmas Day 1921.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="victor-berger" name="Victor Berger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Victor Berger

> **Who:** An immigrant newspaper owner in Milwaukee whose paper was barred from the mail for opposing World War I, and who was sentenced to 20 years in prison.
> **When and where:** Milwaukee, Wisconsin, 1892 to 1929.

Victor Berger's newspaper lost its right to go by mail in 1917 because he opposed World War I, and a federal judge sentenced him to 20 years in prison. Berger was born in 1860 to a Jewish family in Austria-Hungary. He came to the United States in 1878 and moved to Milwaukee three years later. There he taught German in the public schools.

In 1892 Berger bought a German-language newspaper. In 1898 he and Eugene V. Debs founded the Social Democratic Party. Twelve years later, Milwaukee voters made him the first Socialist elected to the US House of Representatives. In 1911 he started a daily paper in English, the *Milwaukee Leader*.

Berger opposed the war and said so in his paper. In 1917 an official in Postmaster General Albert S. Burleson's department, the third assistant postmaster general, took away the *Leader*'s permit to go by mail at the low rate for newspapers. In 1918 federal prosecutors charged Berger under the Espionage Act. He and four other Socialist leaders were convicted in Chicago. In early 1919 Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis sentenced each of them to 20 years in prison.

In 1918, while his case went on, Milwaukee voters elected Berger to Congress again. In November 1919 the other members of the House refused to let him take his seat. Voters elected him again in a special election, with 55 percent of the vote, and the members of the House refused him again. In 1921 the justices of the Supreme Court overturned his conviction. Voters elected him to Congress three more times, in 1922, 1924 and 1926. He died in 1929 after a streetcar accident.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-berger" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio, and who owned it, 1920 to 1934" -->
Radio station KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcast the results of the presidential election between Warren G. Harding and James M. Cox on the evening of November 2, 1920. KDKA belonged to the Westinghouse company. Its studio was a shed on the roof of a building at the Westinghouse works in East Pittsburgh.

By the end of 1921 eight stations were on the air, and by November 1, 1922, federal officials had licensed 564. At first, money for radio came from two places: selling radio sets and selling advertising. On August 28, 1922, the Queensboro Corporation paid station WEAF in New York $50 for about ten minutes of advertising for its apartments. Over the following years, advertising became the main way American radio was paid for.

In 1926 the National Broadcasting Company, NBC, bought WEAF and set up a permanent network. A network is a group of stations in different cities that carry the same programs. The Columbia Broadcasting System, CBS, built another. The biggest makers of radio equipment and the biggest broadcasters made business deals with each other that could have given them a monopoly, control of radio with no real competition. Members of Congress passed the Radio Act of 1927, which set up a federal commission to decide which station could use which wavelength, the spot on the radio dial where a station is heard. In 1934 they passed the Communications Act, which set up the Federal Communications Commission, the FCC, to oversee radio and messages sent by wire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The president on the radio, 1933 to 1944" -->
President Franklin D. Roosevelt first spoke to the country by radio on Sunday night, March 12, 1933. On March 6 he had closed every bank in the country. Roosevelt explained banking in plain words to some 60 million listeners, and he told them it was safer to keep money in a reopened bank than under the mattress. When the banks opened again, people put in more money than they took out. It was the first of the talks that Harry Butcher of CBS named "fireside chats." Historians count between 27 and 31 of them, from 1933 to 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The War of the Worlds broadcast, 1938" -->
The actor Orson Welles and his Mercury Theatre on the Air performed H. G. Wells's novel *The War of the Worlds* on CBS on the evening of October 30, 1938. Welles was 23. The writer Howard Koch, the producer John Houseman and Paul Stewart had rewritten the story as fake news bulletins about Martians landing in New Jersey. The program did not stop to say it was a play until almost 40 minutes in.

Some listeners believed it and called the police, newspapers and radio stations. The next morning, newspapers across the country reported a nationwide panic. That night the C. E. Hooper company had asked 5,000 households what they were listening to. Two of every 100 named the Welles program. The historian A. Brad Schwartz found that only some listeners were fooled. He found that their worried phone calls convinced many journalists that the show had caused a panic across the country, and the journalists reported one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures by wire, 1935" -->
Until 1935, newspapers got photographs by mail, train or airplane, and a picture could take up to 85 hours to arrive. On January 1, 1935, Associated Press staff sent their first photograph over wires. It was a picture taken from the air of a plane crash near Morehouseville, New York. Forty-seven newspapers in 25 states received it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edward-r-murrow" name="Edward R. Murrow" movie="Good Night, and Good Luck (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edward R. Murrow

> **Who:** A CBS radio reporter whose eyewitness reports from Europe before and during World War II made him famous across the United States.
> **When and where:** London, 1937 to 1945.
> **Movie:** *Good Night, and Good Luck* (2005), directed by George Clooney, about Murrow's television reports on Senator Joseph McCarthy in 1953 and 1954.

Edward R. Murrow's radio reports from Europe for CBS, from 1938 to 1940, made him famous across the United States. He was born in Greensboro, North Carolina, on April 25, 1908. He graduated from Washington State College. He helped bring German scholars who had been driven out by the Nazis to the United States. The Nazis were Adolf Hitler's party, which ruled Germany.

Murrow joined CBS in 1935. In 1937 CBS executives sent him to London to run the network's European office. On the radio he described Germany's moves across Europe as an eyewitness: the takeover of Austria and the Munich Conference, a meeting of European leaders, in 1938, the takeover of Czechoslovakia in 1939, and the Battle of Britain in 1940, when German planes attacked Britain.

After the war Murrow ran the news department at CBS. In 1954 he made a television program about Senator Joseph McCarthy, who had charged that Communists were working in the government. Murrow died on April 27, 1965.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edward-r-murrow" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War II: secrets, propaganda and the Black press, 1941 to 1945" -->
Twelve days after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, on December 19, 1941, President Roosevelt set up the Office of Censorship. Censorship means controlling what people may send or print. Its director, Byron Price, had been an editor at the Associated Press. The office had about 13,500 workers, and most of them read letters and cables going into and out of the country. A cable was a message sent by wire under the ocean.

For newspapers and radio, Price wrote a code of rules that editors agreed to follow. The code listed topics to keep out of the news: troop and ship movements, how war factories were doing, the president's travels, and even the weather. Price persuaded the reporters Drew Pearson and William L. Laurence not to write about the building of the atomic bomb. On June 13, 1942, Roosevelt set up the Office of War Information. Its staff ran information programs, at home and in other countries, to build understanding of how the war was going and of the government's war plans.

Federal prosecutors charged far fewer people for their words than in World War I. By one count, dozens were charged, against thousands in 1917 to 1919. Roosevelt kept pressing his attorney general, Francis Biddle, to "indict the seditionists," meaning to charge with crimes the people he saw as turning others against the government. Writers for Black newspapers such as the *Chicago Defender* reported on the armed forces' separate units for Black troops, white officers placed over Black soldiers, blood supplies kept apart by race, and violence against Black servicemen. They ran a "Double V" campaign: victory over the enemy abroad and victory over prejudice at home. Federal officials threatened to charge Black publishers with sedition. The *Defender*'s publisher, John H. Sengstacke, insisted on meeting Biddle. Biddle agreed to block the charges as long as the Black papers did not make their criticism of the war any stronger.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers behind barbed wire, 1942 to 1945" -->
On February 19, 1942, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, a written command from the president. Under it, Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt ordered Japanese Americans on the West Coast out of their homes, some with only 48 hours' notice. Soldiers and federal officials forced them into camps because of their Japanese ancestry. The National Archives counts about 122,000 people, and nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens. Another count gives 110,000 to 120,000. The First Amendment Encyclopedia describes them as concentration camps.

Inside the camps, Japanese Americans wrote, printed and handed out their own newspapers. The papers in the first camps, run by the army, were copied on mimeograph machines, which print copies from a cut paper stencil. Army officials required those papers to be written in English only. All ten camps run by the War Relocation Authority had papers in both English and Japanese. Two of them were the *Manzanar Free Press* in California and the *Heart Mountain Sentinel* in Wyoming.

Officials checked what the papers printed. Army officials censored the papers in the first camps. In the later camps, officials of the War Relocation Authority interfered with what the papers printed, and in some cases they censored the papers outright.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Television begins, 1927 to 1941" -->
Philo Farnsworth, who was 21, sent the first picture by all-electronic television, a straight line, in his laboratory in San Francisco on September 7, 1927. On July 1, 1941, station WNBT in New York showed the first television advertisement allowed by law, before a baseball game. It showed a Bulova watch face over a map of the United States. It lasted about ten seconds and cost $9.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1950 about 9 of every 100 American homes had a television set, and by 1960, 87 of every 100 did. After the DuMont network closed in 1955, three national television networks were left: NBC, CBS and ABC.

Managers of one Mississippi station, WLBT, cut civil rights news off the air. A church leader and two Mississippi leaders fought the station before the government and in court, and its owners lost their license. In 1971 and 1972 reporters printed a secret history of the Vietnam War and tied a burglary to the White House, and officials in Washington tried to stop them. By 1996 two cable channels showed news all day and night, and the World Wide Web had been open to the public since 1991.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Television and the blacklist, 1950 to 1954" -->
In June 1950 three former FBI agents published a book called *Red Channels*. They ran a company called American Business Consultants. The book named 151 people who worked in radio and television and claimed they had ties to communism. Communism is a system in which one party runs the government and the government owns the farms and factories. The company's owners then visited networks, advertisers and sponsors. For a fee, they offered to "clear" the people they had named. To be cleared, a person usually had to give the FBI or the House Un-American Activities Committee, a committee of Congress, the names of other people. Network executives and advertisers fired people on the list. A list of people whom employers refuse to hire or keep is called a blacklist.

In February 1950 Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin gave a speech in Wheeling, West Virginia. He said that 205 communists worked in the State Department. When senators asked him, he could not name a single one. In 1954 a Senate hearing on McCarthy's charges against Army officers and officials was shown on television across the country. It lasted 36 days. On camera, Joseph Welch, the Army's lawyer, asked McCarthy, "Have you no sense of decency, sir, at long last?"

That same year Edward R. Murrow showed McCarthy's methods on his weekly CBS program *See It Now*. On December 2, 1954, members of the Senate voted 67 to 22 to condemn McCarthy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio and television in Spanish, 1946 to 1961" -->
Raoul A. Cortez was born in Xalapa, Mexico, in 1905. He worked as a reporter for *La Prensa*, a Spanish-language newspaper in San Antonio, Texas. In 1946 he opened KCOR in San Antonio. It was the first all-Spanish radio station owned and run by a Hispanic American. Federal rules during the war limited broadcasts in other languages. To get his license, Cortez told officials that part of the station's purpose was to rally Mexican Americans behind the war effort.

In 1955 Cortez started KCOR-TV, the first television station aimed only at Hispanic viewers. It broadcast on a kind of channel called UHF, and few television sets could pick it up, so advertisers were hard to find. In 1961 he sold the station. The new owners renamed it KWEX.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The station that cut the news: WLBT, 1955 to 1971" -->
The managers of WLBT, the NBC television station in Jackson, Mississippi, cut civil rights news off the air, and a group of citizens fought for years to take away the station's license. The Lamar Life Insurance Company started WLBT in 1953. In the fall of 1955 the lawyer Thurgood Marshall appeared on an NBC program to talk about the Supreme Court's decisions against segregated schools. Segregation means keeping Black and white people apart by law or by force. WLBT staff cut him off and showed a sign that read "Sorry, Cable Trouble from New York." The general manager, Fred Beard, said he did it "because the TV networks were overloading the circuits with Negro propaganda."

Station staff also cut away from NBC's evening news when it turned to civil rights. WLBT reporters used racial slurs on the air. The White Citizens' Council, a group that fought to keep segregation, ran a bookstore in the station's lobby. About half the people in WLBT's viewing area were Black, and the station's managers refused them time to answer.

In May 1963 Medgar Evers spoke on WLBT to answer the mayor of Jackson. Evers was the Mississippi field secretary of the NAACP, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, a civil rights group. Three weeks later Byron De La Beckwith shot Evers in the back in the driveway of his home and killed him. That year WLBT again ran the "Cable Trouble" sign, this time over NBC's report on white people attacking protesters at a lunch counter in Jackson. WLBT's own crew had filmed the attack.

In 1963 the Reverend Everett Parker of the United Church of Christ joined Aaron Henry, head of the Mississippi NAACP, and A. D. Beittel, the president of Tougaloo College. They gathered complaints from viewers, knowing that they could be attacked for it. In 1964 they asked the Federal Communications Commission not to renew WLBT's license, the government permit a station needs to broadcast. The commissioners gave the station a one-year renewal without a hearing.

In 1966 judges on a federal appeals court, in an opinion by Warren Burger, ruled that viewers had the right to take part in these cases. In 1969 the judges ordered the commission to take away the owners' license. The owners appealed and lost in 1971. A board of Black and white citizens then ran the station, and it hired some of the first Black television managers in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Civil rights news on the page and on the screen, 1955 to 1965" -->
In August 1955 Roy Bryant, his half-brother J. W. Milam and at least one other man took 14-year-old Emmett Till by force from his relatives' home in Mississippi. Three days later Till's beaten body was found in the Tallahatchie River, weighed down with a 75-pound metal fan from a cotton gin, a machine that cleans cotton. An all-white jury found Bryant and Milam not guilty of murder. Afterward, both men told the writer William Bradford Huie, for *Look* magazine, that they had kidnapped and murdered Till.

*Jet*, a weekly news magazine for Black readers published in Chicago by John H. Johnson's company, printed photographs of Till's body on September 15, 1955. *Jet* sold about 425,000 copies a week at the time. The Till issue sold out, and its publishers printed more.

A riot broke out on the night of September 30, 1962, at the University of Mississippi, where James Meredith was about to enroll as its first Black student. People in the crowd threw a pipe, rocks, bricks and bottles at US marshals. Hundreds of people were hurt, and two men died. Paul Guihard, a 30-year-old reporter for the French news agency Agence France-Presse, was covering the story. Someone shot him in the back at close range, and the bullet hit his heart. No one has ever been charged with killing him. The Justice Department closed the case in 2011 without naming anyone.

Alabama state troopers and other officers led by Major John Cloud attacked civil rights marchers with clubs and tear gas on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma on March 7, 1965. At least 50 marchers went to the hospital. Television cameras filmed the attack, and the film was flown to New York. At about 9:30 that night, the ABC newscaster Frank Reynolds interrupted the network's showing of *Judgment at Nuremberg*, a film about Nazi war crimes, to show it. Nearly 50 million people had tuned in to the film.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="walter-cronkite" name="Walter Cronkite" movie="Walter Cronkite: Witness to History (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Walter Cronkite

> **Who:** The CBS anchor who read the evening news to much of the country for 19 years, and who told viewers in 1968 that the Vietnam War was stuck.
> **When and where:** New York, 1962 to 1981.
> **Movie:** *Walter Cronkite: Witness to History* (2006), a PBS *American Masters* documentary about him.

In 1972 a poll named Walter Cronkite, the anchor of the *CBS Evening News*, "the most trusted man in America." An anchor is the person who reads the news and introduces the reports on a news program. Cronkite was born in St. Joseph, Missouri, on November 4, 1916. He grew up in Houston, Texas, where he wrote for his school newspapers.

In World War II Cronkite reported for the United Press news service. He flew on bombing raids over Germany and covered the D-Day landings in 1944. In 1950 Edward R. Murrow hired him for CBS. In 1962 Cronkite became the anchor of the *CBS Evening News*. Soon after, the program grew from 15 minutes to 30 minutes. It was the first half-hour nightly news program on network television. In 1963 Cronkite told viewers that President John F. Kennedy had been killed. He covered the Moon landing in 1969 and nearly every American spaceflight with a crew from 1961 to 1981.

In 1968 Cronkite went to Vietnam after the Tet Offensive, a large attack by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces. On February 27, 1968, he told viewers that the war was "mired in stalemate." A stalemate is a fight that neither side can win. President Lyndon Johnson told his staff, "If I've lost Cronkite, I've lost Middle America." By Middle America he meant ordinary American families. Some people believed that this helped Johnson decide not to run for president again that year.

Cronkite ended each program with "And that's the way it is." He left the anchor desk in 1981. He died in New York on July 17, 2009.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="walter-cronkite" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vietnam and the reporters, 1965 to 1971" -->
As late as 1964 fewer than two dozen American reporters were in Indochina, the region that includes Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. By 1968 about 600 journalists from many countries were in Vietnam. More than 60 journalists were killed during the war, and the record of their deaths does not say who killed them. US military officers did not censor the reports from Vietnam. The military's daily news briefings in Saigon were nicknamed "the five o'clock follies." Follies are silly shows.

Many people later blamed the press for turning Americans against the war. Experts who studied the reports found that most of the reporting supported the war until 1968. They found that support at home fell most as more American soldiers died.

On March 16, 1968, American soldiers of Charlie Company killed unarmed villagers at My Lai in South Vietnam. The counts run from at least 175, found by the Army's own inquiry, to 504, the number of names on a memorial in the village. The soldiers also raped women and girls. Rape means forcing someone into sex by violence or threat. In 1969 Seymour Hersh, a freelance reporter, one who worked for himself rather than for one newspaper, followed a tip and found Lieutenant William Calley. Hersh wrote the first public account of the killings. He won a Pulitzer Prize, a top award for reporting, in 1970. In March 1971 Calley was found guilty of murdering 22 civilians. He was the only soldier convicted, and no one was convicted of rape.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pentagon Papers, 1971" -->
In 1967 Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara ordered a secret history of what American leaders had done in Vietnam since World War II. It filled 47 volumes. Daniel Ellsberg, who had worked on it, gave copies to *The New York Times* without permission. The *Times* began printing stories about it on June 13, 1971. The papers recorded that President Johnson had planned open war in 1964, a year before the public knew. They also recorded that he ordered the bombing of North Vietnam in 1965, even though intelligence officials had judged that it would not work.

After three days, Justice Department lawyers got a court order that stopped the *Times* from printing more. An order that stops something from being printed before it comes out is called prior restraint. *The Washington Post* also had the papers. On June 30, 1971, the justices of the Supreme Court voted 6 to 3 that the newspapers could keep printing.

Prosecutors charged Ellsberg under the Espionage Act, and he faced up to 115 years in prison. Men working for President Richard Nixon, led in this case by the former CIA officer E. Howard Hunt, broke into the office of Ellsberg's psychiatrist. They were looking for information to damage Ellsberg. In 1973 the judge dismissed all the charges because of this break-in and other wrongdoing by government officials.

In 1972, in a case called *Branzburg v. Hayes*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the Constitution gives reporters no right to refuse to tell a grand jury who their sources are. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof to put someone on trial. A reporter's sources are the people who give the reporter information.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="woodward-and-bernstein" name="Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein" movie="All the President's Men (1976)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein

> **Who:** Two young *Washington Post* reporters who showed that a burglary in 1972 was tied to President Nixon's White House.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1972 to 1974.
> **Movie:** *All the President's Men* (1976), about their reporting, with Robert Redford as Woodward.

Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, two young reporters at *The Washington Post*, tied a 1972 burglary to President Richard Nixon's White House. Early on June 17, 1972, police arrested five men inside the office of the Democratic National Committee, the national leaders of the Democratic Party, in the Watergate building in Washington. One of them, James W. McCord Jr., was the security chief of Nixon's re-election committee. Woodward, born in Illinois in 1943, had covered minor crimes for the *Post* for nine months. Bernstein covered politics in Virginia. With a third reporter, Alfred E. Lewis, they wrote about the arrests for the next morning's paper.

Nixon's press secretary, Ron Ziegler, called it a "third-rate burglary attempt." Most early news stories said the White House had nothing to do with it. Woodward and Bernstein kept reporting. A secret source they called "Deep Throat" gave them information. On October 10, 1972, they reported that the break-in came from "a massive campaign of political spying and sabotage" run by White House officials for Nixon's re-election. Sabotage means secretly wrecking someone else's work.

CBS News aired the first part of a two-part report on the story on October 27, 1972. Nixon's aide Charles Colson then threatened William Paley, the head of CBS, and the second part was cut short. The records do not say who at CBS made the cut. That November Nixon won re-election, carrying every state except Massachusetts. In 1973 the *Post* won the Pulitzer Prize for public service for Woodward and Bernstein's reporting.

The reporting was one step among several that led to Nixon's resignation. For two weeks in May 1973, television stations showed a Senate committee's Watergate hearings live. More than 150 public television stations showed them again in the evening. A month after the hearings began, 97 of every 100 Americans had heard of Watergate. A White House aide, Alexander Butterfield, told the committee that Nixon kept a voice-activated tape recorder in rooms of the White House. On July 24, 1974, the justices of the Supreme Court ordered Nixon to hand over the tapes. One tape showed that on June 23, 1972, he had ordered the FBI to hold back its investigation. Members of the House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment, formal charges that could lead to his removal from office. On August 9, 1974, Nixon resigned.

In 2005 W. Mark Felt, who had been the second-highest official at the FBI, said that he was Deep Throat.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woodward-and-bernstein" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reporters killed in the United States, 1976 to 1990" -->
On June 2, 1976, Don Bolles, a 47-year-old reporter for the *Arizona Republic*, went to a Phoenix hotel to meet a man who had promised him information about land fraud, cheating people in land deals, and organized crime. The man never came. When Bolles started his car, a bomb under it exploded. Doctors had to cut off both his legs and one arm, and he died on June 13. In 1977 John Harvey Adamson pleaded guilty to building and planting the bomb. A jury later convicted Max Dunlap of murder and of conspiracy to murder, which means planning a murder with others. He died in prison in 2009. Reporters from many other papers came to Arizona and spent five months finishing Bolles's reporting.

Between 1981 and 1990, killers murdered five Vietnamese American journalists who worked at small papers for refugees from the Vietnam War. Reporters at ProPublica who looked into the killings tied them to the politics of the papers. Duong Trong Lam, 27, was shot outside his home in San Francisco in 1981. In Houston in 1982 a gunman chased Nguyen Dam Phong from his house and shot him seven times. Pham Van Tap died of smoke from a fire that someone set in his office in Garden Grove, California, while he slept. A message sent to the Vietnamese press after his death said he was killed for printing advertisements for companies that did business with Vietnam. Le Triet was shot at his home in Virginia on September 22, 1990. The FBI suspected a group led by former South Vietnamese military officers. No one was ever arrested or charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Public broadcasting, the fairness rule, cable news and talk radio, 1967 to 1996" -->
In the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967, members of Congress set up the Corporation for Public Broadcasting to hand out federal money to public television and radio stations. People at the corporation started PBS in 1969 and NPR, National Public Radio, in 1970.

On June 1, 1980, Ted Turner's company started CNN, the first television channel to show news 24 hours a day. It reached viewers through cable, wires that carried many channels into homes.

From 1949 the Federal Communications Commission enforced a rule called the fairness doctrine. Stations had to cover important public questions fairly and air more than one side. People attacked on the air had to get a chance to answer. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld the rule in 1969. In June 1987 members of Congress passed a bill to make it a law. President Ronald Reagan vetoed the bill, which means he refused to sign it. He wrote that the rule "requires Federal officials to supervise the editorial practices of broadcasters." That year the commissioners, led by their chairman, Dennis Patrick, voted 4 to 0 to end the rule.

In 1988 Rush Limbaugh began a national radio talk show on which he gave his conservative political opinions, views that favor keeping old ways and limiting government. Within five years an estimated 20 million people listened each week. Some commentators argue that the end of the fairness doctrine helped talk shows like his grow. The question is not settled.

On October 7, 1996, Rupert Murdoch's company started the Fox News Channel. Roger Ailes, a television producer and former Republican political adviser, ran it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The internet, 1969 to 1996" -->
In late 1969, researchers at the University of California, Los Angeles, sent the first message over ARPANET to the Stanford Research Institute. ARPANET was a computer network paid for by the Defense Department. In 1991 Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working in Switzerland, opened the World Wide Web to the public. The web is the system of linked pages that people open over the internet, the worldwide network of connected computers. In 1993 a team at the University of Illinois released Mosaic, the first widely used program for viewing web pages with pictures.

In February 1996 President Bill Clinton signed the Telecommunications Act. Under it, one company could own more television stations, and many media companies merged after it passed. One part made it a crime to send "indecent" material to children online. In 1997 the justices of the Supreme Court struck down that part. They ruled that the internet has the same protection for free speech as printed words.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In a 2025 survey by the Pew Research Center, 86 of every 100 American adults said they got news at least sometimes from a phone, computer or tablet, and 64 of every 100 from television. Newspapers lost most of their advertising money. Researchers at Northwestern University counted close to 3,500 newspapers that closed between 2005 and 2025.

People angry at what reporters wrote killed journalists in their newsrooms and outside their homes. Police arrested reporters at protests and hit them. In 2025 members of Congress took back the federal money for public radio and television.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The news on phones and feeds, 2004 to 2025" -->
Mark Zuckerberg and three other Harvard students started Facebook in February 2004. Three former workers at the PayPal company started YouTube in 2005, and Google bought it in November 2006 for $1.65 billion. Twitter began in 2006. A feed is the list of posts a site shows each user. In 2018 Facebook's managers changed its feed to show fewer posts from news outlets and more posts from users' friends.

Under a law of 1996, known as Section 230, a website cannot be treated as the publisher of what its users post. That means the company that runs the site is not legally responsible, as a newspaper publisher would be, for what users write there.

In 2011, 35 of every 100 American adults owned a smartphone, according to the Pew Research Center. By 2025, 91 of every 100 did. In a Pew survey in August 2025, 53 of every 100 adults said they got news at least sometimes from social media. Thirty-eight of every 100 said they regularly got news on Facebook, and 35 on YouTube. Twenty regularly got news on Instagram, 20 on TikTok and 12 on X, the site called Twitter until 2023. In the same survey, 7 of every 100 said they often read news in a printed newspaper or magazine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="False news, measured, 2018" -->
In 2018 three researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, published a study of news on Twitter. They followed about 126,000 news stories as people passed them along on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. About 3 million people shared them more than 4.5 million times. Six fact-checking groups had judged which stories were true and which were false. The researchers found that false stories were 70 percent more likely to be shared again than true ones. A true story took about six times as long as a false one to reach 1,500 people. False stories about politics spread fastest.

Some accounts on Twitter were run by computer programs, called bots. When the researchers left out the bots, the result stayed the same. So people, the researchers concluded, spread the false stories.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owns the feed, 2022 to 2026" -->
In October 2022 Elon Musk bought Twitter for $44 billion. He laid off about half its workers and cut back content moderation, the work of checking posts and removing those that break a site's rules. He began selling blue check marks for $8 a month. Musk renamed the site X in 2023. In March 2025 he sold X to his artificial intelligence company, xAI, in a deal that valued X at $33 billion. In 2026 his rocket company, SpaceX, bought xAI. In January 2025 Mark Zuckerberg announced that Facebook would end the fact-checking programs it had used for about ten years.

TikTok belonged to ByteDance, a company in China. In April 2024 President Joe Biden signed a law that ordered ByteDance to sell TikTok within a year or see the app banned. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld the law in January 2025. President Donald Trump then delayed the ban several times. In January 2026 a new company took over TikTok in the United States. The software company Oracle, the investment firm Silver Lake and a fund owned by the government of the United Arab Emirates each own 15 percent of it, and ByteDance kept 19.9 percent.

In 2025 David Ellison bought Paramount, the company that owns CBS, and he hired Bari Weiss to run CBS News. In December 2025 the leaders of CBS News pulled a *60 Minutes* report about a prison in El Salvador three hours before it was to air. The correspondent, Sharyn Alfonsi, wrote that Weiss "spiked our story," which means stopped it, after CBS lawyers had cleared it. In 2026 Ellison's company agreed to buy Warner Bros. Discovery, which owns CNN, for $111 billion. According to NPR, both deals depend on money from his father, Larry Ellison, a founder of Oracle. The top lawyers of 12 states, all of them Democrats, sued over the deal. In September 2026 Paramount's owners settled the lawsuit by agreeing to set up a board meant to keep the owners out of news decisions at CBS and CNN. The purchase was not yet complete at the end of September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers close: the news deserts, 2005 to 2025" -->
American newspapers took in about $49.4 billion from advertising in 2005 and an estimated $9.8 billion in 2022, according to the Pew Research Center.

Researchers at the Medill school of journalism at Northwestern University count newspapers every year. Their 2025 report found that close to 3,500 newspapers had closed since 2005, almost 40 of every 100. More than 130 closed in the year before the report. Since 2005 the newspaper business had lost more than three of every four of its jobs.

A county with no local newspaper, news website or station is called a news desert. In 2005 just over 150 counties had no local news source, according to the Medill report. In 2025 there were 212, and about 80 of every 100 of them were mostly rural. Another 1,525 counties had only one local source. About 50 million people lived in counties with one source or none. In 2005 there were 3,995 different owners of American newspapers, and in 2025 there were just under 1,900.

The *Chicago Defender*, the paper that urged Black Southerners to move north in the 1910s, ended its print edition in 2019 and publishes only online.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeff-german" name="Jeff German" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jeff German

> **Who:** A Las Vegas newspaper reporter who was stabbed to death by an elected official he had written about.
> **When and where:** Las Vegas, Nevada, 1980s to 2022.

In 2022 Robert Telles, an elected official in Las Vegas, stabbed the reporter Jeff German to death after German reported on Telles's office. German's work as a reporter in Las Vegas spanned four decades, and at the time of his death he worked for the *Las Vegas Review-Journal*. He wrote about organized crime and dishonest officials, and he hosted a *Review-Journal* podcast about the mob called *Mobbed Up*.

That year German wrote about the office of Robert Telles, who had been elected Clark County Public Administrator, a county official. German's stories described bullying in the office and the punishing of workers who complained. They also described an "inappropriate relationship" between Telles and a staff member. Telles denied it. In June 2022 he lost his election.

On September 2, 2022, Telles stabbed German to death outside German's home. Police found Telles's DNA on German's hands and fingernails. DNA is the chemical code inside every person's cells, and it can show who was at a place. In Telles's home, police found a straw hat and sneakers, cut into pieces, like the ones the killer wore on a security camera video.

A jury found Telles guilty of murder on August 28, 2024. In October 2024 Judge Michelle Leavitt added more time because German was over 60 and Telles had used a deadly weapon. Telles was sentenced to 28 years to life in prison.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeff-german" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A newsroom attacked: Annapolis, 2018" -->
On June 28, 2018, Jarrod Ramos walked into the newsroom of the *Capital Gazette* in Annapolis, Maryland, with a shotgun, smoke grenades and extra ammunition. He killed five people who worked there: Gerald Fischman, Rob Hiaasen, John McNamara, Rebecca Smith and Wendi Winters. Ramos held a grudge because in 2011 the paper had reported that he was convicted of harassing a woman he had gone to high school with. In 2021 a jury found that he was responsible for his crimes, and a judge sentenced him to five life terms in prison with no chance of release.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="darnella-frazier" name="Darnella Frazier" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Darnella Frazier

> **Who:** A 17-year-old who recorded a Minneapolis police officer killing George Floyd, and whose video became evidence at his trial.
> **When and where:** Minneapolis, Minnesota, 2020 to 2021.

Darnella Frazier's phone video of a police officer killing George Floyd reached people around the world. On May 25, 2020, Frazier, who was 17, was walking with her nine-year-old cousin in Minneapolis. She saw the police officer Derek Chauvin kneeling on the neck of George Floyd, a Black man, and she recorded it. Her video showed Chauvin kneeling on Floyd's neck for nine minutes and 29 seconds, and it recorded Floyd's murder.

According to NPR, the video played a major part in starting protests against police violence around the world. At Chauvin's trial in 2021, prosecutors played it again and again, and Frazier testified. The jury found Chauvin guilty of second-degree murder, third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter, three different legal grades of killing a person. Manslaughter is killing a person without meaning to kill. The journalist Ann Marie Lipinski said, "There is no case without her."

On June 11, 2021, the Pulitzer Prize Board gave Frazier a special citation, an honor outside its usual prizes. The citation identifies "the crucial role of citizens in journalists' quests for truth and justice." Frazier called recording the video a "traumatic life-changing experience."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="darnella-frazier" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A small-town paper raided: Marion, Kansas, 2023" -->
On August 11, 2023, Police Chief Gideon Cody of Marion, Kansas, led officers in a raid on the weekly *Marion County Record* and on the home of its publisher, Eric Meyer. The officers took computers and reporters' phones. Cody claimed that Meyer and the reporter Phyllis Zorn had committed identity theft or computer crimes by checking a local business owner's driving record. Identity theft means using another person's private information as if you were that person. Meyer's mother, Joan Meyer, was 98 and owned part of the paper. A police camera recorded her telling officers, "Get out of my house!" She died of a heart attack the next afternoon.

Special prosecutors later found that no one at the paper had committed a crime. They said Cody had reached a wrong conclusion through "a poor investigation." They said they could not charge anyone over Joan Meyer's death. Cody was charged with felony obstruction of justice, a serious crime, for asking the business owner to delete text messages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reporters arrested, hit and deported, 2017 to 2026" -->
Since 2017 the US Press Freedom Tracker, run by the Freedom of the Press Foundation, has counted attacks on journalists in the United States. By September 2026 it had counted 1,487 assaults and 445 arrests or criminal charges. In 2020 and 2021 together it counted nearly 800 assaults.

In 2025 the tracker counted at least 32 journalists arrested or held and at least 170 assaulted. Most were covering protests against deportation, the sending of immigrants out of the country. On June 9, 2025, police in Los Angeles surrounded and held at least 10 journalists at one protest. In January 2026 the tracker counted at least nine assaults on journalists in the Minneapolis area. Most of those journalists were hit by pepper balls or by pieces of stun grenades, both of them crowd-control weapons. The tracker's January report does not say who fired each one. Pepper balls are small balls fired from a gun that burst into a stinging powder. On January 15, 2026, federal agents held the photographer John Abernathy on the ground.

Mario Guevara was a Spanish-language reporter who covered immigration near Atlanta. He was arrested on June 14, 2025, while he livestreamed a protest near Atlanta, which means he was showing it live on the internet. The Committee to Protect Journalists' account of the case does not name the police who arrested him. Federal officials held him and argued that his filming of police was a danger to public safety. On October 3, 2025, they deported him, sending him out of the country to El Salvador. The Committee to Protect Journalists said it was the first time it had recorded this kind of punishment for reporting.

Starting in 2010, Julian Assange published secret documents about the US wars in Afghanistan and Iraq on his WikiLeaks website. In June 2024 he pleaded guilty under the Espionage Act of 1917.

On February 11, 2025, White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt told the Associated Press that its reporters could no longer come into the Oval Office with the press pool, the small group of reporters that covers the president up close. The ban was to last until the AP agreed to call the Gulf of Mexico the Gulf of America. In April a federal judge, Trevor McFadden, ordered the AP's access restored. In October 2025 officials at the Pentagon made reporters agree to have their material checked in order to keep their passes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Public broadcasting loses its federal money, 2025 to 2026" -->
In July 2025 Republican majorities in both houses of Congress passed the Rescissions Act, and President Trump signed it. The act took back about $1.07 billion already promised to the Corporation for Public Broadcasting for 2026 and 2027, according to the research group USAFacts. That money had gone to NPR, PBS and local public radio and television stations. In May 2025 Trump had signed an order calling for an end to government money for news outlets that did not meet standards of "fairness, accuracy, and nonpartisanship." Nonpartisanship means not taking the side of any political party.

In January 2026 the corporation's board voted to close it after 58 years. Between July 2025 and May 2026, public radio and television stations cut about 550 jobs, according to a count reported by Nieman Lab. Rural stations and stations run by Native nations had gotten a large share of their money from the federal government, and they were hit hardest. By mid-2026 at least one public television station had shut down, and more had announced plans to close. No full count had been published. Stations of every kind saw the number of people giving them money rise between 2024 and 2025.

Officials also pressed commercial broadcasters, the stations paid for by advertising. In 2025 Brendan Carr, the chair of the Federal Communications Commission, pressed ABC to take the talk show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* off the air. Carr acted after Kimmel made comments about which political side was held by the man accused of killing the activist Charlie Kirk. In 2026 Carr opened an investigation of the talk show *The View* under the rule that stations must give political candidates equal time.
