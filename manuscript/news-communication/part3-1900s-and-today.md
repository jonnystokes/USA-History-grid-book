## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 17 minutes**

Radio carried the news into American homes as it happened, and in two world wars federal officials worked to control what Americans printed and heard. Magazine reporters investigated companies and city governments from 1902 on and printed long reports about what they found. During World War I, federal officials ran the government's first large propaganda office. They also barred dozens of newspapers from the mail and filed more than 2,000 criminal cases against people for what they said or printed.

The Westinghouse company owned one of the first radio stations, KDKA, from 1920, and then networks paid for by advertisers took over. In World War II, government officials asked newspapers and radio stations to keep military secrets. Japanese Americans forced into camps printed their own newspapers, and camp officials checked what they printed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The muckrakers, 1902 to 1912" -->
Some reporters in the early 1900s dug into one subject and then wrote long reports about it, and editors at magazines such as *McClure's*, *Cosmopolitan* and *Collier's* printed them. The January 1903 issue of *McClure's* contained three such reports.

**Three reports in one issue.** One was part of Ida Tarbell's history of the Standard Oil Company. By 1880 that company controlled 90 to 95 percent of the oil refining in the United States, the work of turning crude oil from the ground into products people can use. Another was Lincoln Steffens's report on corruption, officials using their jobs to get money or favors illegally, in the city government of Minneapolis. The third was Ray Stannard Baker's report on coal miners.

**The Jungle.** Upton Sinclair spent about seven weeks undercover, hiding who he was, in the meatpacking plants of Chicago, the factories where animals were killed and the meat was cut up and packed. His novel *The Jungle* came out as a book in February 1906. He wanted readers to care about the workers, but readers were most upset about what went into their meat. "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach," Sinclair wrote.

**Patent medicines.** In a series that ran in *Collier's* in 1905 and 1906, Samuel Hopkins Adams reported on patent medicines, bottled cures sold with false claims. He wrote that Americans would "swallow huge quantities of alcohol, an appalling amount of opiates and narcotics" in them. By opiates he meant drugs made from the opium poppy.

President Theodore Roosevelt signed two new laws on June 30, 1906. Under the Meat Inspection Act, federal inspectors worked in the packing plants. The Pure Food and Drug Act banned food and medicine with false labels or harmful added ingredients from being sold across state lines.

**Muckrakers.** David Graham Phillips attacked members of the US Senate in a series called "The Treason of the Senate." Roosevelt answered with a speech on April 14, 1906. He compared these writers to the Man with the Muck Rake in John Bunyan's religious book *The Pilgrim's Progress*, a man raking up muck, mud and filth, "who could look no way but downward."

Roosevelt meant it as an insult. The writers took his word "muckraker" as a name to be proud of. By 1912 this kind of reporting had mostly disappeared from the magazines, in part because large companies stopped buying advertisements in them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-s-abbott" name="Robert S. Abbott" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert S. Abbott

> **Who:** The son of formerly enslaved parents who founded the *Chicago Defender* and sent it into the South, where it urged Black families to move north.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1905 to 1940.

**He printed "(white)" after white people's names.**

Robert S. Abbott started the *Chicago Defender* with 300 copies in 1905, and by 1920 it outsold every other Black newspaper in the country. He was born on St. Simons Island, Georgia, in 1868. His parents, Thomas and Flora Abbott, had been enslaved.

**Shut out.** Abbott learned printing at Hampton Institute in Virginia. After he graduated in 1896 he moved to Chicago, where he was shut out of the printing business because he was Black. The record does not name the shops that turned him away. He earned a law degree in 1899, but because he was Black he could not get work as a lawyer in Indiana and Kansas.

Abbott printed the first issue of the *Chicago Defender* on May 5, 1905, and sold the 300 copies himself. In his paper's headlines and articles he attacked lynching and white supremacy, the belief that white people should rule over everyone else. One headline read, "When the Mob Comes and You Must Die Take at Least One with You."

White papers such as the *Chicago Tribune* printed "colored" after the names of Black people. Abbott printed "(white)" after the names of white people.

**North.** At that time 90 of every 100 Black Americans lived in the South, and Abbott sent the paper there. He urged readers to leave the South. In the *Defender* he printed job listings, train schedules, and photos of what he presented as the best schools, parks and housing in Chicago. For four years starting in 1916, more than 10,000 Black Southerners left the South each month. Between 1916 and 1919 about 500,000 left.

**Smuggled south.** Local police in Southern towns tried to stop the paper. They seized copies, and they arrested people who sold it under vagrancy laws, which made it a crime to have no job. Some police departments banned the sale of Black newspapers altogether. The accounts do not name the towns. Abbott arranged for Pullman porters, the Black workers who served passengers on railroad sleeping cars, to carry bundles of the paper south and sell them in secret.

The *Defender* sold more than 250,000 copies a week by 1929, and Abbott was one of the first Black millionaires. He died in Chicago on February 29, 1940.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-s-abbott" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War I: the government's propaganda office, 1917 to 1919" -->
The United States entered World War I on April 6, 1917. That month President Woodrow Wilson set up the Committee on Public Information and put George Creel, a former newspaper reporter, in charge. It was the federal government's first large office for propaganda, information made and spread to push people toward one opinion.

Creel's staff sent articles to newspapers, and Creel later estimated that their material filled 20,000 newspaper columns a week. The committee's staff printed their own paper, the *Official Bulletin*, and about 115,000 copies went out. Its artists made posters, and one showed a German soldier as a gorilla carrying a club.

Staff in one division of the committee watched the hundreds of newspapers printed in other languages in the United States. After the war, many Americans concluded that Creel and his staff had pushed the war too hard, and that they had made it harder for people to speak against it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War I: papers banned from the mail and people jailed for words, 1917 to 1921" -->
Members of Congress passed the Espionage Act, named for espionage, or spying, on June 15, 1917. Under it, it was a crime to get in the way of the draft or to stir up disloyalty in the armed forces. Papers that urged treason could not go through the mail.

**Banned from the mail.** Postmaster General Albert S. Burleson told local postmasters to report anything suspicious. By 1918 his officials had barred 74 newspapers from the mail. The New York City postmaster, Thomas G. Patten, stopped *The Masses* magazine from going through the mail, and its November-December 1917 issue was its last.

Newspapers printed in German and other languages faced one more rule. Under the Trading with the Enemy Act of October 6, 1917, they had to file English translations of their war news with the local postmaster.

**The Sedition Act of 1918.** Members of Congress added the Sedition Act to the Espionage Act the next year, on May 16, 1918. Under it, it was a crime to speak or write anything "disloyal" about the government, the Constitution, the armed forces or the flag. The punishment was up to 20 years in prison and a $10,000 fine.

Federal prosecutors filed more than 2,000 cases under the two laws, and more than 1,000 ended in convictions. They used the Sedition Act mainly against pacifists, people who oppose all war. They also used it against anarchists, who want to do away with government, and socialists, who want the public rather than private owners to own big businesses.

The Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs gave a speech against the war to about 1,200 people in Canton, Ohio, on June 16, 1918. A judge sentenced him to 10 years in prison, and the justices of the Supreme Court upheld his conviction in 1919. President Warren G. Harding shortened his sentence, and Debs walked out of prison on Christmas Day 1921.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="victor-berger" name="Victor Berger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Victor Berger

> **Who:** An immigrant newspaper owner in Milwaukee whose paper was barred from the mail for opposing World War I, and who was sentenced to 20 years in prison.
> **When and where:** Milwaukee, Wisconsin, 1892 to 1929.

**Voters kept electing him, and the House kept refusing to seat him.**

Victor Berger was born to a Jewish family in Austria-Hungary. He came to the United States in 1878 and taught German in Milwaukee's public schools. He bought a German-language newspaper in 1892, and in 1898 he and Eugene V. Debs founded the Social Democratic Party. Milwaukee voters made him the first Socialist elected to the US House of Representatives in 1910. A year later he started a daily paper in English, the *Milwaukee Leader*.

**The permit.** Berger opposed the war and said so in his paper. In 1917 an official in Postmaster General Albert S. Burleson's department, the third assistant postmaster general, took away the *Leader*'s "second-class mailing privilege," the permit that let it go by mail as a newspaper.

Federal prosecutors charged Berger under the Espionage Act in 1918. He and four other Socialist leaders were convicted in Chicago, and in early 1919 Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis sentenced each of them to 20 years in prison.

**The empty seat.** Milwaukee voters elected Berger to Congress again in 1918, while his case went on. In November 1919 the other members of the House refused to let him take his seat. Voters elected him again in a special election, a vote held to fill the empty seat, with 55 percent of the vote, and the members of the House refused him again.

The justices of the Supreme Court overturned his conviction in 1921. Voters elected him to Congress three more times, in 1922, 1924 and 1926. He died in 1929 after a streetcar accident.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-berger" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio, and who owned it, 1920 to 1934" -->
Radio station KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcast the results of the presidential election between Warren G. Harding and James M. Cox on the evening of November 2, 1920. KDKA belonged to the Westinghouse company. Its studio was a shed on the roof of a building at the Westinghouse works in East Pittsburgh.

Eight stations were on the air by the end of 1921, and by November 1, 1922, federal officials had licensed 564. At first, money for radio came from two places: selling radio sets and selling advertising. The Queensboro Corporation paid station WEAF in New York $50 on August 28, 1922, for about ten minutes of advertising for its apartments. Over the following years, advertising became the main way American radio was paid for.

**Networks.** The National Broadcasting Company, NBC, bought WEAF in 1926 and set up a permanent network, a group of stations in different cities that carry the same programs. Other networks followed, among them the Columbia Broadcasting System, CBS.

**Rules for the air.** By the middle of the 1920s, so many stations were on the air that their signals crossed and drowned each other out. Federal judges had ruled that the secretary of commerce, who gave out the licenses, could not stop giving them out even when the dial was full. The broadcasters themselves asked the federal government to restore order.

The biggest makers of radio equipment and the biggest broadcasters had also made business deals with each other. Those deals could have given a few companies a monopoly over radio, with no real competition from anyone else.

Members of Congress passed the Radio Act of 1927. Under it, the members of a federal commission decided which station could use which wavelength, the spot on the radio dial where a station is heard. They gave each of the 732 stations a set place on the dial, and they could turn down a new station when no room was left. In 1934 Congress's members passed the Communications Act and with it set up the Federal Communications Commission, the FCC, to oversee radio and messages sent by wire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The president on the radio, 1933 to 1944" -->
President Franklin D. Roosevelt gave his first fireside chat, a talk to the country by radio, on Sunday night, March 12, 1933. He had closed every bank in the country on March 6, two days after he took office.

In the talk Roosevelt explained in simple terms how banks worked to some 60 million listeners. He told them it was safer to keep money in a reopened bank than under the mattress. When the banks opened again, people put in more money than they took out. Harry Butcher of CBS gave these talks the name "fireside chats." According to Encyclopaedia Britannica, scholars count between 27 and 31 of them, from 1933 to 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The War of the Worlds broadcast, 1938" -->
The actor Orson Welles and his Mercury Theatre on the Air performed H. G. Wells's novel *The War of the Worlds* on CBS on the evening of October 30, 1938. Welles was 23. The writer Howard Koch, the producer John Houseman and Paul Stewart had rewritten the story as fake news bulletins about Martians landing in New Jersey. The program did not stop to say it was a play until almost 40 minutes in.

Some listeners believed it and called the police, newspapers and radio stations. The next morning, editors across the country printed stories about a nationwide panic.

**The count.** That night the C. E. Hooper company had asked 5,000 households what they were listening to, and 2 of every 100 named the Welles program. A. Brad Schwartz, who wrote a book about the broadcast, *Broadcast Hysteria*, found that only some listeners were fooled. Their worried phone calls convinced many journalists that the show had caused a panic across the country, and the journalists reported one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures by wire, 1935" -->
Newspapers mostly got photographs by mail, train or airplane until 1935, and a picture could take up to 85 hours to arrive. The AT&T telephone company had run a service that sent photographs by wire from 1926, but it closed in 1933. Associated Press staff sent their first photograph over wires on January 1, 1935. It was a picture taken from the air of a plane crash near Morehouseville, New York. Forty-seven newspapers in 25 states received it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edward-r-murrow" name="Edward R. Murrow" movie="Good Night, and Good Luck (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edward R. Murrow

> **Who:** A CBS radio reporter whose eyewitness reports from Europe before and during World War II made him famous across the United States.
> **When and where:** London, 1937 to 1945.
> **Movie:** *Good Night, and Good Luck* (2005), directed by George Clooney, about Murrow's television reports on Senator Joseph McCarthy in 1953 and 1954.

**From London he reported the war to Americans by radio.**

Edward R. Murrow's radio reports from Europe for CBS, from 1938 to 1940, made him famous across the United States. He also helped bring to the United States German scholars whom the Nazis, Adolf Hitler's party, which ruled Germany, had driven out.

Murrow joined CBS in 1935. CBS executives sent him to London in 1937 to run the network's European office. On the radio he described Germany's moves across Europe as an eyewitness. In 1938 he reported Germany's takeover of Austria and the Munich Conference, a meeting of European leaders. He reported the takeover of Czechoslovakia in 1939, and the Battle of Britain, when German planes attacked Britain, in 1940.

**McCarthy.** After the war Murrow ran the news department at CBS. In 1954 he made a television program about Senator Joseph McCarthy. McCarthy had charged that Communists, people who wanted one party to run the government and the government to own the farms and factories, were working in the government. Murrow died on April 27, 1965.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edward-r-murrow" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War II: secrets, propaganda and the Black press, 1941 to 1945" -->
Japanese planes attacked the US Navy base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on December 7, 1941. Twelve days later President Roosevelt set up the Office of Censorship. Its director, Byron Price, had been an editor at the Associated Press. The office had about 13,500 workers, and most of them read letters and cables, messages sent by wire under the ocean, going into and out of the country.

**The code.** Price and his staff gave newspapers and radio stations a code of rules, and editors agreed to follow it of their own free will. The code listed topics to keep out of the news: troop and ship movements, how war factories were doing, the president's travels, and even the weather. Price persuaded the reporters Drew Pearson and William L. Laurence not to write about the building of the atomic bomb.

Roosevelt set up the Office of War Information on June 13, 1942. Its staff ran programs, at home and in other countries, that told people how the government said the war was going and what its war plans were.

Federal prosecutors charged far fewer people for their words than in World War I. Philip A. Dynia, writing in the First Amendment Encyclopedia, counts dozens charged, against thousands in 1917 to 1919. Roosevelt kept pressing his attorney general, Francis Biddle, to "indict the seditionists," meaning to charge with crimes the people he saw as turning others against the government.

**Double V.** Writers for Black newspapers such as the *Chicago Defender* reported on the armed forces' separate units for Black troops, white officers placed over Black soldiers, blood supplies kept apart by race, and violence against Black servicemen. They ran a "Double V" campaign: victory over the enemy abroad and victory over prejudice at home.

Federal officials threatened to charge Black publishers with sedition. The *Defender*'s publisher, John H. Sengstacke, insisted on meeting Biddle. Biddle agreed to block the charges as long as Black editors did not make their criticism of the war any stronger.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers behind barbed wire, 1942 to 1945" -->
President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, a written command from the president, on February 19, 1942. Under it, Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt ordered Japanese Americans on the West Coast out of their homes, some with only 48 hours' notice. Soldiers and federal officials forced them into camps because of their Japanese ancestry.

**How many.** The National Archives counts about 122,000 people, and nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens. Another count gives 110,000 to 120,000. The First Amendment Encyclopedia describes the camps as concentration camps, places where people are held by force without a trial.

Inside the camps, Japanese Americans wrote, printed and handed out their own newspapers. The papers in the first camps, run by the army, were copied on mimeograph machines, which print copies from a cut paper stencil. Army officials required those papers to be written in English only. All ten camps run by the War Relocation Authority had papers in both English and Japanese, among them the *Manzanar Free Press* in California and the *Heart Mountain Sentinel* in Wyoming.

Officials checked what the papers printed. Army officials censored the papers in the first camps. In the later camps, officials of the War Relocation Authority interfered with what the papers printed, and in some cases they censored the papers outright.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Television begins, 1927 to 1941" -->
Philo Farnsworth, who was 21, sent the first picture by all-electronic television, a straight line, in his laboratory in San Francisco on September 7, 1927. Station WNBT in New York showed the first television advertisement allowed by law on July 1, 1941, before a baseball game. It showed a Bulova watch face over a map of the United States. It lasted about ten seconds and cost $9.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 18 minutes**

Television became the way most Americans got the news. About 9 of every 100 American homes had a television set in 1950, and by 1960, 87 of every 100 did. After the DuMont network closed in 1955, three national television networks were left: NBC, CBS and ABC.

Managers of one Mississippi station, WLBT, cut civil rights news off the air. A church leader and two Mississippi leaders fought the station before the government and in court, and its owners lost their license.

Reporters printed a secret history of the Vietnam War in 1971 and tied a burglary to the White House in 1972, and officials in Washington tried to stop them. By 1996 cable channels such as CNN and Fox News showed news all day and night, and the World Wide Web had been open to the public since 1991.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Television and the blacklist, 1950 to 1954" -->
Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin gave a speech in Wheeling, West Virginia, in February 1950. He said that 205 communists, followers of a system in which one party runs the government and owns the farms and factories, worked in the State Department. When senators asked him, he could not name a single one.

**The book.** Three former FBI agents who ran a company called American Business Consultants published a book called *Red Channels* in June 1950. The book named 151 people who worked in radio and television and claimed they had ties to communism.

The company's owners then visited networks, advertisers and sponsors. For a fee, they offered to "clear" the people they had named. To be cleared, a person usually had to give the FBI or the House Un-American Activities Committee, a committee of Congress, the names of other people. Network executives and advertisers fired people on the list, which became a blacklist, a list of people whom employers refuse to hire or keep.

**On camera.** A Senate hearing on McCarthy's charges against Army officers and officials was shown on television across the country in 1954. It lasted 36 days. On camera, Joseph Welch, the Army's lawyer, asked McCarthy, "Have you no sense of decency, sir, at long last?"

Edward R. Murrow showed McCarthy's methods on his weekly CBS program *See It Now* that same year. Members of the Senate voted 67 to 22 on December 2, 1954, to condemn McCarthy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio and television in Spanish, 1946 to 1961" -->
Raoul A. Cortez was born in Xalapa, Mexico, in 1905. He worked as a reporter for *La Prensa*, a Spanish-language newspaper in San Antonio, Texas. In 1946 he opened KCOR in San Antonio, the first all-Spanish radio station owned and run by a Hispanic American.

Federal officials had limited broadcasts in languages other than English during World War II. To get his license, Cortez told officials that part of the station's purpose was to rally Mexican Americans behind the war effort.

Cortez started KCOR-TV in 1955, the first television station aimed only at Hispanic viewers. It broadcast on a kind of channel called UHF, and few television sets could pick it up, so advertisers were hard to find. He sold the station in 1961, and the new owners renamed it KWEX.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The station that cut the news: WLBT, 1955 to 1971" -->
The managers of WLBT, a television station in Jackson, Mississippi, that carried NBC's programs, cut civil rights news off the air, and a group of citizens fought for years to take away the station's license. The Lamar Life Insurance Company started WLBT in 1953.

**"Cable Trouble."** The lawyer Thurgood Marshall appeared on an NBC program in the fall of 1955 to talk about the Supreme Court's decisions against segregation, keeping Black and white children in separate schools by law. WLBT staff cut him off and showed a sign that read "Sorry, Cable Trouble from New York." The general manager, Fred Beard, said he did it "because the TV networks were overloading the circuits with Negro propaganda," using a word of that time for Black people.

Station staff also cut away from NBC's evening news when it turned to civil rights. WLBT reporters used racial slurs on the air. The White Citizens' Council, a group that fought to keep segregation, ran a bookstore in the station's lobby. About half the people in WLBT's viewing area were Black, and the station's managers refused them time to answer.

**Medgar Evers.** Medgar Evers was the Mississippi organizer for the NAACP, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, a civil rights group. In May 1963 he spoke on WLBT to answer the mayor of Jackson, Allen Thompson. Three weeks later Byron De La Beckwith shot Evers in the back in the driveway of his home and killed him.

That year WLBT staff again ran the "Cable Trouble" sign, this time over NBC's report on white people attacking protesters at a lunch counter in Jackson. WLBT's own crew had filmed the attack.

**The complaint.** The Reverend Everett Parker of the United Church of Christ joined Aaron Henry, head of the Mississippi NAACP, and A. D. Beittel, the president of Tougaloo College, in 1963. They gathered complaints from viewers, knowing that they could be attacked for it. In 1964 they asked the Federal Communications Commission not to renew WLBT's license, the government permit a station needs to broadcast. The commissioners gave the station a one-year renewal without a hearing.

Judges on a federal appeals court ruled in 1966, in an opinion by Warren Burger, that viewers had the right to take part in these cases. In 1969 the judges ordered the commission to take away the owners' license. The owners appealed and lost in 1971. A board of Black and white citizens then ran the station, and the board hired some of the first Black television managers in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Civil rights news on the page and on the screen, 1955 to 1965" -->
Roy Bryant, his half-brother J. W. Milam and at least one other person took 14-year-old Emmett Till by force from his relatives' home in Mississippi in August 1955. Bryant and Milam later described beating him and shooting him in the head.

Three days later Till's body was found in the Tallahatchie River, weighed down with a 75-pound metal fan from a cotton gin, a machine that cleans cotton. The men had fastened the fan to his neck with barbed wire. An all-white jury found Bryant and Milam not guilty of murder. Afterward, both men told the writer William Bradford Huie, for *Look* magazine, that they had kidnapped and murdered Till.

**The photographs.** *Jet*, a weekly news magazine for Black readers published in Chicago by John H. Johnson's company, printed photographs of Till's body on September 15, 1955. *Jet* sold about 425,000 copies a week at the time. The Till issue sold out, and its publishers printed more.

**The University of Mississippi.** A crowd of more than 2,000 white segregationists rioted at the University of Mississippi on the night of September 30, 1962, to stop James Meredith from enrolling as its first Black student. Of the 212 people that marshals and soldiers held afterward, only 24 were students at the university, Justice Department lawyers wrote.

People in the crowd threw a pipe, rocks, bricks and bottles at US marshals. Hundreds of people were hurt, and two men died. One of them was Paul Guihard, a 30-year-old reporter for the French news agency Agence France-Presse, who was covering the story.

Someone shot Guihard in the back at close range, and the bullet hit his heart. No one has ever been charged with killing him. Justice Department lawyers closed the case in 2011 without naming anyone.

**Selma.** Alabama state troopers and other officers led by Major John Cloud attacked civil rights marchers with clubs and tear gas on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma on March 7, 1965. At least 50 marchers went to the hospital. Television cameras filmed the attack, and the film was flown to New York.

The ABC newscaster Frank Reynolds interrupted the network's showing of *Judgment at Nuremberg*, a film about Nazi war crimes, at about 9:30 that night to show the film of the attack. Nearly 50 million people had tuned in to the film.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="walter-cronkite" name="Walter Cronkite" movie="Walter Cronkite: Witness to History (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Walter Cronkite

> **Who:** The CBS anchor who read the evening news to much of the country for 19 years, and who told viewers in 1968 that the Vietnam War was stuck.
> **When and where:** New York, 1962 to 1981.
> **Movie:** *Walter Cronkite: Witness to History* (2006), a PBS *American Masters* documentary about him.

**"If I've lost Cronkite, I've lost Middle America."**

Walter Cronkite was the anchor of the *CBS Evening News*, the person who reads the news and introduces the reports. A poll in 1972 named him "the most trusted man in America." He grew up in Houston, Texas, where he wrote for his school newspapers.

**War reporter.** In World War II Cronkite reported for the United Press news service. He flew on bombing raids over Germany and covered the D-Day landings in 1944. Edward R. Murrow hired him for CBS in 1950.

**The anchor desk.** Cronkite became the anchor of the *CBS Evening News* in 1962. Soon after, the program grew from 15 minutes to 30 minutes, the first half-hour nightly news program on network television. In 1963 Cronkite told viewers that President John F. Kennedy had been killed. He covered the Moon landing in 1969 and nearly every American spaceflight with a crew from 1961 to 1981.

**Vietnam.** Cronkite went to Vietnam in 1968, after the Tet Offensive, a large attack by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces. On February 27, 1968, he told viewers that the war was "mired in stalemate," stuck in a fight that neither side could win. President Lyndon Johnson told his staff, "If I've lost Cronkite, I've lost Middle America," meaning ordinary American families. Encyclopaedia Britannica records that some people held this to be the reason Johnson decided not to run for president again that year.

Cronkite ended each program with "And that's the way it is." He left the anchor desk in 1981. He died in New York on July 17, 2009.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="walter-cronkite" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vietnam and the reporters, 1965 to 1971" -->
As late as 1964 fewer than two dozen American reporters were in Indochina, the region that includes Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. By 1968 about 600 journalists from many countries were in Vietnam. More than 60 journalists were killed during the war, and the record of their deaths does not say who killed them.

US military officers did not censor the reports from Vietnam. The military's daily news briefings in Saigon were nicknamed "the five o'clock follies," after follies, silly shows.

Some Americans later blamed the press for turning people against the war, and Encyclopaedia Britannica records that the argument goes on. Experts who studied the news coverage, Britannica states, found that most of the reporting supported the war until 1968. They found that support at home fell most as more American soldiers were killed.

**My Lai.** American soldiers of Charlie Company killed unarmed villagers at My Lai in South Vietnam on March 16, 1968. The counts run from at least 175, found by the Army's own inquiry, to 504, the number of names on a memorial in the village. The soldiers also raped women and girls. Rape means forcing someone into sex by violence or threat.

Seymour Hersh, a freelance reporter, one who worked for himself rather than for one newspaper, followed a tip in 1969 and found Lieutenant William Calley. Hersh wrote the first public account of the killings, and he won a Pulitzer Prize, a top award for reporting, in 1970. Calley was found guilty in March 1971 of murdering 22 civilians. He was the only soldier convicted, and no one was convicted of rape.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pentagon Papers, 1971" -->
Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara ordered a secret history in 1967 of what American leaders had done in Vietnam since World War II. It filled 47 volumes. Daniel Ellsberg, who had worked on it, gave copies to *The New York Times* without permission. The *Times* began printing stories about it on June 13, 1971.

The papers recorded that President Johnson had planned open war in 1964, a year before the public knew. They also recorded that he ordered the bombing of North Vietnam in 1965, even though intelligence officials had judged that it would not work.

**Prior restraint.** After three days, Justice Department lawyers got a prior restraint, a court order that stops something from being printed before it comes out, against the *Times*. *The Washington Post* also had the papers. The justices of the Supreme Court voted 6 to 3 on June 30, 1971, that the newspapers could keep printing.

Prosecutors charged Ellsberg under the Espionage Act, and he faced up to 115 years in prison. Men working for President Richard Nixon, led in this case by the former CIA officer E. Howard Hunt, broke into the office of Ellsberg's psychiatrist, a doctor who treats mental illness. They were looking for information to damage Ellsberg. In 1973 the judge dismissed all the charges because of this break-in and other wrongdoing by government officials.

The justices of the Supreme Court ruled in 1972, in a case called *Branzburg v. Hayes*, that the Constitution gives reporters no right to refuse to tell a grand jury who their sources are, the people who give them information. That same year two reporters at *The Washington Post* began getting information from a source whose name stayed hidden until 2005.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="woodward-and-bernstein" name="Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein" movie="All the President's Men (1976)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein

> **Who:** Two young *Washington Post* reporters who showed that a burglary in 1972 was tied to President Nixon's White House.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1972 to 1974.
> **Movie:** *All the President's Men* (1976), about their reporting, with Robert Redford as Woodward.

**Five men were caught inside the Democrats' office at Watergate.**

Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, two young reporters at *The Washington Post*, tied a 1972 burglary to President Richard Nixon's White House. Police arrested five men inside the office of the Democratic National Committee, the national leaders of the Democratic Party, in the Watergate building in Washington early on June 17, 1972. One of them, James W. McCord Jr., was the security chief of Nixon's re-election committee.

Woodward had covered minor crimes for the *Post* for nine months. Bernstein covered politics in Virginia. With a third reporter, Alfred E. Lewis, they wrote about the arrests for the next morning's paper.

**Deep Throat.** Nixon's press secretary, Ron Ziegler, called it a "third-rate burglary attempt." Most early news stories said the White House had nothing to do with it. Woodward and Bernstein kept reporting, and a secret source they called "Deep Throat" gave them information.

They reported on October 10, 1972, that the bugging of the Democrats' office, which means hiding microphones there to listen in, came from "a massive campaign of political spying and sabotage," or secret wrecking, run by White House officials for Nixon's re-election.

**CBS cut short.** CBS News aired the first part of a two-part report on the story on October 27, 1972. Nixon's aide Charles Colson then threatened William Paley, the head of CBS, and the second part was cut short. The records do not say who at CBS made the cut. That November Nixon won re-election, carrying every state except Massachusetts. In 1973 the *Post* won the Pulitzer Prize for public service for Woodward and Bernstein's reporting.

**Hearings on television.** The reporting was one step among several that led to Nixon's resignation. Television stations showed a Senate committee's Watergate hearings live for two weeks in May 1973, and more than 150 public television stations showed them again in the evening. A month after the hearings began, 97 of every 100 Americans had heard of Watergate. A White House aide, Alexander Butterfield, told the committee that Nixon kept a voice-activated tape recorder in rooms of the White House.

**The tapes.** The justices of the Supreme Court ordered Nixon to hand over the tapes on July 24, 1974. One tape showed that on June 23, 1972, he had ordered the FBI to hold back its investigation. Members of the House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment, formal charges that could lead to his removal from office. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974.

W. Mark Felt, who had been an associate director of the FBI, one of its top officials, said in 2005 that he was Deep Throat.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woodward-and-bernstein" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reporters killed in the United States, 1976 to 1990" -->
**A car bomb in Phoenix.** Don Bolles, a 47-year-old reporter for the *Arizona Republic*, went to a Phoenix hotel on June 2, 1976, to meet a man who had promised him information. It was to be about land fraud, cheating people in land deals, and organized crime, crime run by gangs. The man never came. When Bolles started his car, a bomb under it exploded.

Doctors had to cut off both his legs and one arm, and he died on June 13. In 1977 John Harvey Adamson pleaded guilty to building and planting the bomb. A jury later convicted Max Dunlap of murder and of conspiracy to murder, which means planning a murder with others. Dunlap died in prison in 2009.

Reporters from many other papers came to Arizona and spent five months finishing Bolles's reporting.

**Five journalists for refugee papers.** Killers murdered five Vietnamese American journalists between 1981 and 1990. They worked at small papers for refugees from the Vietnam War. Reporters at ProPublica who looked into the killings in 2015 tied them to what the papers printed about Vietnam and its government.

Duong Trong Lam, 27, was shot outside his home in San Francisco in 1981. A gunman chased Nguyen Dam Phong from his house in Houston in 1982 and shot him seven times.

Pham Van Tap was killed by the smoke from a fire that someone set in his office in Garden Grove, California, while he slept. A message sent to the Vietnamese press after his death said he was killed for printing advertisements for companies that did business with Vietnam.

The last two worked for the same magazine in Virginia, *Van Nghe Tien Phong*. A gunman shot and killed Do Trong Nhan, 56, who laid out the magazine's pages, in his car as he got ready to drive to work. Ten months later, on September 22, 1990, killers shot and killed the writer Le Triet and his wife, Dang-Tran Thi Tuyet, in their car in the driveway of their home.

FBI agents suspected a group led by former South Vietnamese military officers, but they never found enough evidence to charge anyone. No one was ever arrested or charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Public broadcasting, the fairness rule, cable news and talk radio, 1967 to 1996" -->
Lawmakers, federal officials and owners changed who paid for the news on radio and television, and what it had to include, between 1967 and 1996.

Members of Congress set up the Corporation for Public Broadcasting in the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967, to hand out federal money to public television and radio stations. People at the corporation started PBS in 1969 and NPR, National Public Radio, in 1970.

Ted Turner's company started CNN, the first television channel to show news 24 hours a day, on June 1, 1980. It reached viewers through cable, wires that carried many channels into homes.

**The fairness doctrine.** The members of the Federal Communications Commission required stations from 1949 on to follow a rule called the fairness doctrine. Station owners had to cover important public questions fairly and air more than one side, and people attacked on the air had to get a chance to answer. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld the rule in 1969.

Members of Congress passed a bill in June 1987 to make the rule a law. President Ronald Reagan vetoed the bill, refusing to sign it. He wrote that the rule "requires Federal officials to supervise the editorial practices of broadcasters." That year the commissioners, led by their chairman, Dennis Patrick, voted 4 to 0 to end the rule.

**Talk radio.** Rush Limbaugh began a national radio talk show in 1988 on which he gave his conservative political opinions, views that favor keeping old ways and limiting government. Within five years an estimated 20 million people listened each week. His show began the year after the fairness doctrine ended. Whether the end of the rule helped talk shows like his grow is argued and not settled.

Rupert Murdoch's company started the Fox News Channel on October 7, 1996. Roger Ailes, a television producer and former Republican political adviser, ran it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The internet, 1969 to 1996" -->
Researchers at the University of California, Los Angeles, sent the first message over ARPANET, a computer network paid for by the Defense Department, to the Stanford Research Institute in late 1969.

Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working in Switzerland, opened the World Wide Web, the system of linked pages that people open over the internet, to the public in 1991. A team at the University of Illinois released Mosaic in 1993, the first widely used program for viewing web pages with pictures.

President Bill Clinton signed the Telecommunications Act in February 1996. Under it, one company could own more television stations, and many media companies merged after it passed. Under one part of it, sending "indecent" material to children online was a crime. The justices of the Supreme Court struck down that part in 1997. They ruled that the internet has the same protection for free speech as printed words.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

- 1969: Federal judges order the FCC to take WLBT's license from the owners who cut civil rights news off the air.
- June 30, 1971: The justices of the Supreme Court vote 6 to 3 that newspapers may keep printing the Pentagon Papers.
- July 24, 1974: The justices order President Nixon to hand over his tapes.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to today · about 14 minutes**

Since 2000 the news has moved from paper to phones and feeds, and the men who own the biggest sites have changed what those feeds show. In a 2025 survey by the Pew Research Center, 86 of every 100 American adults said they got news at least sometimes from a phone, computer or tablet, and 64 of every 100 from television. Newspapers lost most of their advertising money. Researchers at Northwestern University counted close to 3,500 newspapers that closed between 2005 and 2025.

People angry at what reporters wrote killed journalists in their newsrooms and outside their homes. Police and federal agents arrested and held reporters at protests. In 2025 members of Congress took back the federal money for public radio and television.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The news on phones and feeds, 2004 to 2025" -->
Mark Zuckerberg and three other Harvard students started Facebook in February 2004. Three former workers at the PayPal company started YouTube in 2005, and Google bought it in November 2006 for $1.65 billion. Twitter began in 2006.

**The feed.** Zuckerberg announced changes to Facebook's feed, the list of posts the site shows each user, in January 2018. Users would see fewer posts from news outlets and businesses and more posts from their friends and family.

**Section 230.** Under a law of 1996, known as Section 230, a website cannot be treated as the publisher of what its users post. That means the company that runs the site is not legally responsible, as a newspaper publisher would be, for what users write there.

Smartphones spread quickly. In 2011, according to the Pew Research Center, 35 of every 100 American adults owned one, and by 2025, 91 of every 100 did.

In a Pew survey in August 2025, about half of American adults said they got news at least sometimes from social media, most often on Facebook and YouTube. Only 7 of every 100 said they often read news in a printed newspaper or magazine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="False news, measured, 2018" -->
Three researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, published a study of news on Twitter in 2018. They followed about 126,000 chains of retweets, posts shared again on Twitter, from 2006 to 2017. Each chain began with one news story that people passed along. About 3 million people shared the stories more than 4.5 million times.

**What they found.** Six fact-checking groups had judged which stories were true and which were false. The researchers found that false stories were 70 percent more likely to be shared again than true ones. A true story took about six times as long as a false one to reach 1,500 people. False stories about politics spread fastest.

Some accounts on Twitter were run by computer programs, called bots. When the researchers left out the bots, the result stayed the same. So people, the researchers concluded, spread the false stories.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owns the feed, 2022 to 2026" -->
Elon Musk bought Twitter for $44 billion in October 2022. He laid off about half its workers and cut back content moderation, the work of checking posts and removing those that break a site's rules. He began selling blue check marks for $8 a month.

Musk renamed the site X in 2023. In March 2025 he sold X to his artificial intelligence company, xAI, in a deal that valued X at $33 billion. In 2026 his rocket company, SpaceX, bought xAI.

On Facebook, Mark Zuckerberg made his own change. He announced in January 2025 that the company would end the fact-checking programs it had used for about ten years.

**TikTok.** TikTok belonged to ByteDance, a company in China. President Joe Biden signed a law in April 2024 that required ByteDance to sell TikTok within a year or see the app banned. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld the law in January 2025, and President Donald Trump then delayed the ban several times.

A new company took over TikTok in the United States in January 2026. The software company Oracle, the investment firm Silver Lake and a fund owned by the government of the United Arab Emirates each own 15 percent of it, and ByteDance kept 19.9 percent.

**CBS and CNN.** David Ellison bought Paramount, the company that owns CBS, in 2025, and he hired Bari Weiss to run CBS News. A *60 Minutes* report about a prison in El Salvador was pulled from the air in December 2025, three hours before it was to run. The correspondent, Sharyn Alfonsi, wrote that Weiss "spiked our story," which means stopped it, after CBS lawyers had cleared it.

Ellison's company also agreed to buy Warner Bros. Discovery, which owns CNN, for $111 billion. According to NPR, both deals depend on money from his father, Larry Ellison, a founder of Oracle. The top lawyers of 12 states, all of them Democrats, sued over the deal.

Paramount's owners settled the lawsuit in September 2026 by agreeing to set up a board meant to keep the owners out of news decisions at CBS and CNN. The purchase was not yet complete at the end of September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Newspapers close: the news deserts, 2005 to 2025" -->
American newspapers took in less than a fifth as much money from advertising in 2022 as in 2005. Researchers at the Pew Research Center put the figures at about $49.4 billion in 2005 and an estimated $9.8 billion in 2022.

**Where the money went.** Advertisers spent more and more online, and most of that money went to big technology companies that do not report the news, such as Google and Facebook. Pew researchers wrote in 2014 that these companies could reach far more people than any news outlet, and aim their ads better than any one newspaper's website could. In 2014 five companies, including Google and Facebook, took in 61 of every 100 dollars spent on online ads in the United States.

Researchers at the Medill school of journalism at Northwestern University count newspapers every year. In their 2025 report they found that almost 2 of every 5 local newspapers in the country had closed since 2005, close to 3,500 papers in all. Papers were still closing in 2025, at more than two a week. Since 2005 the newspaper business had lost more than three of every four of its jobs.

**News deserts.** The Medill researchers call a county with no local newspaper, news website or station a news desert. The number of news deserts grew from just over 150 counties in 2005 to 212 in 2025, and most of them were in the countryside. Another 1,525 counties had only one local source left. In all, about 50 million people lived in a county with one local source or none.

**Fewer owners.** The number of companies and people who owned American newspapers fell by more than half, from 3,995 in 2005 to just under 1,900 in 2025.

The *Chicago Defender*, the paper that urged Black Southerners to move north in the 1910s, ended its print edition in 2019. Its owners now publish it only online.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeff-german" name="Jeff German" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jeff German

> **Who:** A Las Vegas newspaper reporter who was stabbed to death by an elected official he had written about.
> **When and where:** Las Vegas, Nevada, 1980s to 2022.

**The official he wrote about stabbed him outside his home.**

Robert Telles, an elected official in Las Vegas, stabbed the reporter Jeff German to death in 2022 after German reported on Telles's office. German had reported in Las Vegas for four decades, and at the time of his death he worked for the *Las Vegas Review-Journal*. He wrote about organized crime and dishonest officials, and he hosted a *Review-Journal* podcast about the mob called *Mobbed Up*.

**The stories.** That year German wrote about the office of Robert Telles, who had been elected Clark County Public Administrator. When a person in Clark County dies and no family member can step in at once, workers in that office guard the dead person's property and money until they can hand it to the people who should get it.

German's stories described bullying in the office and the punishing of workers who complained. They also described an "inappropriate relationship" between Telles and a staff member. Telles denied it. In June 2022 he lost his election.

**The evidence.** Telles stabbed German to death outside German's home on September 2, 2022. Police found Telles's DNA, the chemical code inside every person's cells that can show who was at a place, on German's hands and fingernails. In Telles's home, police found a straw hat and sneakers, cut into pieces, like the ones the killer wore on a security camera video.

A jury found Telles guilty of murder on August 28, 2024. In October 2024 Judge Michelle Leavitt added more time because German was over 60 and Telles had used a deadly weapon. Telles was sentenced to 28 years to life in prison.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeff-german" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A newsroom attacked: Annapolis, 2018" -->
Jarrod Ramos walked into the newsroom of the *Capital Gazette* in Annapolis, Maryland, on June 28, 2018, with a shotgun, smoke grenades and extra ammunition. He killed five people who worked there: Gerald Fischman, Rob Hiaasen, John McNamara, Rebecca Smith and Wendi Winters.

Ramos held a grudge because in 2011 the paper's reporters had written that he was convicted of harassing a woman he had gone to high school with. In 2021 a jury found that he was responsible for his crimes, and a judge sentenced him to five life terms in prison with no chance of release.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="darnella-frazier" name="Darnella Frazier" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Darnella Frazier

> **Who:** A 17-year-old who recorded a Minneapolis police officer killing George Floyd, and whose video became evidence at his trial.
> **When and where:** Minneapolis, Minnesota, 2020 to 2021.

**She was 17 and out walking with her nine-year-old cousin.**

Darnella Frazier's phone video of a police officer killing George Floyd reached people around the world. She was walking with her cousin in Minneapolis on May 25, 2020, when she saw the police officer Derek Chauvin kneeling on the neck of George Floyd, a Black man, and she recorded it. Her video showed Chauvin kneeling on Floyd's neck for nine minutes and 29 seconds, and it recorded Floyd's murder.

**The trial.** According to NPR, the video played a major part in starting protests against police violence around the world. At Chauvin's trial in 2021, prosecutors played it again and again, and Frazier testified.

The jury found Chauvin guilty of three different legal grades of killing a person: second-degree murder, third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter, which means killing a person without meaning to kill. The journalist Ann Marie Lipinski said, "There is no case without her."

The members of the Pulitzer Prize Board gave Frazier a special citation, an honor outside their usual prizes, on June 11, 2021. The citation identifies "the crucial role of citizens in journalists' quests for truth and justice." Frazier called recording the video a "traumatic life-changing experience."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="darnella-frazier" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A small-town paper raided: Marion, Kansas, 2023" -->
Police Chief Gideon Cody of Marion, Kansas, led officers in a raid on the weekly *Marion County Record* and on the home of its publisher, Eric Meyer, on August 11, 2023. The officers took computers and reporters' phones. Cody claimed that Meyer and the reporter Phyllis Zorn had committed identity theft, pretending to be someone else by using that person's private information, or computer crimes. He said they had done it by checking a local business owner's driving record.

**Joan Meyer.** Meyer's mother, Joan Meyer, was 98 and owned part of the paper. A police camera recorded her telling officers, "Get out of my house!" She died of a heart attack the next afternoon.

Special prosecutors, lawyers brought in from outside to handle the case, later found that no one at the paper had committed a crime. They said Cody had reached a wrong conclusion through "a poor investigation." They said they could not charge anyone over Joan Meyer's death. Cody was charged in August 2024 with felony obstruction of justice, a serious crime of getting in the way of an investigation, for asking the business owner to delete text messages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reporters arrested, hit and deported, 2017 to 2026" -->
Staff of the US Press Freedom Tracker, a project of the Freedom of the Press Foundation, have counted attacks on journalists in the United States since 2017. More than half of all the assaults they counted came in two years, 2020 and 2021: nearly 800 of the 1,487 they had counted by September 2026. They also counted 445 arrests or criminal charges.

**Protests against deportation.** The tracker counted at least 32 journalists arrested or held in 2025 and at least 170 assaulted. Most were covering protests against deportation, the sending of immigrants out of the country. Police in Los Angeles surrounded and held at least 10 journalists at one protest on June 9, 2025.

**Minneapolis.** In January 2026 the tracker counted at least nine assaults on journalists in the Minneapolis area. Most of those journalists were hit by pepper balls, small balls fired from a gun that burst into a stinging powder, or by pieces of stun grenades, both of them crowd-control weapons. The tracker's January report does not say who fired each one. Federal agents held the photographer John Abernathy on the ground on January 15, 2026.

**Mario Guevara.** Mario Guevara, a Spanish-language reporter who covered immigration near Atlanta, was arrested on June 14, 2025, while he livestreamed a protest, showing it live on the internet. The Committee to Protect Journalists' account of the case does not name the police who arrested him.

Federal officials held Guevara and argued that his filming of police was a danger to public safety. They deported him on October 3, 2025, sending him out of the country to El Salvador. The Committee to Protect Journalists said it was the first time it had recorded this kind of punishment for reporting.

**WikiLeaks.** Julian Assange published secret documents about the US wars in Afghanistan and Iraq on his WikiLeaks website starting in 2010. Federal prosecutors charged him in 2019 under the Espionage Act of 1917, the law once used against the editor Victor Berger and against Daniel Ellsberg. In June 2024 Assange pleaded guilty under that law.

**The press pool.** White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt told the Associated Press, the AP, on February 11, 2025, that its reporters could no longer come into the Oval Office with the press pool, the small group of reporters that covers the president up close. The ban was to last until the AP agreed to call the Gulf of Mexico the Gulf of America.

A federal judge, Trevor McFadden, ordered the AP's access restored in April. In October 2025 officials at the Pentagon made reporters agree to have their material checked in order to keep their passes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Public broadcasting loses its federal money, 2025 to 2026" -->
Republican majorities in both houses of Congress passed the Rescissions Act in July 2025, and President Trump signed it. With the act, members of Congress took back about $1.07 billion already promised to the Corporation for Public Broadcasting for 2026 and 2027, according to the research group USAFacts. That money had gone to NPR, PBS and local public radio and television stations.

Trump had signed an order in May 2025 calling for an end to government money for news outlets that did not meet standards of "fairness, accuracy, and nonpartisanship," which means not taking the side of any political party.

The members of the corporation's board voted in January 2026 to close it after 58 years. Public radio and television stations cut about 550 jobs between July 2025 and May 2026, according to a count reported by Nieman Lab.

Rural stations and stations run by Native nations had gotten a large share of their money from the federal government, and they were hit hardest. By mid-2026 at least one public television station had shut down, and more had announced plans to close. No full count had been published. Stations of every kind saw the number of people giving them money rise between 2024 and 2025.

**Pressure on commercial stations.** Officials also pressed commercial broadcasters, the stations paid for by advertising. The members of the Federal Communications Commission give out the licenses that those stations need to broadcast.

The talk-show host Jimmy Kimmel spoke on his show in 2025 about which political side the man accused of killing the activist Charlie Kirk was on. Brendan Carr, the chair of the FCC, then pressed ABC to take *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* off the air. In 2026 Carr opened an investigation of the talk show *The View* under the rule that stations must give political candidates equal time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- Did the First Amendment stop the government from punishing people for what they printed? No. Seven years after it took effect, federal judges jailed editors under the Sedition Act, and during World War I prosecutors won more than 1,000 convictions for words.
- Who was shut out of carrying and printing the news? From 1802 to 1865 federal law let only free white persons carry the mail, and white mobs drove Black editors out of Memphis and Wilmington. Black and Cherokee Americans started papers of their own.
- Why have so many local newspapers closed since 2005? Advertisers moved their money online, where most of it went to technology companies such as Google and Facebook, so newspapers' advertising money fell by more than four-fifths between 2005 and 2022. Readers moved to phones and feeds too, and by 2025, 212 counties had no local news source.

**What changed:** In 1673 a post rider took about two weeks to carry the mail from New York to Boston. By 1920 radio listeners heard election results on election night, and by 2025 most American adults got at least some news on a phone, computer or tablet.

**Five dates to keep**
- September 25, 1690: *Publick Occurrences* appears in Boston and is stopped four days later.
- December 15, 1791: The First Amendment takes effect.
- March 3, 1865: Congress ends the whites-only rule for mail carriers.
- November 2, 1920: KDKA broadcasts the election results.
- June 30, 1971: The Supreme Court lets the Pentagon Papers be printed.

**Not known:** No record survives of what news Native traders passed along with the copper and shells they carried before 1500.

**Where to go:** Part of the Boston Post Road, the first post rider's trail in 1673, is now US Route 1, and you can drive it.
