## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 26 of 37 · Health, Disease, and Medicine · 10 eras · about 110 minutes**

**For most of American history nobody knew what caused disease, and after doctors learned how to stop it, the people with the least power were often the last to be protected and the first to be tested on.**

Cotton Mather, a Boston minister, wrote to scientists in London in 1716 about a scar on the arm of Onesimus, an African man Mather enslaved. Onesimus said it came from an operation that gave him a small case of smallpox, a disease of fever and blisters that often killed. The small case, he said, would keep him from ever catching it again. When Boston doctors tried the method in 1721, about 2 in 100 of the people they treated died, against 14 or 15 in 100 of those who caught smallpox the ordinary way.

This chapter follows sickness and healing through ten eras, from what ancient bones show to COVID-19. In the first eras, look for the new diseases that came after 1492 and emptied whole Native towns. Then for a long stretch doctors guessed at what caused disease and bled and purged their patients, until scientists showed in the late 1800s and in 1900 that germs and mosquitoes spread it. Through all of it, notice who paid for the new knowledge: enslaved women operated on with nothing to stop the pain, Black men in Alabama kept from treatment for 40 years, and children fed a virus on purpose.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why did new diseases kill so many Native people after 1492?
- How did Americans learn to stop smallpox, yellow fever and polio?
- Who was used in medical experiments without agreeing, and what changed because of it?

**Five minutes?** Read "The Tuskegee study, 1932 to 1972" in 1900 to 1950, then "The Tuskegee study ends, 1972 to 1997" in 1950 to 2000.
**Can skip:** "Midwives and surgeons in New Amsterdam", "Medical care for sailors, 1798", "New operations and new pictures of the body, 1954 to 1963". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

Almost everything known about sickness in this land before 1500 comes from bones and teeth, because nobody here wrote about illness then. They show that people had diseases, but not the ones that arrived after 1492 and killed great numbers of them. Smallpox, measles, influenza, yellow fever and cholera had never reached the Americas. Neither had mumps, chickenpox, malaria, diphtheria, typhus, leprosy or bubonic plague. Every one of these twelve diseases is an infection caused by a germ, a living thing too small to see.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What do the bones show?" -->
Some diseases leave marks on bone, so archaeologists, scientists who learn about the past from what people left behind, can find them in skeletons hundreds of years old. Two researchers, Debra Martin and Alan Goodman, wrote in 2002 that Native people before 1492 "were most definitely not living in a pristine, disease-free environment," pristine meaning untouched and pure.

Tuberculosis was here. It is caused by bacteria, one kind of germ, and it most often infects the lungs, though it can spread to the bones, including the spine. Skeletons with its damage appear after about the year 900. Most of them come from inland places in the middle of the continent and in the Southwest.

Bones also show diseases in the same family as syphilis, a disease that is passed during sex. Two of them, yaws and bejel, spread mainly by touching skin and mouths, not by sex. Bejel is also called non-venereal syphilis, which means syphilis that is not passed by sex.

A team of scientists studied the DNA of these bacteria, taken from five people buried in the Americas long ago. DNA is the set of chemical instructions inside every living cell. In 2025 the team reported that the bacteria behind syphilis and its whole family most likely began in the Americas. Their results fit that idea for every sample of these bacteria that scientists have studied, ancient or modern.

Martin and Goodman list other illnesses that were present: rabies, whooping cough, polio, hepatitis (an infection of the liver), herpes (a virus that causes sores), tularemia (a fever people catch from animals), and stomach infections from tiny living things called giardia and amoebas. They add that almost all of these were probably rare in any one group of people.

**Farming and weak blood.** People who took up farming became sicker in some ways. At Dickson Mounds in Illinois, people moved over many years from hunting and gathering to farming. Researchers studied the skeletons of people buried there, among them farmers who lived in the 1100s. Those farmers came to depend more and more on maize, which is corn, and the body cannot get much iron from maize.

Among the Dickson Mounds people, signs of anemia, having too few red blood cells, rose sharply. Anemia leaves a person weak and tired. In a skull it shows as spongy patches full of tiny holes on the top of the head and in the eye sockets. Those patches form where the marrow, the soft center of the bone, swelled to make more blood. Bone infections among the same people became nearly four times as severe, a team of researchers found in 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the crowd diseases were missing" -->
The diseases that later struck this land in epidemics can keep spreading only where great crowds of people live. An epidemic is a disease that spreads to many people in one place at the same time.

A person sick with measles can pass it on for less than two weeks. Then the sick person either dies or becomes immune, which means the body can fight off that disease and will not catch it again. So measles keeps spreading only where new children who have never had it are always being born.

One scientist worked out in 1957 how big a population must be for measles to stay in a place for good. His answer was 200,000 to 300,000 people, with about 7,000 of them still able to catch it. Another put the number at 500,000 in 1975.

In small towns spread far apart, a disease like that infects everyone who can catch it. Then it dies out, because nobody is left to infect. The diseases that last in small towns are slow ones, like yaws and pinta, another skin disease in the syphilis family, the scholar Linda Newson explained in 1993.

When the crowd diseases arrived after 1492, nobody here had been exposed to them and nobody was immune. The historian Alfred Crosby gave what followed a name in 1976: "virgin soil epidemics." In a virgin soil epidemic, everyone in a town can be infected at the same time.

Newson also reported research on genes, the instructions inside every cell that pass from parents to children. It suggested that among Native Americans, the body's defenses against germs varied less from person to person, which may have made whole towns easier to infect. Newson called that a partial explanation.

**Not known:** how many people lived north of Mexico in 1492. Scholars' estimates run from about 1 million, made in 1949, to about 18 million, made in 1983. Others fall between, such as about 1.85 million (made in 1976) and about 3.8 million (made in 1992). How many people died after 1492 depends on which starting number is right.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 6 minutes**

Three Europeans saw Native people in this land begin dying of diseases they had never had before, and each wrote down what he saw. None of the three could name the disease. One was a Spanish man stranded on the Texas coast in 1528. Another was a Portuguese man in Hernando de Soto's army in 1540. The third was an English scientist on the Carolina coast in 1585 and 1586, and only his account points to who carried the disease.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Texas coast, 1528 and 1529" -->
About 80 Spanish men came ashore in two boats on or near Galveston Island, on the Texas coast, in November 1528. They were survivors of a Spanish expedition, a group sent out to explore and conquer, led by a commander named Narváez. One of them, Cabeza de Vaca, later wrote an account of what happened. He wrote that the people of the island spoke two languages, and he called them the Capoque and the Han.

The islanders watched most of the Spaniards die. Only 15 of the 80 stayed alive. Five Spaniards camped on the coast grew so desperate that they ate the bodies of their own dead as each one died. Their names were Sierra, Diego López, Corral, Palacios and Gonçalo Ruiz. Nobody ate the body of the last one to die. Cabeza de Vaca wrote that the islanders were in an uproar when they learned of it.

Then a disease of the bowels, the long tube in the belly that food passes through after the stomach, killed half of the island's people, Cabeza de Vaca wrote. He did not name the disease. The islanders believed the Spaniards had caused the deaths, and they planned to kill the survivors. The Native man who had charge of Cabeza de Vaca talked them out of it. He told them that if the Spaniards had such power, they would have saved their own men, whom the islanders had watched die.

**Healing for food.** The islanders then told the Spaniards to heal the sick, and they gave them no food until they did. Cabeza de Vaca described how the island's own healers worked. A healer made small cuts in the skin over the place that hurt and sucked at the cuts. Healers also burned the skin at the painful spot on purpose, a treatment called cautery, and then blew on it.

Cabeza de Vaca tried the burning on himself and wrote that it helped him. The Spaniards blessed the sick, breathed on them, and said two Christian prayers, the Lord's Prayer and the Hail Mary.

Years later, on a long walk west, people brought Cabeza de Vaca a man with an arrowhead lodged in his chest above the heart. The year and the place are not certain, because accounts of the route differ. Cabeza de Vaca cut open the man's chest with his knife and pulled the arrowhead out. He closed the cut with two stitches, using a deer bone as a needle, and stopped the bleeding with hair from an animal skin. He wrote that the next day he cut the stitches and the man was well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Empty towns at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
The army of the Spanish commander Hernando de Soto reached Cofitachequi in the spring of 1540. It was a town on a river in what is now South Carolina or Georgia, and accounts place it in different spots.

A Portuguese member of the army, known only as the Gentleman of Elvas, wrote that there were large empty towns nearby, grown over with grass. People at Cofitachequi told the Spaniards that a disease had struck the land two years before. The people of those towns had moved away to other towns. The Gentleman of Elvas did not name the disease, and he did not say how many people died.

That disease arrived before de Soto's army did. No record shows where it came from. Accounts differ about whether de Soto's own army carried new diseases inland. The records do show that de Soto's men killed and enslaved Native people and took their food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Roanoke, 1585 and 1586" -->
English colonists sent by Sir Walter Raleigh lived on Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, in 1585 and 1586. The island lay in the land of the Carolina Algonquian peoples. The colonists visited Algonquian towns, and within a few days after they left a town, people there began to die quickly.

The scientist Thomas Harriot, who was with the colonists, wrote down the deaths. In some towns about 20 people died, in some 40, in some 60, and in one town 120. Harriot wrote that, as far as he could learn, the deaths happened only in towns the English had visited. The oldest people told him nothing like it had ever happened before. During the same months, he wrote, no English colonist died or became seriously sick.

Harriot did not name the disease. Peter Mires, writing in 1994 in the journal *Historical Archaeology*, compared Harriot's report with John Smith's later accounts. Smith had heard about an epidemic among the Accomac, a people the Roanoke colonists visited in the winter of 1585 to 1586. Mires concluded that the English colonists carried the disease, and that it was probably influenza. He called influenza probable, not certain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-harriot" name="Thomas Harriot" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Harriot

> **Who:** An English mathematician and scientist who wrote down, town by town, an epidemic among the Carolina Algonquians. His name is also spelled Hariot.
> **When and where:** Roanoke Island and the Carolina Algonquian towns, 1585 and 1586. His report was printed in London in 1588.

**The Algonquians said invisible bullets were killing them.**

Thomas Harriot recorded what the Carolina Algonquians believed was killing them in 1585 and 1586. Some believed the English God was killing people for the English. Others believed that more English people were still to come, to kill them and take their places. These coming English were already in the air, invisible and without bodies, and they were shooting "invisible bullets" into people at the English colonists' request.

Algonquian healers sucked what they called "strings of blood" out of the sick. They said these were the strings tied to the invisible bullets.

Harriot had taught how to find the way across the open ocean at Sir Walter Raleigh's house in London. He sailed with the colony that reached Roanoke Island on June 26, 1585, and there he and the artist John White mapped the area.

Wingina was the wiroans, or chief, of the people the English lived among. Twice he was so sick that he nearly died, and he sent for the English to pray for him. Wingina and others also asked the English to pray that their enemies would die of the same disease. Harriot wrote that he told them such prayers were ungodly. When their enemies did die, they came to thank the English anyway.

Harriot explained the deaths as "the special work of God" for the English. He also described how the Algonquians used a plant they called uppowoc, which is tobacco, as a medicine. They breathed in its smoke through clay pipes, and Harriot believed it kept them healthy.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 10 minutes**

Epidemics emptied Native towns along the Atlantic coast in the 1600s, sometimes years before colonists settled beside them. English colonists also died in large numbers in their own first years, from disease and hunger. The people who treated the sick in the colonies included a weaver with no known medical training and a minister.

In New Amsterdam, officials of a Dutch trading company paid the town's midwives. At the end of the century yellow fever reached Boston, after the virus that causes it and the mosquito that carries it had crossed the Atlantic on the ships of the slave trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown, 1607" -->
A group of 104 English men and boys built a fort at Jamestown, in Virginia, in 1607. The fort stood in the land of the Paspahegh, one of the nations led by the Powhatan paramount chief, a leader over the chiefs of many nations. By the end of September 1607, half of the 104 colonists were dead, most of them from sickness.

George Percy, one of the colonists, wrote down each death with its date and cause. John Asbie died on August 6 of the "bloudie Flixe," Percy's spelling of bloody flux. Flux was the old word for diarrhea, so a bloody flux was diarrhea with blood in it. Percy also listed "Swellings" and "Burning Fevers," and deaths from wounds in fighting.

Percy blamed hunger most of all. Each day, five men shared one small can of barley boiled in water. They drank from the river. At high tide its water was salty, and at low tide it was "full of slime and filth." Many nights three or four men died.

Then Native people of the area, whom Percy called "our mortall enemies," brought bread, corn, fish and meat to the fort in great plenty. Percy wrote that "divers Kings," meaning several Native leaders, brought more. He did not name them.

**The river and the drought.** The geographer Carville Earle pointed to the river in 1979. At Jamestown the river water was part salt and part fresh, so the colonists' waste stayed in it instead of washing away. Tree rings show a drought that lasted seven years, from 1606 to 1612. The historian Dennis Blanton wrote that colonists who were hungry and thirsty from the drought were the most likely to fall sick.

Only about 60 of about 240 colonists in the fort lived through the winter of 1609 to 1610. That winter is called the Starving Time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An epidemic on the New England coast, 1616 to 1619" -->
A disease killed Native people along about 200 miles of the coast of southern New England, as far as Cape Cod, from 1616 to 1619. As far as the records show, the European fishermen and traders nearby did not catch it.

No count of the dead exists. Estimates of the share who died rest on weak records, and they run from one third to nine tenths of the people in the area. Before the epidemic, the Wampanoag Nation had about 21,000 to 24,000 people, and the Patuxet town had about 2,000. The colonist Daniel Gookin later wrote down what Native people told him about the sick. "The bodies all over were exceedingly yellow," they said, both before the people died and after. Gookin's account dates from 1674.

No one has settled what the disease was. The ideas put forward include yellow fever, smallpox, plague, typhus and several other diseases. Two researchers, John Marr and John Cathey, proposed leptospirosis in 2010. Leptospirosis is an infection by bacteria that rats carry in their urine. Marr and Cathey suggested that rats from European ships spread it into the water and soil, and they called their idea a proposal for others to test, not an answer.

When the Plymouth colonists arrived in 1620, the Patuxet town was empty. The colonists built Plymouth on its site. Governor William Bradford wrote that so many had died that the living could not bury the dead. He wrote that in many places in the area, skulls and bones still lay above the ground where houses had stood.

Squanto, also called Tisquantum, was one of the few Patuxet survivors. Bradford wrote that Squanto told other Native people the English kept the plague buried in the ground. Squanto said the English could send it among them whenever they wished.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth's first winter, 1620 and 1621" -->
In two or three months of their first winter, about half of the roughly 100 Mayflower passengers at Plymouth died. Sometimes two or three died in one day. Bradford blamed the cold, the lack of houses, and scurvy and other diseases from the long voyage. Scurvy is a disease caused by too little vitamin C, which comes from fresh fruit and vegetables. The gums swell and bleed, the teeth loosen, and the person grows too weak to work.

At the worst, only six or seven people were well enough to care for everyone else. They cut wood, made fires, cooked, made beds and washed the sick people's clothes. William Brewster and Myles Standish were two of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-fuller" name="Samuel Fuller" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Fuller

> **Who:** A wool weaver with no known medical training who came on the Mayflower and became the doctor for Plymouth and its neighbors.
> **When and where:** Plymouth, Salem and Charlestown, in Massachusetts, 1620 to 1633.

**A weaver of woolen cloth became the doctor for three towns.**

Samuel Fuller treated the sick of Plymouth and the towns near it for about thirteen years, although no record shows he ever trained as a doctor. Before the voyage he lived in Leiden, in the Netherlands, where he wove woolen cloth. He came to Plymouth on the Mayflower in 1620. His brother Edward and Edward's wife died in Plymouth's first winter.

Settlers at Salem fell sick in 1629 with scurvy and with a fever that had spread on their ships. Their leader, John Endicott, asked Governor Bradford to send Fuller. Endicott had heard that Fuller had cured people "by letting blood and other means."

Letting blood, or bloodletting, meant cutting open a vein, most often in the arm, and letting blood run out into a bowl. Doctors of the time believed it balanced the fluids of the body. It does not cure scurvy or fever, and it left sick people weaker. In 1630 Fuller let blood from about 20 newly arrived colonists at Charlestown.

A fever killed more than 20 men, women and children at Plymouth in 1633, and many Native people nearby. Fuller treated the sick. Then he died of the same fever, sometime between August and late September 1633. The colonist Nathaniel Morton later wrote that Fuller "was their surgeon and physician, and did much good in his place."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-fuller" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox on the Connecticut River, 1634" -->
About 1,000 Native people closed themselves inside a fort on the Connecticut River in the winter of 1633 to 1634, upriver from Plymouth's trading house. A disease struck them, and more than 950 of them died, Bradford wrote. Many of the bodies lay rotting above the ground, unburied. Bradford did not name the nation or the disease.

That spring, smallpox struck the Native people living near the trading house itself. Smallpox is caused by a virus, a germ even smaller than bacteria, and it spread from person to person through close contact, face to face. It caused a fever and a rash, and the rash turned into blisters and then scabs. In general, about 3 of every 10 people who caught smallpox died, though the share in a single epidemic could be higher or lower. Many survivors had scars for life, most of all on their faces.

Bradford described the sick lying on hard mats. The blisters broke open and ran, and the skin stuck to the mats. When a sick person turned over, the skin of a whole side came off at once. So many were sick that no one could make a fire, fetch water or bury the dead. Some crawled on their hands and knees to get water and died on the way.

The English at the trading house brought the sick wood, water and food, and buried the dead. None of the English there caught the disease. Very few of the Native people survived. Their chief sachem, or leader, died, and so did almost all of his friends and relatives. Bradford did not write down his name. No record shows who brought the disease to the river.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Midwives and surgeons in New Amsterdam" -->
The officials who ran New Amsterdam paid some of the people who cared for its sick, and in 1658 they set up a place for sick patients. They were officials of the Dutch West India Company, a trading company, and they governed the whole colony of New Netherland. New Amsterdam stood on Manhattan, in the homeland of the Munsee Lenape.

Company officials appointed and paid the town's midwives, women who help other women give birth. The midwife Lysbert Dircksen lived in a house built for her with public money in 1638. The officials also made rules for the town's surgeons, such as Hans Kierstede, who arrived in 1638 and worked there for nearly 30 years. Under rules of 1652 and 1657, barbers from the ships needed permission to treat wounds on shore, and surgeons had to report how each wounded person had been hurt.

The Company surgeon Jacob Hendrichsen Varvanger asked in 1658 for a place to care for sick patients. Members of the colony's council agreed. They made Hilletje Wilbruch the matron, the woman in charge, and paid her 100 florins, which were Dutch coins, a year. Richard Amerman, who wrote about medicine in New Netherland, called it the first hospital.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tryntje-jonas" name="Tryntje Jonas" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tryntje Jonas

> **Who:** A midwife from Amsterdam who served as an official midwife of New Amsterdam.
> **When and where:** New Amsterdam, now New York City, by 1644.

**The mother named one man as the father, then another.**

Tryntje Jonas was the official midwife of New Amsterdam by 1644. Officials of the Dutch West India Company appointed her and paid her, and she had her own house on Pearl Street. She came from Amsterdam. Her daughter Anneke Jans arrived in New Netherland in 1630.

Tryntje Jonas swore to the colony's secretary on July 7, 1644, what a mother had said about her baby's father. She had been called to help a woman named Hillegont Joris give birth. Hillegont Joris first named "Jan, the pilot" as the father of the child. Afterward she named Laurens Cornelisz. The secretary copied the statement into the colony's record book, where it survives. The record does not say why the midwife was asked to report this.

After Tryntje Jonas died, her daughter Anneke Jans collected the pay that the Company still owed her mother for her work. The date of her death is not known.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tryntje-jonas" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox advice and yellow fever, 1677 to 1693" -->
After smallpox spread through Boston in 1676 and 1677, the minister Thomas Thacher of the Old South Church wrote a guide for families. It was printed in 1677 or 1678 on a single large sheet called a broadside. Librarians at Harvard University's medical library call it the first medical paper published in the American colonies.

Thacher told families not to put a smallpox patient under heavy bedclothes, in a hot room, or give strong hot drinks. He told them to give weak beer, a thin porridge of cornmeal and water, and boiled apples. He warned that bloody or black urine, and diarrhea, were danger signs.

**Yellow fever.** Yellow fever came from Africa. It is a disease caused by a virus, and a mosquito called Aedes aegypti carries the virus from one person to another when it bites. The virus and the mosquito both crossed the Atlantic aboard the ships of the slave trade, a team of scientists reported in 2007. Nobody knew about the mosquito's part until the early 1900s.

Yellow fever struck Boston in 1693. The makers of the PBS history series *American Experience* call Boston the first British colony in North America to have a yellow fever epidemic. That June a fleet of English warships arrived in Boston, and the first Boston death from the fever came on June 25. No surviving count gives the number of dead. Charleston and Philadelphia had yellow fever soon after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox along the slave-raiding paths, 1696 to 1700" -->
Smallpox broke out in Virginia in 1696 and spread south and west along the trading paths. It traveled with Native slave raiders and the captives they took for the colonial slave trade. By 1699 it had reached the Tunica people, in what is now Louisiana. It came back in waves until 1715.

By one estimate, some Native nations lost more than 60 of every 100 people. A South Carolina colonist wrote that smallpox had killed so many Native people that there was "little reason to believe they will be capable of doing any harm to us for several years to come."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 7 minutes**

Doctors in Boston and Charleston began protecting people against smallpox in these fifty years by inoculation: giving a healthy person a small case of smallpox on purpose, so that the person would not catch it again. Epidemics still killed thousands of people. Diphtheria killed about 5,000 people in New England, most of them children, and smallpox killed about half of the Cherokee Nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox and inoculation in Boston, 1721" -->
The ship *Seahorse* sailed into Boston from the island of Barbados on April 22, 1721, with sailors sick with smallpox. Boston had about 10,600 people. Close to 5,800 of them caught smallpox that year, and 844 died.

The minister Cotton Mather led the Boston ministers who backed inoculation. He had first heard of it from Onesimus, a man he enslaved. To inoculate someone, a doctor took pus from a smallpox sore and dried it. Then the doctor rubbed or scratched the pus into a cut in a healthy person's skin.

Almost all of Boston's doctors feared that inoculation would spread the disease. Zabdiel Boylston was the one Boston doctor who tried it, starting in June 1721. By the end of the epidemic, about 287 people had been inoculated, most of them by Boylston, and 6 of them died. That is about 2 in every 100. Among people who caught smallpox the ordinary way, about 14 or 15 in every 100 died.

**The fight.** The doctor William Douglass led the people who opposed inoculation. He called it a "poisonous humor," humor being an old word for a fluid of the body. James Franklin printed attacks on inoculation in his newspaper, the *New-England Courant*. The town's selectmen, its elected officials, ordered Boylston to stop. He started again two days later.

Someone threw a bomb through a window of Mather's house on November 14, 1721. The note that came with it ended with the words "I will inoculate you with this, with a pox on you!" The records do not name who threw it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="onesimus" name="Onesimus" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Onesimus

> **Who:** An enslaved African man who described inoculation to Cotton Mather years before Boston first tried it.
> **When and where:** Boston, enslaved by Cotton Mather from 1706 to 1716.

**He showed his enslaver the scar on his arm.**

Onesimus told Cotton Mather how people in Africa protected themselves against smallpox. Mather later used that knowledge to back inoculation during Boston's smallpox epidemic of 1721.

Members of Mather's North Church gave Onesimus to Mather as a gift in December 1706. Another account gives the year as 1707. Mather named him Onesimus, after an enslaved man in the Bible. The name he was born with is not recorded. He was probably Coromantee, one of the Akan peoples of the Gold Coast, which is now Ghana.

Mather wrote to the Royal Society in London, a group of leading scientists, in 1716. He wrote that he had asked Onesimus whether he had ever had smallpox. Onesimus answered "both, Yes, and No." He had "undergone an Operation, which had given him something of the Small-Pox, and would forever preserve him from it." He showed Mather the scar on his arm.

Mather wrote down the method in Onesimus's words: people "take Juice of Small-Pox" and "Cut the Skin, and put in a drop." Mather wrote that he heard this from Onesimus months before he read of the same method in reports from Europe.

Onesimus married and had two children. Both died before they were ten. His son Onesimulus died in 1714. His daughter Katy died of tuberculosis.

Onesimus paid money in 1716 toward buying another enslaved man, Obadiah, to take his place. Mather then freed him, but with conditions. Onesimus still had to work for Mather's household, shoveling snow and chopping wood, whenever Mather called for him. He also had to repay five pounds that Mather said he had stolen. Few records of his life after 1716 survive.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="onesimus" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zabdiel-boylston" name="Zabdiel Boylston" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Zabdiel Boylston

> **Who:** The one Boston doctor who tried inoculation in 1721, when almost all the others feared it.
> **When and where:** Boston, 1721 and 1722. Born 1679, died 1766.

**His first three patients were his son and two enslaved people.**

Zabdiel Boylston inoculated three people on June 26, 1721. One was his son Thomas, who was about six. The other two were people Boylston enslaved: Jack, a man of 36, and Jack's son Jackey, who was two and a half. By law Jack and Jackey were Boylston's property, so they could not refuse. The accounts of that day do not say whether Jack agreed.

Boylston wrote down how each one's illness went. Thomas and Jack each had about "an hundred" blisters full of pus. On the ninth day Boylston gave them a medicine to make them vomit. All three recovered.

He went on to inoculate between 242 and 248 of the about 287 people inoculated in the epidemic, by different counts. Six of them died. When the selectmen ordered him to stop, he started again two days later. For a time he stayed on his farm, because he could not leave it without risk of being attacked.

Later he sailed to London, where the scientists of the Royal Society made him a member. In 1726 he published a book about his inoculations, *An Historical Account of the Small-Pox Inoculated in New England*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zabdiel-boylston" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elizabeth-phillips" name="Elizabeth Phillips" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Elizabeth Phillips

> **Who:** A licensed midwife from London who delivered babies in Boston for more than forty years.
> **When and where:** Boston and Charlestown, Massachusetts, 1719 to 1761.

**Her gravestone says she delivered more than 3,000 babies.**

The gravestone stands in the Phipps Street Burying Ground in Charlestown, Massachusetts. It says that Elizabeth Phillips "brought into this world above 3000 Children." She delivered them in and around Boston.

She was born in Westminster, in London, in about 1685. The Bishop of London gave her a license to work as a midwife in 1718. She came to Boston the next year and worked there until she died in 1761.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-phillips" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The throat distemper, 1735 to 1740" -->
A disease began in Kingston, New Hampshire, in May 1735, and spread along the coast into Maine and Massachusetts. People called it the throat distemper, distemper being an old word for a sickness. Doctors today identify it as diphtheria, an infection by bacteria.

The bacteria killed the living tissue of the throat and nose. The dead tissue formed a thick coating across the throat, until the child could no longer breathe.

About 5,000 people in New England died of it by 1740, and more than three quarters of them were children. That count comes from Ernest Caulfield's 1939 book on the epidemic. In the town of Hampton Falls, New Hampshire, 210 people had died by July 1736, and 160 of them were under ten. The *Boston Gazette* newspaper printed treatments: bloodletting, borax (a white powder made from a mineral) or honey on the throat, and drinks made from herbs.

In Ipswich, Massachusetts, Mark and Hephzibah Howe lost eight children in November 1736. They were Abijah (1), Mark (2), Aaron (5), Mary (7), Lucy (9), Moses (11), Love (12) and Hannah (13).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox among the Cherokee and Catawba, 1738 and 1739" -->
Smallpox struck the Cherokee towns in the southern Appalachian Mountains in 1738 and 1739. Between 7,000 and 10,000 Cherokee people died, about half of the nation. About half of the Catawba Nation died too.

How the disease reached them is disputed. The trader James Adair wrote in 1775 that it came into Charleston on slave ships from West Africa. Historians writing for NCpedia, a North Carolina encyclopedia, give two other routes. In one, traders brought it. In the other, Cherokee warriors carried it home after fighting beside the British against the Spanish in Florida.

Cherokee healers treated the sick in sweat houses and then plunged them into icy streams. The NCpedia historians write that this only added to the number who died. Adair wrote that some survivors whom the disease had scarred killed themselves.

In Charleston, during the same epidemic, the doctor James Kilpatrick inoculated 800 to 1,000 people. Eight of them died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 10 minutes**

Philadelphia doctors opened a hospital for the sick poor in 1752 and a medical school in 1765, while epidemics went on killing people in the colonies and the Native nations. Smallpox spread through the colonies and the Native nations during two wars, and in 1763 the British commander at Fort Pitt and a trader tried to spread it on purpose.

Yellow fever killed about 4,000 to 5,000 people in Philadelphia in 1793, about one person in ten. Under a 1798 law that members of Congress passed and President John Adams signed, federal officials took money from sailors' pay for their medical care.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="doctor-caesar" name="Caesar" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Caesar

> **Who:** An enslaved healer whose cure for poison led South Carolina's lawmakers to pay his enslaver for his freedom.
> **When and where:** St. Paul's Parish, South Carolina, near the Edisto River. Freed in 1750. Died in 1754.

**Lawmakers paid 500 pounds for one man's cure for poison.**

South Carolina's lawmakers bought Caesar's freedom in 1750 in exchange for his cure for poison. Caesar was enslaved by John Norman of Beach Hill, near the Edisto River. He was known for curing people of poisoning and of rattlesnake bite.

White South Carolinians feared that the enslaved people who cooked their food would poison them. In 1740 the colony's lawmakers had made poisoning a crime punished by death. In November 1749 the members of the Commons House, the colony's lawmaking body, voted to pay Norman 500 pounds for Caesar's freedom. They made the payment final on May 31, 1750. The lawmakers also promised Caesar 100 pounds a year for life, and he received his first payment in 1751.

The *South Carolina Gazette* newspaper printed Caesar's cure in May 1750. It was made by boiling plantain roots, horehound and goldenrod with rum, sugar, tobacco and lye made from wood ash.

The same lawmakers passed a law in 1751 aimed at enslaved healers, "commonly called doctors." Under it, enslaved healers could not give anyone medicine without permission from white people.

Caesar died in early 1754, at about 77 years old. He left an estate worth 62 pounds, and it included an enslaved woman. The record of his estate does not give her name or say how he came to own her. His wife Lilly and his daughter Hannah were never freed by the lawmakers. They were still enslaved by John Norman.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="doctor-caesar" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pennsylvania Hospital and the first medical school" -->
Dr. Thomas Bond and Benjamin Franklin founded Pennsylvania Hospital in Philadelphia on May 11, 1751. They founded it to care for "the poor, sick and insane." Franklin's petition, a written request, described people "deprived of their rational Faculties," meaning they had lost their reason. It stated that they could be "contained and confined" there.

The first patients came on February 6, 1752, to a house on Market Street that the hospital used until its own building was ready. Writers for the Mütter Museum, a museum of medical history in Philadelphia, give the year as 1753. The permanent building opened in 1756. Its basement held rooms for patients with mental illness. The first floor was for men, and the second floor was for women.

Older places in the colonies also housed people in need. Philadelphia's almshouse, a home for the poor, opened in 1732. A New York workhouse of 1736, a place where poor people lived and were put to work, later became Bellevue Hospital. The place for the sick that Dutch officials set up in New Amsterdam in 1658 was older than both.

Dr. John Morgan founded a medical school at the College of Philadelphia, now the University of Pennsylvania, in 1765. The museum's writers call it the first medical college in the colonies. Students learned at the bedsides of the hospital's patients.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox as a weapon at Fort Pitt, 1763" -->
Two British men at Fort Pitt gave two Delaware men blankets from a smallpox hospital in 1763, hoping to spread the disease. Fort Pitt was a British fort where Pittsburgh stands now. That year Delaware, or Lenape, and Shawnee and Mingo warriors surrounded the fort, during the war led by the Ottawa leader Pontiac. Smallpox had broken out among the soldiers inside.

**The blankets.** Captain Simeon Ecuyer, the fort's commander, and the trader William Trent met two Delaware men, Turtle's Heart and Mamaltee, on June 24, 1763. Most accounts give that date, though one copy of Trent's journal dates the entry May 24. Ecuyer and Trent gave them two blankets and a handkerchief taken from the fort's smallpox hospital. Trent wrote in his journal, "I hope it will have the desired effect." Ecuyer signed the bill that charged the army for the blankets.

**The letters.** In July, General Jeffery Amherst, the British commander in North America, wrote to Colonel Henry Bouquet. He asked: "Could it not be contrived to send the Small Pox among those disaffected tribes of Indians?" By contrived he meant arranged, and by disaffected, unfriendly. Bouquet answered that he would "try to inocculate the Indians by means of Blankets."

Amherst also wrote of trying "to extirpate this Execrable Race," which means to wipe out completely a people he called hateful. Ecuyer and Trent had given away the blankets in June, before these letters, and on their own.

**Known:** Smallpox spread among the Native nations, and a man named Gershom Hicks reported in 1764 that about 100 people died of it between the spring of 1763 and April 1764.
**Not known:** whether the blankets caused any of it. The historian Elizabeth Fenn writes that no one can know whether the attempt worked, and a month after the meeting Turtle's Heart and Mamaltee showed no sign of smallpox.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox in the Revolution, 1775 to 1782" -->
Lord Dunmore, Virginia's royal governor, promised freedom in 1775 to people enslaved by the rebels if they joined his army. In the spring and summer of 1776, smallpox and other fevers spread through his camps. An estimated 500 Black soldiers of his Ethiopian Regiment died on Gwynn's Island, in Virginia, along with about 150 white soldiers.

General George Washington ordered the Continental Army to be inoculated on February 6, 1777. He wrote that if smallpox spread through the army, "we should have more to dread from it, than from the Sword of the Enemy." Dr. William Shippen ran the inoculations in secret, in winter, at Morristown, New Jersey, and in Philadelphia, so the British would not learn of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What started the Doctors' Riot of 1788?" -->
Medical students in New York were digging up the dead. To learn how the body is built, students cut open dead bodies and studied the parts, which is called dissection. They dug up bodies from the Negroes Burial Ground, where Black New Yorkers buried their dead ("Negro" was a word for Black people used at the time). They also dug up bodies from the paupers' graveyard, where the poor were buried.

Free and enslaved Black New Yorkers asked the city's Common Council, the group that ran the city, to stop it in February 1788. Their petition described "young gentlemen in this city who call themselves students of the physic," meaning students of medicine, who were digging up "bodies of our deceased friends and relatives." The council members took no action to stop the digging.

**The riot.** Boys outside New York Hospital saw a human arm from the dissecting room in April 1788. Crowds attacked the hospital. The mayor had a doctor and medical students taken to the city jail to protect them, and about 5,000 people then gathered at the jail.

Militiamen, ordinary citizens called up to serve as soldiers, had orders not to fire. A rock hit the politician John Jay in the head, a brick hit General Baron von Steuben, and the militiamen opened fire. The accounts of the riot do not name an officer who gave the order. As many as 20 people were killed, among them at least 3 rioters and 3 militiamen.

New York lawmakers banned grave robbing in 1789. The same law let judges order that the bodies of executed murderers, arsonists and burglars be dissected.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yellow fever in Philadelphia, 1793" -->
Yellow fever killed 4,041 people in Philadelphia from August to November 1793, by the count of Mathew Carey, who wrote a book about the epidemic. Other counts run to about 5,000, which was about one person in every ten in the city. About 20,000 people left the city. Nobody in 1793 knew that mosquitoes carried the disease.

Dr. Benjamin Rush treated patients by bleeding them heavily and giving them purges, medicines that emptied the bowels. Rush's purges were made with mercury, a metal. Losing that much blood could send a weak patient into shock, which happens when too little blood is left in the body. A person in shock becomes confused, the heart beats fast, and the blood pressure drops. A French doctor, Jean Devèze, used milder treatment at the Bush Hill hospital.

Mayor Matthew Clarkson issued a public order about the epidemic on September 10, 1793. From September 14, a committee of 26 citizens worked with him to run the city's response.

Absalom Jones and Richard Allen, two Black Philadelphians who led volunteers in nursing the sick, printed an account of the epidemic in 1794. They wrote that some prisoners were let out of jail after the elders of the African church asked, on the condition that they nurse the sick at Bush Hill. Two thirds of those prisoners were Black. Jones and Allen's account does not name the officials who let them out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-rush" name="Benjamin Rush" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Rush

> **Who:** A Philadelphia doctor who treated patients in the 1793 yellow fever epidemic, and a signer of the Declaration of Independence.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1793. He worked at Pennsylvania Hospital from 1783 to 1813.

**He told Philadelphians that Black people could not catch yellow fever.**

Benjamin Rush printed that belief in the newspapers. In fact Black Philadelphians caught the fever and died of it while they nursed the sick.

Rush bled his patients heavily during the 1793 epidemic and gave them purges made with mercury. Several doctors died, and most of the rest were worn out by sickness or work. Then Rush asked Absalom Jones and Richard Allen, who both knew how to bleed a patient, to care for the sick. He told them where to get medicine, how to give it, and at what stage of the fever to bleed. When they could not tell what to do, he came himself or sent his student Edward Fisher.

At Pennsylvania Hospital, Rush moved patients with mental illness out of the basement and onto hospital floors with windows.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-rush" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jones-and-allen" name="Absalom Jones and Richard Allen" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Absalom Jones and Richard Allen

> **Who:** Two Black Philadelphians who led Black volunteers to nurse the sick and bury the dead.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, September to November 1793. Their account was printed in 1794.

**They buried several hundred people and never asked for pay.**

Absalom Jones and Richard Allen led members of the Free African Society, a group of Black Philadelphians, in nursing the sick and burying the dead in 1793. In early September a notice in the newspapers asked "the people of colour" to help the sick. It stated that they could not catch the fever. Jones, Allen and a few others met to talk over how to act. The next day they asked the mayor how they could help.

They carried away and buried the dead, several hundred poor people and strangers in all. Five men they hired helped them, and two of those men were Allen's brothers. Rush taught them when to bleed a patient, and they bled more than 800 people.

The fever killed Black Philadelphians too. In 1792, 67 Black people were buried in the city. In 1793 the number was 305. When some Black nurses fell sick, the families they worked for put them out of their houses. Jones and Allen knew of one who died in a stable.

Mathew Carey wrote that Black nurses had overcharged the sick and stolen from them. In 1794 Jones and Allen printed their own account to answer him. For burying the dead, they wrote, they had "never received, nor never asked any compensation," meaning any pay. They also named Mary Scott, a Black nurse who refused to take more than her wage from a Mrs. Mason. Mrs. Mason then gave her six pounds a year for life.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jones-and-allen" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Medical care for sailors, 1798" -->
President John Adams signed the Act for the Relief of Sick and Disabled Seamen on July 16, 1798, after members of Congress passed it. Under it, federal officials took 20 cents a month out of every merchant sailor's pay to pay for sailors' medical care. For a time, the first marine hospital, a hospital for sailors, stood on Castle Island in Boston Harbor. Today's United States Public Health Service began as this program.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
