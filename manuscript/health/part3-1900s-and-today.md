## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1900 army doctors proved that a mosquito spreads yellow fever. In 1918 an influenza epidemic killed about 675,000 people in the United States. In 1932 doctors of the United States Public Health Service began watching 399 Black men in Alabama who had syphilis, and for 40 years they did not treat them. In 1937 a medicine mixed with a poison killed more than 100 people, and the next year a new law required drug makers to prove a drug safe before selling it. From 1942 doctors had penicillin, which kills many kinds of bacteria. From 1946 to 1948 Public Health Service doctors exposed 1,308 people in Guatemala to diseases passed during sex, on purpose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Proving the mosquito, 1900 and 1901" -->
In 1900 an army board led by Major Walter Reed studied yellow fever near Havana, Cuba. The other members were James Carroll, Jesse Lazear and Aristides Agramonte. Lazear raised mosquitoes and let them feed on yellow fever patients. A mosquito that had fed on a patient at least 12 days before passed the disease on when it bit someone else. Carroll and a soldier who volunteered caught yellow fever from these bites in late August. Lazear was probably bitten by an infected mosquito himself, and he died of yellow fever on September 25, 1900.

In November 1900 the board tested two buildings. In one, volunteers slept in bedding soiled by yellow fever patients, and they stayed well. In the other, a clean building, infected mosquitoes bit volunteers, and several caught yellow fever. All of them recovered. The volunteers signed written contracts that named the danger, and they were paid. After workers killed the mosquitoes in Havana, yellow fever there nearly stopped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clara-maass" name="Clara Maass" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Clara Maass

> **Who:** A New Jersey nurse who died after letting infected mosquitoes bite her in a yellow fever experiment.
> **When and where:** Havana, Cuba, 1901. Born in East Orange, New Jersey, in 1876.

Clara Maass died of yellow fever at 25, after she let infected mosquitoes bite her in Havana in 1901. She came from a poor German immigrant family in East Orange, New Jersey. She started training as a nurse at 17, at the Newark German Hospital, and she was its head nurse by 21. She nursed soldiers in the 1898 war with Spain and then in the Philippines.

In Havana the army paid $100 to volunteers, and $200 to those who caught the fever. In March 1901 Maass let infected mosquitoes bite her. She had a mild case and got well. In August 1901 she volunteered again, caught yellow fever, and died. Army leaders ordered the yellow fever commission broken up soon after her death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clara-maass" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mary Mallon, held on an island" -->
Mary Mallon was a cook in New York who carried typhoid germs in her body without being sick herself. Health officials counted 47 people infected through her cooking, and 3 of them died.

Officials of the New York City Board of Health held her on North Brother Island, in the East River, from 1907 for more than two years. In a letter in June 1909, she wrote that they kept her "a prisoner without being sick nor needing medical treatment." She also wrote, "I have been in fact a peep show for everybody." Officials took her back to the island in 1915. She stayed there until she died on November 11, 1938. She became known as "Typhoid Mary."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fewer medical schools, 1910" -->
In 1910 Abraham Flexner published a survey of the country's 148 medical schools. Leaders of the Carnegie Foundation had hired him to do it. He called several schools "beyond repair." In the years after, the number of medical schools fell to 66. The history magazine JSTOR Daily reports that members of the American Medical Association, most of them white men, used the report to close schools. Seven medical schools had trained Black doctors. Only two were left: Howard University in Washington, D.C., and Meharry Medical College in Nashville, Tennessee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Polio in New York, 1916" -->
Polio is a disease caused by a virus that can attack the nerves in the spine. It can leave a person unable to move the arms, the legs or the muscles used for breathing. This loss of movement is called paralysis.

In the summer of 1916 polio spread through New York City. Most of the sick were babies and small children. Fewer than 1 in 10 of the city's cases were in children older than five. Across the country there were more than 27,000 cases and more than 6,000 deaths. Counts of deaths in New York City alone run from about 2,000 to about 6,000.

Dr. Haven Emerson, the city's health commissioner, led the response. Newspapers printed the names and addresses of the sick every day. Health workers put signs on their houses and kept their families inside. A family that could not give the sick child a separate room and toilet had to send the child to a hospital. Police stood at the railroads, roads and rivers to turn back families trying to leave the city. The *New York Times* blamed Italian immigrants for the disease, and some New Yorkers beat Italian immigrants in the streets.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1918 influenza" -->
Influenza, or flu, is a disease caused by a virus that infects the nose, throat and lungs. In 1918 a new kind of influenza spread around the world. About 500 million people caught it, and at least 50 million died. About 675,000 of the dead were in the United States. Many of them were healthy adults between 20 and 40. There was no vaccine against it and no antibiotic, a medicine that kills bacteria, to treat the lung infections that followed. The sick had high fevers and heavy nosebleeds. Many died of pneumonia.

In Philadelphia, Dr. Wilmer Krusen, the city's director of public health, let a parade go ahead on September 28, 1918. About 200,000 people crowded onto Broad Street. Krusen said in public that the flu was not a threat, although army camps nearby were full of it. Within three days every bed in the city's 31 hospitals was full. City officials closed schools, churches and theaters on October 3. More than 12,000 people in Philadelphia died within weeks.

In 1918 the flu reached Brevig Mission, an Alaska Native village. Within five days, 72 of the 80 villagers were dead. Miners from the town of Nome thawed the frozen ground with steam and dug one grave for all of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josie-mabel-brown" name="Josie Mabel Brown" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josie Mabel Brown

> **Who:** A Navy nurse from Missouri who worked the influenza wards at Great Lakes, Illinois, and described them in an interview when she was about 100.
> **When and where:** Great Lakes Naval Training Station, Illinois, fall 1918 to 1919. Born on a Missouri farm in 1886.

Josie Mabel Brown nursed sailors dying of influenza at the Great Lakes Naval Training Station in Illinois in 1918. At the worst of the epidemic, 6,000 of the 173,000 men there were in the hospitals.

Brown was born on a farm in southwestern Missouri in 1886. She finished nurse training in 1917 and was called into the Navy. In the fall of 1918 she was ordered to Great Lakes.

On her first ward, one man lay dying on the bed and another lay on the floor. A third waited on a stretcher for the bed. The nurses wrapped each dead man in a sheet and tied a shipping tag to one big toe. The ambulance brought four sick men and took away four dead ones.

The nurses had no time to take temperatures. Patients had nosebleeds so strong that "the blood would just shoot across the room," she said. In some men, air leaked from torn lungs into the body, and she could see bubbles under the skin of their arms.

She worked 16 hours a day until just before Christmas 1918. In March 1919 she caught a fever of 104 or 105 herself, and she recovered. She told her story to her niece in 1986 and died at 104.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josie-mabel-brown" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium in the body, 1917 to 1932" -->
Radium is a metal that gives off radiation, a stream of energy that damages living cells. The body treats radium like calcium and builds it into the bones. There it keeps giving off radiation from the inside.

From 1917, young women painted watch dials with glowing radium paint at the U.S. Radium factory in Orange, New Jersey. They shaped their brushes into a point with their lips. The radium destroyed their bone marrow, the soft tissue inside bones that makes blood, so their bodies could not make enough blood. Their jaw bones died and broke apart. Some grew cancers of the bone. In 1924 a dentist, Theodor Blum, found the jaw bone of a dial painter, Grace Fryer, wasting away, and he called the damage "radium jaw."

Grace Fryer and four other dial painters sued. In June 1928 they settled with the company. Each woman received $10,000, though some sources say $15,000, plus $600 a year for as long as she lived, and her medical costs. The company's officers never admitted that the company was to blame. The settlement agreement was signed on June 8, 1928.

William Bailey sold radium dissolved in water as a medicine called Radithor. He falsely claimed to be a doctor and paid doctors to prescribe it. Eben Byers, a Pittsburgh businessman, drank about 1,400 bottles from 1927 to 1930. In 1931 a government investigator found that the bone in Byers's body was falling apart and holes were forming in his skull. He died on March 31, 1932, of a cancer of the jaw bone caused by the radium.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native health, 1928" -->
In 1928 a team of investigators led by Lewis Meriam reported on the lives of Native people on reservations and in government boarding schools. They wrote that "the health of the Indians as compared with that of the general population is bad." Both the death rate and the death rate of babies were high.

The investigators found tuberculosis "extremely prevalent," which means very common. They found trachoma, an eye disease that "produces blindness," to be "a major problem." In the government boarding schools the children's food was "deficient in quantity, quality, and variety." That means there was not enough of it, and it was poor and the same every day. "The medical service rendered the boarding school children is not up to a reasonable standard," they wrote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuskegee study, 1932 to 1972" -->
From 1932 to 1972, doctors of the United States Public Health Service watched hundreds of Black men in Alabama live with syphilis, and they kept the men from being treated. They wanted to see what the disease did to a body when no one treated it.

Syphilis is a disease caused by bacteria. It is passed during sex, or from a mother to her unborn baby. Left untreated, it goes through stages. First comes a painless sore where the germ entered. Weeks later come a rash, a fever and a sore throat. Then the germ can hide in the body for years with no signs. After 10 to 30 years it can damage the heart and the large blood vessels, the brain and the nerves. It can cause blindness, and it can cause dementia, which is the loss of memory and reason. It can kill. A baby born with syphilis can die.

In 1932 Dr. Taliaferro Clark, head of the Public Health Service's office for diseases passed by sex, began the study in Macon County, Alabama, near the town of Tuskegee. Dr. Raymond Vonderlehr ran it there and later took Clark's place. Most of the men were poor farmers. Many were sharecroppers, who farmed someone else's land for a share of the crop. The doctors chose 399 men who had syphilis and 201 men who did not, to compare them. They did not plan to cure the infected men. The study was planned to last six months, and it ran for 40 years.

The doctors told the men they had "bad blood" and that they were being treated for it. They never told the men the name of their disease, and they never asked for their consent. The men got free exams, hot meals on exam days and rides. From 1935 they were also promised about $50 toward their burials, if they let doctors cut open their bodies after death to study them. Eunice Rivers, a Black nurse, kept in touch with the men for all 40 years. She gave them aspirin and a "spring tonic" that did nothing against syphilis. Dr. Oliver Wenger, who ran the clinic in the region, wrote, "we have no further interest in these patients until they die."

The doctors also gave the men spinal taps. In a spinal tap a doctor pushes a needle between the bones of the lower back and draws out fluid from around the spinal cord. The doctors wanted to see whether syphilis had reached the brain and nerves. A spinal tap often causes a lot of pain and other harm. The doctors' letter to the men called it their "Last Chance For Special Free Treatment."

The doctors worked to keep treatment away from the men. They gave local doctors lists of the men's names and asked them not to treat them. In 1941 the army drafted some of the men and told them to start treatment. Public Health Service officers gave the draft board a list of 256 names to leave untreated, and the board members agreed. Penicillin became the usual cure for syphilis in the 1940s. Sources give 1943, 1947 and the early 1950s. The study doctors never offered it to the men. Vonderlehr later wrote, "I hope that the availability of antibiotics has not interfered too much with this project."

The doctors printed what they saw in the men. In 1936 they reported that the infected men had far more disease of the heart and blood vessels. Only 16 of every 100 infected men showed no sign of illness, against 61 of every 100 of the uninfected men. About ten years later, nearly twice as many infected men as uninfected men had died. The doctors worked out that the disease had cut the men's lives by about a fifth. In 1955 they reported that more than 30 of every 100 infected men whose bodies they examined had died because syphilis destroyed their heart and blood vessels or their brain and spinal cord.

By the time the study was made public in 1972, 28 of the men had died of syphilis and 100 more had died of problems it caused. Forty of their wives had caught it. Nineteen of their children were born with it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-pollard" name="Charles Pollard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Pollard

> **Who:** An Alabama farmer who was kept in the Tuskegee study for 40 years without being told he had syphilis, and who then brought the lawsuit for the men.
> **When and where:** Near Tuskegee, Macon County, Alabama, 1932 to 1972.

Charles Pollard learned from the news in July 1972 that he had had syphilis for 40 years. He was 66. He farmed 66 acres of land he had inherited, about three miles from Tuskegee. His father was the son of two enslaved people.

In 1932 men from the study told Pollard he could get a free physical exam at a one-room school the next afternoon. "So I went on over and they told me I had bad blood," he said. "And that's what they've been telling me ever since." For 40 years doctors came around "from time to time and check me over."

Pollard went to Fred Gray, a lawyer in Tuskegee. Their lawsuit, *Pollard v. United States*, ended in a settlement of $10 million for the men and their families. In 1997 Pollard was one of the survivors at the White House when President Bill Clinton apologized to them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-pollard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A poison sold as medicine, and a new law, 1937 and 1938" -->
In 1937 a poison in a new medicine killed more than 100 people, and in 1938 a new law required drug makers to prove a drug safe before selling it. The S. E. Massengill Company of Bristol, Tennessee, sold a liquid medicine called Elixir Sulfanilamide for strep throat. Its chemist, Harold Watkins, dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol, a chemical used in antifreeze. Diethylene glycol is a poison. No law required the company to test the medicine for safety before selling it.

In September and October 1937 the medicine killed more than 100 people in 15 states, many of them children. It shut down their kidneys. They stopped passing urine and had strong stomach pain, vomiting and seizures before they died. A mother wrote to President Franklin Roosevelt about her daughter Joan: "we can see her little body tossing to and fro and hear that little voice screaming with pain." The owner, Dr. Samuel Massengill, said, "I do not feel that there was any responsibility on our part." Watkins killed himself.

On June 25, 1938, President Roosevelt signed the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which Congress had passed. Under it, a company had to prove to officials of the Food and Drug Administration that a new drug was safe before it could sell the drug.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Penicillin, 1942" -->
Penicillin is a drug made from a mold. It kills many kinds of bacteria, the germs behind infections such as blood poisoning, pneumonia and syphilis. It was the first antibiotic made in large amounts.

In March 1942 Anne Miller of New Haven, Connecticut, had an infection of the blood after a miscarriage, which is the loss of a baby before it can live outside the womb. For weeks her fever stayed at 103 degrees or higher. Her doctors got 5.5 grams of penicillin, half of all the penicillin in the United States at the time. She got her first dose on March 14. By the next morning her fever was gone. She was the first patient in the United States whose life was saved by penicillin.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Exposed on purpose in Guatemala, 1946 to 1948" -->
From 1946 to 1948, doctors of the United States Public Health Service, led by Dr. John Cutler, gave people in Guatemala syphilis, gonorrhea and chancroid on purpose, or tried to. All three are diseases passed during sex. The doctors did not ask the people for their consent.

The doctors exposed 1,308 people to these diseases: prisoners, soldiers and patients in the national mental hospital. They ranged from 10 to 72 years old. The records show some kind of treatment for 678 of them. The doctors infected women who were paid for sex and sent them to the prisoners. They also rubbed pus from sick men onto people's genitals, and they put germs into people's spinal fluid.

For tests, the doctors also took blood or spinal fluid from 5,128 people, among them children in orphanages and schools and people with leprosy. Leprosy is a disease caused by bacteria that damages the skin and nerves.

The money came from the Public Health Service, the National Institutes of Health and the Pan American Sanitary Bureau. Guatemalan officials supplied staff and buildings. Cutler's records note 83 deaths while the experiments went on. The records do not show how many of those deaths the experiments caused. The doctors never published their results. The public learned of the experiments in 2010.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal money for hospitals, 1946" -->
On August 13, 1946, the Hospital Survey and Construction Act became law. People call it the Hill-Burton Act, after its sponsors, Senators Lister Hill of Alabama and Harold Burton of Ohio. From 1947 to 1971, federal lawmakers set aside $3.7 billion under it to build hospitals, and states and towns added $9.1 billion.

Under the law, hospitals built with the money could keep Black and white patients apart, as long as the separate care was called equal. Accounts differ on who asked for that rule. Judges struck it down in 1963.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Other harm doctors saw in these years" -->
Pellagra killed about 100,000 Americans between 1900 and 1940, out of about 3 million who had it. Pellagra is a disease caused by too little niacin, a vitamin, in the diet. Most of the sick were poor people in the South who lived on little but cornmeal, molasses and dried pork. Scientists identified niacin in 1937.

From 1930 to 1932, almost 5,000 workers dug the Hawks Nest tunnel in West Virginia for the Union Carbide company, through the contractor Rinehart and Dennis. Most of them were Black men who had come from other parts of the South. They drilled through rock full of silica with no masks and no way to control the dust. The accounts do not name the company men who decided that. Silica is the mineral in sand. Breathing its fine dust scars the lungs until a person cannot breathe, a disease called silicosis. Death counts run from 476 to close to 1,000.

In Puerto Rico, under a law that took effect in 1937, members of a government Eugenics Board ordered 97 women sterilized by force. Sterilized means that a person can never become pregnant. Doctors did it by cutting or tying the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, or by removing the womb. Many thousands of other women were pushed into having the same operation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Polio paralyzed more than 21,000 Americans in 1952, most of them children. A vaccine tested on about 1.8 million children was announced in 1955, and the last polio case caught inside the United States came in 1979. In 1965 President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare, health insurance for people 65 and older, and Medicaid, for people with little money. A reporter exposed the Tuskegee study in 1972, and a federal health official ordered it ended that November. In the 1970s doctors in federally paid clinics and in the Indian Health Service sterilized poor Black and Native women and girls without their real consent. In 1981 doctors reported the first cases of the disease later named AIDS. By the end of 2000, 448,060 Americans had died of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henrietta-lacks" name="Henrietta Lacks" movie="The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks (2017, HBO)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henrietta Lacks

> **Who:** A Black woman whose cancer cells doctors took without asking her. Her cells were the first human cells to keep growing in a laboratory.
> **When and where:** Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, 1951. Born Loretta Pleasant in Roanoke, Virginia, on August 1, 1920.
> **Movie:** *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2017), a drama made for HBO about her and her daughter Deborah.

In 1951 doctors at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore cut cells from Henrietta Lacks's body without her permission or knowledge. Her cells were the first human cells to keep growing and dividing in a laboratory. At that time no rule required doctors to ask.

She grew up in Clover, Virginia, in a log cabin that had been slave quarters on a plantation. Her white great-grandfather and great-uncle had owned it. In January 1951 she went to Johns Hopkins with a lump in her womb. Doctors found cancer of the cervix, the opening of the womb. They treated her with radium placed inside her body, and then with X-rays. During her treatments, doctors cut two samples from her cervix.

Dr. George Gey grew the cells. They kept dividing, and he named them HeLa, from the first letters of her first and last names. By 1954 Jonas Salk's team used HeLa cells in its polio vaccine work. Another doctor, Chester Southam, injected HeLa cells into cancer patients, prisoners and healthy people to see whether cancer could be passed on.

She died on October 4, 1951, at 31, after the cancer spread through her body. Her family did not learn about the cells until 1975. In 2023 her family settled a lawsuit against a company, Thermo Fisher Scientific, that had made money from HeLa cells.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henrietta-lacks" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The polio vaccine, 1952 to 1979" -->
In 1952 doctors in the United States reported more than 21,000 people paralyzed by polio. Fewer than 1 in 100 children who catch polio are paralyzed. When polio paralyzes the muscles used for breathing, the person needs a machine to breathe. The iron lung was a metal tube about seven feet long. The patient lay inside with only the head outside. Bellows changed the air pressure inside the tube to pull air into the lungs.

Jonas Salk of the University of Pittsburgh made a vaccine from polio virus that had been killed. The money for his work came from the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, a charity that raised it in dimes and dollars.

Dr. Thomas Francis Jr. of the University of Michigan ran the test in 1954. About 1.8 million children in the United States, Canada and Finland took part. From April 26 to July 10, 1954, more than 443,000 children got at least one shot of vaccine. More than 210,000 got a placebo, a shot with no medicine in it. Neither the child nor the nurse knew which one it was. The children were called Polio Pioneers, and their parents signed permission forms. On April 12, 1955, Francis announced that the vaccine was 80 to 90 percent effective. Federal officials licensed it the same year.

That April, Cutter Laboratories in Berkeley, California, made 120,000 doses that still held live virus. About 40,000 children who got it had a mild form of polio. Fifty-six were paralyzed, and five died. The children passed the virus to others, and 113 more people were paralyzed and 5 more died. At a hearing in June 1955, members of Congress blamed mainly the federal laboratory that was supposed to check the vaccine.

Albert Sabin's vaccine, which people swallowed instead of getting a shot, was licensed from 1961 to 1963. The last case of polio caught inside the United States was in 1979. Between 15 and 40 years after they got sick, 25 to 40 of every 100 people paralyzed by polio as children get new muscle pain and new weakness. This is called post-polio syndrome.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jonas-salk" name="Jonas Salk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jonas Salk

> **Who:** The doctor who made the first polio vaccine and did not patent it.
> **When and where:** University of Pittsburgh, 1947 to 1955. Born in New York City in 1914.

Jonas Salk made the polio vaccine that was licensed in 1955, and he never took out a patent on it. A patent is a legal right to be the only one who may make and sell an invention.

He started work at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine in 1947. In 1952 he made a vaccine from polio virus that had been killed, so that it could not cause the disease.

His teacher, Thomas Francis Jr., ran the 1954 test. At first Salk did not want some children to get a dummy shot, because the dummy shot could not protect them against polio. He gave way, because only that kind of test could prove the vaccine worked.

After the results came out, the television reporter Edward R. Murrow asked Salk who owned the patent. Salk answered, "Could you patent the sun?" Leaders of the charity that paid for the work and of the university had already looked into a patent. They had judged that the method probably could not be patented. Salk died in La Jolla, California, in 1995.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jonas-salk" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="paul-alexander" name="Paul Alexander" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Paul Alexander

> **Who:** A Dallas boy paralyzed by polio at six who lived in an iron lung for more than 70 years and became a lawyer.
> **When and where:** Dallas, Texas, 1952 to 2024. Born in Dallas on January 30, 1946.

Paul Alexander lived in an iron lung for more than 70 years after polio paralyzed him in 1952. He was six, and there was an outbreak of polio in Dallas. Within days he could not move below his neck or breathe on his own. Doctors saved him with a tracheotomy, an operation that cuts an opening in the front of the neck into the windpipe so that air can get in. Then they put him in an iron lung.

From 1954 he taught himself to breathe outside the machine for hours at a time by swallowing air into his lungs. Every night he went back into it.

He held a stick in his mouth to turn pages, to paint, and later to type. He studied at Southern Methodist University and the University of Texas, earned a law degree in 1984, and worked as a lawyer in Dallas. In 2020 he published a book about his life, *Three Minutes for a Dog*.

"I've never thought of myself as a cripple," he said in 2017. "I'm Paul Alexander, human being." Guinness World Records named him the longest-surviving iron lung patient. He died in Dallas on March 11, 2024, at 78.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paul-alexander" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Indian Health Service, 1954 to 1976" -->
In 1954 President Dwight Eisenhower signed the Indian Health Transfer Act. Under it, Public Health Service officials took over health care for Native people from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, in a new Indian Health Service. That same year Dr. Thomas Parran reported to the Interior Department that Alaska Natives suffered "sickness, crippling conditions and premature deaths to a degree exceeded in very few parts of the world." Health care for Alaska Natives moved to the Indian Health Service in 1955, and its staff led the work against tuberculosis.

Under a 1959 law, Public Health Service workers brought clean water and sewers to reservations, and tribes helped decide where and how. In 1976 President Gerald Ford signed the Indian Health Care Improvement Act. Under it, Medicare and Medicaid could pay the Indian Health Service for the care its staff gave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New operations and new pictures of the body, 1954 to 1963" -->
On December 23, 1954, at a Boston hospital, Dr. Joseph Murray moved a healthy kidney from Ronald Herrick into his identical twin brother, Richard. Richard was dying of kidney disease. The body's defenses attack an organ from almost anyone else, which is called rejection. Because the twins were identical, Richard's body accepted the kidney. He lived eight more years, married and had two children.

In 1958 scientists at Brookhaven National Laboratory in New York built the first machine to make technetium-99m, a radioactive chemical. A doctor injects a tiny amount into a patient. It gives off rays that a camera outside the body picks up, to make pictures of the bones, the heart and other organs. Doctors first reported scans with it in 1963. Doctors around the world now use it in tens of millions of scans a year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fallout on Rongelap, 1954" -->
On March 1, 1954, United States scientists set off a hydrogen bomb called Castle Bravo at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands. It was nearly three times as strong as they expected. Fallout, the radioactive dust and ash that falls after a nuclear blast, came down on Rongelap, an island of the Marshallese people about 110 miles away. It looked like snow, and children played in it. Twenty-three Japanese fishermen on the boat *Lucky Dragon* got sick, and one died.

Officials moved more than 230 people off Rongelap, Rongerik and Utirik. They moved the Rongelap and Utirik people about 72 hours after the blast. Radioactive iodine from the fallout collected in their thyroid glands, which sit in the neck. In the years after, doctors found more thyroid lumps and thyroid cancers among them. Atomic Energy Commission doctors studied the exposed people for decades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radiation tested on people, 1944 to 1974" -->
In 1994 President Bill Clinton appointed a committee to investigate radiation experiments paid for by the federal government. Its 1995 report counted nearly 4,000 such experiments from 1944 to 1974. Among them, doctors injected plutonium, a radioactive metal, into hospital patients who did not know it. Other experiments were done on children and on prisoners.

The committee wrote that in the 1940s and 1950s it was common for doctors to use patients in research "without their awareness or consent." In some studies on children, the radioactive doses raised the children's lifetime risk of thyroid cancer. The committee also found studies in which patients died soon after receiving high doses of radiation.

Miners who dug uranium underground for nuclear weapons breathed radon, a radioactive gas that comes off uranium ore. At least several hundred of them died of lung cancer. The committee found that federal officials neither required the mines to be ventilated, which means fresh air pumped in, nor properly warned the miners. A 1990 law pays some of the miners and their families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thalidomide, 1960 to 1962" -->
In 1960 the drug company Richardson-Merrell asked the Food and Drug Administration for permission to sell thalidomide. It was a sleeping pill that doctors also gave pregnant women for morning sickness. Officials in more than 20 other countries had already approved it. Dr. Frances Kelsey, a new FDA reviewer, refused to approve it because the company's evidence was weak. For more than a year, under steady pressure from the company, she kept asking for more.

In November 1961 doctors in Europe linked thalidomide to babies born with arms and legs that were very short or missing. Around the world more than 10,000 babies were born harmed by it, and about 40 of every 100 died at birth or soon after.

Richardson-Merrell staff had already given more than 2.5 million tablets to more than 1,000 American doctors to "test." Nearly 20,000 patients took them, and several hundred of those patients were pregnant. At least 17 American babies were born with thalidomide damage. Company officials withdrew the application in March 1962. In October 1962, members of Congress passed the Kefauver-Harris Amendments with no votes against. Drug makers then had to prove that a drug worked, report its harms to the FDA, and ask people's consent before testing drugs on them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smoking and asbestos, 1954 to 1998" -->
On January 11, 1964, Surgeon General Luther Terry released a report on smoking by ten experts who had read more than 7,000 studies. The surgeon general is the country's top public health doctor. The report found that smokers died at a rate 70 percent higher than non-smokers. The average smoker was 9 to 10 times as likely to get lung cancer, and a heavy smoker at least 20 times. Smoking was the main cause of chronic bronchitis, a long-lasting swelling of the air passages, and was linked to emphysema and heart disease.

Ten years earlier, in January 1954, the big tobacco companies had paid for an advertisement in more than 400 newspapers that disputed the studies linking cigarettes to lung cancer. In 1957 Surgeon General Leroy Burney said the evidence showed that smoking caused lung cancer.

Lung cancer grows in the cells that line the air passages of the lungs. It causes a cough that does not go away, trouble breathing, and coughing up blood. Smoking causes about 9 of every 10 lung cancers in men and 8 of every 10 in women. In emphysema, the thin walls between the lungs' tiny air sacs break down, so the person cannot get enough air.

Under a 1965 law, every pack of cigarettes had to carry a health warning. From 1970 cigarette ads were banned from television and radio. On April 14, 1994, the heads of seven tobacco companies told Congress under oath that nicotine is not addictive. Under a 1998 settlement, the tobacco companies agreed to pay 46 states at least $206 billion over 25 years. In 2024 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the federal disease agency called the CDC, counted more than 480,000 deaths a year in the United States from smoking and from breathing other people's smoke. That is nearly 1 of every 5 deaths.

Asbestos is a group of minerals that split into thin fibers. Builders used it to fireproof and insulate buildings and ships. Its fibers, breathed in, stay in the lungs and scar them. They cause lung cancer and mesothelioma, a cancer of the thin lining of the chest and belly. The sickness can start 10 to 40 years or more after the person breathed the dust. Shipyard workers, insulation workers, miners and firefighters were among those who breathed it. Johns-Manville, the biggest maker of asbestos, went bankrupt in 1982 while facing thousands of lawsuits from sick workers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Medicare and Medicaid, 1965 and 1966" -->
In 1950 only 1 of every 8 Americans over 65 had health insurance, and two thirds of them had incomes below $1,000 a year. Health insurance is a plan that pays doctor and hospital bills.

On July 30, 1965, President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare and Medicaid into law at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri. Former President Harry Truman sat beside him. Truman had asked Congress for national health insurance 20 years earlier. Medicare is health insurance for people 65 and older. Medicaid is health insurance for people with little money, paid for by the states and the federal government together. Nearly 20 million people signed up for Medicare in its first three years.

Many hospitals in the South still kept Black and white patients apart. In 1963, in the case *Simkins v. Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital*, judges of a federal appeals court ruled that hospitals built with Hill-Burton money could not do this. In 1964 the justices of the Supreme Court refused to hear an appeal.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 barred federal money for any place that treated people differently by race. Medicare was to begin paying hospitals on July 1, 1966. Before then, almost 1,000 federal workers volunteered to inspect about 6,000 hospitals. They checked beds, waiting rooms, lunch rooms and hiring. Local Klan members chased inspectors on the roads. Some inspectors were jailed on false charges, bullets were fired at their rental cars, and crosses were burned on the lawns of at least two of them. The records do not name who fired or who burned the crosses. By July 1, hospitals holding 97 of every 100 hospital beds in the country had met the rule.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hepatitis experiments at Willowbrook, 1956 to 1971" -->
Willowbrook State School on Staten Island, New York, was a state home for children with intellectual disabilities. It was built for 4,000 children and held 6,000 by 1965.

From 1956 to 1971, Dr. Saul Krugman of New York University and Dr. Robert McCollum of Yale studied hepatitis there. Hepatitis is a disease that inflames the liver. In one study they fed 60 healthy children live hepatitis virus taken from other people's stool. The children's skin and eyes turned yellow, their livers swelled, and they vomited and would not eat. Every one of them became ill, some badly.

Parents signed permission letters, often because signing got their child a place in the crowded school. Critics said the letters played down that the children would be infected on purpose. At times the only beds open were in the study ward.

In 1972 the television reporter Geraldo Rivera filmed the school's crowded wards and filthy bathrooms, and staff who beat and sexually abused the residents. That March, parents of 5,000 residents sued the State of New York. The last children left Willowbrook in 1987.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuskegee study ends, 1972 to 1997" -->
The Tuskegee study ended in November 1972, four months after a reporter made it public. In 1966 Peter Buxtun, a Public Health Service worker in San Francisco, had written to his bosses that the study was wrong. Officials of the CDC, who by then ran the study, answered that it should go on until every man had died and his body had been cut open and examined. At a review in 1969 they decided again to keep it going.

Buxtun gave the story to Jean Heller, a reporter for the Associated Press. It ran in the *Washington Star* on July 25, 1972, and on the front page of the *New York Times* the next day. At the start of that year, 74 of the untreated men were still alive. In October a government panel advised stopping the study. In November 1972 Dr. Merlin DuVal, the federal Assistant Secretary for Health, ordered it ended.

In 1973 Senator Edward Kennedy held hearings, and federal officials began paying for the survivors' medical care. Their wives, widows and children were added in 1975. In 1974 federal officials settled the men's lawsuit for $10 million. That year members of Congress passed the National Research Act. Since then, a study on people that uses federal money must be approved by a review board. Every person in it must first be told what will be done and what the risks are, and must agree. This is called informed consent.

On May 16, 1997, President Bill Clinton apologized at the White House to five of the survivors, Charlie Pollard, Herman Shaw, Carter Howard, Fred Simmons and Frederick Moss. "What the United States government did was shameful, and I am sorry," he said.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sterilized without consent, the 1970s" -->
To sterilize a person is to operate so that the person can never have a baby. In an operation called a tubal ligation, a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb. In a hysterectomy, a surgeon cuts out the womb.

In the Relf sisters' case, described below, a federal court found in 1974 that 100,000 to 150,000 poor people a year were being sterilized in programs paid for by the federal government. Some doctors told women on welfare, government money for poor families, that they would lose it unless they agreed. The judge barred both practices.

In 1976 investigators of the General Accounting Office, which checks how federal money is spent, reported on the Indian Health Service. In four of its 12 regions, its doctors had sterilized 3,406 Native women from 1973 to 1976. The National Library of Medicine describes these operations as done without the women's permission. Thirty-six of the women were under 21, although a court had ordered that women that young not be sterilized. The investigators' report does not name the doctors. In 1974 Dr. Connie Pinkerton-Uri, who is Choctaw and Cherokee, had found in her own study that 1 in 4 Native women had been sterilized without consent.

Norma Jean Serena, who was Shawnee and Creek, gave birth in Armstrong County, Pennsylvania, on August 16, 1970. Two days later Drs. Donald Carter and James Houston cut out her fallopian tubes, the tubes that carry eggs to the womb. She said no doctor ever told her. A welfare caseworker, Jean Burgess, had advised the operation. Burgess later said, "I felt that Mrs. Serena should have no further children." Burgess and two other county child welfare workers, Patricia Walker and Natalie Leezer, had also taken three of Serena's children from her in 1970. Serena sued in 1974. The jurors found that the welfare workers had lied about the children's health and about how long the children would stay in foster homes, and they awarded her $17,000. The same jurors found that she had agreed to the operation.

In Puerto Rico, about 1 in 3 women of child-bearing age had been sterilized by 1968. Some chose it because it was the only birth control offered to them. Others were told it could be undone, or were pushed into it by their employers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="relf-sisters" name="Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf

> **Who:** Two Black sisters in Alabama who were surgically sterilized as children by a federally paid clinic, after their mother was told they would get birth control shots.
> **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, June 1973.

In June 1973 surgeons in Montgomery, Alabama, sterilized Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf. The girls were 12 and 14. Accounts differ on which sister was older. The surgeons tied or cut the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so the girls could never have children. The published accounts do not name the surgeons.

Workers from a family planning clinic run by Montgomery Community Action, and paid for with federal money, had told their mother, Minnie Relf, that her daughters would get birth control shots. She could not read or write, and she signed the form with an X. Both girls had intellectual disabilities. The family lived in public housing that the same agency had found for them in 1971. The same clinic sterilized 11 children in all, and 10 of them were Black.

Their father, Lonnie Relf, saw the scars when the girls came home from the hospital. The same day a nurse asked their sister Katie, who was 17, to agree to be sterilized. Katie locked herself in her room.

Lawyers from the Southern Poverty Law Center sued for the family. The federal officials they sued included Caspar Weinberger, the Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, and Alvin Arnett of the Office of Economic Opportunity. The judge barred federal money for sterilizing people against their will. The case led to the rule that doctors must get informed consent before sterilizing anyone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="relf-sisters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="AIDS, 1981 to 2000" -->
On June 5, 1981, CDC doctors reported a rare kind of pneumonia in five young gay men in Los Angeles. Two of them had died. The disease was later named AIDS. It is caused by HIV, a virus that destroys the white blood cells that fight infection. As those cells die, other infections and cancers kill the person. HIV passes through blood, semen and other body fluids, not through touch.

In 1985 more Americans were diagnosed with AIDS than in all earlier years together. Most people died about 15 months after they were diagnosed. People with hemophilia, whose blood does not clot, took a clotting medicine made from blood given by many donors. Of those treated with it between 1979 and 1984, nearly 9 in 10 caught HIV, hepatitis C or both.

In March 1987 the writer Larry Kramer started ACT UP in New York to push for faster treatment. That month the FDA approved AZT, the first drug for AIDS. On October 11, 1988, more than 1,000 ACT UP members shut down FDA offices in Maryland, and police made 176 arrests. Eight days later FDA officials announced rules to approve drugs faster.

In 1994 AIDS was the leading cause of death for Americans aged 25 to 44. From 1996, doctors gave several drugs together, and the next year AIDS deaths fell by 47 percent. By the end of 2000, 774,467 Americans had been diagnosed with AIDS, and 448,060 of them had died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ryan-white" name="Ryan White" movie="The Ryan White Story (1989, ABC TV film)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ryan White

> **Who:** An Indiana teenager who caught HIV from his hemophilia medicine, was kept out of school, and then spoke across the country about AIDS.
> **When and where:** Kokomo and Cicero, Indiana, 1984 to 1990. Born in Kokomo on December 6, 1971.
> **Movie:** *The Ryan White Story* (1989), a film made for ABC television. Ryan White had a small part in it.

Ryan White was 13 when doctors told him, on December 17, 1984, that he had AIDS. They gave him six months to live. He had hemophilia and took a clotting medicine made from blood given by many people. Some of it carried HIV.

In 1985 James O. Smith, the head of his school district, refused to let him back into Western Middle School. 117 parents and 50 teachers signed a petition against him. Health officials of the state and the CDC said he was no danger to anyone. When he went back for one day in February 1986, 151 of the school's 360 students stayed home. After he returned, school staff made him use throwaway forks and spoons and a separate bathroom. Someone fired a bullet through his family's front window while no one was home.

The family moved to Cicero, Indiana. On his first day at Hamilton Heights High School, the principal, Tony Cook, and students who had learned about AIDS shook his hand. In 1988 he spoke to a presidential commission on AIDS. He died on April 8, 1990, at 18. That August, members of Congress passed the Ryan White CARE Act, which pays for care for people with HIV.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ryan-white" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="health" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
COVID-19 killed 1,245,791 people in the United States from 2020 to September 19, 2026, by the CDC's early count. Drug overdoses killed more than 100,000 Americans a year in 2022 and 2023. Life expectancy, the average age a newborn would reach if that year's death rates held, fell from 78.8 years in 2019 to 76.4 in 2021. It was back to 79.0 in 2024, federal health statisticians reported in January 2026. In 2010 President Barack Obama signed the Affordable Care Act. In 2025, 26.7 million people had no health insurance all year, the Census Bureau reported in September 2026. Doctors can now edit the genes in a patient's own cells to treat sickle cell disease.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading and editing genes, 2003 to 2023" -->
A genome is the full set of DNA instructions in a person's cells. Scientists of the Human Genome Project, begun in 1990, finished the first reading of the human genome in April 2003.

Sickle cell disease bends red blood cells into a curved shape like a sickle, a farm tool with a curved blade. The bent cells block small blood vessels and carry less oxygen. That causes attacks of severe pain, and it can disable or kill a person. About 100,000 people in the United States have it, the Food and Drug Administration reported in 2023. It is most common in African Americans.

On December 8, 2023, FDA officials approved Casgevy, the first treatment made with the gene-editing tool called CRISPR. Doctors take some of the patient's blood-making cells and edit their DNA. Then the patient gets high doses of chemotherapy, strong drugs that kill the cells left in the bone marrow. Doctors put the edited cells back in a single drip into a vein.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Affordable Care Act and the uninsured, 2010 to 2026" -->
In 2010, 49.9 million people in the United States had no health insurance, which was 16.3 percent of everyone, the Census Bureau counted.

On March 23, 2010, President Obama signed the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act. Members of the House had passed it 219 to 212. Most of its parts started in January 2014. Many states opened Medicaid to more adults with low incomes, and people could buy insurance on new marketplaces run by the government. By 2016, about 20 to 24 million more people had insurance, and the share without it had fallen by about half.

In 2025, 26.7 million people, or 7.9 percent, had no insurance for the whole year. The Census Bureau reported this on September 15, 2026. The bureau changed its survey questions in 2013, so the 2010 and 2025 counts are close but not exact matches.

On July 4, 2025, President Donald Trump signed a budget law that cuts federal spending on Medicaid. From 2027 many adults on Medicaid must show 80 hours a month of work or similar activity, or a reason they are excused, to keep it. In July 2025 the Congressional Budget Office, which estimates the cost of laws for Congress, projected that 10 million more people would have no insurance in 2034 because of the law.

Extra federal help with the price of marketplace insurance ended after 2025. In 2026, sign-ups fell by more than a million, to 23.1 million, and the average monthly payment rose from $113 to $178, the health research group KFF reported. Since 2005, 197 rural hospitals have closed or stopped keeping patients overnight, by the December 2025 count of the Sheps Center at the University of North Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="COVID-19, 2020 to 2026" -->
COVID-19 is an illness caused by a coronavirus. It spreads when an infected person breathes out droplets and tiny particles that carry the virus. It causes fever, cough, shortness of breath and loss of taste or smell. In severe cases the person has trouble breathing and pain or pressure in the chest, becomes confused, and the lips or skin turn pale, gray or blue.

The CDC confirmed the first case in the United States on January 20, 2020. On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared a pandemic, a disease spreading around the world. FDA officials allowed the first vaccine on December 11, 2020. Recorded deaths in the United States passed 500,000 on February 21, 2021, and 1 million on May 12, 2022. The federal health emergency ended in May 2023.

The CDC's early count, as of September 24, 2026, is 1,245,791 COVID-19 deaths from 2020 to September 19, 2026. The worst year was 2021, with 463,267 deaths. In 2025 there were 20,682. These early counts rise as late death records come in.

Life expectancy fell furthest for Native Americans. For American Indian and Alaska Native people it fell from 71.8 years in 2019 to 65.2 in 2021, federal health statisticians reported in 2022. COVID-19, accidents and liver disease caused most of that drop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sandra-lindsay" name="Sandra Lindsay" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sandra Lindsay

> **Who:** A nurse who led the intensive care nurses at a hospital in Queens through the pandemic, and was the first American outside a vaccine trial to get a COVID-19 vaccine.
> **When and where:** Long Island Jewish Medical Center, Queens, New York, 2020 and 2021. She came from Jamaica.

On Monday, December 14, 2020, Sandra Lindsay got the first COVID-19 vaccine given in the United States outside a trial. She directed the nurses who cared for the sickest COVID-19 patients at Long Island Jewish Medical Center in Queens, New York.

She had questions about the vaccine at first. She read the studies and listened to experts before she decided. "We needed a more powerful tool," she said.

During the same months her aunt died, and she could not be with her mother. Her grandson Avery was born early in March 2020 and spent about four and a half months in a hospital unit for newborns. Lindsay could not sleep or focus, and she began to meditate.

On July 2, 2021, President Joe Biden gave her the Outstanding Citizen By Choice award, which honors immigrants who became citizens. Her hospital scrubs and her ID badge went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sandra-lindsay" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opioids and overdose deaths, 1999 to 2026" -->
Opioids are strong painkillers such as oxycodone, heroin and fentanyl. Too much of one slows a person's breathing until it stops. Fentanyl is 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine. A drug called naloxone reverses an overdose if someone gives it quickly.

The CDC describes three waves of deaths. The first came from pain pills, after doctors wrote many more opioid prescriptions in the 1990s. The second came from heroin, from 2010. The third came from fentanyl made illegally, from 2013. From 1999 to 2023, about 806,000 Americans died of opioid overdoses, the CDC counted in 2026.

In 2007 Purdue Pharma, the maker of the pain pill OxyContin, pleaded guilty to misleading the public about how easily people became addicted to it. It paid $600 million. Three of its leaders, Michael Friedman, Howard Udell and Paul Goldenheim, pleaded guilty too. Purdue pleaded guilty to federal crimes again in 2020. On June 16, 2025, attorneys general from 55 states and territories joined a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sackler family, who owned it.

From March 29, 2023, people could buy naloxone nasal spray in stores without a doctor's order. Overdose deaths from all drugs peaked at about 112,000 in the 12 months ending August 2023. The CDC's early count for the 12 months ending April 2026 is about 67,000. About 42,000 of those deaths involved opioids.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smoking, measles and vaccines, 2006 to 2026" -->
On August 17, 2006, US District Judge Gladys Kessler ruled that the big tobacco companies had "defrauded the American people by lying for decades about the health risks of smoking and their marketing to children." Smoking and breathing other people's smoke still kill more than 480,000 Americans a year, by the CDC's count last updated in 2024.

Measles is a disease caused by a virus that spreads through the air. By 2000 the United States had stopped its steady spread inside the country. In 2025 there were 2,289 confirmed cases and 3 deaths, by the CDC's count, most among people who had not been vaccinated. In 2026, by September 24, the CDC had counted 3,659 cases and 1 death.

On June 9, 2025, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Secretary of Health and Human Services, removed all 17 members of the panel of experts that advises the CDC on vaccines. He wrote that the panel had become "little more than a rubber stamp for any vaccine." Leaders of the American Medical Association said the move "upends a transparent process that has saved countless lives."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last of the Tuskegee men" -->
The last man from the Tuskegee study died in January 2004. The last widow in the benefit program died in January 2009. Some of the men's children still receive medical care through the program, according to the CDC.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
