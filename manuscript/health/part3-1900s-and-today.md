## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 20 minutes**

The first American patient saved by penicillin, a drug made from a mold that kills bacteria, was Anne Miller. She got her first dose in 1942. In the same years, government doctors kept that same drug from Black men in Alabama who had syphilis. Army doctors proved in 1900 that a mosquito spreads yellow fever.

An influenza epidemic, a flu that spread to huge numbers of people at once, killed about 675,000 people in the United States in 1918. A medicine mixed with a poison killed more than 100 people in 1937, and in 1938 a new law required drug makers to prove a drug safe before selling it.

Doctors of the United States Public Health Service began their study of 399 Black men in Alabama who had syphilis in 1932. For 40 years the doctors kept the men from being treated, while telling them they were being treated. Public Health Service doctors also exposed 1,308 people in Guatemala to diseases passed during sex, on purpose, from 1946 to 1948.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Proving the mosquito, 1900 and 1901" -->
Four army doctors near Havana, Cuba, proved in 1900 that mosquitoes pass yellow fever, a disease caused by a virus, from sick people to healthy ones. Major Walter Reed led the four, and the others were James Carroll, Jesse Lazear and Aristides Agramonte.

Lazear raised mosquitoes and let them feed on yellow fever patients. A mosquito that had fed on a patient at least 12 days before passed the disease on when it bit someone else. Carroll and a soldier who volunteered caught yellow fever from these bites in late August. Lazear was probably bitten by an infected mosquito himself, and he died of yellow fever on September 25, 1900.

**Two buildings.** The doctors tested two buildings in November 1900. In one, volunteers slept in bedding soiled by yellow fever patients, and they stayed well. In the other, a clean building, infected mosquitoes bit volunteers, and several caught yellow fever. All of them recovered. The volunteers signed written contracts that named the danger, and they were paid. After workers killed the mosquitoes in Havana, yellow fever there nearly stopped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clara-maass" name="Clara Maass" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Clara Maass

> **Who:** A New Jersey nurse who died after letting infected mosquitoes bite her in a yellow fever experiment.
> **When and where:** Havana, Cuba, 1901. Born in East Orange, New Jersey, in 1876.

**She let infected mosquitoes bite her twice.**

Clara Maass died of yellow fever at 25, after she let infected mosquitoes bite her in Havana in 1901. She came from a poor German immigrant family in East Orange, New Jersey. She started training as a nurse at 17, at the Newark German Hospital, and she was its head nurse by 21. She nursed soldiers in the 1898 war with Spain and then in the Philippines.

In Havana volunteers were paid $100, and $200 if they caught the fever. Maass let infected mosquitoes bite her in March 1901. She had a mild case and got well. In August 1901 she volunteered again, caught yellow fever, and died on August 24.

Soon after her death, the yellow fever commission was ordered to break up. The museum account of her life does not name who gave the order.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clara-maass" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mary Mallon, held on an island" -->
Mary Mallon was a cook in New York who carried the germs of typhoid, a fever spread through food and water, in her body without being sick herself. Health officials counted 47 people infected through her cooking, and 3 of them died.

Officials of the New York City Board of Health held her on North Brother Island, in the East River, from 1907. In a letter in June 1909, she wrote that they kept her "a prisoner without being sick nor needing medical treatment." She also wrote, "I have been in fact a peep show for everybody."

The officials let her go in February 1910, after she signed a promise not to work as a cook again. In October 1914 she took a job as a cook at the Sloane Maternity Hospital, under the name Mary Brown. Early in 1915 typhoid broke out there, and 25 people caught it and 2 died. Health officials found her in March 1915 and sent her back to the island. She stayed there until she died on November 11, 1938, and she became known as "Typhoid Mary."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fewer medical schools, 1910" -->
Abraham Flexner published a survey of the country's 148 medical schools in 1910. Leaders of the Carnegie Foundation had hired him to do it. He called several schools "beyond repair." In the years after, the number of medical schools fell to 66.

Writers at JSTOR Daily, a history magazine, say that members of the American Medical Association, most of them white men, used the report to close schools and protect their own jobs and earnings. Seven medical schools had trained Black doctors. Only two were left: Howard University in Washington, D.C., and Meharry Medical College in Nashville, Tennessee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Polio in New York, 1916" -->
Polio is a disease caused by a virus that can attack the nerves in the spine. It can leave a person unable to move the arms, the legs or the muscles used for breathing, a loss of movement called paralysis.

In the summer of 1916 polio spread through New York City. More than 9 of every 10 of the city's cases were in children five or younger. One count gives more than 27,000 cases and more than 6,000 deaths across the whole country. Accounts of New York City alone differ, from about 2,000 deaths to about 6,000.

Dr. Haven Emerson was the city's health commissioner. Every day the names and addresses of the sick appeared in the newspapers. Signs went up on their houses, and the accounts of the epidemic do not say who put them up.

**Shut in.** A team of Health Department workers led by Dr. Simon Blatteis kept the families shut inside. Under Health Department rules, a family that could not give the sick child a separate room, a separate toilet and a nurse had to let the department take the child to a hospital, free of charge. Police stood at the railroads, roads and rivers to turn back anyone trying to leave the city.

Writers in the *New York Times* blamed Italian immigrants for the disease. Italian immigrants were shunned, cursed and beaten up, and the accounts of the epidemic do not name who beat them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1918 influenza" -->
Influenza, or flu, is a disease caused by a virus that infects the nose, throat and lungs. In 1918 a new kind of influenza spread around the world. About 500 million people caught it, and at least 50 million died. About 675,000 of the dead were in the United States. Many of them were healthy adults between 20 and 40.

There was no vaccine against it, no medicine that trains the body to fight off a germ before the germ can make a person sick. There was no antibiotic either, a medicine that kills bacteria. Many people died of pneumonia, with their lungs full of fluid.

**The parade.** In Philadelphia, Dr. Wilmer Krusen, the city's director of public health, let a parade to sell war bonds go ahead on September 28, 1918. People bought the bonds to lend money to the government for the war. About 200,000 people crowded onto Broad Street. Krusen said in public that the flu was not a threat, although army camps nearby were full of it.

Within three days every bed in the city's 31 hospitals was full. City officials closed schools, churches and theaters on October 3. More than 12,000 people in Philadelphia died within weeks.

The flu reached Brevig Mission, an Alaska Native village, in 1918. Within five days, 72 of the 80 villagers were dead. Miners from the town of Nome thawed the frozen ground with steam and dug one grave for all of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josie-mabel-brown" name="Josie Mabel Brown" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josie Mabel Brown

> **Who:** A Navy nurse from Missouri who worked the influenza wards at Great Lakes, Illinois, and described them in an interview when she was about 100.
> **When and where:** Great Lakes Naval Training Station, Illinois, fall 1918 to 1919. Born on a Missouri farm in 1886.

**The ambulance brought four sick men and took away four dead.**

Josie Mabel Brown nursed sailors dying of influenza at the Great Lakes Naval Training Station in Illinois in 1918. At the worst of the epidemic, 6,000 of the 173,000 men there were in the hospitals. She had finished nurse training in 1917 and been called into the Navy, and in the fall of 1918 she was ordered to Great Lakes.

On her first ward, one man lay dying on the bed and another lay on the floor. A third waited on a stretcher for the bed. The nurses wrapped each dead man in a sheet and tied a shipping tag to one big toe. The ambulance, she said, "would bring us four live ones and take out four dead ones."

The nurses had no time to take temperatures. Patients had nosebleeds so strong that "the blood would just shoot across the room," she said. In some men, air leaked from torn lungs into the body, and she could see bubbles under the skin of their arms.

She worked 16 hours a day until just before Christmas 1918. In March 1919 she caught a fever of 104 or 105 herself, and she recovered. She told her story to her niece in 1986 and died at 104.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josie-mabel-brown" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**By 1920:** Army doctors had proved that mosquitoes carry yellow fever, and influenza had just killed about 675,000 Americans. Doctors still had no vaccine against the flu and no drug that killed bacteria.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium in the body, 1917 to 1932" -->
Radium is a metal that gives off radiation, a stream of energy that damages living cells. Radium belongs to the same family of elements as calcium, so growing bones take it up as if it were calcium. There it keeps giving off radiation from the inside.

Women painted watch dials with glowing radium paint at the U.S. Radium factory in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917. They shaped their brushes into a point with their lips. The radium destroyed their bone marrow, the soft tissue inside bones that makes blood, so their bodies could not make enough blood. Their jaw bones died and broke apart. Some grew cancers of the bone. In 1924 a dentist, Theodor Blum, examined the dial painter Grace Fryer. Her jaw bone was wasting away, and he called the damage "radium jaw."

Chapter 13, The Elements, tells the dial painters' story and their lawsuit in full. Five of them, Grace Fryer among them, signed an agreement with the company on June 8, 1928. Each woman received $10,000, though some accounts give $15,000, plus $600 a year for as long as she lived, and her medical costs. The company's officers never admitted that the company was to blame.

**A radium medicine.** William Bailey sold radium dissolved in water as a medicine called Radithor. He falsely claimed to have medical training and paid doctors to tell their patients to take it. Eben Byers, a Pittsburgh businessman, drank about 1,400 bottles from 1927 to 1930. A government investigator found in 1931 that the bone in Byers's body was falling apart and holes were forming in his skull. He died on March 31, 1932, of a cancer of the jaw bone caused by the radium.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native health, 1928" -->
A team of investigators led by Lewis Meriam reported in 1928 on the lives of Native people on reservations and in government boarding schools. They wrote that "the health of the Indians as compared with that of the general population is bad." They wrote that the share of Native people who died each year was high, and so was the share of babies who died.

The investigators found tuberculosis, the lung disease that spreads by coughing, "extremely prevalent," which means very common. They found trachoma, an eye disease that "produces blindness," to be "a major problem." In the government boarding schools the children's food was "deficient in quantity, quality, and variety": there was not enough of it, and it was poor and the same every day. "The medical service rendered the boarding school children is not up to a reasonable standard," they wrote, meaning the care doctors gave the children was not good enough.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuskegee study, 1932 to 1972" -->
Doctors of the United States Public Health Service watched hundreds of Black men in Alabama live with syphilis for 40 years, from 1932 to 1972, and they kept the men from being treated. They wanted to see what the disease did to a body when no one treated it.

**What syphilis does.** Syphilis is a disease caused by bacteria. It is passed during sex, or from a mother to her unborn baby. Left untreated, it goes through stages. At first the person gets a painless sore where the germ entered. Weeks later the person gets a rash, a fever and a sore throat.

Then the germ can hide in the body for years with no signs. After 10 to 30 years it can damage the heart and the large blood vessels, the brain and the nerves. It can cause blindness, and it can cause dementia, which is the loss of memory and reason. It can kill, and a baby born with syphilis can die.

**Who ran it.** Dr. Taliaferro Clark began the study in 1932. He was head of the Public Health Service's office for diseases passed by sex. The study took place in Macon County, Alabama, near the town of Tuskegee. Dr. Raymond Vonderlehr ran it there and later took Clark's place. Most of the men were poor farmers. Many were sharecroppers, who farmed someone else's land for a share of the crop.

The doctors chose 399 men who had syphilis and 201 men who did not, to compare them. They planned no treatment at all for the infected men. The study was planned to last six months, and it ran for 40 years.

**The lie.** The doctors told the men they had "bad blood" and that they were being treated for it. They did not tell the men that the disease was syphilis, and they never asked whether the men agreed to be studied. The men got free exams, hot meals on exam days and rides. From 1935 they were also promised about $50 toward their burials, if they let doctors cut open their bodies after death to study them.

Eunice Rivers, a Black nurse, kept in touch with the men for all 40 years. She gave them aspirin and a "spring tonic" that did nothing against syphilis. Dr. Oliver Wenger, who ran the clinic in the region, wrote, "we have no further interest in these patients until they die."

The exams also included spinal taps. In a spinal tap a doctor pushes a needle between the bones of the lower back and draws out fluid from around the spinal cord. The doctors wanted to see whether syphilis had reached the brain and nerves. A spinal tap often causes a lot of pain and other harm. The doctors' letter to the men called it their "Last Chance For Special Free Treatment."

**Keeping the cure away.** Keeping treatment away from the men was part of the study. Its doctors gave local doctors lists of the men's names and asked them not to treat them. In 1941 army officials drafted some of the men and told them to start treatment. Public Health Service officers gave the draft board, the group that chose men for the army, a list of 256 names to leave untreated, and the board members did as they asked.

Accounts differ on when penicillin, the drug made from a mold, became the usual cure for syphilis: 1943, by 1947, or the early 1950s. The study doctors never offered it to the men. Vonderlehr later wrote, "I hope that the availability of antibiotics has not interfered too much with this project."

**What their own reports show.** Reports that the study doctors printed show what the disease did to the men. In 1936 the doctors reported that the infected men had much more disease of the heart and blood vessels than the men without syphilis. Only 16 of every 100 infected men showed no sign of illness, against 61 of every 100 of the uninfected men.

About ten years later, nearly twice as many infected men as uninfected men had died. The doctors worked out that the disease had cut the men's lives by about a fifth.

Their 1955 report covered the infected men whose bodies they had cut open after death. More than 30 of every 100 had died because syphilis destroyed their heart and blood vessels or their brain and spinal cord.

A reporter made the study public in 1972. By then, by one count, at least 28 of the men had died of syphilis itself and 100 more of problems it caused. Forty of their wives had caught it. Nineteen of their children were born with it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-pollard" name="Charles Pollard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Pollard

> **Who:** An Alabama farmer who was kept in the Tuskegee study for 40 years without being told he had syphilis, and who then brought the lawsuit for the men.
> **When and where:** Near Tuskegee, Macon County, Alabama, 1932 to 1972.

**At 66, he learned from the news that he had syphilis.**

That was in July 1972, and Charles Pollard had had syphilis for 40 years. He farmed 66 acres of land he had inherited, about three miles from Tuskegee. His father was the son of two enslaved people.

Men from the study told Pollard in 1932 that he could get a free physical exam at a one-room school the next afternoon. "So I went on over and they told me I had bad blood," he said. "And that's what they've been telling me ever since." For 40 years doctors came around "from time to time and check me over."

Pollard went to Fred Gray, a lawyer in Tuskegee. Their lawsuit, *Pollard v. United States*, ended in a settlement of $10 million. In 1997 Pollard was one of the survivors at the White House when President Bill Clinton apologized to them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-pollard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A poison sold as medicine, and a new law, 1937 and 1938" -->
A poison in a new medicine killed more than 100 people in 1937, and the next year a new law required drug makers to prove a drug safe before selling it. The S. E. Massengill Company of Bristol, Tennessee, sold a liquid medicine called Elixir Sulfanilamide for strep throat. Its chemist, Harold Watkins, dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol, a chemical used in antifreeze and a poison. No law required the company to test the medicine for safety before selling it.

The medicine killed more than 100 people in 15 states in September and October 1937, many of them children. It shut down their kidneys. They stopped passing urine and had strong stomach pain, vomiting and seizures before they died.

A mother wrote to President Franklin Roosevelt about her daughter Joan: "we can see her little body tossing to and fro and hear that little voice screaming with pain." The owner, Dr. Samuel Massengill, said, "I do not feel that there was any responsibility on our part." Watkins killed himself.

President Roosevelt signed the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act on June 25, 1938, after members of Congress passed it. Under it, a company had to prove to officials of the Food and Drug Administration, the FDA, that a new drug was safe before it could sell the drug.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Penicillin, 1942" -->
Penicillin is a drug made from a mold. It kills many kinds of bacteria, the germs behind infections such as blood infections and syphilis. It was the first antibiotic made in large amounts.

Anne Miller of New Haven, Connecticut, had an infection of the blood in March 1942, after a miscarriage, the loss of a baby before it can live outside the womb. For weeks her fever stayed at 103 degrees or higher. Her doctors got 5.5 grams of penicillin, half of all the penicillin in the United States at the time. She got her first dose on March 14. By the next morning her temperature was back to normal. She was the first patient in the United States whose life was saved by penicillin.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Exposed on purpose in Guatemala, 1946 to 1948" -->
Doctors of the United States Public Health Service, led by Dr. John Cutler, gave people in Guatemala syphilis, gonorrhea and chancroid on purpose, or tried to, from 1946 to 1948. All three are diseases passed during sex. The doctors infected people without asking whether they agreed.

The doctors exposed 1,308 people to these diseases: prisoners, soldiers and patients in the national mental hospital. They ranged from 10 to 72 years old. The records show some kind of treatment for 678 of them.

**How they did it.** The doctors used women who were paid for sex to pass on the diseases. They infected most of these women first, then sent them to the prisoners. They also rubbed pus from sick men painfully onto people's genitals and into the opening where urine leaves the body. They injected germs into people's spinal fluid.

For tests, the doctors also took blood or spinal fluid from 5,128 people, among them children in orphanages and schools and people with leprosy, a disease caused by bacteria that damages the skin and nerves.

The money came from the Public Health Service, the National Institutes of Health and the Pan American Sanitary Bureau. Guatemalan officials supplied staff and buildings. Cutler's records note 83 deaths while the experiments went on. A presidential commission studied the experiments in 2011 and found it unclear how many of those deaths the experiments caused. The doctors never published their results. The public learned of the experiments in 2010.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal money for hospitals, 1946" -->
The Hospital Survey and Construction Act became law on August 13, 1946. People call it the Hill-Burton Act, after the two senators who put it forward, Lister Hill of Alabama and Harold Burton of Ohio. Over the years, federal lawmakers set aside $3.7 billion under it to build hospitals, and states and towns added $9.1 billion.

Under the law, the people who ran hospitals built with the money could keep Black and white patients apart, as long as the separate care was called equal. Accounts differ on who asked for that rule. Judges struck it down in 1963.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pellagra, silica dust and forced sterilization" -->
**A disease of diet.** Pellagra killed about 100,000 Americans between 1900 and 1940, out of about 3 million who had it, about 1 in 30. Pellagra is a disease caused by too little niacin, a vitamin, in the diet. Most of the sick were poor farmers, sharecroppers and cotton-mill workers in the South who lived on little but cornmeal, molasses and dried pork. Scientists identified niacin in 1937.

**Silica dust.** Almost 5,000 workers dug the Hawks Nest tunnel in West Virginia from 1930 to 1932, and Chapter 14, Work and Workers, tells its story in full. It was the Union Carbide company's tunnel, and the contractor was Rinehart and Dennis. Most of the workers were Black men who had come from other parts of the South. The rock was full of silica, the mineral in sand.

The men drilled through it with no masks and no way to control the dust. The West Virginia Encyclopedia's account of the tunnel does not name the company men who made that choice. Breathing silica's fine dust scars the lungs until a person cannot breathe, a disease called silicosis. Death counts run from 476 to close to 1,000.

**Forced sterilization in Puerto Rico.** Eugenics was the false idea that a country could improve its people by stopping some people from having children. In Puerto Rico, under a law that took effect in 1937, members of a government Eugenics Board ordered 97 women sterilized by force. Sterilized means that a person can never become pregnant. Doctors did it by cutting or tying the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, or by removing the womb.

Many thousands of other women were pressured into the same operation. Clarence Gamble, an heir to the Procter and Gamble soap fortune, set up birth control clinics in Puerto Rico with money from the Puerto Rico Reconstruction Administration, a federal office. Gamble made sterilization a rule in his clinics. A woman with three living children could be seen there only if she agreed to be sterilized, historians on the history podcast DIG report.

Some women were told the operation could be undone. Factory owners hired sterilized women first, and some women were told they would not get a job unless they were sterilized. Some employers opened clinics inside their factories where workers could be sterilized. Pregnant women were sometimes turned away from hospitals unless they agreed to be sterilized right after giving birth. The historians' accounts do not name the factories or the hospitals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 24 minutes**

A vaccine ended polio in the United States in these fifty years, and new federal insurance paid for the care of older and poorer Americans, while reporters, judges and investigators exposed what doctors had done to people who never agreed. Doctors reported more than 21,000 Americans paralyzed by polio in 1952. A vaccine tested on about 1.8 million children was announced in 1955, and the last polio case caught inside the United States came in 1979.

President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare, health insurance for people 65 and older, and Medicaid, for people with little money, in 1965. A reporter exposed the Tuskegee study in 1972, and a federal health official announced its end that November.

In the 1970s doctors in federally paid clinics sterilized poor Black women and girls, and doctors in the Indian Health Service sterilized Native women. Many of these women and girls never gave permission. Doctors reported the first cases of the disease later named AIDS in 1981. By the end of 2000, 448,060 Americans had died of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henrietta-lacks" name="Henrietta Lacks" movie="The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks (2017, HBO)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henrietta Lacks

> **Who:** A Black woman whose cancer cells doctors took without asking her. Her cells were the first human cells to keep growing in a laboratory.
> **When and where:** Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, 1951. Born Loretta Pleasant in Roanoke, Virginia, on August 1, 1920.
> **Movie:** *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2017), a drama made for HBO about her and her daughter Deborah.

**Doctors cut two samples from her cervix without asking her.**

Doctors at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore cut cells from Henrietta Lacks's body in 1951 without her permission or knowledge. Her cells were the first human cells to keep growing and dividing in a laboratory. At that time no rule required doctors to ask.

She grew up in Clover, Virginia, in a log cabin that had been slave quarters on a plantation. Her white great-grandfather and great-uncle had owned it. In January 1951 she went to Johns Hopkins with a lump in her womb. Doctors found cancer of the cervix, the opening of the womb. They treated her with radium placed inside her body, and then with X-rays. During her treatments, doctors cut the two samples.

Dr. George Gey grew the cells. They kept dividing, and they were named HeLa, from the first two letters of her first and last names. By 1954 Jonas Salk's team used HeLa cells in its polio vaccine work.

Another doctor, Chester Southam, injected HeLa cells into cancer patients, prisoners and healthy people to see whether cancer could be passed on. He did not tell the people he injected that the cells were cancer cells, and prisoners were hardly free to refuse.

The members of New York's Board of Regents, the state board that licenses doctors, later found Southam guilty of fraud, deceit and unprofessional conduct. Those are legal words for cheating, lying and breaking the rules of his profession. They put him on probation, a trial period under watch, for a year.

She died on October 4, 1951, at 31, after the cancer spread through her body. Her family did not learn about the cells until 1975. In 2021 her family sued a company, Thermo Fisher Scientific, for making money from HeLa cells without her permission, and the two sides settled in 2023.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henrietta-lacks" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The polio vaccine, 1952 to 1979" -->
Doctors in the United States reported more than 21,000 people paralyzed by polio in 1952. Fewer than 1 in 100 children who catch polio are paralyzed. When polio paralyzes the muscles used for breathing, the person needs a machine to breathe. The iron lung was a metal tube about seven feet long, and the patient lay inside it. Bellows, a pump with folding sides, changed the air pressure inside the tube to pull air into the lungs.

Jonas Salk of the University of Pittsburgh made a vaccine from polio virus that had been killed. The money for his work came from the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, a charity that later became the March of Dimes.

**The test.** Dr. Thomas Francis Jr. of the University of Michigan ran the test in 1954. About 1.8 million children in the United States, Canada and Finland took part, counting children who got no shot and were only watched. From April 26 to July 10, 1954, more than 443,000 children got at least one shot of vaccine. More than 210,000 got a placebo, a shot with no medicine in it. Neither the child nor the nurse knew which one it was.

The children were called Polio Pioneers, and their parents signed permission forms. The results were announced at the University of Michigan on April 12, 1955: the vaccine was 80 to 90 percent effective. It was approved for use the same year.

**The bad batch.** That April, Cutter Laboratories in Berkeley, California, made 120,000 doses that still held live virus. About 40,000 children who got it had a mild form of polio. Fifty-six were paralyzed, and five died.

The children passed the virus to others, and 113 more people were paralyzed and 5 more died. Cutter's managers took the vaccine off the market on April 27, 1955. At a hearing that June, members of Congress said the staff of the federal laboratory that was supposed to check the vaccine were mostly to blame, because they had not checked it closely.

Albert Sabin's vaccine, which people swallowed instead of getting a shot, was approved for use from 1961 to 1963. The last case of polio caught inside the United States was in 1979. Between 15 and 40 years after they got sick, 25 to 40 of every 100 people paralyzed by polio as children get new muscle pain and new weakness, which is called post-polio syndrome.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jonas-salk" name="Jonas Salk" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jonas Salk

> **Who:** The doctor who made the first polio vaccine and did not patent it.
> **When and where:** University of Pittsburgh, 1947 to 1955. Born in New York City in 1914.

**"Could you patent the sun?" he asked.**

Jonas Salk made the polio vaccine that was approved for use in 1955. He never took out a patent on it, the legal right to be the only one allowed to make and sell an invention.

He started work at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine in 1947. In 1952 he made a vaccine from polio virus that had been killed, so that it could not cause the disease.

Thomas Francis Jr. ran the 1954 test. Salk had first learned about viruses in Francis's laboratory in 1941. At first Salk did not want some children to get a dummy shot, because the dummy shot could not protect them against polio. He gave way, because only that kind of test could prove the vaccine worked.

The television reporter Edward R. Murrow asked Salk who owned the patent, and the question about the sun was Salk's answer. People at the charity that paid for the work and at the university had looked into a patent. They judged that the method was probably not new enough to patent. Salk died in La Jolla, California, in 1995.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jonas-salk" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="paul-alexander" name="Paul Alexander" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Paul Alexander

> **Who:** A Dallas boy paralyzed by polio at six who lived in an iron lung for more than 70 years and became a lawyer.
> **When and where:** Dallas, Texas, 1952 to 2024. Born in Dallas on January 30, 1946.

**He lived inside an iron lung for more than 70 years.**

Polio paralyzed Paul Alexander in 1952, during an outbreak in Dallas, when he was six. Within days he could not move below his neck or breathe on his own. Doctors saved him with a tracheotomy, an operation that cuts an opening in the front of the neck into the windpipe so that air can get in. Then they put him in an iron lung.

He learned to breathe outside the machine for hours at a time, starting in 1954, by swallowing air into his lungs. Every night he went back into it.

He held a stick in his mouth to turn pages, to paint, and later to type. He studied at Southern Methodist University and the University of Texas, earned a law degree in 1984, and worked as a lawyer in Dallas. In 2020 he published a book about his life, *Three Minutes for a Dog*.

"I've never thought of myself as a cripple," he said in 2017, using an old, unkind word for a person who cannot use part of the body. "I'm Paul Alexander, human being." Guinness World Records named him the longest-surviving iron lung patient. He died in Dallas on March 11, 2024, at 78.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paul-alexander" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Indian Health Service, 1954 to 1976" -->
President Dwight Eisenhower signed the Indian Health Transfer Act in 1954. Under it, Public Health Service officials took over health care for Native people from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the federal office that ran the government's dealings with Native nations. The new office was called the Indian Health Service.

That same year Dr. Thomas Parran reported to the Interior Department on the health of Alaska Natives. He wrote that they suffered "sickness, crippling conditions and premature deaths to a degree exceeded in very few parts of the world," meaning that people in very few places anywhere were sicker or died younger. Health care for Alaska Natives moved to the Indian Health Service in 1955, and its staff led the work against tuberculosis.

Under a 1959 law, Public Health Service workers could bring clean water and ways to get rid of sewage to reservations, and tribes took part in the decisions. In 1976 President Gerald Ford signed the Indian Health Care Improvement Act. Under it, Medicare and Medicaid could pay the Indian Health Service for the care its staff gave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**By 1955:** Doctors had a polio vaccine that worked, and Public Health Service officials ran health care for Native people through the new Indian Health Service.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New operations and new pictures of the body, 1954 to 1963" -->
Dr. Joseph Murray moved a healthy kidney from Ronald Herrick into his identical twin brother, Richard, at a Boston hospital on December 23, 1954. Richard was dying of kidney disease. The body's defenses attack an organ from almost anyone else, which is called rejection. Because the twins were identical, Richard's body accepted the kidney. He lived eight more years, married and had two children.

Scientists at Brookhaven National Laboratory in New York built the first machine to make technetium-99m, a radioactive chemical, in 1958. A doctor injects a tiny amount into a patient. It gives off rays that a camera outside the body picks up, to make pictures of the bones, the heart and other organs. Doctors in the United States first reported scans with it in 1963. Doctors around the world now use it in tens of millions of scans a year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fallout on Rongelap, 1954" -->
United States scientists set off a hydrogen bomb called Castle Bravo at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands on March 1, 1954. It was nearly three times as strong as they expected. Fallout, the radioactive dust and ash that falls after a nuclear blast, came down on Rongelap, an island of the Marshallese people about 110 miles away. It looked like snow, and children played in it.

Twenty-three Japanese fishermen on the boat *Lucky Dragon* got sick, and one died. More than 230 people were moved off Rongelap, Rongerik and Utirik. The Rongelap and Utirik people were moved about 72 hours after the blast. The accounts of the evacuation do not name who moved them or who decided when.

Radioactive iodine from the fallout collected in their thyroid glands, which sit in the neck. In the years after, doctors found more thyroid lumps and thyroid cancers among them. Staff of the Atomic Energy Commission, the federal office in charge of nuclear weapons and nuclear power, studied the exposed people. Doctors working for the United States government later gave them follow-up medical care.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radiation tested on people, 1944 to 1974" -->
President Bill Clinton appointed a committee in 1994 to investigate radiation experiments paid for by the federal government. He acted after reports that doctors had injected plutonium, a radioactive metal, into hospital patients who did not know it. In its 1995 report the committee counted nearly 4,000 such experiments from 1944 to 1974. Researchers paid by the federal government had also run experiments on children and on prisoners.

The committee wrote that in the 1940s and 1950s it was common for doctors to use patients in research "without their awareness or consent." In some studies on children, the radioactive doses raised the children's lifetime risk of thyroid cancer. The committee also found studies in which patients died soon after receiving radiation doses as large as those given to treat disease.

Miners who dug uranium underground breathed radon, a radioactive gas that comes off uranium ore. At least several hundred of them died of lung cancer. The committee found that federal officials neither required the mines to be ventilated, which means fresh air pumped in, nor properly warned the miners. Under a 1990 law, the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act, some of the miners get payments from the government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thalidomide, 1960 to 1962" -->
Dr. Frances Kelsey, a new FDA reviewer, refused to approve thalidomide because the company's evidence was weak. The drug company Richardson-Merrell had asked the FDA for permission to sell it in 1960. It was a sleeping pill that doctors also gave pregnant women for morning sickness, and it was already sold in more than 20 other countries. For more than a year, under steady pressure from the company, Kelsey kept asking for more.

Doctors in Europe linked thalidomide in November 1961 to babies born with arms and legs that were very short or missing. Around the world more than 10,000 babies were born harmed by it, and about 40 of every 100 died at birth or soon after.

Richardson-Merrell staff had already given more than 2.5 million tablets to more than 1,000 American doctors to "test." Nearly 20,000 patients took them, and several hundred of those patients were pregnant. At least 17 American babies were born with thalidomide damage.

Company officials withdrew the application in March 1962. Members of Congress passed the Kefauver-Harris Amendments with no votes against in October 1962. Drug makers then had to prove that a drug worked, report its harms to the FDA, and get people's permission before testing drugs on them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smoking and asbestos, 1954 to 1998" -->
Surgeon General Luther Terry, the country's top public health doctor, released a report on smoking on January 11, 1964. Ten experts had written it after reading more than 7,000 articles. They found that smokers died at a rate 70 percent higher than non-smokers. The average smoker was 9 to 10 times as likely to get lung cancer, and a heavy smoker at least 20 times.

Smoking was the main cause of chronic bronchitis, a long-lasting swelling of the air passages, the experts found, and it was linked to emphysema and to heart disease.

Ten years earlier, in January 1954, the big tobacco companies had paid for an advertisement in more than 400 newspapers that disputed the studies linking cigarettes to lung cancer. In 1957 Surgeon General Leroy Burney said the evidence showed that smoking caused lung cancer.

Lung cancer grows in the cells that line the air passages of the lungs. It causes a cough that does not go away, trouble breathing, and coughing up blood. Smoking causes about 9 of every 10 lung cancers in men and 8 of every 10 in women. In emphysema, a lung disease, the thin walls between the lungs' tiny air sacs break down, so the person cannot get enough air.

Under a 1965 law, every pack of cigarettes had to carry a health warning. From 1970 cigarette ads were banned from television and radio. On April 14, 1994, the heads of seven tobacco companies told Congress that nicotine is not addictive. In a 1998 settlement, a deal to end lawsuits, the tobacco companies agreed to pay 46 states at least $206 billion over 25 years.

**Fibers in the lungs.** Asbestos is a group of minerals that split into thin fibers. Workers used it to insulate buildings and ships. Its fibers, breathed in, stay in the lungs and scar them. They cause lung cancer and mesothelioma, a cancer of the thin lining of the chest and belly. The sickness can start 10 to 40 years or more after the person breathed the dust.

Shipyard workers, insulation workers, miners and firefighters were among those who breathed it. Johns-Manville, the biggest maker of asbestos, went bankrupt in 1982 while facing thousands of lawsuits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**By 1965:** Surgeon General Luther Terry's report had found smokers 9 to 10 times as likely to get lung cancer, and members of Congress had made drug makers prove that their drugs worked. The Tuskegee study was still running.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Medicare and Medicaid, 1965 and 1966" -->
Two thirds of Americans over 65 had incomes below $1,000 a year in 1950, and only 1 of every 8 had health insurance, a plan that pays doctor and hospital bills.

President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare and Medicaid into law at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, on July 30, 1965. Former President Harry Truman was with him. Truman had asked Congress for national health insurance 20 years earlier. Medicare is health insurance for people 65 and older. Medicaid is health insurance for people with little money. Nearly 20 million people signed up for Medicare in its first three years.

**Separate wards.** Many hospitals still kept Black and white patients apart. In the South, the people who ran hospitals mostly put Black patients in separate basement wards or separate buildings, or turned them away. In many northern cities, hospital care was just as segregated through unwritten rules.

Judges of a federal appeals court ruled in 1963, in the case *Simkins v. Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital*, that hospitals built with Hill-Burton money could not keep patients apart by race. The justices of the Supreme Court refused to take up the case in 1964.

**The inspectors.** The Civil Rights Act of 1964 barred federal money for any place that treated people differently by race, and Medicare payments to hospitals were set to begin on July 1, 1966. Before then, almost 1,000 federal workers volunteered to inspect about 6,000 hospitals. They checked beds, waiting rooms, lunch rooms and hiring.

Inspectors drove at high speed to get away from local groups of the Ku Klux Klan, a secret white group that used terror against Black people and their supporters. Some inspectors were jailed on false charges. Bullets were fired at their rental cars, and crosses were burned on the lawns of at least two of them. Several hospital workers who helped the inspectors had their homes bombed, and one may have been murdered, the historian David Barton Smith wrote. Smith's account does not name who jailed the inspectors, who fired, who burned the crosses or who set the bombs.

By July 1, hospitals holding 97 of every 100 beds for short-term care in the country had met the rule.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hepatitis experiments at Willowbrook, 1956 to 1971" -->
Willowbrook State School on Staten Island, New York, was a state home for children with intellectual disabilities. It was built for 4,000 children and held 6,000 by 1965.

Dr. Saul Krugman of New York University and Dr. Robert McCollum of Yale ran experiments with hepatitis, a disease that inflames the liver, on children at Willowbrook from 1956 to 1971. In one study they fed 60 healthy children live hepatitis virus taken from other people's stool, their solid waste. The children's skin and eyes turned yellow, their livers swelled, and they vomited and would not eat. Every one of them became ill, some badly.

Parents signed permission letters, often because signing got their child a place in the crowded school. Critics said the letters played down that the children would be infected on purpose. At times the only beds open were in the study ward.

The television reporter Geraldo Rivera filmed the school's crowded wards and filthy bathrooms in 1972, and staff who beat and sexually abused the residents. That March, parents of 5,000 residents sued the State of New York. The last children left Willowbrook in 1987.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuskegee study ends, 1972 to 1997" -->
The Tuskegee study ended in November 1972, four months after a reporter made it public. Peter Buxtun, a Public Health Service worker in San Francisco, had written in 1966 to the head of the service's office for diseases passed by sex, saying the study was wrong.

Officials of the CDC, the federal disease agency, ran the study by then. They decided to keep it going until every man had died. They also decided that doctors should then cut open each man's body and examine it. Members of local chapters of two doctors' groups, the National Medical Association and the American Medical Association, backed that decision. At a review in 1969 CDC officials decided again to keep it going.

**The story breaks.** In the early 1970s Buxtun went to the press. Jean Heller, a reporter for the Associated Press, wrote the story. It ran in the *Washington Star* on July 25, 1972, and on the front page of the *New York Times* the next day.

At the start of that year, 74 of the untreated men were still alive. In October a government panel advised stopping the study. In November 1972 Dr. Merlin DuVal, the top health official in the federal health department, announced the end of the study.

Senator Edward Kennedy held hearings in 1973, and federal officials began paying for the survivors' medical care. Their wives, widows and children were added in 1975. The men's lawsuit ended in a $10 million settlement out of court in 1974. Some accounts date it 1973.

**The new rule.** Members of Congress passed the National Research Act in 1974. Since then, a study on people that uses federal money must be approved by a review board. Every person in it must first be told what will be done and what the risks are, and must agree, which is called informed consent.

President Bill Clinton apologized at the White House on May 16, 1997, to five of the survivors, Charles "Charlie" Pollard, Herman Shaw, Carter Howard, Fred Simmons and Frederick Moss. "What the United States government did was shameful, and I am sorry," he said.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sterilized without consent, the 1970s" -->
To sterilize a person is to operate so that the person can never have a baby. In an operation called a tubal ligation, a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb. In a hysterectomy, a surgeon cuts out the womb.

A federal judge ruled in 1974 in the case of Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf, two Black sisters in Alabama who had been sterilized as children. The judge found that doctors in programs paid for by the federal government were sterilizing an estimated 100,000 to 150,000 poor people a year. Some doctors told women on welfare, government money for poor families, that they would lose it unless they agreed. The judge barred federal money for sterilizing people against their will, and barred the threats to cut off welfare.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="relf-sisters" name="Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf

> **Who:** Two Black sisters in Alabama who were surgically sterilized as children through a federally paid clinic, after their mother was told they would get birth control shots.
> **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, June 1973.

**Their mother was told the girls would get birth control shots.**

Surgeons in Montgomery, Alabama, sterilized Minnie Lee and Mary Alice Relf in June 1973. The girls were 12 and 14, and accounts differ on which sister was older. The surgeons tied or cut the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so the girls could never have children. No record names the surgeons.

Nurses from a family planning clinic had told their mother, Minnie Relf, about the shots. Montgomery Community Action ran the clinic with federal money. She could not read or write, and she signed the form with an X. Both girls had intellectual disabilities. The family lived in public housing that the same agency had found for them in 1971. In all, 11 children from the same clinic's program were sterilized, and 10 of them were Black.

Their father, Lonnie Relf, saw the scars when the girls came home from the hospital. The same day a nurse asked their sister Katie, who was 17, to agree to be sterilized. Katie locked herself in her room.

Lawyers from the Southern Poverty Law Center sued for the family in a case called *Relf v. Weinberger*. The federal officials they sued included Caspar Weinberger, the Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, and Alvin Arnett of the Office of Economic Opportunity.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="relf-sisters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native and Puerto Rican women" -->
**The Indian Health Service.** Investigators of the General Accounting Office, which checks how federal money is spent, reported on the Indian Health Service in 1976. In four of its 12 regions, its doctors had sterilized 3,406 Native women from 1973 to 1976. Writers for the National Library of Medicine and the National Archives describe the operations as done without the women's permission. Thirty-six of the women were under 21, although a federal judge had ordered that women that young not be sterilized.

The investigators' report does not name the doctors. Two years before it, in 1974, Dr. Connie Pinkerton-Uri, who is Choctaw and Cherokee, had found in her own study that 1 in 4 Native women had been sterilized without consent.

**One woman's case.** Norma Jean Serena, who was Shawnee and Creek, gave birth in Armstrong County, Pennsylvania, on August 16, 1970. Two days later Drs. Donald Carter and James Houston cut out her fallopian tubes, the tubes that carry eggs to the womb. She said no doctor ever told her. A welfare caseworker, Jean Burgess, had advised the operation. Burgess later said, "This was a burden on society. I felt that Mrs. Serena should have no further children."

Burgess and two other county child welfare workers, Patricia Walker and Natalie Leezer, had also taken three of Serena's children from her in 1970. Serena sued in 1974. The jurors found that the welfare workers had given a false picture of the children's health and of how long the children would stay in foster homes. They awarded her $17,000 for the taking of her children.

On the operation, the same jurors found against her. They decided that she had agreed to it, though she said no doctor ever told her about it.

In Puerto Rico, about 1 in 3 women of child-bearing age had been sterilized by 1968, and 37.4 of every 100 by 1976, historians on the history podcast DIG report. Some chose it because it was the only birth control offered to them. Others were told it could be undone, or were pushed into it by their employers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="AIDS, 1981 to 2000" -->
CDC doctors reported a rare kind of pneumonia in five young gay men in Los Angeles on June 5, 1981. Two of them had died. The disease was later named AIDS. It is caused by HIV, a virus that destroys the white blood cells that fight infection. As those cells die, other infections and cancers kill the person. HIV passes through blood, semen and other body fluids, not through touch.

More Americans were diagnosed with AIDS in 1985 than in all earlier years together. By early 1986 about half of the adults ever diagnosed had died, and patients died about 15 months after they were diagnosed. People with hemophilia, whose blood does not clot, took a clotting medicine made from blood given by many donors. Of those treated with it between 1979 and 1984, nearly 9 in 10 caught HIV, hepatitis C or both.

**ACT UP.** The writer Larry Kramer started ACT UP, the AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power, in New York in March 1987. That month FDA officials approved AZT, the first drug for AIDS. On October 11, 1988, more than 1,000 ACT UP members shut down FDA offices in Maryland, and police made 176 arrests. Eight days later FDA officials announced rules to approve drugs faster.

AIDS was the leading cause of death for Americans aged 25 to 44 in 1994. From 1996, doctors gave several drugs together, and the next year AIDS deaths fell by 47 percent. By the end of 2000, 774,467 Americans had been reported with AIDS, and 448,060 of them had died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ryan-white" name="Ryan White" movie="The Ryan White Story (1989, ABC TV film)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ryan White

> **Who:** An Indiana teenager who caught HIV from his hemophilia medicine, was kept out of school, and then spoke across the country about AIDS.
> **When and where:** Kokomo and Cicero, Indiana, 1984 to 1990. Born in Kokomo on December 6, 1971.
> **Movie:** *The Ryan White Story* (1989), a film made for ABC television. Ryan White had a small part in it.

**When he came back to school, 151 of 360 students stayed home.**

Ryan White was 13 when doctors told him, on December 17, 1984, that he had AIDS. They gave him six months to live. He had hemophilia and took a clotting medicine made from blood given by many people. Some of it carried HIV.

James O. Smith, the head of his school district, refused in 1985 to let him back into Western Middle School. In all, 117 parents and 50 teachers signed a petition, a written request, to keep him out. Health officials of the state and the CDC said he was no danger to anyone. When he went back for one day in February 1986, 151 of the school's 360 students stayed home.

After he returned, school staff made him use throwaway forks and spoons and a separate bathroom. Someone fired a bullet through his family's living room window while no one was home.

The family moved to Cicero, Indiana. On his first day at Hamilton Heights High School, the principal, Tony Cook, and students who had learned about AIDS shook his hand. In 1988 he spoke to a presidential commission on AIDS. He died on April 8, 1990, at 18. That August, members of Congress passed the Ryan White CARE Act. Under it, federal money pays for care for people with HIV.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ryan-white" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="health" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 9 minutes**

COVID-19 and drug overdoses cut American lives short in these years, even as doctors learned to edit the genes in a patient's own cells to treat sickle cell disease. COVID-19 killed 1,245,791 people in the United States from 2020 to September 19, 2026, by the early count of the CDC, the federal disease agency. Drug overdoses killed more than 100,000 Americans a year in 2022 and 2023.

Life expectancy, the average age a newborn would reach if that year's death rates held, fell from 78.8 years in 2019 to 76.4 in 2021. It was back to 79.0 in 2024, federal health officials who count births and deaths reported in January 2026. President Barack Obama signed the Affordable Care Act in 2010. In 2025, 26.7 million people had no health insurance all year, the Census Bureau reported in September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading and editing genes, 2003 to 2023" -->
A genome is the full set of DNA instructions in a person's cells. Scientists of the Human Genome Project, begun in 1990, finished the first reading of the human genome in April 2003.

Sickle cell disease bends red blood cells into a curved shape like a sickle, a farm tool with a curved blade. The bent cells slow the flow of blood in small blood vessels and carry less oxygen. That causes attacks of severe pain, and it can disable or kill a person. About 100,000 people in the United States have it, the Food and Drug Administration reported in 2023. It is most common in African Americans.

FDA officials approved Casgevy on December 8, 2023. It was the first treatment made with the gene-editing tool called CRISPR that the FDA has approved. Doctors take some of the patient's blood-making cells and edit their DNA. Then the patient gets high doses of chemotherapy, strong drugs that kill the cells left in the bone marrow. Doctors put the edited cells back in a single drip into a vein.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Affordable Care Act and the uninsured, 2010 to 2026" -->
The Census Bureau counted 49.9 million people in the United States with no health insurance in 2010. That was 16.3 percent of everyone, about 1 person in 6.

President Obama signed the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act on March 23, 2010. Members of the House had passed it 219 to 212. Most of its parts started in January 2014. Lawmakers in many states opened Medicaid to more adults with low incomes, and people could buy insurance on new insurance marketplaces, websites where people compare plans and sign up. By 2016, about 20 to 24 million more people had insurance, and the share without it had fallen by about half.

The Census Bureau reported on September 15, 2026, that 26.7 million people, or 7.9 percent, had no insurance for the whole of 2025. That is about 1 person in 13. Census Bureau staff changed their survey questions in 2013, so the 2010 and 2025 counts were not measured in exactly the same way.

**New Medicaid rules.** President Donald Trump signed a budget law with new rules for keeping Medicaid on July 4, 2025. From 2027, adults who got Medicaid when their state widened it under the Affordable Care Act must show 80 hours a month of work or similar activity to keep it. They can instead show a reason they are excused. In July 2025 staff of the Congressional Budget Office, which estimates the cost of laws for Congress, estimated that 10 million more people would have no insurance in 2034 because of the law.

Extra federal help with the price of marketplace insurance ended after 2025. In 2026, sign-ups fell by more than a million, to 23.1 million, the health research group KFF reported. The average monthly payment rose from $113 to $178.

Rural hospitals have closed too. Since 2005, 197 rural hospitals have closed or stopped keeping patients overnight, by the December 2025 count of the Sheps Center at the University of North Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="COVID-19, 2020 to 2026" -->
COVID-19 is an illness caused by a coronavirus. It spreads when an infected person breathes out droplets and tiny particles that carry the virus. It causes fever, cough, shortness of breath and loss of taste or smell. In severe cases the person has trouble breathing and pain or pressure in the chest, becomes confused, and the lips or skin turn pale, gray or blue.

The first case in the United States was confirmed in a laboratory on January 20, 2020. On March 11, 2020, officials of the World Health Organization declared a pandemic, a disease spreading around the world. FDA officials allowed the first vaccine on December 11, 2020. Recorded deaths in the United States passed 500,000 on February 21, 2021, and 1 million on May 12, 2022. The federal health emergency ended in May 2023.

The CDC's early count, as of September 24, 2026, is 1,245,791 COVID-19 deaths from 2020 to September 19, 2026. The year with the most deaths was 2021, with 463,267. In 2025 there were 20,682. These early counts rise as late death records come in.

For American Indian and Alaska Native people, life expectancy fell from 71.8 years in 2019 to 65.2 in 2021. That was a drop of 6.6 years, federal health officials who count births and deaths reported in 2022, and it brought them to the same life expectancy that all Americans had in 1944. COVID-19, accidents and liver disease caused most of that drop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sandra-lindsay" name="Sandra Lindsay" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sandra Lindsay

> **Who:** A nurse who led the intensive care nurses at a hospital in Queens through the pandemic, and was the first American outside a vaccine trial to get a COVID-19 vaccine.
> **When and where:** Long Island Jewish Medical Center, Queens, New York, 2020 and 2021. She came from Jamaica.

**She got the first COVID-19 vaccine given outside a trial.**

Sandra Lindsay got that shot on Monday, December 14, 2020, the first given in the United States outside a trial. She directed the nurses who cared for the sickest COVID-19 patients at Long Island Jewish Medical Center in Queens, New York.

She had questions about the vaccine at first. She did her own research and listened to experts before she decided. "We needed a more powerful tool," she said.

During the pandemic her aunt died, and she could not be with her mother. Her grandson Avery was born too soon, in March 2020, and spent about four and a half months in a hospital unit for newborns. Lindsay could not sleep or focus, and she began to meditate.

President Joe Biden gave her the Outstanding Citizen By Choice award, which honors immigrants who became citizens, on July 2, 2021. Her hospital scrubs and her ID badge went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sandra-lindsay" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opioids and overdose deaths, 1999 to 2026" -->
Opioids include strong painkillers such as oxycodone and fentanyl, and the illegal drug heroin. Too much of one slows a person's breathing, and the person can die. Fentanyl is 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine. A drug called naloxone reverses an overdose if someone gives it quickly.

The CDC describes three waves of deaths. The first came from pain pills, after doctors in the 1990s began writing more prescriptions, or written orders, for opioids. The second came from heroin, from 2010. The third came from fentanyl made illegally, from 2013. From 1999 to 2023, about 806,000 Americans died of opioid overdoses, the CDC counted in 2026.

**Guilty pleas.** Purdue Pharma, the maker of the pain pill OxyContin, pleaded guilty in 2007 to misleading the public about how easily people became addicted to it. It paid $600 million. Three of its leaders, Michael Friedman, Howard Udell and Paul Goldenheim, pleaded guilty too. Purdue pleaded guilty to federal crimes again in 2020.

Fifty-five attorneys general, the top lawyers of state and territory governments, joined a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sackler family, who owned it, on June 16, 2025. In the settlement, Purdue and the Sacklers agreed to pay money to end lawsuits against them.

FDA officials approved a naloxone nasal spray for sale in stores without a doctor's order on March 29, 2023. Overdose deaths from all drugs peaked at about 112,000 in the 12 months ending August 2023. The CDC's early count for the 12 months ending April 2026 is about 67,000. About 42,000 of those deaths involved opioids.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smoking, measles and vaccines, 2006 to 2026" -->
US District Judge Gladys Kessler ruled on August 17, 2006, that the big tobacco companies had cheated the public. In her words, they had "defrauded the American people by lying for decades about the health risks of smoking and their marketing to children." Smoking and breathing other people's smoke still kill more than 480,000 Americans a year, by the CDC's count last updated in 2024. That is nearly 1 of every 5 deaths in the country.

Measles is a disease caused by a virus. It spreads through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes, and a person can catch it in a room up to 2 hours after the sick person has left, CDC doctors write. It starts with a high fever, a cough and red, watery eyes, and then a rash spreads down from the face.

As many as 1 of every 20 children with measles gets pneumonia, the most common way measles kills young children. About 1 child in 1,000 gets a swelling of the brain.

Measles had stopped spreading nonstop inside the United States for at least a year by 2000, which health officials call elimination. In 2025 there were 2,289 confirmed cases and 3 deaths, by the CDC's count. In an early count of 2,144 of those cases, 93 of every 100 were in people who had not been vaccinated. In 2026, by September 24, the CDC had counted 3,659 cases and 1 death.

**The vaccine panel.** Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Secretary of Health and Human Services, removed all 17 members of the panel of experts that advises the CDC on vaccines on June 9, 2025. He wrote that the panel had become "little more than a rubber stamp for any vaccine," meaning a panel that approved every vaccine it was shown. Leaders of the American Medical Association said the move "upends a transparent process that has saved countless lives," meaning it threw out an open way of deciding about vaccines.

**The three questions, answered**
- Why did new diseases kill so many Native people after 1492? The crowd diseases, such as smallpox and measles, had never reached the Americas, so nobody here was immune and a whole town could fall sick at once. Smallpox killed about half of the Cherokee in 1738 and 1739.
- How did Americans learn to stop smallpox, yellow fever and polio? Boston doctors tried inoculation in 1721, using a method Onesimus described, and safer vaccination followed in 1800. Army doctors proved in 1900 that mosquitoes spread yellow fever, and after the Salk and Sabin vaccines the last polio case caught inside the country came in 1979.
- Who was used in medical experiments without agreeing, and what changed because of it? Enslaved women operated on by J. Marion Sims, the Black men of the Tuskegee study, prisoners and patients in Guatemala, and children at Willowbrook. After the Tuskegee study became public, members of Congress passed the National Research Act in 1974, and researchers in a federally paid study on people must now get each person's informed consent.

**What changed:** At the start of this chapter nobody knew what caused disease, and by its end doctors could prevent polio and measles with vaccines and edit the genes in a patient's own cells.

**Five dates to keep**
- 1721: Boston doctors inoculate against smallpox.
- 1900: Army doctors near Havana prove that mosquitoes spread yellow fever.
- 1932: Public Health Service doctors begin the Tuskegee study.
- 1955: The polio vaccine is shown to work and approved for use.
- 2020: COVID-19 reaches the United States.

**Not known:** Nobody knows how many people lived north of Mexico in 1492, so nobody knows how many died of the new diseases.
