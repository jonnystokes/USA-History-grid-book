## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A safer protection against smallpox, called vaccination, reached the United States in 1800. In 1837 smallpox killed about nine in ten Mandan people on the upper Missouri River, after a federal official had left them out of a vaccination program. Cholera, a disease spread in dirty water, killed thousands of people in the growing cities in 1832 and 1849. In 1846 a Boston dentist showed surgeons that a gas called ether could keep a patient from feeling an operation. From 1845 to 1849 an Alabama doctor, J. Marion Sims, practiced a new operation on enslaved women and gave them nothing to stop the pain. In 1849 Elizabeth Blackwell became the first woman in the United States to earn a medical degree.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vaccination arrives, 1800 and 1802" -->
To vaccinate a person, a doctor scratched matter from cowpox into the skin. Cowpox is a mild disease of cows. It protected the person against smallpox. The older method, inoculation, gave the person real smallpox, and some inoculated people died of it.

On July 8, 1800, Dr. Benjamin Waterhouse of Cambridge, Massachusetts, vaccinated his young son Daniel Oliver and a servant, Samuel Carter. He used cowpox matter on threads sent from England. It is the first recorded vaccination in the United States. Doctors in other places claimed earlier ones, but Waterhouse kept official records. He then vaccinated three more of his children and a second servant, Kesiah Flag.

Waterhouse sent vaccine to other doctors and to President Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson vaccinated his family, the enslaved people at his plantation, Monticello, and his neighbors. No record says whether the enslaved people at Monticello agreed.

On August 16, 1802, Waterhouse and six other doctors vaccinated nineteen boys in Boston. That fall the doctors exposed the boys to smallpox on purpose. The members of Boston's Board of Health then reported that cowpox was "a complete preventive against all the effects of the Small-pox."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox on the upper Missouri, 1832 and 1837" -->
On May 5, 1832, President Andrew Jackson signed a law, passed by Congress, that set aside $12,000 to vaccinate Native people. Secretary of War Lewis Cass designed the program and ran it through federal Indian agents. By February 1833, agents and doctors had vaccinated more than 17,000 people. Cass left the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara nations on the upper Missouri River out of the program.

On June 18, 1837, the American Fur Company's steamboat *St. Peters* stopped at Fort Clark, beside the Mandan villages in what is now North Dakota. A deckhand had shown signs of smallpox weeks earlier, and three Arikara women who had caught it were on board. The fort's trader, Francis Chardon, learned from the captain that people on the boat had smallpox. The crew unloaded goods and loaded furs in less than a day, and the boat went on upriver. The historian R. G. Robertson blames the captain, Bernard Pratte Jr., who would not keep the sick apart from other people. Robertson calls that "criminal negligence."

In July Chardon wrote that the Mandan "die so fast that it is impossible" to keep count. About 2,000 Mandan lived in the villages in the spring. By October only 23 to 138 were alive, and the counts differ. The Arikara lost about two of every three people. Along the Missouri River more than 17,000 people died. About two thirds of the Blackfoot died, about half of the Assiniboine, about one third of the Crow and about one quarter of the Pawnee.

The Mandan leader Four Bears caught smallpox. Before he died he spoke about the white traders, as Chardon recorded it: "I have loved the Whites ... and how have they repaid it! With ingratitude!"
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cholera in the river cities, 1832 and 1849" -->
Cholera is an infection of the gut. People catch it from food or water that carries its germ. It causes diarrhea that can start two hours after a person swallows the germ. The body loses so much water and salt that the person can die. Cholera first reached the United States in 1832, and it killed about 3,500 of New York City's roughly 250,000 people that year.

In 1849 cholera came up the Mississippi River from New Orleans on steamboats. St. Louis had grown from about 5,000 people in 1830 to 77,000 by 1850, and it had no sewers. About three of every four people drank from shallow wells, often dug near outhouses. Pigs ate the garbage in the streets.

Doctors did not know what caused cholera. They blamed night air, bad food and alcohol. They told people to stop eating vegetables, and then meat. They lit fires at night to clean the air, and some bled their patients.

In June 1849 more than 60 people a day died in St. Louis, and in early July more than 100 a day died. On June 27 the city's lawmakers gave a Committee of Public Health $50,000. The committee members stopped steamboats at a quarantine station on an island in the river, where a health officer checked the passengers. The city engineer drained standing water, and city officials banned hogs from the city. By late July, deaths had dropped to about 10 a day.

Reports from 1849 give 4,557 cholera deaths in St. Louis that year. Many deaths were never reported, so the real number was higher. About 7 of every 10 of the dead were Irish and German immigrants. In 1854 two researchers identified the germ that causes cholera and showed that it spreads in dirty water.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ether, 1842 and 1846" -->
Anesthesia means using a drug so that a patient does not feel pain during an operation. Before it, surgeons worked as fast as they could, because a long operation could send a patient into shock.

On March 30, 1842, Dr. Crawford Long of Jefferson, Georgia, cut a tumor from the neck of James Venable while Venable breathed ether. A tumor is a lump of tissue that grows where it should not. Long had noticed that people who breathed ether for fun at parties felt little pain when they got hurt. He did not publish what he had done until 1849.

On October 16, 1846, at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, the dentist William T. G. Morton gave ether to Edward Gilbert Abbott, a young house painter. Abbott breathed the vapor through an inhaler that Morton had made. Dr. John Collins Warren cut a tumor from Abbott's neck. Abbott said afterward that he had not been fully asleep but "had felt no sensation of pain."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="J. Marion Sims's operations on enslaved women, 1845 to 1849" -->
From 1845 to 1849, Dr. J. Marion Sims of Montgomery, Alabama, operated again and again on enslaved Black women and girls to work out a new surgery. He gave them nothing to stop the pain.

The women had an injury called a vesicovaginal fistula. It is a hole torn through the wall between the bladder, where urine is held, and the vagina, the passage a baby is born through. A long, blocked childbirth crushes the tissue and makes the hole. Afterward urine runs out of the woman's body all the time. Her skin becomes sore and infected, and she smells of urine. Some women also had a hole into the bowel, so stool leaked too.

White slaveholders sent the women to Sims. The owners paid for their clothes and taxes, and Sims fed and housed them in a small hospital behind his office. He named three of the women, Anarcha, Betsey and Lucy. One count gives twelve women and girls in all. Another doctor, Nathan Bozeman, later named four more: Ann McRee, 16, Lavinia Boudurant, 13, Delia, 23, and Julia McDuffie, 20. Sims wrote that he promised to operate only with "the full consent of the patients." An enslaved woman had no power to refuse.

Sims gave the women nothing to stop the pain during any of the operations. Ether was shown in Boston in 1846, partway through these years, and Sims still did not use it. He wrote that the operation was "not painful enough to justify the trouble." Many white doctors of the time wrongly believed that Black people felt less pain. After the operations Sims often gave the women opium, a drug that dulls pain and brings sleep.

For each operation the woman was placed naked on her knees and elbows so that Sims could see inside her. Other doctors came to watch. Lucy, who was about 18, was operated on for about an hour while about twelve doctors watched. Sims left a sponge inside her bladder to draw off urine. She nearly died of an infection that spread through her blood. Sims wrote: "Lucy's agony was extreme."

In 1849 Sims closed the hole in Anarcha on about the thirtieth operation. He sewed it shut with silver wire, which caused fewer infections than silk thread. A tube drained urine from her bladder while it healed. He published the method in 1852. Sims also operated on enslaved babies sick with neonatal tetanus, the historian Stephen Kenny found. Neonatal tetanus is an infection in newborn babies that locks the jaw and other muscles in spasms.

In April 2018 New York City officials voted to take down Sims's statue in Central Park.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anarcha" name="Anarcha" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anarcha

> **Who:** An enslaved young woman on whom J. Marion Sims operated about thirty times, with nothing to stop the pain, to work out his fistula surgery.
> **When and where:** Montgomery, Alabama, 1845 to 1849.

Sims operated on Anarcha about 30 times over about four years before his surgery worked. He gave her no drug to stop the pain, and she knelt on her knees and elbows each time.

Her childbirth had been long and blocked. It tore holes from her vagina into her bladder and into her rectum, the end of the bowel. Urine and stool leaked from her body all the time. Sources give her age as 17 when she gave birth. One estimate says she was 13 when Sims first treated her.

Her owner sent her to Sims. She lived in his small hospital in Montgomery for about four years. In 1849 the operation closed the hole into her bladder. The records do not show what happened to Anarcha, Betsey and Lucy after Sims finished with them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anarcha" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elizabeth-blackwell" name="Elizabeth Blackwell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Elizabeth Blackwell

> **Who:** The first woman to earn a medical degree in the United States.
> **When and where:** Geneva, New York, 1847 to 1849, then New York City. Born in Bristol, England, in 1821. Died in Hastings, England, in 1910.

On January 23, 1849, Elizabeth Blackwell graduated at the head of her class from Geneva Medical College in New York. She was the first woman in the United States to earn a medical degree.

About 29 medical schools had turned her down. In 1847 the men studying at Geneva Medical College voted to let her in. Two accounts explain the vote. In one, the students thought the question was a joke by their teachers. In the other, they thought it would be funny to have a woman in the class.

Later in 1849 she worked at a hospital for mothers and babies in Paris. She caught an eye infection from a baby she was treating and lost the sight of that eye. She had to give up her plan to become a surgeon.

In 1857 she opened the New York Infirmary for Indigent Women and Children with her sister, Dr. Emily Blackwell, and Dr. Marie Zakrzewska. Indigent means poor. In 1868 she added a medical college for women beside it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-blackwell" -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Disease killed more soldiers than bullets did in the Civil War. From 1862 to 1868 smallpox killed more than 60,000 people who had just been freed from slavery. Officers of New York's new health board ordered filth cleared from the streets, but yellow fever still killed about 20,000 people along the Mississippi River in 1878. Most American doctors did not yet believe that germs cause infection. In 1881 President James Garfield's doctors probed his bullet wound with unwashed fingers, and he died of infection. By 1900 women were training as nurses and doctors. Among them were the first Black woman to earn a nursing license and the first Native American woman to earn a medical degree.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Disease in the Civil War armies, 1861 to 1865" -->
Between about 620,000 and 850,000 soldiers died in the Civil War, and the count is disputed. For every three soldiers killed in battle, about five died of disease.

The diseases that killed them included diarrhea and dysentery, which are infections of the gut. Others were typhoid fever, an infection spread by food and water that has touched human waste, and pneumonia, an infection of the lungs. Measles, malaria and scurvy killed soldiers too.

Soldiers dug latrine trenches, the ditches they used as toilets, too shallow, and they did not cover the waste with dirt. One army surgeon found a camp's latrine uphill of "the stream, from which all water in the camp is obtained."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clara-barton-health" name="Clara Barton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clara Barton

> **Who:** A clerk at the US Patent Office who took supplies to Civil War battlefields and later founded the American Red Cross.
> **When and where:** Antietam, Maryland, September 17, 1862. Born in North Oxford, Massachusetts, on December 25, 1821.

Clara Barton brought a wagon of bandages and supplies to the battlefield at Antietam, Maryland, on September 17, 1862. She had spent a year collecting them. She held wounded men's heads, gave them water and cooked for them at a farmhouse. She brought lanterns so that surgeons could keep operating after dark.

As she bent to give a soldier a drink, a bullet passed through her sleeve and killed him. She wrote that it cut "through his chest from shoulder to shoulder." A surgeon there, Dr. James Dunn, called her "the angel of the battlefield."

Before the war Barton had been a teacher and then a clerk. On May 21, 1881, she and others formed the American Red Cross in Washington, D.C. She was its president until 1904.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clara-barton-health" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hannah-ropes" name="Hannah Ropes" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hannah Ropes

> **Who:** The head nurse of a Civil War hospital in Washington, who reported a thief on the staff to the Secretary of War and died of typhoid caught at work.
> **When and where:** Union Hotel Hospital, Georgetown, Washington, D.C., 1862 and 1863. Born in New Gloucester, Maine, in 1809.

Hannah Ropes ran the nurses at a Civil War hospital in Washington until typhoid killed her in January 1863. Her son Edward was a Union soldier. In 1862, at 53, she volunteered as a nurse. She became matron, the woman in charge of the nurses, at the Union Hotel Hospital, an old hotel turned into a hospital for 300 to 400 patients.

The writer Louisa May Alcott nursed under her from December 1862. Alcott wrote in her diary: "A more perfect pestilence-box than this house I never saw." A pestilence is a deadly disease that spreads.

Ropes found that the hospital steward, the man in charge of supplies, was stealing supplies meant for the wounded. The chief surgeon ignored her complaints. She took them to the Secretary of War, Edwin Stanton, and the steward and the surgeon were both arrested.

She caught typhoid in late December 1862 and died on January 20, 1863. Alcott fell sick in the same outbreak and survived. Ropes's diary and letters were printed as a book in 1980.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hannah-ropes" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox among freed people, 1862 to 1868" -->
From 1862 to 1868, smallpox killed more than 60,000 people who had just been freed from slavery, the historian Jim Downs counts. Women and children died at higher rates than men.

During the war, people who escaped slavery came to Union army camps called contraband camps. The camps were crowded and dirty, and many people in them had too little food, clothing and shelter. Smallpox broke out in Washington, D.C., in 1862 and spread to nearby Alexandria, Virginia.

Doctors already knew how to stop smallpox. They could vaccinate people and keep the sick apart from the healthy. Union army officers and Freedmen's Bureau officials ran the camps. They put sick freed people in tents made of old, ripped army tarps, with blankets full of insects. White soldiers were treated in hospitals. The accounts of the epidemic do not name the men in charge.

Harriet Jacobs, who had escaped slavery herself, came to Alexandria to open a school and worked against the epidemic.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Health boards and yellow fever, 1866 to 1879" -->
In February 1866, New York City leaders set up a Metropolitan Board of Health. By April its officers had given 7,000 orders to clear away piled horse manure, dead animals and heaps of garbage. Crews spread chloride and lime, chemicals that kill germs, where cholera struck. Cholera killed 1,137 New Yorkers in 1866. That was a smaller share of the city than in 1832 or 1849.

In 1878 yellow fever spread up the Mississippi River from New Orleans. It began after the ship *Emily B. Souder* arrived in New Orleans in late May, and crew members fell ill and died. The first official yellow fever death in the city that year was a four-year-old girl, in July. Along the river about 120,000 people caught the fever and about 20,000 died.

In Memphis, Tennessee, about 25,000 of the city's 47,000 people fled. More than 5,000 people died in the city. Annie Cook ran a brothel, a house where men paid women for sex. She turned it into a hospital, nursed the sick, and died of the fever.

In 1879 members of Congress set up a National Board of Health. Nobody yet knew that a mosquito spreads yellow fever.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germs and the death of President Garfield, 1881" -->
Germs are living things too small to see, such as bacteria, and many of them cause disease. The English surgeon Joseph Lister urged doctors to kill germs on their hands and tools, to stop wounds from getting infected. Most American doctors thought this was too much trouble.

On July 2, 1881, Charles Guiteau shot President James Garfield at a railroad station in Washington, D.C. Doctors pushed their unwashed fingers and unwashed metal tools into the wound to search for the bullet. Dr. Willard Bliss, who took charge, probed the wound several times a day and never cleaned his hands first. One doctor, whose name is not recorded, punctured Garfield's liver while probing.

Garfield died on September 19, 1881. Doctors who examined his body found pneumonia in both lungs and his body full of pus. Historians now think Garfield would probably have lived if his doctors had kept the wound clean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women become nurses and doctors" -->
The New England Hospital for Women and Children in Boston had only women doctors. It also trained nurses in a course that took 16 months. Of 42 students who began in 1878, four finished in 1879. One of them was Mary Eliza Mahoney, who had worked at the hospital for 15 years before. She became the first Black woman in the United States to earn a professional nursing license. In 1908 she helped found the National Association of Colored Graduate Nurses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="susan-la-flesche-picotte" name="Susan La Flesche Picotte" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Susan La Flesche Picotte

> **Who:** An Omaha woman who became the first Native American woman to earn a medical degree, and went home to be the doctor for her own nation.
> **When and where:** The Omaha Reservation, Nebraska. Born 1865, graduated 1889, died 1915.

In 1889 Susan La Flesche became the first Native American woman in the United States to earn a medical degree. She was born in 1865 on the Omaha Reservation in northeastern Nebraska. Her father was Chief Joseph La Flesche, and her mother was Mary, also called One Woman.

As a child she watched a sick Native woman die because the local white doctor would not treat her. She studied at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. She finished its three-year course in two years and graduated first in her class.

Back on the reservation she cared for more than 1,300 people spread over 450 square miles. In 1913 she opened a hospital at Walthill, Nebraska, on the reservation. She died in 1915.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="susan-la-flesche-picotte" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Typhoid in the army camps, 1898" -->
During the war with Spain in 1898, soldiers in army camps in the United States caught typhoid. Army doctors counted 20,738 cases and 1,590 deaths. Typhoid caused 87 of every 100 deaths from disease in the army. Major Walter Reed and two other army doctors, Victor Vaughan and Edward Shakespeare, studied the camps. They found that the disease spread mostly from man to man, and some of it by flies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
