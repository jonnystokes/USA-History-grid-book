## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 15 minutes**

White audiences paid to watch white actors play Black people as fools, while Black actors who played Shakespeare were attacked and arrested. The first known Black theatre company in the United States opened in New York in 1821, and police arrested its actors in the middle of a play. In 1824 a young actor from the company, Ira Aldridge, left for England. In the United States he faced discrimination, which means he was treated worse because he was Black.

**Blackface.** A white actor named Thomas Dartmouth Rice blacked his face with burnt cork in 1828 and played a Black man as a fool. White audiences paid to laugh at the act, and by 1843 four white men in the same makeup were performing together as a group.

Actors also played Shakespeare in city theatres and on boats on the rivers. In 1849 the fans of two rival actors fought outside a New York theatre. City officials gave soldiers the order to fire into the crowd, and the soldiers killed at least 22 people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The African Theatre, New York, 1821 to 1823" -->
The first known Black theatre company in the United States opened in New York in 1821. Its founder, William Brown, was a free Black man from the West Indies who had worked as a steward, a servant on a ship. He ran a tea garden behind his house at 38 Thomas Street, an outdoor place for refreshments and shows, with music on Sunday afternoons.

Brown opened a theatre in 1821 with about 300 seats on the second floor of a building at Mercer and Bleecker Streets. Its actors were Black. It opened with Shakespeare's *Richard III*. Accounts give the first night as September 17, 21 or 24, 1821. A ticket cost 25 cents, or 50 cents for a better seat.

**A curtain for a robe.** On the first night, an enslaved man played the king in a robe made from a window curtain. After that, an actor named James Hewlett took over the part. The company also played Shakespeare's *Othello* and other plays such as *The Poor Soldier*. White New Yorkers came too, and some of them made trouble.

A group of people that included a man named George Bellmont broke into the theatre. They attacked the actors and wrecked the costumes and scenery. Police arrested Bellmont and several of the others.

**The arrests.** Brown then moved his company next door to the Park Theatre, run by a white manager named Stephen Price. Brown's company played *Richard III* on the same nights as Price's company. White members of the audience shouted at Brown's actors and broke up the show. According to the Shakespeare Theatre Company, which has published Hewlett's history, Price started a riot because he hoped to destroy Brown's company.

During one performance, police arrested the actors and put them in jail. The police let them go only after they promised to stop acting Shakespeare. The surviving records do not give the date or name the officers.

**The end.** Mordecai Noah, the editor of a newspaper called the *National Advocate*, mocked the company in print. He warned that free Black New Yorkers might one day "outvote the whites." In October 1822 Noah's newspaper included a notice that the theatre was closing because of an outbreak of a disease called yellow fever. The last known playbill of the company, a printed poster for a show, is from June 1823.

In London, the English comedian Charles Mathews mocked the company in his show *A Trip to America*, although he had never been to its theatre. In May 1824 James Hewlett answered him in a letter in the *National Advocate*: "Was this well for a brother actor?"
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-alexander-brown" name="William Alexander Brown" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Alexander Brown

> **Who:** A Black ship's steward from the West Indies who started the first known Black theatre company in the United States, and wrote the first known play by a Black writer to be acted there.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1816 to 1823.

**His own play told of a 1796 war on St. Vincent.**

William Brown wrote *The Drama of King Shotaway*, the first known play by a Black writer to be staged in the United States. Accounts give his full name two ways, as William Alexander Brown and as William Henry Brown.

Brown bought a house on Thomas Street in New York in 1816. On Sunday afternoons he offered music and other shows in the tea garden behind it. Neighbors and police complained about the tea garden again and again until it closed. In September 1821 he opened his theatre with *Richard III*, and he moved his company several times after the attacks and arrests.

Brown staged *King Shotaway* in 1823. It told the story of the war that the Black Caribs of the Caribbean island of St. Vincent fought against English and French settlers in 1796. The Black Caribs, now called the Garifuna, were a people whose ancestors were both free Africans and Native people of the Caribbean. No copy of the play survives. The last known playbill of Brown's company dates from June 1823. What happened to Brown after that is not known.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-alexander-brown" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ira-aldridge" name="Ira Aldridge" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ira Aldridge

> **Who:** A Black actor from New York who left the United States in 1824 and spent more than forty years acting Shakespeare in Britain and Europe.
> **When and where:** Born in New York about July 24, 1807. Went to England in 1824. Died on tour in Łódź, Poland, on August 7, 1867.

**A London critic said his skin was not dark enough for Othello.**

Ira Aldridge became a successful Shakespearean actor in Britain and across Europe. He is not known to have acted on a stage in the United States after he left. He went to the African Free School in New York, and as a teenager he acted with William Brown's company. Because he was Black, he faced discrimination in the United States, and in 1824 he sailed to England.

In October 1825 he played Oroonoko in a play called *The Revolt of Surinam, or A Slave's Revenge*, at a London theatre. A writer in *The Times* of London made fun of the shape of his lips. The same writer said his skin was not dark enough for him to play Othello.

Aldridge played Othello anyway, and King Lear and Macbeth. In 1828 he became the manager of the theatre in Coventry, England. In 1833 he took over the part of Othello at the Theatre Royal, Covent Garden, in London. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* describes that first appearance there as "highly successful."

He also played white characters, such as Shylock and Richard III, wearing greasepaint, a thick stage makeup, and a wig. On the last night of a run, he spoke to the audience against slavery. Rulers in Prussia, Austria and Russia gave him honors, and in 1863 he became a British citizen.

After the Civil War, Aldridge planned a tour of the United States and agreed to act in New York in September 1867. He died in Poland that August, before he could make the trip.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ira-aldridge" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blackface minstrelsy: the act and the makeup" -->
In blackface minstrelsy, white actors darkened their faces with burnt cork and acted Black people as fools. They built caricatures of Black people, acts that made fun of them by blowing up some of their features, and played them as singing, dancing, grinning fools. White audiences paid to watch.

Writers at the Jim Crow Museum at Ferris State University state that Rice and the actors who copied him helped to spread the belief that Black people were lazy, stupid and less than human. They write that the shows also helped white people argue for keeping Black people apart from them.

**"Jim Crow."** Thomas Dartmouth Rice was a white actor who did short comic acts between the scenes of plays. In 1828 he first went on stage as "Jim Crow," a Black character acted as a fool, with his skin blacked with burnt cork. Rice said he copied a Black man he had seen. Some accounts say the man was old, enslaved and walked with difficulty. Others say he was a ragged stable boy. No record names him.

Rice took the act from Louisville to Cincinnati, Pittsburgh and Philadelphia, and he reached New York in 1832. The act made him rich and famous. He died poor in New York on September 19, 1860.

People were using "Jim Crow" as an insult for Black people by 1838. By the end of the 1800s, "Jim Crow" was the name for the segregation laws and customs, which kept Black Americans apart from white Americans by race. Those laws came after 1877 and lasted until the mid-1960s.

**The first group.** Four white men in New York performed together in blackface as the Virginia Minstrels on January 31, 1843, the first group act of its kind. Years later, Black performers took up minstrelsy too, and they also blacked their faces with burnt cork. They acted the same caricatures that white actors had invented. In the 1840s one young Black dancer was already working in blackface.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-henry-lane" name="William Henry Lane (Master Juba)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Henry Lane (Master Juba)

> **Who:** A young Black dancer, known on stage as Master Juba, who danced in blackface and who toured with a white minstrel company.
> **When and where:** New York and London, 1840s. Born about 1825, died about 1854.

**"A black man imitating a white man imitating a black man."**

William Henry Lane was a Black dancer, known on stage as Master Juba. He learned to dance in the Five Points, a neighborhood in New York. By the early 1840s he was dancing for food and for coins people tossed him. Charles Dickens described a young Black dancer in the Five Points in a book in 1842, and later writers decided the dancer was Lane.

The reference book *Notable Black American Men* states that he was the only Black dancer of his time to get top billing, which means his name came first on the posters, in a minstrel company whose other members were all white. He toured with that company in the United States and in Britain.

**The fake contests.** A letter printed in a newspaper called the *Sunday Flash*, in 1841 or 1842, made a charge against the showman P. T. Barnum. The letter said that Barnum had made the Black boy up in blackface and advertised him as John Diamond, an Irish-American dancer who performed in blackface. It also said that Barnum staged fake dance contests with him: "Not one dollar is ever bet or staked."

The historian Eric Lott called the act "a black man imitating a white man imitating a black man." Lane later danced against the real John Diamond in public dance contests.

Lane went to London in 1848 with the Ethiopian Serenaders, a white minstrel company. A writer for *The Times* of London said that Lane "was compelled to repeat his efforts three times," which means the crowd made him dance again and again. He died in about 1854, when he was about 29.

**Not known:** No record states the cause of his death. Overwork and too little food probably killed him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-henry-lane" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shakespeare on the rivers" -->
Actors carried plays to river towns by boat. The actor Noah Ludlow bought a flatboat, a wide boat with a flat bottom, 25 feet long, at Olean, New York, in 1816. He named it Ludlow's Noah's Ark. He and eleven others floated down the Allegheny, Ohio and Mississippi rivers and stopped to perform wherever they could.

**The Floating Theatre.** William Chapman, a British-born actor, built a boat called the Floating Theatre at Pittsburgh in 1831. It was 14 feet wide and 100 feet long, the first boat built to be a theatre. Chapman and his family acted plays such as Shakespeare's *The Taming of the Shrew* at each landing, with songs and dances added.

When they reached New Orleans, they broke up the boat and went back to Pittsburgh on a steamboat to build another. From 1836 Chapman used a small steamboat to tow the theatre back up the river.

A night on the Floating Theatre began with a play. Then came a speech and a set of short scenes and songs. A ticket cost fifty cents, and people who had no cash could pay with fruit, potatoes or bacon. Minstrel shows in the same years acted comic versions of Shakespeare, with titles such as "Julius Sneezer."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Astor Place riot, May 10, 1849" -->
Soldiers in New York fired into a crowd outside the Astor Opera House, on Astor Place, on May 10, 1849, and at least 22 people died. City officials and the soldiers' own general had given the order. The crowd had come to stop the English actor William Charles Macready from playing Shakespeare's *Macbeth*.

The quarrel began between two actors. Edwin Forrest, the American star, was jealous of Macready. In 1846, in a theatre in Edinburgh, Scotland, Forrest hissed at Macready from a private box.

**The first night.** Forrest's supporters stopped Macready's *Macbeth* inside the opera house on May 7 by throwing rotten eggs, potatoes, shoes and pieces of ripped-up seats. Forty-seven New Yorkers, including the writers Herman Melville and Washington Irving, signed a letter asking Macready to play again.

Captain Isaiah Rynders and the writer Ned Buntline put up posters that spoke to working men. Rynders belonged to Tammany Hall, a political group in New York. The posters read "WORKINGMEN! SHALL AMERICANS OR ENGLISH RULE IN THIS CITY?" The two men also handed out free tickets.

**The order to fire.** A crowd of about 10,000 gathered outside the opera house on May 10. Mayor Caleb Woodhull had called out the militia, an army of part-time soldiers who have other jobs. General Charles Sandford brought about 350 soldiers of the Seventh Regiment, one unit of that army. People in the crowd threw stones at the building and at the soldiers.

Frederick Talmadge was the city's recorder, an elected city officer who also served as a judge. He told General William Hall, "You must order your men to fire." Sandford said his men would not "stand and be pounded to death with stones." Then the sheriff, whose name no record gives, said, "you have permission to fire." Sandford gave the order. The soldiers fired first over the crowd's heads. Then they lowered their guns and fired into the crowd.

**The dead.** The counts differ. Betsy Golden Kellem, writing in *Smithsonian* magazine in 2017, counts 18 dead that night and five more who died of their wounds in the next few days, 23 in all. Miguel Hernandez, writing in the *New York Almanack*, counts 25. The historian Nigel Cliff counts between 22 and 31.

Kellem writes that many of those shot were bystanders, people who were only watching. Seven of the dead were Irish immigrants. The dead included carpenters, clerks, printers and laborers, both men and women. About 48 people in the crowd were hurt, along with 50 to 70 police officers and 141 soldiers. The next night, in more fighting near Astor Place, a young boy was killed.

A jury later found that the events "justified the authorities in giving the order to fire." In September 1849 Buntline and nine others were found guilty of starting the riot, fined and jailed. Kellem does not name the judge. Kellem calls the shooting "the most significant loss of civilian life in New York since the Revolution."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-forrest" name="Edwin Forrest" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Forrest

> **Who:** An American-born star actor whose fans rioted against his English rival, William Charles Macready, at Astor Place in 1849.
> **When and where:** Born in Philadelphia on March 9, 1806. Acted from about 1817 to 1872. Died December 12, 1872.

**He paid prizes for new plays with a big part for himself.**

Edwin Forrest was an American star actor. In 2006 the Theatre Alliance of Greater Philadelphia, a group of the city's theaters, called him "America's first superstar of the stage and the highest paid actor in the world in the mid-nineteenth century." He first acted at eleven, at the South Street Theatre in Philadelphia. He became a star playing Othello at New York's Bowery Theatre in 1826.

**Metamora.** From 1828 Forrest paid prizes for new American plays that had a leading part written for him. The first winner was *Metamora*, by John Augustus Stone. Its hero is Metacom, a leader of the Wampanoag nation, whom the English called King Philip. Forrest, a white actor, played him from December 15, 1829. At the end of the play, Metamora kills his wife to save her from the settlers, and then he dies. The theatres made record profits from the play, and Stone got very little of the money.

An English visitor, Thomas Hamilton, called Forrest "a coarse and vulgar actor." Hamilton also wrote that his audience "were enraptured," meaning they loved him. In 1846 Forrest hissed Macready in Edinburgh, and in May 1849 Forrest's fans in New York rioted against Macready. No record shows that Forrest led the crowd or was at Astor Place that night.

Forrest beat the writer Nathaniel Parker Willis with a whip in Washington Square, in New York, in 1850. Willis took him to court and won $2,500. Forrest's wife, Catherine, won their divorce case on January 24, 1852, after a six-week trial in New York. Forrest had to pay her money every year, and he kept acting almost until he died, partly to pay it.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 15 minutes**

The biggest touring shows after 1850 told the stories of Black and Native people, and white showmen decided how. From 1852 until well after 1900, white actors in blackface toured the country playing the enslaved people in stage versions of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel written against slavery. Buffalo Bill's Wild West opened in 1883, and Native performers in it acted out attacks on settlers. Black minstrels such as Billy Kersands worked in the same burnt-cork makeup that white minstrels wore.

**Other stages.** John Wilkes Booth, a working actor, shot President Abraham Lincoln in 1865 in a theatre where he often performed. From 1881 theatre managers in New York and Boston built a new kind of show called vaudeville. In 1894 the first paying customers in New York watched films, and most of those films showed stage performers doing their acts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A novel against slavery, acted in blackface" -->
Harriet Beecher Stowe published *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel against slavery, in 1852. That year she refused to let it be made into a play, writing that "it would not be advisable." She never earned a cent from any stage version of it.

Theatre managers staged it anyway. The version that lasted opened in Troy, New York, on September 27, 1852. The manager, G. C. Howard, paid his 22-year-old cousin, George L. Aiken, $40 and a gold watch to write it in a week. When Aiken's play reached New York in July 1853, it ran for about 300 performances.

**Blackface again.** White actors in blackface played the enslaved characters. In Troy, the company's leading man, Green Germon, did not want to play a blacked-up character. He feared audiences would then expect minstrel dances and "Jim Crow" songs from him. The manager's wife, Mrs. Howard, played the enslaved girl Topsy and danced a minstrel dance called a breakdown. In January 1854 Thomas Dartmouth Rice, the actor who had created "Jim Crow," played Uncle Tom at New York's Bowery Theatre.

P. T. Barnum put on a version by Henry J. Conway at his American Museum in New York. His advertisement stated that this version "does not foolishly and unjustly elevate the Negro above the white man in intellect or morals," using a word of that time for a Black person. The advertisement promised white audiences that the play would not show Black people as being as smart or as good as white people.

**Tom shows.** People called these stage versions Tom shows. In the 1890s between 400 and 500 touring companies were playing the story. An agent's price list from 1893 offered actors to play Uncle Tom by the week, with "prime" ones at $60, "fair" ones at $50 and "culls," the leftovers, at $40.

One report counted a quarter of a million performances by 1902. A dozen companies were still touring the story in 1927.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charlotte-cushman" name="Charlotte Cushman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charlotte Cushman

> **Who:** A Boston-born actress who played men's parts, including Romeo and Hamlet, as well as women's parts.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston on July 23, 1816. Acted in the United States and Britain from 1835 to 1875. Lived in Rome from 1852. Died in Boston on February 18, 1876.

**She played Romeo while her sister Susan played Juliet.**

*Encyclopaedia Britannica* describes Charlotte Cushman as "the first native-born star on the American stage." A Philadelphia theater group has called Edwin Forrest, who was born in Philadelphia ten years before her, "America's first superstar of the stage," so the two descriptions do not agree on who came first. Cushman first trained to sing opera. Her voice failed after she sang parts that were too high for it, and she turned to acting. In 1836 she played Lady Macbeth.

Cushman played men's parts as well as women's. In London in 1845, and again in 1854 and 1855, she played Romeo while her sister Susan played Juliet. She also played Hamlet.

Cushman lived with the writer Matilda Hays from 1848, and people in Europe knew the two women as a couple. In 1852 Cushman settled in Rome, among a group of American women who were artists. She later lived with the sculptor Emma Stebbins. Cushman helped the career of the sculptor Edmonia Lewis, whose mother was of Ojibwe and African American descent.

During the Civil War, Cushman acted to raise money for the care of Union soldiers. In 1869 doctors began treating her for breast cancer. She kept acting and reading on stage. She announced her farewell at least seven times before her last performance in Boston on May 15, 1875.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charlotte-cushman" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The actor who shot Lincoln" -->
The man who killed President Abraham Lincoln was an actor. John Wilkes Booth came from a family of actors, and he often performed at Ford's Theatre in Washington. On April 14, 1865, he learned that Lincoln would come that night to see a comedy called *Our American Cousin*.

That night Booth found the president's box almost unguarded. He went in, barred its outer door from the inside, and waited for a line that he knew would bring a big laugh. Then he burst into the box and shot Lincoln once in the back of the head with a small pistol called a derringer. Booth also slashed Major Henry Rathbone, one of Lincoln's guests, in the shoulder with a knife. The play stopped at the line "you sockdologizing old man-trap," a joking insult made of slang of the time.

Booth jumped from the box to the stage below. He broke his left leg in the fall, though some people believe the leg broke later. Accounts differ on what he shouted: "Sic semper tyrannis," Latin for "Thus always to tyrants," or "The South is avenged!", or both. Lincoln died the next morning.

**The theatre after.** The federal government bought the theatre in 1866. Edwin Stanton, the Secretary of War, ordered it turned into offices. Workers were digging in the basement on June 9, 1893, to put in an electric light plant, when a brick pillar that held up the floors gave way. Part of the inside of the building fell in. Twenty-two clerks who worked there were killed, and at least 68 were hurt.

An inquest jury, a jury that looks into a death, met to find out why. It found three men guilty of criminal negligence, which means carelessness so serious it is a crime. The three were Colonel Fred C. Ainsworth, the head of the office whose clerks worked there, the engineer, and George Dant, the contractor in charge of the building. The jury blamed Dant most, because he had not propped up the brick pillars before the digging began. The charges against all of them were later dropped.

The restored theatre held its first public performance since Lincoln's death in 1968.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-booth" name="Edwin Booth" movie="Prince of Players (1955)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Booth

> **Who:** An American actor of Shakespeare who played Hamlet a record hundred nights in a row, and the older brother of John Wilkes Booth.
> **When and where:** Born in Bel Air, Maryland, on November 13, 1833. Acted mostly in New York and on tour. Died in New York on June 7, 1893.
> **Movie:** *Prince of Players* (1955), a feature film about his life.

**He pulled the president's son out from beside a moving train.**

Edwin Booth once saved the life of Robert Lincoln, the president's son. Late in 1863 or early in 1864, on a train platform in Jersey City, New Jersey, Robert slipped into the gap between the platform and a moving train. Booth grabbed his coat collar and pulled him out. Robert Lincoln described the rescue in a letter in 1909.

Booth played Hamlet about a hundred nights in a row in New York in 1864 and 1865. That was a record, and no actor broke it until 1922. He was the son of the actor Junius Brutus Booth. Edwin supported the Union in the Civil War. His brother John Wilkes supported the Confederacy, the group of Southern states whose leaders broke away from the United States to keep slavery.

**After the shooting.** After his brother killed President Lincoln, Edwin Booth stayed off the stage for about nine months. He came back as Hamlet in January 1866. He opened his own theatre in New York in 1869. It never made a profit, and in 1874 it went bankrupt, which means Booth could not pay its debts.

A salesman named Mark Gray fired two shots at him on a stage in Chicago in 1879 and missed. In 1888 Booth bought a house in New York and founded The Players, a private club, in it. He kept an upper floor as his home, and he died there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-booth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How vaudeville began" -->
Vaudeville was a show made of many short, separate acts, one after another. Tony Pastor, a theatre manager, took over a theatre on 14th Street in New York in 1881. He sold no alcohol and kept the acts clean, so that women and families would come. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* credits Pastor with the first show of the kind later called vaudeville.

**Boston.** Benjamin Franklin Keith opened a museum of oddities on Washington Street in Boston in 1883. Its first exhibit was a very small baby, three months old, shown to paying customers as "Baby Alice." Keith added a theatre with 123 seats. He ran a "continuous" show there from 10 in the morning to 11 at night, so customers could walk in at any time.

From 1885 Keith and Edward F. Albee ran the Bijou Theatre in Boston together, and the two men went on to build a chain of vaudeville theatres.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billy-kersands" name="Billy Kersands" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Billy Kersands

> **Who:** A Black minstrel comedian, the most popular Black comedian of his day, who performed in burnt-cork blackface.
> **When and where:** Born about 1842 in Baton Rouge, Louisiana. Toured the United States and Europe. Died on June 30, 1915, in Artesia, New Mexico.

**He filled his mouth with billiard balls to get laughs.**

Billy Kersands was a Black comedian who blacked his face with burnt cork and played the character that white minstrels had invented. He worked in Black minstrel companies such as Sam Hague's Georgia Minstrels. On stage he played a slow, foolish Black man. He filled his wide mouth with billiard balls, or with a cup and saucer. Tom Fletcher, a Black performer who knew him, wrote that Kersands once told Queen Victoria that if his mouth were any bigger, his ears would have to be moved.

A tavern owner named Charles Callender bought the Black minstrel company that Kersands worked in. That was in 1872. When Kersands and other stars asked for more pay and better treatment, Callender fired them. Most of them later came back to him.

**The pay.** Kersands earned about $15 a week in 1879, and by 1882 he was reportedly earning $80 a week, only a little less than a white minstrel star. On tours of Europe he earned up to $250 a week. White and Black audiences in the South came to see him. Because he drew such crowds, some theatre owners relaxed their rules limiting Black customers to separate sections of the theatre.

People asked Kersands why he never moved to vaudeville. Fletcher recorded his answer: "All of my money came from the people of the South, the white and the colored (Black), while playing down there." Kersands died shortly after a show, at about 73.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="billy-kersands" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Wild West: Lakota performers" -->
William F. Cody, called Buffalo Bill, started a show called Buffalo Bill's Wild West near North Platte, Nebraska, in 1883. In its arena, Native performers acted out attacks on stagecoaches, wagon trains and a settler's cabin. Then cowboys and soldiers drove them off, and in the last scene Cody rode to the rescue.

**Sitting Bull's contract.** The Hunkpapa Lakota leader Sitting Bull signed a contract on June 6, 1885, to tour with the show for four months. It paid him $50 a week and let him sell his own photographs and autographs. He signed at the Standing Rock Agency, the office of the Indian agent, the federal official who ran the reservation there, land that US officials had set aside for a Native nation. James McLaughlin, the Indian agent at Standing Rock, signed as a witness.

**The pay.** Most Lakota men in the show earned less. In July 1890 the Lakota performer No Neck said that the men had been paid $25 a month.

In early 1891 Cody and Nate Salsbury, who had started the show with him, wrote to the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the federal official over all the agents. They said the men would be paid $25 a month, that men they called "chiefs" would get $30 to $60 and that women would get $10. To hire Lakota people from their reservations, Cody needed permits, written permission from federal officials.

**Deaths on tour.** Several performers died on the show's tours of Europe. Surrounded by the Enemy, an Oglala Lakota man, died of a lung infection in London in 1887 and was buried there. Four months before him, a one-year-old boy named Red Penny, the son of Little Chief and Good Robe, died and was buried in the same London cemetery.

Chief Hawick died of typhoid fever on January 1, 1890, and Featherman died of smallpox, two diseases that often killed people then. Both men had been left in a hospital in Marseilles, France. Wounds One Another died after he fell from a train in Germany, with $108 in wages still owed to him. No full count of the deaths survives.

More and more Lakota performers died on the tours, and others came home in poor condition. John Noble, the Secretary of the Interior, ordered an investigation because of those deaths and the state of the men coming home. He also stopped giving new permits for Native performers.

In July 1890 No Neck told reporters that no more Lakota would go for less than $50 a month. He also said that Cody had given him "a suit of clothes, paid my wages, and gave me $100 besides," and had told him to give "a good report of the treatment of the Indians."

**Wounded Knee.** Soldiers of the 7th Cavalry, a regiment of soldiers on horseback, were taking the guns of Spotted Elk's band of Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek, in South Dakota, on December 29, 1890. The soldiers opened fire with carbines, which are short rifles, and Hotchkiss guns, which are small cannon. They killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children.

After the killings, army officers held Lakota Ghost Dancers, people who took part in a religious ceremony called the Ghost Dance, as prisoners at Fort Sheridan, Illinois. In March 1891 federal officials released 23 of the prisoners to Cody, on the condition that they tour Europe with his show. General Nelson Miles, Cody's close friend, commanded the army in the region.

The prisoners included Short Bull and Kicking Bear. Short Bull said of Wounded Knee, "twenty-three of my own relations were killed in this fight, men, women and children, this is like butchery." In Cody's arena, the prisoners played "hostiles," the Indians who attacked.

The historian Sam Maddra studied these tours and concludes that the Lakota who joined the show got wages, travel and standing among their people. She adds that, perhaps more important, they got away from the control of government officials. They also earned far more than farming on the reservation paid.

**Dances made crimes.** United States officials and missionaries worked in the same years to stop Native ceremonies and dances. In 1883 Hiram Price, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued rules that made the Sun Dance and other dances "Indian offenses," or crimes, for the Courts of Indian Offenses on the reservations.

For a first offense, the rules specify stopping the person's rations, the food handed out to them on the reservation, for up to ten days. For a later offense, the rations could be stopped for 15 to 30 days, or the person could be locked in the agency jail for up to 30 days.

Missionaries from the Moravian Church arrived at Quinhagak, Alaska, in 1885. They called the masked dances of the Yup'ik people "heathen rites," meaning the religion of people who were not Christian. No traditional dancing took place in Quinhagak for more than a century, until 2013. *Archaeology* magazine's 2015 account of the village does not name the missionaries or say how the dancing was stopped.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What did the first films show?" -->
Many of the first films were recordings of performers doing their stage acts. In December 1892 Thomas Edison's staff began building a film studio at West Orange, New Jersey. They called it the Black Maria. It was a studio lined with tar paper, with a roof on hinges that flipped open, and the whole building could turn on a track.

**The first paying customers.** The Holland brothers opened a Kinetoscope parlor at 1155 Broadway in New York on April 14, 1894. A Kinetoscope was a machine that showed one short film to one person looking into it. The parlor had ten machines and ten films, each 15 to 20 seconds long, all shot in the Black Maria.

They showed Sandow, a strongman, and Bertoldi, a contortionist who bent the body into unusual shapes. The other films showed wrestlers, a trapeze act, a Highland dancer, blacksmiths and a barber shop.

**A stage kiss.** A film projector called the Vitascope showed films on a screen to a seated audience in a theatre for the first time on April 23, 1896, at Koster and Bial's Music Hall, a vaudeville theatre in New York.

That same month, the actors May Irwin and John Rice made a film of a stage kiss. They had kissed at the end of a Broadway musical, *The Widow Jones*, in 1895. They acted the same kiss again in front of Edison's camera. The film, *The Kiss*, runs about 18 seconds.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Black and Native performers found paying work on the touring stage in these fifty years, mostly in parts that white managers had written to mock them or to act out their defeat.
**In 1850:** Theatres played Shakespeare and minstrel shows, and Cody's Wild West, vaudeville and films did not yet exist.
**In 1900:** Hundreds of companies had been touring *Uncle Tom's Cabin* in blackface, Keith and Albee ran a chain of vaudeville theatres, and New Yorkers had been paying to watch films for six years.

**Next:** After 1900 films began to tell stories of their own, and in 1911 the first film studio opened in Hollywood.
