## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1828 a white actor named Thomas Dartmouth Rice blacked his face with burnt cork and played a Black man as a fool. White audiences paid to laugh at the act, and by 1843 white men in the same makeup were filling whole evenings with it. The first known Black theatre company in the United States had opened in New York in 1821. Police arrested its actors in the middle of a play, and its best young actor, Ira Aldridge, left for England to find work.

In the same years, actors played Shakespeare in city theatres, on boats on the rivers and in rooms over taverns. In 1849 the fans of two rival actors fought outside a New York theatre. City officials gave soldiers the order to fire into the crowd, and at least 22 people died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The African Theatre, New York, 1821 to 1823" -->
The first known Black theatre company in the United States opened in New York in 1821. Its founder, William Brown, was a free Black man from the West Indies who had worked as a steward, a servant on a ship. He ran a tea garden behind his house at 38 Thomas Street, with music on Sunday afternoons. In 1821 he opened a theatre with about 300 seats on the upper floor of a house at Mercer and Bleecker Streets. All of its actors were Black. It opened with Shakespeare's *Richard III*. Sources give the first night as September 17, 21 or 24, 1821.

On the first night, an enslaved man played the king in a robe made from a window curtain. After that, an actor named James Hewlett took over the part. The company also played Shakespeare's *Othello* and comedies such as *The Poor Soldier*. White New Yorkers began to come as well as Black ones.

White men attacked the company. A group that included a man named George Bellmont broke into the theatre. They beat the actors and wrecked the costumes and scenery. Police arrested Bellmont and several of the others.

Brown then moved his company next door to the Park Theatre, run by a white manager named Stephen Price. Brown's company played *Richard III* on the same nights as Price's company. White members of the audience shouted at Brown's actors and broke up the show. According to the Shakespeare Theatre Company, which has published Hewlett's history, Price started the trouble because he hoped to destroy Brown's company. During one performance, police arrested the actors and put them in jail. The police let them go only after they promised to stop acting Shakespeare. The surviving records do not give the date or name the officers.

Mordecai Noah, the editor of a newspaper called the *National Advocate*, mocked the company in print. He warned that free Black New Yorkers might one day "outvote the whites." In October 1822 his paper printed a notice that the theatre was closing because of an outbreak of yellow fever. The last known playbill of the company is from June 1823.

In London, the English comedian Charles Mathews mocked the company in his show *A Trip to America*, although he had never been to its theatre. In May 1824 James Hewlett answered him in a letter in the *National Advocate*: "Was this well for a brother actor?"
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-alexander-brown" name="William Alexander Brown" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Alexander Brown

> **Who:** A Black ship's steward from the West Indies who started the first known Black theatre company in the United States, and wrote the first known play by a Black writer to be acted there.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1816 to 1823.

William Brown wrote *The Drama of King Shotaway*, the first known play by a Black writer to be staged in the United States. Sources give his full name two ways, as William Alexander Brown and as William Henry Brown.

In 1816 Brown bought a house on Thomas Street in New York. On Sunday afternoons he offered music and other shows in the tea garden behind it. In September 1821 he opened his theatre with *Richard III*, acted by a Black cast for Black audiences.

Neighbors and police complained about the tea garden, and it closed. Brown moved his company several times. White men broke in and beat his actors. Police arrested his actors in the middle of a play and freed them only when they promised to stop acting Shakespeare.

In 1823 Brown staged *King Shotaway*. It told the story of the war that the Black Caribs of the island of St. Vincent fought against English and French settlers in 1796. No copy of the play survives. The last known playbill of Brown's company dates from June 1823. What happened to Brown after that is not known.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-alexander-brown" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ira-aldridge" name="Ira Aldridge" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ira Aldridge

> **Who:** A Black actor from New York who left the United States in 1824 and spent more than forty years as a leading Shakespearean actor in Britain and Europe.
> **When and where:** Born in New York about July 24, 1807. Went to England in 1824. Died on tour in Łódź, Poland, on August 7, 1867.

Ira Aldridge became one of the best-known actors of Shakespeare in Europe, and he never acted on a stage in the United States as a grown man. He went to the African Free School in New York. As a teenager he acted with William Brown's company.

In 1824, after New York police had arrested Black actors for playing Shakespeare, Aldridge sailed to England. In October 1825 he played Oroonoko, an African prince sold into slavery, at a London theatre. A writer in *The Times* of London made fun of the shape of his lips. The paper said his skin was not dark enough for him to play Othello.

Aldridge played Othello anyway, and King Lear and Macbeth. He also played white characters, such as Shylock and Richard III, in white face paint called greasepaint and a wig. On the last night of a run, he spoke to the audience against slavery. In 1828 he became the manager of the theatre in Coventry, England. Rulers in Prussia, Austria and Russia gave him honors, and in 1863 he became a British citizen.

After the Civil War ended slavery in the United States, Aldridge agreed to act in New York in September 1867. He died in Poland that August, before he could make the trip.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ira-aldridge" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blackface minstrelsy: the act and the makeup" -->
In blackface minstrelsy, white actors darkened their faces with burnt cork and acted Black people as fools. A caricature is a picture or an act that makes fun of people by blowing up some of their features. Minstrel actors built caricatures of Black people and acted them as singing, dancing, grinning fools. The audiences who paid to watch were white. The Jim Crow Museum at Ferris State University states that Rice and the actors who copied him spread the belief that Black people were lazy, stupid and less than human, and should be kept apart from white people.

Thomas Dartmouth Rice was a white actor who did short comic acts between the scenes of plays. In 1828 he first went on stage as "Jim Crow," a Black character acted as a fool, with his skin blacked with burnt cork. Rice said he copied a Black man he had seen singing. Some accounts say the man was old, enslaved and walked with difficulty. Others say he was a ragged stable boy. No record names him.

Rice took the act from Louisville to Cincinnati, Pittsburgh and Philadelphia, and he reached New York in 1832. The act made him rich and famous. He died poor in New York on September 19, 1860. By 1838 white Americans were using "Jim Crow" as an insult for Black people. By the end of the 1800s the words named the laws and customs that kept Black Americans apart from white Americans and took away their rights.

In 1843 four white men in New York acted a whole evening in blackface and called themselves the Virginia Minstrels. Years later, Black performers took up minstrelsy too, and they also blacked their faces with burnt cork. They acted the same caricatures that white actors had invented. In the 1840s one young Black dancer was already working in blackface.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-henry-lane" name="William Henry Lane (Master Juba)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Henry Lane (Master Juba)

> **Who:** A young Black dancer, known on stage as Master Juba, who had to dance in blackface to work, and who toured with a white minstrel company.
> **When and where:** New York and London, 1840s. Born about 1825, died about 1854.

William Henry Lane was a Black dancer who had to perform in blackface. He was the only Black performer of his time known to tour with a white minstrel company. He grew up in the Five Points, a poor New York neighborhood where Irish immigrants and free Black people lived side by side. By the early 1840s he was dancing in the street for food and coins.

A letter printed in a newspaper called the *Sunday Flash*, in 1841 or 1842, made a charge against the showman P. T. Barnum. The letter said that Barnum blacked the boy's face with burnt cork and billed him as John Diamond, an Irish-American dancer who performed in blackface. It also said that Barnum staged fake dance contests with him. The historian Eric Lott called the act "a black man imitating a white man imitating a black man." Lane later beat the real John Diamond in public dance contests.

In 1848 Lane went to London with the Ethiopian Serenaders, a white minstrel company. *The Times* of London reported that the crowd made him repeat his dance three times. He died in about 1854, when he was about 29. Historians think that overwork and too little food killed him, but no record states the cause of his death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-henry-lane" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shakespeare on the rivers" -->
Actors carried plays to river towns by boat. In 1816 the actor Noah Ludlow and eleven others bought a flatboat 25 feet long. They floated down the Allegheny, Ohio and Mississippi rivers and stopped to perform wherever they could.

In 1831 the English-born actor William Chapman built a boat called the Floating Theatre at Pittsburgh. It was 14 feet wide and 100 feet long, the first boat built to be a theatre. Chapman and his family acted plays such as Shakespeare's *The Taming of the Shrew* at each landing, with songs and dances added. When they reached New Orleans, they broke up the boat and went back to Pittsburgh on a steamboat to build another. From 1836 Chapman used a small steamboat to tow the theatre back up the river.

A night on the Floating Theatre began with a play. Then came a speech and a set of short scenes and songs. A ticket cost fifty cents, and people who had no cash could pay with fruit, potatoes or bacon. Minstrel shows in the same years acted comic versions of Shakespeare, with titles such as "Julius Sneezer."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="May 10, 1849: the Astor Place riot" -->
On May 10, 1849, soldiers in New York fired into a crowd outside the Astor Place Opera House, on the orders of city officials and their own general. The crowd had come to stop the English actor William Charles Macready from playing Shakespeare's *Macbeth*. Historians count between 22 and 31 dead.

The quarrel began between two actors. Edwin Forrest, the American star, and Macready each accused the other of plotting against his career. In 1846, in a theatre in Edinburgh, Scotland, Forrest stood up in his box and hissed at Macready. Most of Forrest's fans in New York were working men. The Astor Place Opera House expected its audience to wear kid gloves and white vests.

On May 7, Forrest's supporters bought tickets for the upper seats. They stopped Macready's *Macbeth* by throwing rotten eggs, potatoes, shoes and pieces of ripped-up seats. Forty-seven New Yorkers, including the writers Herman Melville and Washington Irving, signed a letter asking Macready to play again. Isaiah Rynders, a New York political boss, and the writer Ned Buntline put up posters. The posters read "WORKINGMEN! SHALL AMERICANS OR ENGLISH RULE IN THIS CITY?" The two men also handed out free tickets.

On May 10 a crowd of about 10,000 gathered outside the opera house. Mayor Caleb Woodhull had called out the militia. A militia is an army of part-time soldiers who have other jobs. General Charles Sandford brought about 350 soldiers of the Seventh Regiment. People in the crowd threw paving stones at the building and at the soldiers. A city judge, Recorder Frederick Talmadge, told General William Hall, "You must order your men to fire." The sheriff, whose name no record gives, then told the generals, "you have permission to fire." Sandford gave the order. The soldiers fired first over the crowd's heads. Then they lowered their guns and fired into the crowd.

*Smithsonian* magazine counts 18 dead that night and five more who died of their wounds in the next few days, 23 in all. The *New York Almanack* counts 25. The historian Nigel Cliff counts between 22 and 31. *Smithsonian* writes that many of those shot were bystanders. Seven of the dead were Irish immigrants. The dead included carpenters, clerks, printers and laborers, both men and women. About 48 people in the crowd were hurt, along with 50 to 70 police officers and 141 soldiers. The next night, in more fighting near Astor Place, a young boy was killed.

A jury later found that the events "justified the authorities in giving the order to fire." Buntline and nine others were convicted of starting the riot. They were fined and sent to jail. *Smithsonian* calls it the largest loss of civilian life in New York since the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-forrest" name="Edwin Forrest" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Forrest

> **Who:** An American-born star actor, whose feud with an English actor ended in the Astor Place riot.
> **When and where:** Born in Philadelphia on March 9, 1806. Acted from about 1817 to 1872. Died December 12, 1872.

Edwin Forrest was an American star actor whom some historians call "the first real American theatrical star." He first acted at eleven, in a woman's part, at a theatre in Philadelphia. He became a star playing Othello at New York's Bowery Theatre in 1826.

From 1828 Forrest paid prizes for new American plays that had a leading part written for him. The first winner was *Metamora*, by John Augustus Stone. Its hero is Metacom, a leader of the Wampanoag nation, whom the English called King Philip. Forrest, a white actor, played him from December 15, 1829. At the end of the play, Metamora kills his wife to save her from the settlers, and then he dies. The theatres made record profits from the play, and Stone got very little of the money.

An English visitor, Thomas Hamilton, called Forrest "a coarse and vulgar actor." Hamilton also wrote that his audience "were enraptured," meaning they loved him. In 1846 Forrest hissed Macready in Edinburgh, and in May 1849 Forrest's fans in New York rioted against Macready. No record shows that Forrest led the crowd or was at Astor Place that night.

In 1850 Forrest beat the writer Nathaniel Parker Willis with a whip in Washington Square, in New York. In 1852 a court granted Forrest's wife, Catherine, a divorce and ordered him to pay her money every year. He kept acting almost until he died.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1852 until well after 1900, white actors in blackface toured the country playing the enslaved people in stage versions of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel written against slavery. In 1865 a working actor, John Wilkes Booth, shot President Abraham Lincoln in a theatre where he often performed. From 1881 theatre managers in New York and Boston built a new kind of show called vaudeville. Buffalo Bill's Wild West opened in 1883 and hired Lakota men to act out attacks on settlers. Black minstrels such as Billy Kersands worked in the same burnt-cork makeup that white minstrels wore. In 1894 the first paying customers in New York watched films, and most of those films showed stage performers doing their acts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tom shows" -->
Harriet Beecher Stowe published *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel against slavery, in March 1852. She refused to let it be made into a play. She never earned a cent from any stage version of it.

Theatre managers staged it anyway. The version that lasted opened in Troy, New York, on September 27, 1852. The manager, G. C. Howard, paid his 22-year-old cousin, George L. Aiken, $40 and a gold watch to write it in a week. When Aiken's play reached New York in July 1853, it ran for about 300 performances.

White actors in blackface played the enslaved characters. In Troy, the company's leading man, Green Germon, at first refused to black up for the part of Uncle Tom. He said audiences would then expect minstrel dances and "Jim Crow" songs from him. The manager's wife, Mrs. Howard, played the enslaved girl Topsy and danced a minstrel dance called a breakdown. In January 1854 Thomas Dartmouth Rice, the actor who had created "Jim Crow," played Uncle Tom at New York's Bowery Theatre.

P. T. Barnum's museum in New York put on its own version. Barnum advertised that it "does not foolishly and unjustly elevate the Negro above the white man in intellect or morals."

These stage versions came to be called Tom shows. In the 1890s between 400 and 500 touring companies were playing the story. In 1893 an agent in Chicago offered to hire out actors to play Uncle Tom for $40 to $60 a week. By 1902, one report counted a quarter of a million performances. A dozen companies were still touring the story in 1927.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charlotte-cushman" name="Charlotte Cushman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charlotte Cushman

> **Who:** A Boston-born actress who played men's parts, including Romeo and Hamlet, as well as women's parts.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston on July 23, 1816. Acted in the United States and Britain from 1835 to 1875. Lived in Rome from 1852. Died in Boston on February 18, 1876.

*Encyclopaedia Britannica* calls Charlotte Cushman "the first native-born star on the American stage." Other historians give that title to Edwin Forrest. Cushman first trained to sing opera. Her voice failed after she sang parts that were too high for it, and she turned to acting. In 1836 she played Lady Macbeth.

Cushman played men's parts as well as women's. In London in 1845, and again in 1854 and 1855, she played Romeo while her sister Susan played Juliet. She also played Hamlet.

From 1848 Cushman lived with the writer Matilda Hays, and people in Europe knew the two women as a couple. In 1852 Cushman settled in Rome, among a group of American women who were artists. She later lived with the sculptor Emma Stebbins. Cushman helped the career of the sculptor Edmonia Lewis, whose parents were Black and Native American.

During the Civil War, Cushman acted to raise money for the care of Union soldiers. In 1869 doctors began treating her for breast cancer. She kept acting and reading on stage. She announced her farewell at least seven times before her last performance in Boston on May 15, 1875.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charlotte-cushman" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="April 14, 1865: an actor at Ford's Theatre" -->
The man who killed President Abraham Lincoln was an actor who knew the theatre as a place of work. John Wilkes Booth came from a family of actors, and he often performed at Ford's Theatre in Washington. On the morning of April 14, 1865, he learned that Lincoln would come that night to see a comedy called *Our American Cousin*.

That night Booth found the president's box almost unguarded. He went in and barred its outer door from the inside. He waited for a line that he knew would bring a big laugh. Then he stepped into the box and shot Lincoln once in the back of the head with a small pistol called a derringer. He cut Major Henry Rathbone, one of Lincoln's guests, with a knife. The play stopped at the line "you sockdologizing old man-trap."

Booth jumped from the box to the stage and ran out a side door to a waiting horse. People in the audience said he shouted "Sic semper tyrannis," Latin for "Thus always to tyrants," or "The South is avenged!", or both. Lincoln died the next morning.

The federal government bought the theatre in 1866. Edwin Stanton, the Secretary of War, ordered it turned into offices. On June 9, 1893, workers were digging in the basement to put in an electric light plant, and a brick pillar that held up the floors gave way. Part of the inside of the building fell in. Twenty-two clerks who worked there were killed, and at least 68 were hurt. An inquest jury, a jury that looks into a death, found the contractor, George Dant, and three other men in charge of the building guilty of criminal negligence. That means carelessness so serious it is a crime. The charges against all of them were later dropped. No play was acted in the building again until 1968.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-booth" name="Edwin Booth" movie="Prince of Players (1955)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Booth

> **Who:** The leading American actor of Shakespeare in his time, and the older brother of John Wilkes Booth.
> **When and where:** Born in Bel Air, Maryland, on November 13, 1833. Acted mostly in New York and on tour. Died in New York on June 7, 1893.
> **Movie:** *Prince of Players* (1955), a feature film about his life.

Edwin Booth played Hamlet about a hundred nights in a row in New York in 1864 and 1865. That was a record, and no actor broke it until 1922. He was the son of the actor Junius Brutus Booth. Edwin supported the Union in the Civil War, and his brother John Wilkes supported the Confederacy.

Late in 1863 or early in 1864, a young man on a crowded train platform in Jersey City, New Jersey, slipped into the gap between the platform and a moving train. Booth grabbed his coat collar and pulled him out. The young man was Robert Lincoln, the president's son. Robert Lincoln described the rescue in a letter in 1909.

After his brother killed President Lincoln, Edwin Booth stayed off the stage for about nine months. He came back as Hamlet in January 1866. He opened his own theatre in New York in 1869, and it went bankrupt in 1874. In 1879 a salesman named Mark Gray fired two shots at him on a stage in Chicago and missed. In 1888 Booth founded The Players, a club for actors and artists. He died in the club's building.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-booth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vaudeville begins" -->
Vaudeville was a show made of many short, separate acts, one after another. In 1881 Tony Pastor, a singer and theatre manager, took over a theatre on 14th Street in New York. He sold no alcohol and kept the acts clean, so that women and families would come. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* credits Pastor with the first show of the kind later called vaudeville.

In 1883 Benjamin Franklin Keith opened a museum of oddities in a shop in Boston. Its first exhibit was a very small baby, three months old, shown to paying customers as "Baby Alice." Keith added a theatre with 123 seats. He ran a "continuous" show there from 10 in the morning to 11 at night, so customers could walk in at any time. From 1885 Keith and Edward F. Albee ran the Bijou Theatre in Boston together. The two men went on to build a chain of vaudeville theatres.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billy-kersands" name="Billy Kersands" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Billy Kersands

> **Who:** A Black minstrel comedian and dancer, the most popular Black comedian of his day, who performed in burnt-cork blackface.
> **When and where:** Born about 1842 in Baton Rouge, Louisiana. Toured the United States and Britain from the 1860s. Died on June 30, 1915, in Artesia, New Mexico.

Billy Kersands was a Black comedian who blacked his face with burnt cork and played the character that white minstrels had invented. He began touring with minstrel companies in the early 1860s. On stage he played a slow, foolish Black man. He filled his wide mouth with billiard balls, or with a cup and saucer, to get laughs.

In 1872 Charles Callender, a tavern owner, bought the Black minstrel company that Kersands worked in. When Kersands and other stars asked for more pay and better treatment, Callender fired them. Most of them later came back to him.

Kersands earned about $15 a week in 1879 and about $80 a week by 1882. On tours of Europe he earned up to $250 a week. White and Black audiences in the South came to see him. Because he drew such crowds, some theatre owners relaxed the rules that kept Black customers in separate sections of the theatre.

People asked Kersands why he never moved to vaudeville. Tom Fletcher, a Black performer who knew him, recorded his answer: "All of my money came from the people of the South, the white and the colored, while playing down there." Kersands died shortly after a show, at about 73.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="billy-kersands" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Wild West: Lakota performers" -->
In 1883 William F. Cody, called Buffalo Bill, started a show called Buffalo Bill's Wild West, near North Platte, Nebraska. In its arena, Native performers acted out attacks on stagecoaches, wagon trains and a settler's cabin. Then Cody and his cowboys drove them off.

On June 6, 1885, at the Standing Rock Agency, the Hunkpapa Lakota leader Sitting Bull signed a contract to tour with the show for four months. It paid him $50 a week and let him sell his own photographs and autographs. James McLaughlin, the federal Indian agent at Standing Rock, signed as a witness.

Most Lakota men in the show earned less. In 1891 Cody wrote that he paid most Lakota men $25 a month. Men he called "chiefs" got $30 to $60 a month, and women got $10. To hire Lakota people from their reservations, Cody needed permits from federal officials.

Several performers died on the show's tours of Europe. Surrounded by the Enemy, an Oglala Lakota man, died of a lung infection in London in 1887 and was buried there. Four months before him, a one-year-old boy named Red Penny, the son of Little Chief and Good Robe, died and was buried in the same London cemetery. Chief Hawick died of typhoid fever on January 1, 1890, and Featherman died of smallpox. Both had been left in a hospital in Marseilles, France. Wounds One Another died after he fell from a train in Germany. No full count of the deaths survives.

In 1890, after reports of deaths and mistreatment, John Noble, the Secretary of the Interior, ordered an investigation. He also stopped giving new permits for Native performers. That July, the Lakota performer No Neck told reporters that the men had been paid $25 a month. He said no more Lakota would go for less than $50. He also said that Cody had given him "a suit of clothes, paid my wages, and gave me $100 besides," and had told him to give "a good report of the treatment of the Indians."

On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the 7th Cavalry killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people at Wounded Knee, in South Dakota. Most of them were women and children. After the killings, army officers held Lakota Ghost Dancers as prisoners at Fort Sheridan, Illinois. Ghost Dancers were people who took part in the Ghost Dance, a religious ceremony. In March 1891 federal officials released 23 of the prisoners to Cody, on the condition that they tour Europe with his show. General Nelson Miles, Cody's close friend, commanded the army in the region. The prisoners included Short Bull and Kicking Bear. Short Bull said of Wounded Knee, "twenty-three of my own relations were killed in this fight, men, women and children." In Cody's arena, the prisoners played "hostiles," the Indians who attacked.

The historian Sam Maddra studied these tours and concludes that the Lakota who joined the show got wages, travel and some freedom from government control. They also earned far more than farming on the reservation paid.

In the same years, United States officials and missionaries worked to stop Native ceremonies and dances. In 1883 Hiram Price, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued rules that made the Sun Dance and other dances "Indian offenses," or crimes. In 1885 Moravian missionaries arrived at Quinhagak, Alaska. They called the masked dances of the Yup'ik people "heathen rites," meaning the religion of people who were not Christian. No traditional dancing took place in Quinhagak for more than a century, until 2013.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1893 to 1896: the first films show stage acts" -->
Many of the first films were recordings of performers doing their stage acts. In December 1892 Thomas Edison's staff began building a film studio at West Orange, New Jersey. They called it the Black Maria. It was a shed lined with tar paper, with a roof that opened to let in sunlight. The whole building turned on a track to follow the sun.

On April 14, 1894, the Holland brothers opened a Kinetoscope parlor at 1155 Broadway in New York. A Kinetoscope was a machine that showed one short film to one person looking into it. The parlor had ten machines and ten films, each 15 to 20 seconds long, all shot in the Black Maria. They showed the strongman Eugen Sandow, a contortionist, a performer who bends the body into unusual shapes, wrestlers, trapeze artists, a Highland dancer, blacksmiths and a barber shop.

On April 23, 1896, a film was projected onto a screen for a seated audience at Koster and Bial's Music Hall, a vaudeville theatre in New York. That same month, the actors May Irwin and John Rice made a film of a stage kiss. They had kissed at the end of a Broadway musical, *The Widow Jones*, in 1895. They acted the same kiss again in front of Edison's camera. The film, *The Kiss*, runs about 18 seconds.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->
