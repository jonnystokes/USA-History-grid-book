## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, nobody in this land used money. There were no coins, no paper bills, no banks and no prices. People got what they needed by barter, which means trading one thing straight for another. They also gave gifts and owed each other duties, both between neighbors and between nations.

Some goods went a long way. People carried shells from the Gulf coast, copper from Lake Superior and obsidian from the West hundreds of miles along rivers and trails. Obsidian is a black glass that forms in volcanoes and can be chipped into sharp blades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum" -->
Wampum was not money before Europeans came. People used it to keep records, to make agreements between nations, in ceremonies and as gifts.

Wampum is beads made from shell. The white beads came from whelk shells. The purple-black beads came from the shell of the quahog clam. People strung the beads on cords or wove them into belts.

Among the Haudenosaunee, the beads of a belt recorded spoken agreements and histories. People presented the belts at councils and read them there.

Wampum first passed as money in the 1600s, after colonists arrived. Colonists and Native traders used it that way in their dealings with each other.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, far to the south in lands that Spain ruled, Spanish officials forced Indigenous men to dig silver. A mint is a workshop where coins are made. Workers at Spanish mints struck the silver into a coin called the piece of eight. Almost none of those coins reached the land that became the United States during the 1500s. Nobody made money in this land yet.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The piece of eight" -->
The piece of eight was a silver coin worth 8 reales. A real was a Spanish unit of money. In English the coin was also called the Spanish dollar. Workers at Spanish mints in the Americas made it from silver dug at Potosí, in what is now Bolivia, and at Zacatecas, in Mexico. Silver was found at Potosí in 1545.

Indigenous men did the digging at Potosí. In 1573 Francisco de Toledo, the Spanish king's viceroy, or governor, in South America, set up a system that forced thousands of them to work in the mines. It was called the mita. Toledo took the name and the idea of turns of work from an older system used by the Inca. Under Toledo's mita, more than 11,000 men from the highland provinces between Potosí and Cuzco were sent to the mines each year.

The men worked in shifts, one week in the mines and then two weeks of rest. Many brought their families and carried most of their own food. They were paid a little, and most of that pay went to the tax they owed the colonial government. The work was dangerous and unhealthy. No reliable count shows how many of the men died.

Historians of money call the piece of eight the first money used around the whole world. Traders carried it from South America to Europe, and across the Pacific Ocean to Asia.

What little Spanish coin there was in this land was at garrison towns, where soldiers were posted. One was St. Augustine, in Florida. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded it in September 1565 at Seloy, a village of the Timucua, in the homeland of the Timucua-speaking peoples. The Spanish soldiers there were paid from Spanish colonial funds, with the coin of a foreign empire.

Later, the United States dollar was modeled on the piece of eight. Spanish coins stayed legal tender in the United States until February 21, 1857. Legal tender is money that the law says must be accepted to pay a debt. Americans still call a quarter "two bits." The piece of eight was divided into eight parts called bits, so two bits made a quarter of a dollar.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies were short of coins from the start. Little English coin reached them. Colonists spent the coins they did get on goods shipped in from overseas, and those coins went back across the ocean. To trade with each other, colonists paid in tobacco, corn, beaver skins and wampum. Massachusetts leaders opened a mint of their own without England's permission. In 1690 they printed the first paper money issued by any government in the Western world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money made of goods" -->
Colonists called payment in goods "country pay." Colonial lawmakers set how much each kind of goods was worth when it was used to pay a debt. Money of this kind is called commodity money, because the money is a useful thing in itself.

English money counted in pence, shillings and pounds. Twelve pence made a shilling, and twenty shillings made a pound.

In Virginia, lawmakers rated tobacco as money from the 1620s, and in 1642 they made it legal tender. Virginia, Maryland and North Carolina used tobacco as money through much of the colonial period. In the northern colonies, beaver pelts and corn also passed as money.

New England colonists used wampum as small change. The nations that made wampum had not used it as money before colonists came. On November 15, 1637, the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmakers, voted to accept wampum at 6 beads a penny for sums under 12 pence. On October 18, 1650, they set the rate at 8 white beads or 4 black beads a penny. They also made wampum legal tender for debts up to 40 shillings, but not for taxes. On May 22, 1661, they repealed the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pine-tree shilling, 1652" -->
In 1652 members of the Massachusetts General Court voted to set up a mint in Boston. They did not ask England first. By English law, only the king could make coins. In 1652 England had no king. Workers at the mint struck silver coins worth threepence, sixpence and a shilling.

The best-known of these coins is the pine-tree shilling, named for the tree stamped on it. Workers at the mint struck pine-tree shillings from about 1667 to 1682. But nearly all of the colony's silver coins carry the date 1652.

Historians of coins explain the date this way. Charles II became king of England in 1660. A coin dated 1652 could pass as a leftover from the years without a king. A coin with a later date would show that the colony was still making coins against the king's right.

The mint closed in 1682. In 1684 the Massachusetts charter, the document that gave the colony the right to govern itself, was canceled on behalf of the king. The illegal mint was one of the complaints listed against the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-hull" name="John Hull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Hull

> **Who:** A Boston silversmith who ran the colony's mint without the king's permission and struck the pine-tree shilling.
> **When and where:** Boston, Massachusetts. Mintmaster from 1652 to 1682. Lived 1624 to 1683.

John Hull made the coins of Massachusetts for thirty years without the king's permission. He was born in 1624 and came to Boston from England as a boy, in 1635. In Boston he trained as a silversmith, a craftsman who makes things out of silver.

In 1652 members of the Massachusetts General Court named him mintmaster, the man in charge of the mint. He was 28. His appointment was dated May 27, 1652. Robert Sanderson worked with him as his partner. Hull's contract paid him a share of every batch of coins he struck.

The work made Hull one of the richest men in New England. He later became the colony's treasurer, the official in charge of its money.

A story says Hull gave his daughter Hannah her weight in pine-tree shillings as a wedding gift. The story is a legend. The writer Nathaniel Hawthorne retold it in his book *Grandfather's Chair* and added details of his own.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-hull" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1690: the first government paper money" -->
In 1690, soldiers from Massachusetts attacked the French city of Quebec, in Canada, during King William's War. The attack failed. When the soldiers came home, the colony's treasury did not hold enough coins to pay them. According to accounts from the time, the troops were close to mutiny, which means rising up against their officers.

Massachusetts lawmakers decided to pay the soldiers in paper. They began voting on it in December 1690. The first bills totaled 40,000 pounds. The paper bills were called bills of credit. Each bill stated that the colony's treasurer would accept it "in all Public payments." It also stated that the bill could later be exchanged for coins.

Historians call these bills the first paper money issued by any government in the Western world. The Western world means Europe and the lands that Europeans settled. China had used paper money centuries earlier.

People took the bills and passed them from hand to hand. By 1714 Massachusetts lawmakers had issued 240,000 pounds in paper money. By 1718 the other New England colonies were issuing their own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the early 1700s more colonies began printing paper money, and in time every colony printed its own. Some of that paper kept its value and some lost it, depending on how much the lawmakers printed. Benjamin Franklin argued for paper money in Pennsylvania and then printed it. In South Carolina, which had no banks, people borrowed by pledging land and enslaved people as security for their loans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper that held its value and paper that lost it" -->
Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland compared the paper money of the colonies. In Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey, lawmakers printed limited amounts, and the bills held their value. In New England and the Carolinas, lawmakers printed large amounts. Prices counted in paper money rose there, and each bill was worth less silver than before.

Virginia's tobacco money gained a paper form. Virginia lawmakers set up public warehouses for tobacco, with government inspectors, in the 1720s and 1730s. An inspector checked a planter's tobacco and stored it. He then wrote a receipt, called a tobacco note, for the inspected tobacco in the warehouse. People passed tobacco notes from hand to hand as money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-money" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Pennsylvania printer who argued for paper money, then printed it with leaf patterns that counterfeiters could not copy.
> **When and where:** Pennsylvania, from 1729.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about his whole life, not only his work on money.

Benjamin Franklin printed paper money for three colonies and made it hard to fake. In 1729, at the age of 23, he published a pamphlet, a short printed booklet, called *A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of a Paper-Currency*. In it he argued that Pennsylvania needed more paper money. The pamphlet helped his print shop win the contract to print Pennsylvania's bills. His firm printed money for Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware.

A counterfeiter is a person who makes fake money. To stop counterfeiters, Franklin printed the shapes of real leaves on the bills. Every leaf has its own pattern of veins, and a counterfeiter could not carve that pattern into a printing plate by hand. The method is called nature printing. Franklin most likely worked it out from leaf prints he made with his friend Joseph Breintnall. Researchers think the printing plates were cast in metal from plaster molds of real leaves.

In 2023 physicists at the University of Notre Dame studied 600 notes printed by Franklin's firm. They found the leaf prints. They also found tiny flakes of the mineral mica in the paper, and colored fibers mixed into it. Printers kept putting leaf prints like Franklin's on colonial money into the Revolution.

Colonial bills carried the words "To Counterfeit is Death." Counterfeiting was a crime punished by death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper money lent against land and people" -->
In colonial South Carolina, enslavers used the people they held as security to borrow money. The colony had no banks, and its paper money lost most of its value in its first 25 years.

South Carolina's colonial government began printing paper money in 1703. At first, 1.5 South Carolina pounds were worth one English pound. By the late 1720s it took about 7 South Carolina pounds to equal one English pound.

With no banks, people borrowed through private agreements called mortgages and bonds. A mortgage is a loan in which the borrower pledges property as security. If the borrower does not pay, the lender can take that property. A mortgage could be on land. It could also be on movable property, called chattel. The law recorded enslaved people as chattel.

Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library described in 2024 how these loans worked. He wrote: "A slave owner could mortgage their human chattel to a slave broker to secure credit used to purchase more slaves, then use their collective labor to satisfy the debt." A slave broker was a dealer in enslaved people. The enslaver pledged the people he held to get a loan. He used the loan to buy more people. Then he paid off the debt with what the people's forced work earned.

Hundreds of colonial land mortgages survive in the Charleston County Register of Deeds. Thousands of early chattel mortgages survive in the South Carolina Department of Archives and History.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Members of the British Parliament banned new paper money as legal tender in New England in 1751 and in all thirteen colonies in 1764. Colonists added the ban to their complaints against Britain. During the Revolution, members of the Continental Congress paid for the war with paper money, and by 1781 that paper was close to worthless. After the war, American leaders built a money system on purpose. They chose a dollar divided into tenths and hundredths in 1785, chartered a national bank in 1791 and created a mint in 1792.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Currency Acts" -->
Parliament is the lawmaking body of Britain. In the Currency Act of 1751, its members barred Massachusetts Bay, Rhode Island, Connecticut and New Hampshire from issuing new paper bills as legal tender. They passed the act because New England's paper money had lost so much of its value.

In the Currency Act of April 19, 1764, members of Parliament extended the ban to all thirteen colonies. The colonies were already short of coins, and the ban left colonists with even less money to pay debts and buy goods. Colonists listed the Currency Acts among their complaints against Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Not worth a Continental" -->
The Continental Congress was the body of delegates from the colonies that led the Revolution. Its members had no power to tax. From June 1775 its members paid for the war by printing paper money, called Continental currency. In all, they issued $241,552,780 in Continental bills. The British printed fake Continental bills on purpose, so that the real bills would lose their value faster.

In Philadelphia, one silver dollar was worth about 4 paper dollars at the end of 1777. At the end of 1778 it was worth about 8. By 1781 a silver dollar was worth about 100 paper dollars, and people stopped using the paper. In North Carolina the rate reached 210 paper dollars to one silver dollar.

The collapse wiped out about $226 million in Continental bills, counted by the amounts printed on them. Counted in silver, the loss was about $40 million. It fell on whoever was holding the bills as they lost value, and most of them were ordinary people. Americans began to call anything worthless "not worth a Continental." By the late 1800s the saying had spread to Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiting was a hanging crime" -->
Owen Sullivan, whose name was also spelled Syllavan, was hanged in New York in 1756 for faking that colony's paper money. He was put to death under the warning printed on colonial bills: "To Counterfeit is Death."

Sullivan was born in Ireland in about 1720. He was an engraver, a man who cuts designs into metal plates for printing. He had also worked as an armorer, someone who makes and repairs weapons, for colonial soldiers. He ran gangs of counterfeiters who passed fake colonial bills from New England to New York.

Before that, Sullivan had been punished in Rhode Island for faking that colony's bills. The *Boston Evening Post* reported the punishment on October 9, 1752. It said he had "lately had both his Ears crop'd, and been branded on both his Cheeks with the Letter C." Cropping meant cutting off part or all of each ear. Branding meant burning the letter C, for counterfeiter, into the skin of each cheek with a hot iron. The marks were meant to show anyone who saw him what he had been convicted of. The newspaper did not name the officials who carried out the punishment.

Sullivan went on counterfeiting. Judges of the Supreme Court for the City and County of New York found him guilty of faking and passing New York's bills of credit from 1737. He was hanged on Monday, May 10, 1756. A pamphlet about his life, which says it was "taken from his own mouth," was printed the same year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the dollar" -->
In 1784 Thomas Jefferson wrote notes on what the new nation's money should be. He proposed the Spanish dollar as the basic unit. He wanted it divided into tenths and hundredths. The English system of pounds, shillings and pence did not divide by tens.

On July 6, 1785, members of Congress voted "that the money unit of the United States of America shall be one dollar." They also voted that the coins should "increase in decimal ratio," which means counting up by tens. The United States was the first nation to adopt a coinage fully based on tens.

In the Coinage Act of April 2, 1792, members of Congress set up the United States Mint in Philadelphia. The Mint's building was the first federal building put up under the Constitution. The act defined the dollar as the unit of money. It also set the decimal system of dollars, dimes, cents and mills in law. A mill is one tenth of a cent.

The act listed coins in three metals. The gold coins included the eagle. The silver coins ran from the dollar down to the half-dime. The copper coins were the cent and the half-cent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-money" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** The first Secretary of the Treasury. He planned the national bank and the United States Mint.
> **When and where:** Secretary of the Treasury, 1789 to 1795. Lived 1757 to 1804.
> **Movie:** *Alexander Hamilton* (2007), a documentary in the PBS series *American Experience*.

Alexander Hamilton planned the national bank and the Mint in reports he wrote for Congress. In 1789 he was appointed the first Secretary of the Treasury, the head of the government department in charge of money. He served until 1795.

In December 1790 Hamilton sent Congress his *Report on a National Bank*. He proposed a bank that would hold the government's money and issue banknotes that people could trust. A banknote is paper money printed by a bank.

Members of Congress passed a bill to charter the First Bank of the United States, in Philadelphia, for twenty years. A charter is a legal paper that allows a company to exist and states what it may do. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison argued that the Constitution did not give Congress the power to create a bank. In the House, Madison asked whether that power was "among the powers vested by the Constitution in the Legislature." President George Washington asked Hamilton to answer the objections, so that he would have "the arguments for and against the measure." Then Washington signed the bill, on February 25, 1791. In 1811 members of Congress voted down a new charter by one vote in each house.

In January 1791 Hamilton sent Congress his *Report on the Establishment of a Mint*. In it he defined the dollar in both silver and gold, with 15 ounces of silver worth 1 ounce of gold. Members of Congress created the Mint the next year.
