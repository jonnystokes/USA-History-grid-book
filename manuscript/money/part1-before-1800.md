## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 17 of 37 · Money · 10 eras · about 70 minutes**

**Most American money since 1690 has been a written promise to pay, and when the people who made the promise could not keep it, the people holding the money took the loss.**

Soldiers from Massachusetts came home from a failed attack on Quebec in 1690, and the colony's treasury held too few coins to pay them. In December the colony's lawmakers began voting to pay them in paper. Each bill stated that the treasurer would accept it "in all Public payments" and that it could later be traded for coins. No government in Europe or its colonies had printed paper money before. People took the bills and passed them from hand to hand.

This chapter follows money from the shells and copper traded before 1500 to bitcoin. As you read, watch what stood behind a dollar, from Spanish silver to nothing but the law. Watch, too, who took the loss when a promise to pay was broken: whoever held Continental bills in 1781, savers at the banks that failed from 1837 to 1933, and about 31,000 depositors of the Freedman's Bank who never got any money back. In Louisiana in 1837, enslaved people pledged for loans were sold to pay the debts.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- What has stood behind the dollar, and what stands behind it now?
- Why did people rush to pull their money out of banks, and who lost when a bank failed?
- How did enslavers and bankers use enslaved people to raise money?

**Five minutes?** Read "1930 to 1933: the runs, the holiday and deposit insurance" in 1900 to 1950. The Lydia Lobsiger story follows it.
**Can skip:** "The ATM", "War bonds", "Payday loans". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 1 minute**

Wampum, beads made from shell, later passed as money between colonists and Native traders. Before 1500, though, nobody in this land used money of any kind: there were no coins, paper bills, banks or prices. People got what they needed by barter, trading one thing straight for another, and by gifts, and Chapter 16 tells how they carried shells, copper and obsidian, a volcanic glass, hundreds of miles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Was wampum money?" -->
Wampum was not money before Europeans came. People used it to keep records, to make agreements between nations, in ceremonies and as gifts.

The white beads came from the shells of whelks, large sea snails. The purple-black beads came from the shells of quahogs, hard-shelled clams of the Atlantic coast. People strung the beads on cords or wove them into belts.

Among the Haudenosaunee, a league of Native nations in what is now New York State, the beads of a belt recorded spoken agreements and histories. People presented the belts at meetings of leaders, called councils, and read them there.

Wampum first passed as money in the 1600s, when colonists and Native traders used it in their dealings with each other.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

The American dollar was later based on a Spanish silver coin, and Spanish officials forced Native men to dig the silver for it. The mines lay far to the south, in lands the king of Spain ruled. Workers at Spanish mints, workshops where coins are made, struck the silver into a coin called the piece of eight. Almost none of those coins reached the land that became the United States in the 1500s. This land still had no mint of its own.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The piece of eight" -->
The piece of eight was a silver coin worth 8 of the Spanish money units called reales. In English it was also called the Spanish dollar. Workers at Spanish mints in the Americas made it from silver dug at Potosí, in what is now Bolivia, and at Zacatecas, in Mexico. Silver was found at Potosí in 1545.

Native men of the Andes, the high mountains of South America, did the digging at Potosí. Francisco de Toledo, the viceroy who governed Peru for the king of Spain, began forcing thousands of them to work in the mines in 1573. They worked in turns, under a system called the mita. Toledo took the name and the idea of turns of work from an older system used by the Inca.

Toledo's rules applied to Native men aged 18 to 50 in the highland provinces between Potosí and Cuzco. Each year about one man in seven from those villages had to go to the mines, more than 11,000 men a year.

The men worked in shifts, one week in the mines and then two weeks of rest. Many brought their families and carried most of their own food. They were paid a little, and most of that pay went to the tax they owed the colonial government. The work was dangerous and unhealthy. By sending the men away from their home villages, Toledo broke up life there too.

Across the Andes, disease killed far more Native people than work in the mines did. No reliable count shows how many of the forced workers died from the work at Potosí.

Traders carried the piece of eight from South America to Europe, and across the Pacific Ocean to Asia. Writers on the history of money, among them Alejandra Irigoin and Bridget Millmore, call it the first money used around the whole world.

Almost none of that silver reached this land. Some did reach St. Augustine, the Spanish military post in Florida that Chapter 16 describes. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés had founded it in September 1565 at Seloy, a Timucua village. Its soldiers were paid from Spanish colonial funds, in the coin of a foreign empire.

Later, Thomas Jefferson and members of Congress based the United States dollar on the piece of eight. Until February 21, 1857, Spanish coins were legal tender in the United States: the law said people had to accept them to pay a debt. The piece of eight was divided into eight parts called bits, so two bits made a quarter of a dollar. Americans still call a quarter "two bits."

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 4 minutes**

English colonists never had enough coins, so they paid each other in goods and then began to make money of their own. Little English coin reached the colonies. Colonists spent what they did get on goods shipped in from overseas, and those coins went back across the ocean. To trade with each other, they paid in tobacco, corn, beaver skins and wampum. Massachusetts leaders opened a mint without England's permission. In 1690 they began printing paper money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money made of goods" -->
Colonists called payment in goods "country pay." Colonial lawmakers set how much each kind of goods was worth when it was used to pay a debt. Money of this kind is called commodity money, because the money is a useful thing in itself.

English money was counted in pence, shillings and pounds. Twelve pence made a shilling, and twenty shillings made a pound.

Virginia lawmakers set a value on tobacco so it could be used as money from the 1620s, and in 1642 they made it legal tender. People in Virginia, Maryland and North Carolina paid with tobacco through much of the colonial period. In the northern colonies, beaver pelts and corn passed as money too.

New England colonists used wampum for small payments, though the nations that made it had not used it as money before colonists came. Members of the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmakers, accepted wampum for small sums from 1637, made it legal tender for small debts in 1650 and canceled that law in 1661.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pine-tree shilling, 1652" -->
Members of the Massachusetts General Court voted in 1652 to set up a mint in Boston. They did not ask anyone in England first. By English law, only the king could make coins. In 1652 England had no king.

**Why England had no king.** Supporters of the king had fought a civil war against supporters of Parliament, England's lawmakers. Afterward the judges of a court set up by Parliament found King Charles I guilty of making war on Parliament and on the people of England. Their sentence called him a "traitor" and a "murderer." Fifty-nine of the judges, Oliver Cromwell among them, signed the order for his death. On that order the king was beheaded in London on January 30, 1649.

Workers at the Boston mint struck silver coins worth threepence, sixpence and a shilling. One was the pine-tree shilling, named for the tree stamped on it. They struck pine-tree shillings from about 1667 to 1682, but nearly all of the colony's silver coins carry the date 1652.

**A date as a cover.** Coin experts who write in the magazine CoinWeek and in older books on early American coins explain the 1652 date this way. Charles II became king of England in 1660. A coin dated 1652 could pass as a leftover from the years without a king. A coin with a later date would show that the men of Massachusetts were still making coins without the king's permission.

The mint closed in 1682. In 1684 officials acting for King Charles II canceled the Massachusetts charter, the legal paper that gave the colony the right to exist and stated what it could do. The illegal mint was one of their complaints against the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-hull" name="John Hull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Hull

> **Who:** A Boston silversmith who ran the colony's mint against English law and struck the pine-tree shilling.
> **When and where:** Boston, Massachusetts. Mintmaster from 1652 until the mint closed in 1682. Lived 1624 to 1683.

**He kept a share of every batch of coins he struck.**

John Hull ran the Massachusetts mint from 1652 until it closed in 1682, without permission from England. He was a blacksmith's son from Market Harborough, England, and came to Boston with his parents in 1635, when he was 11. He trained as a silversmith, a craftsman who makes things out of silver.

Members of the Massachusetts General Court named him mintmaster, the man in charge of the mint, in 1652, when he was 27. His appointment was dated May 27. Robert Sanderson worked with him as his partner. Under his contract he was paid in coins from every batch, and that pay made him one of the richest men in New England. He later became the colony's treasurer, the official in charge of its money.

A story says Hull gave his daughter Hannah her weight in pine-tree shillings as her dowry, the money or goods a bride's family gives when she marries. The story is a legend. The writer Nathaniel Hawthorne retold it in his book *Grandfather's Chair* and added details of his own.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-hull" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first government paper money, 1690" -->
The soldiers in the chapter's opening were paid in paper bills called bills of credit, and the first ones, voted from December 1690, totaled 40,000 pounds. The soldiers had come home from a failed attack on the French city of Quebec, in Canada, during King William's War. According to accounts from the time, the unpaid troops were close to mutiny, an uprising against their officers.

These bills were the first paper money printed by any government in Europe or the lands Europeans had settled. China had used paper money centuries earlier.

**The paper spreads.** By 1714 Massachusetts lawmakers had put out 240,000 pounds in paper money. By 1718 lawmakers in the other New England colonies were printing their own.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 4 minutes**

Lawmakers in colony after colony printed paper money in the early 1700s, until every colony had its own. The bills held their value only where the lawmakers printed limited amounts.

Benjamin Franklin argued for paper money in Pennsylvania and then printed it. In South Carolina, which had no private banks, enslavers borrowed money by pledging land and the people they enslaved. If a loan was not paid back, the lender could take the land or the enslaved people who had been pledged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paper that held its value and paper that lost it" -->
Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, who study how money and trade work, compared the paper money of the colonies. In Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey, lawmakers printed limited amounts, and the bills held their value. In New England and the Carolinas, lawmakers printed large amounts. Prices counted in paper money rose there, and each bill was worth less silver than before.

**South Carolina's paper.** Lawmakers there began printing paper money in 1703, when 1.5 South Carolina pounds were worth one English pound. By the late 1720s it took about 7.

In Virginia, people began to pass paper receipts for tobacco as money. Virginia lawmakers set up public warehouses for tobacco, with government inspectors, in the 1720s and 1730s. A planter, a farmer who grew tobacco to sell, brought his tobacco to a warehouse. An inspector checked it, and it stayed in the warehouse. The inspector then wrote a receipt, called a tobacco note, for the inspected tobacco. People passed tobacco notes from hand to hand as money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-money" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Pennsylvania printer who argued for paper money, then printed it with leaf patterns that were almost impossible for counterfeiters, people who make fake money, to copy.
> **When and where:** Pennsylvania, from 1729.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about his whole life, not only his work on money.

**He printed the veins of real leaves on paper money.**

Benjamin Franklin printed paper money for three colonies and made it hard to fake. In 1729, when he was 23, he published a pamphlet, a short printed booklet, arguing that Pennsylvania needed more paper money. Its title was *A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of a Paper-Currency*, a modest look at what paper money is and why it is needed.

Partly because of the pamphlet, Franklin won the contract to print Pennsylvania's bills in his shop. His firm printed money for Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware.

To stop counterfeiters, Franklin printed the shapes of real leaves on the bills. Every leaf has its own pattern of veins. A counterfeiter could almost never carve that pattern into a printing plate by hand. The method is called nature printing. Franklin almost certainly worked it out from leaf prints he made with his friend Joseph Breintnall. The printing plates were probably cast in metal from plaster molds of real leaves.

Scientists at the University of Notre Dame looked closely at 600 notes printed by Franklin's firm. In their 2023 study they found the leaf prints, tiny shiny flakes of a mineral called mica in the paper, and colored fibers mixed into it. Printers kept putting leaf prints like Franklin's on colonial money into the Revolution.

Colonial bills carried the words "To Counterfeit is Death." Under colonial laws, judges could sentence a counterfeiter to death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Loans against land and enslaved people" -->
South Carolina's lawmakers lent some of the colony's paper money out on land. In 1712 they set up a public land bank, through which officials lent paper money to people who pledged their land as security, something a borrower promises to hand over if a loan is not paid back.

People also borrowed from one another through private agreements. One kind was a bond, a written promise to pay back a loan. Another was a mortgage, a loan in which the borrower pledges property as security, so the lender can take that property if the borrower does not pay.

A mortgage could be on land, or on movable property, called chattel. In these loan papers, enslavers and lenders listed enslaved people as chattel. So when an enslaver did not pay, the lender could take the people he had pledged.

Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library described in 2024 how these loans worked, including loans from slave brokers, dealers in enslaved people. "A slave owner could mortgage their human chattel to a slave broker to secure credit used to purchase more slaves," he wrote. The enslaver pledged the people he enslaved to get a loan and used the loan to buy more people. Then he paid off the debt with what the people's forced work earned.

Hundreds of colonial land mortgages survive in the Charleston County Register of Deeds, the county office that keeps records of land. Thousands of early chattel mortgages survive in the South Carolina Department of Archives and History.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 6 minutes**

Paper money was one of the colonists' quarrels with Britain, and then it was how they paid for the Revolution. Members of the British Parliament banned new paper money as legal tender in New England in 1751 and in all thirteen colonies in 1764. Colonists added the ban to their complaints against Britain. During the war, members of the Continental Congress paid with paper bills that were nearly worthless by 1781.

After the war, Thomas Jefferson, Alexander Hamilton and members of Congress planned a new money system. Members of Congress chose a dollar divided into tenths and hundredths in 1785, set up a national bank in 1791 and created a mint in 1792.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Currency Acts" -->
Members of Parliament, Britain's lawmakers, passed the first Currency Act in 1751 because New England's paper money had lost so much of its value. Under the act, Massachusetts Bay, Rhode Island, Connecticut and New Hampshire could no longer print new paper bills as legal tender.

A second Currency Act followed thirteen years later. On April 19, 1764, members of Parliament extended the ban to all thirteen colonies. Colonists were already short of coins. Without new legal-tender paper, they had even less money to pay debts and buy goods. Colonists listed the Currency Acts among their complaints against Britain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Not worth a Continental" -->
People working for the British side printed fake Continental bills on purpose, so that the real bills would lose their value faster. Continental bills were the paper money that members of the Continental Congress, the men from the thirteen colonies who led the Revolution, printed from June 1775 to pay for the war. In all, they put out $241,552,780 in Continental bills, and Chapter 16 tells how fast the bills lost their value.

**Made in New York.** Most of the British faking was done in New York City, which the British army held for most of the war. Someone placed an advertisement in the *New York Gazette* of April 14, 1777, asking people going into the other colonies to take fake bills with them.

**The general's letter.** General Henry Clinton, the British commander, wrote about the plan in January 1780. His letter went to Lord George Germain, the official in London in charge of the American colonies. Stuart Hatfield, writing about the British faking in the *Journal of the American Revolution* in 2015, calls that letter the clearest sign that British leaders approved the plan.

People had stopped using the paper by 1781. In North Carolina it took 210 paper dollars to buy what one silver dollar bought.

Continental bills with about $226 million printed on them ended up worthless. Counted in silver, the loss was about $40 million. Whoever was holding the bills as they lost value lost that money, and most of them were ordinary people. The saying "not worth a Continental" comes from these bills, and by the late 1800s people in Britain were using it too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiting was a hanging crime" -->
Officials in New York hanged Owen Sullivan, whose name was also spelled Syllavan, in 1756 for faking that colony's paper money. Colonial bills were printed with the warning "To Counterfeit is Death," and New York officials carried it out.

Sullivan was born in Ireland in about 1720. As an engraver, he cut designs into metal plates for printing, and as an armorer he had made and repaired weapons for colonial soldiers. He ran gangs of counterfeiters who passed fake colonial bills from New England to New York.

Before that, officials in Rhode Island had punished Sullivan for faking that colony's bills. On October 9, 1752, the *Boston Evening Post* printed a report from Providence, Rhode Island. The report states that he had "lately had both his Ears crop'd, and been branded on both his Cheeks with the Letter C."

Cropping meant cutting off part or all of each ear. Branding meant burning the letter C, for counterfeiter, into the skin of each cheek with a hot iron. The C marked him as a counterfeiter to anyone who saw his face. The report does not name the officials who carried out the punishment.

Sullivan went on counterfeiting. At a session of the Supreme Court for the City and County of New York, he was found guilty of faking and passing New York's bills of credit from 1737. He was hanged on Monday, May 10, 1756. A pamphlet about his life, which says it was "taken from his own mouth," was printed the same year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why a dollar has a hundred cents" -->
A dollar has a hundred cents because the men who planned American money chose to count it by tens. In notes he wrote in 1784, Thomas Jefferson proposed the Spanish dollar as the basic unit, divided into tenths and hundredths. The English system of pounds, shillings and pence did not divide by tens.

Members of Congress voted on July 6, 1785, "that the money unit of the United States of America shall be one dollar." They also voted that the coins should "increase in decimal ratio," counting up by tens. With that vote, the United States became the first nation whose coins were fully based on tens.

Under the Coinage Act of April 2, 1792, members of Congress set up the United States Mint in Philadelphia. The Mint's building was the first building the national government put up under the Constitution. The act defined the dollar as the unit of money. Under it, the decimal system of dollars, dimes, cents and mills became law (a mill is one tenth of a cent).

The act listed coins in three metals: gold coins, including a ten-dollar coin called the eagle, silver coins from the dollar down to the half-dime, and copper coins, the cent and the half-cent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-money" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** The first Secretary of the Treasury, who planned the national bank and the United States Mint.
> **When and where:** Secretary of the Treasury, 1789 to 1795. Lived 1757 to 1804.
> **Movie:** *Alexander Hamilton* (2007), a documentary in the PBS series *American Experience*.

**Jefferson and Madison said the Constitution did not allow his bank.**

Alexander Hamilton planned the national bank and the Mint in reports he wrote for Congress. President George Washington chose him on September 11, 1789, to be the first Secretary of the Treasury, the head of the government department in charge of money.

Hamilton sent Congress his *Report on a National Bank* in December 1790. He proposed a bank that would hold the government's money and put out banknotes, paper money printed by a bank, that people could trust.

Lawmakers in Congress then passed a bill to charter the First Bank of the United States, in Philadelphia, for twenty years. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison argued that the Constitution did not allow Congress to create a bank. In the House, Madison asked whether the power to start a bank was "among the powers" listed for Congress in the Constitution.

President Washington asked Hamilton to answer the objections, so that he would have "the arguments for and against the measure." Then Washington signed the bill, on February 25, 1791. Twenty years later, in 1811, a new charter lost by one vote in each house of Congress.

Hamilton had already sent Congress his *Report on the Establishment of a Mint*, in January 1791. In it he defined the dollar in both silver and gold, with 15 ounces of silver worth 1 ounce of gold. Members of Congress built the Coinage Act of 1792 on his plan and set up the Mint under it.
