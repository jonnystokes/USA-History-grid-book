## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, members of Congress and two presidents set up the two protections against bank runs that the country still uses: the Federal Reserve and deposit insurance. In the Gold Standard Act of March 14, 1900, members of Congress made gold the only metal behind the dollar, at $20.67 an ounce. That ended silver's place as a standard in law. It gave depositors no protection when their bank failed. After the Panic of 1907, members of Congress created the Federal Reserve in 1913. After thousands of banks failed in the early 1930s, they created deposit insurance. In 1933 President Franklin Roosevelt also ordered Americans to turn in their gold coins. By 1945 a dollar in an American's hand was paper that could not be traded for gold.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1907" -->
In October 1907 depositors ran on New York's trust companies, and a private banker, not a government official, organized the rescue. A trust company was a kind of bank that took deposits and managed people's money.

The trouble began with a failed attempt to corner the stock of United Copper. To corner a stock means to buy up so much of it that the buyers can set its price. The attempt failed and ruined the banks of the men behind it. Depositors then feared for every bank tied to those men.

On October 22, 1907, so many depositors demanded their money from the Knickerbocker Trust Company that it suspended, meaning it stopped paying out money. It was one of the largest trust companies in New York. Depositors then ran on the Trust Company of America and others. Depositors stood in lines around the block, and some hired people to hold their place in line. Charles T. Barney, the Knickerbocker's former president, shot himself on November 14, 1907.

The United States had no central bank then. A central bank is a bank that the government sets up to hold other banks' reserves and to lend to them in a crisis.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan-money" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan

> **Who:** A New York banker who ran the rescue in the Panic of 1907 from his own library.
> **When and where:** New York City, October and November 1907.

J.P. Morgan was 70 years old when he took charge of stopping the Panic of 1907. He worked from his private library at 36th Street and Madison Avenue in New York City.

Teams of examiners working for Morgan went through the books of the trust companies. They decided which ones were sound enough to save. Treasury officials deposited $25 million of the government's money with Morgan's group of banks. Morgan also got the presidents of banks and trust companies to pledge money to shared rescue funds.

On the night of November 2 to 3, 1907, Morgan gathered the presidents of the trust companies in his library and had the doors locked. The standard accounts say he kept the key in his pocket. He kept the presidents in the room until they agreed to put up $25 million to save the weakest trusts. They signed the deal near 5 in the morning. Memoirs differ on small details, such as who held the key and the exact hour. The Morgan Library & Museum's own exhibition describes the locked-door meeting.

After the panic, members of Congress saw that the country's money system had depended on one 70-year-old private banker. In 1908 they passed the Aldrich-Vreeland Act, which set up the National Monetary Commission to design something better.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve, 1913" -->
The Federal Reserve, the central bank of the United States, began with a secret meeting in November 1910. Six men traveled to the Jekyll Island Club, on an island off Georgia. They told people they were going duck hunting. They were Senator Nelson Aldrich, Treasury official A. Piatt Andrew, the bankers Henry Davison, Frank Vanderlip and Paul Warburg, and Aldrich's secretary, Arthur Shelton. On the island they wrote a plan for a central bank.

The men who were there did not admit to the meeting in public until the 1930s. The Federal Reserve's own published history describes the trip. Members of Congress voted down their plan, called the Aldrich Plan. Three years later they used much of its design in the law they passed.

President Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913. Under the act, twelve regional Reserve Banks were set up, run under a board in Washington. The Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914.

The Federal Reserve is a bank for banks. It holds their reserves, which are the money a bank keeps on hand instead of lending it out, and it moves money between them. Its officers can lend to a sound bank that is facing a run, so the bank can pay its depositors and stay open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black-owned banks, and Greenwood's losses" -->
Many banks in the early 1900s would not serve Black customers, so Black Americans started their own banks. Black depositors and business owners later lost money when banks failed and when a white mob burned Tulsa's Black district in 1921.

Maggie Lena Walker opened the St. Luke Penny Savings Bank in Richmond, Virginia, in November 1903, and became its first president. She was the first Black woman to found a bank in the United States. On the first day, people deposited more than $8,000, in amounts from 31 cents to over $100. During the Great Depression of the 1930s, Walker merged it with other Black-owned banks into the Consolidated Bank and Trust Company. That bank lasted into the 2000s as the longest continuously run Black-owned bank in the country. Walker lived from 1864 to 1934.

Jesse Binga opened a private bank in Chicago in 1908, mainly for Black customers, because many banks turned them away. In 1921 he opened the Binga State Bank. Binga lived at 5922 South Park Avenue, in a white neighborhood. His home and his offices were bombed at least five times. The records do not say who set the bombs.

In 1930 Illinois state officials closed the Binga State Bank when it could not meet a requirement to hold $400,000 in cash. Accounts date the closing to June or to July 31, 1930. Binga lost his own fortune. Many depositors lost their savings, and the records do not count them. In 1933 a jury convicted Binga of embezzlement, which means taking money a person was trusted to look after. He was sentenced to ten years. Governor Dwight H. Green of Illinois pardoned him in 1941.

On May 31 and June 1, 1921, a white mob attacked Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, where about 10,000 people lived. The mob included men whom the Tulsa police had deputized, which means sworn in as temporary officers. National Guard units took part in the attack. The attackers destroyed more than 1,000 homes and businesses. Historians put the number of people killed at between 50 and 300. The count is uncertain because many of the dead were never identified and lie in unmarked graves. Most Black Tulsans lost their homes, and thousands lived in tents through the winter of 1921 to 1922. No white person went to prison for the murders or the burning. An all-white grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether to charge people with crimes, blamed Black Tulsans.

Greenwood's residents filed more than $1.8 million in damage claims with the city of Tulsa between June 1921 and June 1922. City officials denied all but one. The one claim they paid went to a white shop owner, for guns taken from his store. Officers of the insurance companies refused to pay for the burned property. They called the attack a "riot," and their policies did not cover riot damage. No rebuilding money came from the city, the state or the federal government.

Greenwood's residents rebuilt the district. In 1925 it hosted the national meeting of the National Negro Business League. By the 1940s Greenwood had 242 Black-owned businesses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1930 to 1933: the runs, the holiday and deposit insurance" -->
From 1930 to 1933 more than 9,000 American banks failed, and their depositors lost about $1.34 billion. No one insured those savings, so the losses fell on the depositors.

Banks had failed at a rate of more than 600 a year even in the 1920s, mostly small banks in farm country. The count grew after 1929. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation later counted 1,350 banks that suspended in 1930, 2,293 in 1931, 1,453 in 1932 and 4,000 in 1933. That makes 9,096 in all. The Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library puts the number at about 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks. By one estimate, about nine million savings accounts had been lost by March 1933.

On December 11, 1930, the Bank of United States in New York City failed. It was the largest bank failure in the country up to then. It had about 400,000 depositors. Many were immigrants who worked in the clothing factories, and small savers in the Bronx and Brooklyn.

Federal Reserve officials did not stop these runs. A history published by the FDIC in 1998 says Federal Reserve officials "did little to ease the liquidity problems of banks." Liquidity means having cash on hand to pay depositors. Most of the banks that failed in 1930 were not members of the Federal Reserve, and its officials felt no responsibility for them.

On February 14, 1933, the governor of Michigan closed every bank in the state for eight days, and 900,000 depositors could not reach their money. Runs then spread from state to state.

Franklin Roosevelt became president in March 1933. On March 6, two days after he took office, he closed every bank in the country. This was called the bank holiday. On March 9 members of Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act. On the night of Sunday, March 12, Roosevelt gave the first of his radio talks called fireside chats. About 60 million people listened. He explained how banks work in plain words, and he said money was safer in a reopened bank than under the mattress. Banks reopened from March 13 to 15, after federal officials reviewed them. When they opened, people put in more money than they took out.

On April 5, 1933, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 6102, a written order from the president. It required Americans to turn in their gold coins, gold bars and gold certificates to the government, at $20.67 an ounce. Small keepsakes and collections were exempt. In the Gold Reserve Act of January 30, 1934, members of Congress moved all the country's monetary gold to the Treasury. They set a new price of $35 an ounce. From then on, Americans could not trade their dollars for gold. Foreign governments still could.

In the Banking Act of June 16, 1933, members of Congress created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the FDIC. Its insurance began on January 1, 1934, and covered each depositor up to $2,500. In July 1934 the limit rose to $5,000. If an insured bank failed, its depositors would be paid back from the insurance fund, up to the limit. With that promise, depositors no longer had a reason to rush to the bank to get their money out first.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lydia-lobsiger" name="Lydia Lobsiger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lydia Lobsiger

> **Who:** The first depositor in the United States to be paid back by federal deposit insurance.
> **When and where:** East Peoria, Illinois, 1934. Her grave record gives her life as 1870 to 1956.

Lydia Lobsiger received the first federal deposit insurance check ever paid. It was for $1,250 and dated July 3, 1934.

Her bank was the Fond du Lac State Bank of East Peoria, Illinois. It failed in the spring of 1934. It was the first bank with FDIC insurance to fail. The insurance had begun only a few months earlier, on January 1, 1934.

Newspapers across the country reported what happened. A bank had failed, and a depositor with a small account had not lost her savings. From 1930 to 1933, before the insurance began, depositors in failed banks had lost about $1.34 billion.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lydia-lobsiger" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War bonds" -->
During World War II, about 85 million Americans, roughly half the country, lent money to the government by buying war bonds. Together they paid in about $185.7 billion.

One kind was the Series E bond. A buyer paid $18.75 for it and got back $25 ten years later. Children bought war stamps for 10 cents or 25 cents each and saved them toward a bond.

From 1942 to 1945, officials of the Treasury and the Office of Education ran a program called Schools at War. Students bought stamps and bonds toward goals their schools set. About 200,000 schools and 30 million students took part. They raised more than $2 billion. Their money paid the price of at least 90,000 jeeps and several thousand airplanes for the armed forces. Eleanor Roosevelt spoke in support of the program on the radio on September 25, 1942.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The dollar lost its last link to gold in 1971, and in the same half century Americans began to pay with plastic cards and take cash from machines. On August 15, 1971, President Nixon ended the right of foreign governments to trade dollars for gold. Since then no metal has stood behind the dollar. Prices rose fast for about fifteen years after the mid-1960s, and a dollar saved in 1965 bought about a third as much in 1982. Paul Volcker, head of the Federal Reserve, stopped that rise in the early 1980s by pushing interest rates past 20 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic: the charge card and the credit card" -->
The first card that people could use to pay at many different businesses was the Diners Club card, in 1950. Frank McNamara started the company with Ralph Schneider and other partners. The first cards were made of cardboard. At first a couple dozen restaurants in New York took them. The first use was at Major's Cabin Grill in February 1950.

The Diners Club card was a charge card. The holder had to pay the whole bill each month. A story often told says that McNamara had the idea in 1949 after he forgot his wallet at dinner. Matty Simmons, the company's first publicity man, was there. He said later that the story was made up for publicity. According to Simmons, McNamara simply had the idea and worked on it.

In September 1958, officers of Bank of America mailed about 60,000 ready-to-use cards, called BankAmericards, to people in Fresno, California. None of those people had asked for a card. The BankAmericard was a true credit card. The holder could pay part of the bill and owe the rest later. About 22 percent of the accounts fell behind on payments, against the 4 percent the bank's officers had planned for. There was fraud as well. The bank lost millions of dollars before the card caught on. Officers of Bank of America licensed the card to other banks across the country, and in 1976 it was renamed Visa. In 1970 members of Congress banned mailing credit cards to people who had not asked for them.

Before 1974 no federal law stopped a lender from turning down a woman for credit because she was a woman. A blog post by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a federal agency, states that "discrimination against women applying for credit was common." Mortgage lenders "often discounted a married woman's income, especially if she was of childbearing age." Lenders were more likely to turn down single women. In October 1974 members of Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. Under the act, it became illegal for lenders to treat people differently because of their sex or whether they were married. In March 1976 members of Congress added race, color, religion, national origin, age, and the receipt of public assistance, which is money from a government aid program.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ATM" -->
The first automated teller machine in the United States, a machine that pays out cash without a bank clerk, opened on September 2, 1969. It stood at a Chemical Bank branch in Rockville Centre, New York. It was called the Docuteller. It was built by Docutel, a company in Dallas, from an idea by Don Wetzel. The bank's advertisement said, "On September 2, our banks will open at 9:00 and never close again."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="August 15, 1971: the last tie to gold" -->
After 1934, Americans could not trade dollars for gold, but foreign governments still could, at $35 an ounce. This deal was part of an agreement among countries made after World War II, called the Bretton Woods system. By 1971 far more dollars were held outside the country than the United States had gold to pay for them. Foreign governments had begun a run on the country's gold.

President Nixon spent a weekend at Camp David with his advisers. They included Arthur Burns, the chairman of the Federal Reserve, John Connally, the Treasury Secretary, and Paul Volcker, a Treasury official. On Sunday night, August 15, 1971, Nixon announced that the United States would stop trading dollars for gold. He called the change temporary. No president has reversed it.

Since that night the dollar has been fiat money. Fiat money is money backed by no metal. It is worth what it can buy, and people accept it because it is legal tender by law and because they trust the government that issues it. The greenbacks of 1862 worked the same way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Inflation" -->
From about 1965 to 1982, prices in the United States rose fast year after year. Economists call this the Great Inflation. Inflation means prices going up, so that each dollar buys less.

In the early 1960s prices had risen 1 or 2 percent a year. Food prices rose more than 20 percent over the year to late 1973. Prices overall rose about 11 percent in 1974. That year President Ford handed out buttons that said WIN, for "Whip Inflation Now." Prices went on rising. At the peak, in 1979 and 1980, they rose more than 1 percent a month, and for all of 1980 they rose 13.5 percent.

A dollar saved in 1965 bought about a third as much in 1982, so savings put aside in 1965 had lost about two-thirds of their buying power. Borrowing became costly. In October 1981 the average rate on a 30-year home mortgage was 18.63 percent. That is the highest rate the Freddie Mac mortgage survey has ever recorded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bruce-bent-henry-brown" name="Bruce Bent and Henry Brown" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bruce Bent and Henry Brown

> **Who:** Two New York money managers who set up the first money market fund, a way for small savers to earn more than the legal limit on bank interest.
> **When and where:** New York, 1969 to 1973. Bent was born in 1937. Brown lived from 1926 to 2008.

Bruce Bent and Henry Brown set up a fund that let ordinary savers earn the higher interest that only large investors could get before.

Since 1933 a federal rule called Regulation Q had set the most interest a bank could pay on a savings account. Federal Reserve officials first issued it in September 1933. In July 1973 Federal Reserve officials raised the limit on an ordinary bank savings account from 4.5 percent to 5 percent. Most banks paid exactly the limit. In 1974 prices rose about 11 percent. Money in a savings account at 5 percent lost about 6 percent of its buying power that year.

Large investors could earn more by buying short-term debt from the government and from large companies, such as Treasury bills. Buying it directly took far more money than most families had. Bent and Brown met while working at TIAA-CREF, a pension company. In August 1969 Bent proposed a fund that would pool the money of many small savers and buy that debt for them.

They set up the Reserve Fund in 1970. A saver needed $1,000 to start, and each share cost $100. The fund opened to savers in 1971 or 1972, depending on the source. Bent and Brown had no sales staff. They phoned financial advisers and handed out brochures. On January 7, 1973, the *New York Times* ran an article about the fund. By the end of 1973 it held $100 million.

Other firms copied the idea. Federal Reserve History counts 1 money market fund in 1972, 36 in 1975, 90 in 1980 and 649 in 1990. The limits of Regulation Q were phased out by 1986 under a law members of Congress passed in 1980. The Reserve Fund later grew into the Reserve Primary Fund, which held about $62 billion in 2008.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bruce-bent-henry-brown" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="paul-volcker" name="Paul Volcker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Paul Volcker

> **Who:** The chairman of the Federal Reserve who brought down the Great Inflation with interest rates above 20 percent, at the cost of a recession with 10.8 percent unemployment.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1979 to 1983.

Paul Volcker stopped the Great Inflation by letting interest rates rise past 20 percent. He was six feet seven inches tall and known for smoking cheap cigars. President Jimmy Carter appointed him chairman of the Federal Reserve in August 1979. Prices were then rising more than 13 percent a year.

On Saturday night, October 6, 1979, Volcker announced a new policy. He and the other Federal Reserve officials would control how much money was in the economy. They would let interest rates rise as high as that took them. The federal funds rate, the rate banks charge one another for overnight loans, passed 20 percent in 1981.

In the recession of 1981 and 1982, unemployment reached 10.8 percent, the highest since the 1930s. A recession is a time when business shrinks and people lose jobs. Farmers in debt drove tractors to the Federal Reserve's building in protest. Home builders in debt mailed Volcker sawed-off pieces of two-by-four lumber.

Inflation fell from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.2 percent in 1983. It stayed low for about twenty years after that.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paul-volcker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The savings and loan crisis" -->
From 1989 to 1995, federal officials closed 747 failed savings and loans, and taxpayers paid as much as $124 billion of the cost. A savings and loan, also called a thrift, took in people's savings and made long-term home loans at fixed interest rates.

When interest rates climbed in the late 1970s and early 1980s, the thrifts lost money. The limits of Regulation Q kept them from paying savers the rates they could get elsewhere. The old fixed-rate home loans they held lost value. By 1983 closing the thrifts that could not pay their debts would have cost about $25 billion. Their insurance fund, the FSLIC, held only $6 billion. In the Garn-St Germain Act of 1982, members of Congress let thrifts make new kinds of loans beyond home mortgages, and many of those loans carried more risk.

In 1989 members of Congress passed a law that closed the FSLIC and moved thrift deposit insurance to the FDIC. Under the same law, members of Congress created the Resolution Trust Corporation to shut down failed thrifts. By the end of 1995 its officials had closed the 747 savings and loans, which together had more than $407 billion in assets. Savers with insured accounts were paid in full.

Banks failed in the same years. The FDIC counted 120 failed insured banks in 1985, 203 in 1987 and 280 in 1988. In 1984 federal officials rescued Continental Illinois National Bank, which had $45 billion in assets, instead of letting it fail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="money" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most American money today is numbers in bank computers, moved by card and by phone. In 2025 Americans used cash for only 14 percent of their payments, according to the Federal Reserve's yearly payment diary. Bank runs came back twice. In 2008 savers ran from money market funds, and in 2023 depositors ran from Silicon Valley Bank. Both times, federal officials promised to cover money that no insurance protected. Since 2009 some money, such as bitcoin, has been issued by no government and no bank.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008: the run comes back" -->
In September 2008 savers ran on money market funds, the kind of fund Bruce Bent and Henry Brown had started. Many people treated these funds like bank accounts. A share was meant to stay worth exactly $1. The funds had no deposit insurance.

On September 15, 2008, the Lehman Brothers firm failed. The next day, the Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck," which means its shares fell below $1, to 97 cents. The fund held $62.5 billion, and $785 million of it was in Lehman's debt. Investors pulled more than $40 billion out of the fund in two days. On September 17 alone they pulled about $144.5 billion out of money market funds across the country. These figures come from a study in the *Journal of Financial Crises*, published by Yale University.

On September 19, 2008, Treasury officials announced a guarantee for money market funds. President George W. Bush approved it, and Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson put up to $50 billion of a Treasury emergency fund behind it. The guarantee worked like deposit insurance, set up within a week, and the run stopped. Later, officials of the Securities and Exchange Commission charged Bruce Bent and his son with fraud over the Reserve Primary Fund's collapse. On November 12, 2012, a jury cleared them of the fraud charges.

Banks failed too. From 2008 through 2013, 489 insured banks failed, according to a history the FDIC published in 2017. Their failures cost the deposit insurance fund about $73 billion. IndyMac failed on July 11, 2008, with losses of about $12 billion, the most costly failure in the FDIC's history. Washington Mutual failed on September 25, 2008. It had $307 billion in assets, which made it the largest bank failure in the country's history. On October 3, 2008, members of Congress raised the deposit insurance limit from $100,000 to $250,000. In 2010 they made the $250,000 limit permanent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money issued by nobody" -->
Bitcoin is money that no government and no bank issues. It is a public record of who owns which coins, kept by many computers that check one another's work.

On October 31, 2008, a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a paper describing Bitcoin. On January 3, 2009, Nakamoto started the system by creating its first block of records. The code of that first block includes a headline from that day's *Times* of London: "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." No one has shown who Nakamoto is.

Some people hold bitcoin as an investment, hoping its price will rise. Some use it to pay for things. Economists disagree about whether it is money, an asset, or a bet on a price.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="laszlo-hanyecz" name="Laszlo Hanyecz" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Laszlo Hanyecz

> **Who:** A programmer who made the first recorded purchase with bitcoin: two pizzas for 10,000 coins.
> **When and where:** Florida, May 22, 2010.

On May 22, 2010, Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas. It was the first recorded time anyone traded bitcoin for a real thing.

Hanyecz was a programmer in Florida who had worked on Bitcoin's code. In May 2010 he posted an offer on the Bitcoin forum, a website where users wrote to one another. He would give 10,000 bitcoins to anyone who got two large pizzas delivered to him. A forum user named jercos took the deal and ordered the pizzas. He paid about $25 for them. At the time of the offer, the 10,000 bitcoins were worth about $41, according to the bitcoin.it wiki.

The same wiki estimates that at the high point of May 2025, those 10,000 coins would have been worth about $1.1 billion. In a 2019 interview, reported by the news site CoinDesk, Hanyecz said, "A trade happens because both parties think they're getting a good deal." May 22 is now marked each year as Bitcoin Pizza Day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="laszlo-hanyecz" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Payday loans" -->
A payday loan is a small loan meant to be paid back from the borrower's next paycheck. In a study released on April 24, 2013, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found that a typical payday loan cost the borrower an annual percentage rate of 391 percent. An annual percentage rate is the cost of a loan over a full year, written as a share of the amount borrowed.

Storefront payday lenders charged fees of $10 to $20 for every $100 borrowed, the study found. The 391 percent figure is for a $350 loan with the typical fee of $15 per $100. Nearly half of payday borrowers took out more than 10 loans in a year, and 14 percent took out 20 or more. The typical borrower was in debt to a payday lender 199 days of the year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Inflation returns, 2021 to 2026" -->
Prices rose fast again from 2021 to 2023, and Federal Reserve officials raised interest rates faster than at any time since Volcker. In the 12 months to June 2022, prices rose 9.1 percent, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That was the largest rise since November 1981. Energy prices rose 41.6 percent that year and food prices 10.4 percent. Federal Reserve officials raised their main interest rate from near zero in March 2022 to more than 5 percent in 2023. By the middle of 2023, prices were rising at close to 3 percent a year.

Economists still disagree about the causes. Some point to broken supply chains, the network of factories, ships and trucks that moves goods. Some point to federal spending on pandemic relief. Others point to higher energy prices. A report by the Congressional Research Service describes the argument.

In the 12 months to August 2026, prices rose 3.4 percent, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported. Energy prices rose 16.3 percent, and food prices 2.7 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="FTX and stablecoins" -->
FTX was a company where people bought, sold and kept cryptocurrency, digital money such as bitcoin. It went bankrupt in November 2022. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, had used customers' money to buy luxury real estate, to make investments and to give political donations, according to the *Washington Post*. In November 2023 a federal jury convicted him of fraud and of conspiracy to commit fraud and money laundering. On March 28, 2024, Judge Lewis Kaplan sentenced him to 25 years in prison and ordered him to give up more than $11 billion.

The people running the bankruptcy paid customers back what their accounts were worth on the day FTX filed for bankruptcy in November 2022. On that day one bitcoin sold for $16,080. By May 2024 it sold for close to $62,675, according to CBS News. Customers who had held bitcoin did not get back that later value. Another payment went out to customers on July 31, 2026. An FTX press release that month states that customers with ordinary claims had then received 105 percent of those claim amounts, and people with small claims 120 percent.

On July 18, 2025, President Trump signed the GENIUS Act, a law with rules for stablecoins. A stablecoin is a digital coin that its issuer promises to trade for a fixed amount, such as one dollar. Under the law, an issuer must hold at least one dollar in cash, bank deposits or safe government debt for every coin. The law states that stablecoins are not federally insured, according to a 2025 report by the Congressional Research Service. A stablecoin works like the state banknotes of the 1800s: a private promise to pay, backed by what the issuer holds, with no federal insurance behind it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2023: the run on Silicon Valley Bank" -->
In March 2023 federal officials protected every depositor at two failed banks, including money above the insurance limit. On March 9, 2023, depositors took $42 billion out of Silicon Valley Bank in California in a single day. About 88 percent of the bank's deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, so the owners of that money stood to lose it if the bank failed. On March 10, officials of California's Department of Financial Protection and Innovation closed the bank. On Sunday, March 12, officials of New York's Department of Financial Services closed Signature Bank. About 90 percent of its deposits were uninsured.

In 2025 cash was the third most used way to pay in the United States, after credit cards and debit cards. Federal Reserve researchers track this with the Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, in which people record the payments they make.

In 2016 cash was the most used way to pay, at about a third of all payments. In 2024 cash was used for 14 percent of payments, credit cards for 35 percent and debit cards for 30 percent. Adults aged 18 to 24 paid by phone 45 percent of the time that year. In 2025 people made about 47 payments a month, and about 6 of them were in cash, or 14 percent. It was the sixth year in a row that cash came third. People aged 55 and older paid cash about 10 times a month, against about twice for people aged 18 to 24. In the countryside the count was about 9 a month, and in cities and suburbs about 6. Ninety percent of people said they planned to keep using cash. The 2025 figures come from the diary's report released in May 2026.

In the 2024 budget year, each penny cost the U.S. Mint 3.69 cents to make and ship. The Mint's losses on pennies came to about $85 million that year. On February 9, 2025, President Trump said he had told the Treasury Secretary to stop making new pennies. News reports at the time said it was unclear whether he had that power, because members of Congress set the rules for coins. On November 12, 2025, workers at the Philadelphia Mint struck the last pennies made for everyday use. The first federal cents had been made 232 years earlier, in 1793. The expected savings were about $56 million a year. Pennies already made are still legal tender.

On September 14, 2026, members of the House of Representatives passed the Common Cents Act, and senators passed it on September 28, 2026. The bill ends penny production in law. When a customer pays cash and exact change is not available, the total rounds up or down to the nearest five cents. Payments by card or check do not round. As of late September 2026 the bill had gone to President Trump for his signature.
