## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The United States had a national bank twice before 1850, and both banks lost their federal charters. The First Bank's charter ran out in 1811. President Andrew Jackson vetoed a new charter for the Second Bank in 1832, and its federal charter ran out in 1836. From 1836 to 1863 the country had no national paper money. Americans paid with gold and silver coins and with paper notes printed by state banks. A bank's notes were worth less the farther from that bank a person tried to spend them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banknotes lent against enslaved people" -->
By the 1830s, planters in Louisiana had set up banks that lent out their own paper money against mortgages on enslaved people. The notes people carried in their pockets there were bank money backed by human beings.

At the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana, an enslaver pledged "slaves and cultivated land" to the bank in a mortgage. He could then borrow "up to half of the assessed value" of those people and that land. He received the loan in the bank's own paper notes.

To raise money to lend, the banks issued bonds. A bond is a paper that promises to repay a loan with interest. Louisiana lawmakers backed $2.5 million of the Consolidated Association's bonds, at 5 percent interest. Baring Brothers, bankers in London, advanced the money and sold the bonds to investors in Europe. The Union Bank of Louisiana, founded in 1832, issued $7 million in state bonds. The Citizens' Bank of Louisiana, chartered in 1833, had $12 million in bonds that Hope and Company of Amsterdam agreed to sell.

A typical bond was for $1,000. Edward E. Baptist wrote in 2010 that this was "roughly the price of a field hand," an enslaved man who worked in the fields. An investor in Europe could own a share of a debt backed by thousands of enslaved people without owning any one person.

In 1824 the merchant Vincent Nolte lent $48,000 to the Louisiana enslaver Alonzo Walsh. Walsh pledged "from 90 to a 100 head of first rate slaves," worth up to $80,000 at New Orleans auction prices. Head is the word used to count cattle. The names of the people he pledged are not recorded in the account of the loan.

Many enslavers had placed several mortgages on the same person. Then cotton prices fell, from 18 cents a pound in 1834 to under 10 cents by late 1836. By March 1837 ten large New Orleans cotton firms had failed. Planters owed the New Orleans banks alone $33 million. They could expect to clear no more than $10 million from the 1837 crop. When planters could not pay, the people they had mortgaged were sold in bankruptcy sales to raise cash. The accounts of the crash do not name who ran each sale.

In Mississippi and Florida, state officials refused to pay bonds that their states had backed. In Baptist's account, the bondholders in Europe and the northern states lost their money, and the enslavers kept the people they held. In January 2005 the bank JPMorgan Chase reported on two Louisiana banks it grew out of, Citizens Bank and Canal Bank. Officers of those two banks had accepted about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral, which is property pledged for a loan. After borrowers failed to pay, the two banks came to own about 1,250 of those people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bank War" -->
The Bank War was the fight over whether the country should keep a national bank. A national bank is a bank chartered by the national government to hold the government's money. Members of Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States in 1816, for twenty years, after the money troubles of the War of 1812. Its main office was in Philadelphia.

Nicholas Biddle became president of the Bank in 1823. Under him the Bank held the government's deposits and moved money across the country. Its officers also collected the notes of state banks and presented them to those banks for payment in gold and silver coins. A state bank had to hold enough coins to pay, so it could not print as many notes as it liked.

Andrew Jackson and his supporters argued that the Bank was a private company with public power, with owners in other countries and no control by voters. They said it favored rich people with connections. Biddle and the Bank's defenders argued that the Bank kept the country's money the same everywhere and kept its value sound. They said that without it, nothing would stop state banks from printing too much paper. Many historians later agreed with the defenders on that point.

In July 1832, four years before the charter ran out, Henry Clay pushed a new charter, and members of Congress passed it. It was an election year, and Clay was running against Jackson. Jackson vetoed the charter on July 10, 1832. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a bill into law. In his veto message he called the Bank "unauthorized by the Constitution, subversive of the rights of the States, and dangerous to the liberties of the people." Subversive means working to weaken something from the inside. That fall Jackson beat Clay and won a second term.

In 1833 Jackson ordered the government's deposits moved out of the Bank and into state banks he chose. His opponents called them "pet banks." Two Treasury secretaries would not move the money, and Jackson replaced them. Roger Taney, the third, moved it. Biddle struck back in 1833 and 1834 by cutting the Bank's lending to force a new charter. Borrowing became hard, and Biddle lost supporters.

On March 28, 1834, members of the Senate censured Jackson, which means they voted to condemn him formally. In 1837 they voted to remove the censure from the record. The Bank's federal charter ran out in 1836. It went on as a Pennsylvania state bank and failed for good in 1841. Biddle died in 1844, facing lawsuits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-money" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson

> **Who:** The president who vetoed the Second Bank's new charter and moved the government's money out of it.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C. Veto in 1832, removal of the deposits in 1833.
> **Movie:** *Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency* (2007), a two-hour PBS documentary narrated by Martin Sheen. It covers his whole presidency, not only the Bank War.

Andrew Jackson distrusted banks and paper money after he lost heavily on a land deal. In August 1795, at 28, he sold 68,000 acres of land to David Allison. He was selling for himself, for John Overton and for others. The accounts of the sale do not say where the land lay or which Native nation had lived on it.

Allison paid with promissory notes, which are written promises to pay money later. Jackson used the notes to buy supplies for a trading post. In 1797 Allison went bankrupt, and his notes were worth nothing. Jackson was left responsible for the debts behind them. It took him about 15 years to pay his way out. Allison's creditors, the people he owed, had him put in debtors' prison in Philadelphia, and he died there on September 28, 1798.

A 2001 article in *American Heritage* magazine described what Jackson took from all this. By the end, it said, he had settled ideas about "debt, paper money, and speculation: He hated them all." Speculation means buying something only to sell it later at a higher price.

As president, Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's new charter in 1832 and moved the government's money out of it in 1833. No national bank replaced it. For almost thirty years after 1836, American paper money was whatever the state banks printed. People argued at the time, and historians still argue, over whether ending the Bank protected ordinary Americans or left the state banks with nothing to hold them back.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-jackson-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="State banknotes and counterfeit detectors" -->
From 1836 to 1863, paper money meant notes printed by banks that state lawmakers had chartered. Each bank printed its own designs. A note was worth only as much as people trusted the bank that printed it. Far from that bank, people would take its notes only for less than the amount printed on them. The difference was called a discount.

Store owners checked each note against a printed guide before they took it. The guides were called bank note reporters and counterfeit detectors. Publishers put them out weekly or monthly from about 1826 to 1866. Each issue listed the discount on each bank's notes and described the fake notes going around. Shopkeepers kept them by the cash drawer.

Some banks were called wildcat banks. They were set up under state laws, called free-banking laws, that made it easy to open a bank. The story went that some stood so far out in the woods that only wildcats lived nearby. Nobody could get there to trade the notes for coins. Economists today, among them Gerald Dwyer and the team of Rolnick and Weber, have found that the story is exaggerated. Most free banks were not frauds. The people holding their notes did lose money, but less than the legend says.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the Michigan banks lent against" -->
In the late 1830s, Michigan's wildcat bankers lent money against land in the southeast of the state, around Ann Arbor. Until 1807 that land belonged to the Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Odawa and Wyandot nations.

In the Treaty of Detroit, on November 17, 1807, leaders of those four nations signed the land over to the United States. William Hull, the governor of Michigan Territory, signed for the United States. The land included the place that later became Ann Arbor. The Nottawaseppi Huron Band of the Potawatomi puts the land at about 8 million acres. The Ann Arbor District Library puts it at 5,611,532 acres. United States officials paid as little as 1.2 cents an acre, $57,717.32 in all. The library gives the land's estimated value as $6,400,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Alpheus Felch

> **Who:** A Michigan bank commissioner who inspected the state's new banks and found boxes of coin filled with nails and glass.
> **When and where:** Michigan, 1838 and 1839. Born September 28, 1804. Died June 13, 1896.

Alpheus Felch was one of three officials who inspected Michigan's banks in 1838, and he wrote down the frauds he found. He was a lawyer in Monroe, Michigan, from 1833. As a state lawmaker, he was one of four who voted against Michigan's free-banking law.

Michigan lawmakers passed that law on March 15, 1837. Before it, Michigan had 9 banks. Within months there were 18, and then 40. On December 30, 1837, three bank commissioners were appointed to inspect the banks. Felch served as one of them in 1838.

Felch described the inspections in 1880. Some banks kept "grossly insufficient books," meaning records far too poor to show what the bank held. One bank had nothing of value at all. Another showed the commissioners boxes of coin. Under a top layer of coins, the boxes were filled with nails and glass. At three banks in a row, the bankers showed the commissioners the same coins as their own. Local accounts say the bankers sent word ahead of the commissioners' route and carried the coins from bank to bank.

The commissioners could close a bank by court order or hand it to a receiver, an official who takes over a failed bank and pays its debts from what is left. They closed many banks, and criminal trials followed. Michigan lawmakers suspended the free-banking law in 1838. By September 1839 only 9 banks were left. Felch resigned as commissioner in 1839. In 1845 the judges of the Michigan Supreme Court declared the law unconstitutional.

Michigan bank notes lost an estimated 60 percent of their face value, according to the Ann Arbor District Library. In 2014 the economists Dove, Pecquet and Thies found that the true wildcat period lasted less than a year and involved "perhaps a dozen" banks. They put the noteholders' losses from those wildcat banks at about $350,000. They also argued that when the judges voided the law in 1845, many notes became worthless.

Felch went on to serve as a justice of the Michigan Supreme Court from 1842 to 1845, as governor of Michigan from 1846 to 1847 and as a United States senator from 1847 to 1853.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alpheus-felch-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837: the runs" -->
A bank run happens when many people rush to a bank at once to trade its notes or their deposits for coins. A bank keeps only part of its money in coin, so it cannot pay everyone at the same time.

On May 10, 1837, the officers of New York City's banks stopped paying out gold and silver for their notes and deposits. Banks across the country did the same. This was called suspending specie payments. Specie means coins of gold and silver. People who lined up at the banks got paper promises or nothing.

The country had about 850 banks. By about 1843, roughly 4 in 10 of them had failed, wholly or in part. No one counted the depositors who lost their savings. The records give the number of banks that closed, not the number of families who lost money.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1860 more than 10,000 different kinds of banknotes were in use in the United States. Estimates put a third or more of the paper money as fake. During the Civil War, members of Congress created a national paper money in 1862. Then they taxed state banknotes until banks stopped printing them. For the rest of the century Americans argued over what should stand behind a dollar, gold alone or gold and silver together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Greenbacks" -->
On February 25, 1862, members of Congress passed the Legal Tender Act to help pay for the Civil War. Under the act, they authorized $150 million in paper money called United States Notes. Nobody could trade these notes at the Treasury for gold or silver. They were money because the act declared them legal tender. Later acts raised the total to $450 million. The backs of the notes were printed in green ink, so people called them greenbacks.

Salmon P. Chase, the Treasury Secretary, managed the new paper money. He put his own portrait on the first $1 United States Note, in 1862.

In the National Banking Acts of February 25, 1863, and June 3, 1864, members of Congress created national banks with charters from the federal government. These banks issued national bank notes of one design, backed by government bonds. In 1865 members of Congress put a tax of 10 percent on state banknotes. Banks stopped issuing their own notes. The state notes in circulation fell from $143 million in 1865 to $4 million by 1867. For the first time, people everywhere in the country used the same kinds of paper money.

The words "In God We Trust" came onto American money in these same years. In November 1861 a Pennsylvania minister, the Reverend M. R. Watkinson, wrote to Chase asking that the coins recognize God. His letter survives in Treasury records. Chase told officials at the Mint to prepare a motto. In the Coinage Act of April 22, 1864, members of Congress approved a two-cent coin. It was the first United States coin to carry the words IN GOD WE TRUST. The motto became the national motto in 1956. It first appeared on paper money in 1957, after a law passed in 1955.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiters and the Secret Service" -->
Estimates from the 1860s put one-third to nearly one-half of the paper money in circulation as counterfeit. Nobody made a full count, so these figures are estimates.

On July 5, 1865, the Secret Service Division was formed as part of the Treasury Department, to catch counterfeiters. Its first chief was William P. Wood. Treasury Secretary Hugh McCulloch swore him in.

Guarding presidents was not part of the job at first. On September 6, 1901, Leon F. Czolgosz shot President William McKinley at a public event in Buffalo, New York. McKinley died on September 14. Afterward, members of Congress asked the Secret Service to protect presidents. In 1902 its agents took on full-time protection of the president.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Emanuel Ninger

> **Who:** A sign painter who drew fake money by hand and was caught when his ink ran on a wet bar top.
> **When and where:** New Jersey and Manhattan, 1883 to 1896. Lived 1845 to 1924.

Emanuel Ninger drew fake banknotes by hand for thirteen years, until his ink smudged on a wet bar. He was a sign painter, born in Germany, who lived in New Jersey. He drew United States banknotes, mostly $50 and $100 bills, with a pen, a pencil and a brush made of camel's hair.

To make a bill, Ninger laid a real note against glass and traced over it. He used colored inks for the parts printed in color. One bill took him weeks. From 1883 to 1896 he spent his fakes in Manhattan.

On March 28, 1896, Ninger laid one of his bills on a saloon bar. The bar was wet, and the ink smudged. A man working for the bartender followed him and had him arrested. The tellings of the story differ a little on how the arrest happened. All of them include the smudged ink on the wet bar.

Ninger pleaded guilty in May 1896 and was sentenced to six years. Newspapers and experts at the time pointed out that each fake had cost him more work than honest sign painting would have paid.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emanuel-ninger" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Freedman's Savings Bank, 1865 to 1874" -->
The Freedman's Savings Bank held the savings of people freed from slavery. It failed in 1874, and about 31,000 of its depositors never got any of their money back.

President Abraham Lincoln signed its charter on March 3, 1865. The full name was the Freedman's Savings and Trust Company. By 1871 it had about 37 branches in 17 states and Washington, D.C. About 70,000 people opened accounts. Most accounts held between $5 and $50. Over the bank's life, from 1865 to 1874, deposits added up to more than $57 million.

At first the bank could hold only safe government bonds. In 1870 members of Congress changed its charter to let it make loans on land and buildings, and other loans. Henry D. Cooke sat on the bank's board and also served his family's banking business. A history published by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which oversees national banks, states that Cooke used the Freedman's Bank's assets "to benefit the latter," meaning his family's business. The bank's headquarters, on Lafayette Square across from the White House, cost more than $200,000. Charles Meigs, a bank examiner, reported the bank's problems in February 1873 and again in 1874. Then the Panic of 1873 hit its loans.

Frederick Douglass became the bank's president in March 1874. He put $10,000 of his own money into the bank to restore trust in it. Then he saw that it could not be saved. The bank closed on June 29, 1874.

The Comptroller's history records 61,144 depositors. They were owed nearly $3 million. Between 1875 and 1883 the commissioners who wound up the bank paid back 62 percent of what was owed, in five payments. Of the 61,131 depositors who could claim, only 29,996 turned in their passbooks, the small books that recorded their savings. About 31,000 never collected anything.

The bank's signature books and deposit records from 29 branches survive at the National Archives. They list each depositor's name, age, job and family members. Black families today use them to trace their history.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bank runs of 1873 and 1893" -->
There was no deposit insurance in the 1800s. Deposit insurance is a promise, backed by the government, to repay people's savings if their bank fails. Without it, every depositor in a failed bank waited for whatever could be collected from the bank's leftover property.

At least 100 banks failed in the Panic of 1873. Banks failed in Washington, D.C., Pennsylvania, New York, Virginia, Georgia, Indiana, Illinois and Ohio. On September 20, 1873, the New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days. It was the first time it had ever closed.

In 1893 the Treasury's gold reserves fell to about $100 million, from $190 million in 1890. More than 100 banks suspended in June 1893. To suspend meant to shut the bank's doors or stop paying out cash. Between mid-July and mid-August, 340 more banks suspended. Some of them later reopened, so counts differ. Another count puts the banks that failed in 1893 at about 500.

No count exists of how many depositors lost savings in 1873 or 1893, or of how much they lost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold against silver" -->
Americans argued over gold and silver because of what each metal would do to prices and to debts.

With the dollar tied to gold, the supply of money grew slowly. Prices fell through most of the years from 1865 to 1896. A farmer's crop brought fewer dollars each year, but his mortgage payment stayed the same. Supporters of "free silver" wanted the government to coin all the silver brought to it, at 16 ounces of silver to 1 ounce of gold. That would have put more money in use and raised prices, which would have made debts easier to pay. Debtors and the states that mined silver wanted silver. Bankers and bondholders wanted gold alone.

In the Coinage Act of February 12, 1873, members of Congress dropped the standard silver dollar from the list of coins. That left the country on gold alone in practice, just as western mines were producing more and more silver. People on the silver side called the act "the Crime of '73."

Members of Congress passed two laws that required the government to buy some silver. The Bland-Allison Act of 1878 required the Treasury to buy $2 million to $4 million of silver each month and coin it into silver dollars. Members of Congress passed it over President Hayes's veto. In the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890, members of Congress raised the purchases to 4.5 million ounces a month. Treasury officials paid for the silver in notes that could be traded for gold, and people drained the government's gold by trading them in. In 1893, during the panic, members of Congress repealed the Sherman Act because President Cleveland insisted on it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### William Jennings Bryan

> **Who:** A 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska whose speech for silver won him the Democratic nomination for president.
> **When and where:** The Democratic convention in Chicago, July 9, 1896.

William Jennings Bryan turned the argument over money into a race for president. On July 9, 1896, at the Democratic convention in Chicago, he gave the last speech in the debate over the party's platform, its list of positions. He spoke for silver. Bryan was 36 years old and a former congressman from Nebraska.

He ended the speech with words aimed at the supporters of gold. "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns," he said. Then he said, "you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The speech won him the nomination. On the fifth ballot, the delegates chose him as their candidate for president.

The Republican platform backed the gold standard, which means a dollar backed by gold alone. The Republican candidate was William McKinley. Bryan won most of the South, the rural Midwest and the Rocky Mountain states. McKinley was strongest in the cities and in the Northeast, the Upper Midwest and the Pacific Coast.

McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176. He won 7,112,138 votes from the people, 51.0 percent, to Bryan's 6,510,807, 46.7 percent. Bryan ran against McKinley again in 1900 and lost again.
