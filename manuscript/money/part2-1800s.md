## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 11 minutes**

Americans in these years paid with paper money printed by hundreds of different banks. Some of that paper was lent out against enslaved people, and some against land that had belonged to Native nations.

The country had a national bank twice before 1850, and both banks lost their federal charters, the government papers that allowed them to exist. The First Bank's charter ran out in 1811. President Andrew Jackson vetoed, or refused to sign, a new charter for the Second Bank in 1832, and its federal charter ran out in 1836.

With no national bank and no national paper money from 1836 until 1862, people used gold and silver coins and the notes of state banks. A bank's notes were worth less the farther from that bank a person tried to spend them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banknotes lent against enslaved people" -->
Planters in Louisiana, the enslavers who ran large farms, had set up banks by the 1830s that lent out their own paper money against mortgages on enslaved people. In such a loan the enslaver pledged the people he enslaved, and the bank could take them if he did not pay. The paper notes people carried in their pockets in Louisiana had been lent out against enslaved human beings.

At the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana, an enslaver pledged "slaves and cultivated land" to the bank in a mortgage. He could then borrow up to half of what the bank's officers judged those people and that land to be worth. He received the loan in the bank's own paper notes.

To raise money to lend, the banks put out bonds. Each bond was a paper promise to repay a loan with interest, an extra payment for the use of the money. Louisiana lawmakers backed $2.5 million of the Consolidated Association's bonds, at 5 percent interest, which meant the state promised to pay the bonds if the bank did not.

Baring Brothers, bankers in London, paid the money up front and sold the bonds to investors in Europe. Chapter 16 lists the other Louisiana banks that raised money this way and the firms that sold their bonds.

A typical bond was for $1,000. Edward E. Baptist wrote in 2010 that this was "roughly the price of a field hand," an enslaved man who worked in the fields. An investor in Europe could own a share of a debt backed by thousands of enslaved people without owning any one person.

**Before the banks.** The merchant Vincent Nolte lent $48,000 to the Louisiana enslaver Alonzo Walsh in 1824. Walsh pledged "from 90 to a 100 head of first rate slaves," using head, the word for counting cattle. The account of the loan gives no names for these people. At New Orleans auction prices they were worth up to $80,000.

**The crash.** Many enslavers had placed several mortgages on the same person. Then cotton prices fell, from 18 cents a pound in 1834 to under 10 cents by late 1836. By March 1837 ten large New Orleans cotton firms had failed. The enslavers owed the New Orleans banks alone $33 million, and they could expect to make no more than $10 million from the 1837 crop.

When they could not pay, the people they had mortgaged were sold in forced sales to pay their debts. The accounts of the crash do not name who ran each sale. Buyers spent what little cash they had at the first sales, and then the prices of land and of enslaved people fell sharply.

In Mississippi and Florida, state officials refused to pay bonds that their states had backed. In Baptist's account, the bondholders in Europe and the northern states lost their money, and the enslavers kept the people they held.

**A bank's report, 2005.** Officials of the bank JPMorgan Chase published a report in January 2005 on two Louisiana banks it grew out of, Citizens Bank and Canal Bank. Officers of those two banks had accepted about 13,000 enslaved people as security for loans. After borrowers failed to pay, the two banks came to own about 1,250 of those people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bank War" -->
The Bank War was the fight over whether the country should keep a national bank, a bank chartered by the national government to hold the government's money. Members of Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States in 1816, for twenty years, after the money troubles of the War of 1812. Its main office was in Philadelphia.

Nicholas Biddle became president of the Bank in 1823. Under him the Bank held the government's deposits, the money the government kept in the Bank's care, and moved money across the country. Its officers also collected the notes of state banks and presented them to those banks for payment in gold and silver coins. A state bank had to hold enough coins to pay, so its owners could not print as many notes as they wanted.

Andrew Jackson and his supporters argued that the Bank was a private company with public power, with owners in other countries and no control by voters. They said its officers gave favors to rich people with connections. Biddle and the Bank's defenders argued that with the Bank, the country's money was worth the same everywhere and kept its value. They said that without it, nothing would stop state bankers from printing too much paper.

Henry Clay was running against Jackson for president in 1832. That July, four years before the old charter ran out, Clay led the push for a new one, and members of Congress passed it. Jackson vetoed the charter on July 10, 1832.

**The veto.** In his veto message Jackson called the Bank "unauthorized by the Constitution" and "dangerous to the liberties of the people." He said it weakened the rights of the states. That fall Jackson beat Clay and won a second term.

**The deposits.** Jackson ordered the government's money moved out of the Bank in 1833 and into state banks he chose, which his opponents called "pet banks." Two Treasury secretaries would not move the money, and Jackson replaced them. Roger Taney, the third, moved it. Biddle struck back in 1833 and 1834 by cutting the Bank's lending to force a new charter. Borrowing became hard, and Biddle lost supporters.

Members of the Senate censured Jackson on March 28, 1834, voting to blame him officially for what he had done. The Bank's federal charter ran out in 1836. In 1837 senators voted to remove the censure from the record. The Bank went on as a Pennsylvania state bank and failed for good in 1841. Biddle died in 1844, facing lawsuits.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-money" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency (PBS, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson

> **Who:** The president who vetoed the Second Bank's new charter and moved the government's money out of it.
> **When and where:** The United States. Veto in 1832, removal of the deposits in 1833.
> **Movie:** *Andrew Jackson: Good, Evil and the Presidency* (2007), a two-hour PBS documentary narrated by Martin Sheen. It covers his whole presidency, not only the Bank War.

**He sold 68,000 acres for paper notes that turned out worthless.**

Andrew Jackson distrusted banks and paper money after he lost money on a land deal. In August 1795, at 28, he sold 68,000 acres of land to David Allison. The accounts of the sale do not say where the land lay or which Native nation had lived on it. Jackson was selling for himself, for John Overton and for others.

Allison paid with promissory notes, written promises to pay money later, and Jackson used the notes to buy supplies for a trading post. In 1797 Allison could not pay his debts, and his notes were worth nothing. The people holding the notes came to Jackson for payment instead. It took him about 15 years to pay his way out.

Allison's creditors, the people he owed, had him put in debtors' prison in Philadelphia, a jail for people who could not pay what they owed. He died there on September 28, 1798.

A 2001 article in *American Heritage* magazine describes what Jackson took from all this. By the end, the article states, he had settled ideas about debt, paper money and speculation, which is buying something only to sell it later at a higher price. "He hated them all."

As president, Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's new charter in 1832 and ordered the government's money moved out of it in 1833. No national bank replaced it. From 1836 until 1862, American paper money was whatever the state banks printed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-jackson-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="State banknotes and counterfeit detectors" -->
Each bank that state lawmakers chartered printed its own notes, in its own designs. A note was worth only as much as people trusted the bank that printed it. Far from that bank, people would take its notes only for less than the amount printed on them, and the difference was called a discount.

Store owners checked notes against a printed guide before they took them. The guides were called bank note reporters and counterfeit detectors, and publishers put them out weekly or monthly from about 1826 to 1866. Each issue listed the discount on each bank's notes and described the fake notes going around. Shopkeepers kept them by the cash drawer.

Some banks were called wildcat banks. They were set up under state laws, called free-banking laws, with few rules for opening a bank. The story went that some stood so far out in the woods that only wildcats lived nearby, so nobody could get there to trade the notes for coins.

Economists today, among them Gerald Dwyer and the team of Rolnick and Weber, have found that most free banks were not frauds. The people holding their notes did lose money, but less than the legend says.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the Michigan banks lent against" -->
Michigan's wildcat bankers lent money in the late 1830s against land in the southeast of the state, around Ann Arbor. Until 1807 that land belonged to the Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Odawa and Wyandot nations.

In the Treaty of Detroit, a written agreement between nations, leaders of those four nations signed the land over to the United States on November 17, 1807. William Hull, the governor of Michigan Territory, signed for the United States. The land included the place that later became Ann Arbor.

**How much land, for how much.** The treaty page of the Nottawaseppi Huron Band of the Potawatomi states that the land came to about 8 million acres. A history page of the Ann Arbor District Library states 5,611,532 acres. United States officials paid as little as 1.2 cents an acre, $57,717.32 in all. The library's page states the land's estimated value as $6,400,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alpheus-felch-money" name="Alpheus Felch" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Alpheus Felch

> **Who:** A Michigan bank commissioner, later the state's governor and a US senator, who wrote down the frauds he found in 1838.
> **When and where:** Michigan, 1838 and 1839. Lived 1804 to 1896.

**Under a top layer of coins, the boxes held nails and glass.**

Alpheus Felch was one of three officials who inspected Michigan's banks in 1838, and he wrote down the frauds he found. A lawyer in Monroe, Michigan, he had been one of four state lawmakers who voted against Michigan's free-banking law.

Michigan lawmakers passed that law on March 15, 1837. Before it, Michigan had 9 banks. Within months there were 18, and then 40. Three bank commissioners were appointed on December 30, 1837, to inspect them, and Felch served as one of them in 1838.

Felch described the inspections in 1880. Some banks kept "grossly insufficient books," records far too poor to show what the bank held. One bank had nothing of value at all. At another, bankers showed the commissioners boxes of coin. Under a top layer of coins, the boxes were filled with nails and glass.

At three banks in a row, the bankers showed the commissioners the same coins as their own. Local accounts say the bankers sent word ahead of the commissioners' route and carried the coins from bank to bank.

The commissioners could close a bank by court order. They could also hand it to a receiver, an official who takes over a failed bank and pays its debts from what is left. After the commissioners' reports, many banks closed and criminal cases followed in the courts.

Michigan lawmakers suspended the free-banking law in 1838. By September 1839 only 9 banks were left. In 1845 the judges of the Michigan Supreme Court ruled that the law broke Michigan's constitution, the state's basic set of laws.

**How much people lost.** Michigan bank notes lost an estimated 60 percent of the amount printed on them, according to the Ann Arbor District Library. Economists disagree about the total.

Dove, Pecquet and Thies, economists writing in 2014, found that the true wildcat period lasted less than a year and involved "perhaps a dozen" banks. They put the noteholders' losses from those banks at about $350,000. They also argued that many notes became worthless when the judges threw out the law in 1845.

The economist Hugh Rockoff gave a larger figure in 1985, for all of Michigan's failed free banks. He estimated that their losses would have come to about $1 million if the state Supreme Court judges had not stopped the closing-down of those banks.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alpheus-felch-money" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837: the runs" -->
Chapter 16 describes what the Panic of 1837 did to jobs and food prices. At the banks, it began with a rush for coins.

In a money panic, fear spreads and many people try to get their money out of banks at once. At a single bank, that rush is called a run: people crowd in to trade the bank's notes or their deposits for coins. A bank keeps only part of its money in coin, so it cannot pay everyone at the same time.

The officers of New York City's banks stopped paying out gold and silver for their notes and deposits on May 10, 1837. Bank officers across the country did the same. This was called suspending specie payments, specie meaning coins of gold and silver. People who lined up at the banks got paper promises or nothing.

**Known:** Of the country's about 850 banks, roughly 4 in 10 had failed, wholly or in part, by about 1843.
**Not known:** No surviving record counts the depositors who lost their savings.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 10 minutes**

Members of Congress gave the country one national paper money during the Civil War. For the rest of the century, Americans argued over what should stand behind it: gold alone, or gold and silver together.

Before the war, more than 10,000 different kinds of banknotes were in use by 1860. Federal officials estimate that from one-third to nearly one-half of that paper money was fake. Members of Congress created the national paper money in 1862 and then taxed state banknotes until bankers stopped printing them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Greenbacks" -->
The new national paper money was printed with green ink on the back, so people called the notes greenbacks. Members of Congress passed the Legal Tender Act on February 25, 1862, to help pay for the Civil War. Under the act, they approved $150 million in paper money called United States Notes, and later acts raised the total to $450 million.

**No gold behind them.** Nobody could trade these notes at the Treasury for gold or silver. They were money because members of Congress made them legal tender, which means the law said people had to accept them to pay debts.

Salmon P. Chase, the Treasury Secretary, managed the new paper money. He put his own portrait on the first $1 United States Note, in 1862.

Members of Congress created national banks, which got their charters from the national government, in the National Banking Acts of February 25, 1863, and June 3, 1864. These banks put out national bank notes of one design, backed by government bonds.

**The tax.** In 1865 members of Congress put a tax of 10 percent on state banknotes. Bankers stopped printing their own notes. The state notes still in use fell from $143 million in 1865 to $4 million by 1867. By then, people everywhere in the country used the same national paper money.

The words "In God We Trust" first appeared on American coins in these same years. In November 1861 a Pennsylvania minister, the Reverend M. R. Watkinson, wrote to Chase asking that God be named on the coins. His letter survives in Treasury records. Chase told officials at the United States Mint to prepare a motto.

A two-cent coin was the first United States coin to carry the words IN GOD WE TRUST. Members of Congress approved it in the Coinage Act of April 22, 1864. The motto became the national motto in 1956. It first appeared on paper money in 1957, after a law passed in 1955.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiters and the Secret Service" -->
Fake bills made up somewhere between a third and a half of the paper money in use in the 1860s, but nobody made a full count at the time. History pages of the US Marshals Service and the Secret Service give about one-third. A 2025 release from the Department of Homeland Security gives nearly one-half.

Treasury officials formed the Secret Service Division on July 5, 1865, as part of the Treasury Department, to catch counterfeiters. Its first chief was William P. Wood, and Treasury Secretary Hugh McCulloch swore him in.

Guarding presidents was not part of the job at first. On September 6, 1901, Leon F. Czolgosz shot President William McKinley at a public event in Buffalo, New York. McKinley died on September 14. Afterward, members of Congress asked the Secret Service to protect presidents. In 1902 its agents took on full-time protection of the president.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emanuel-ninger" name="Emanuel Ninger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Emanuel Ninger

> **Who:** A sign painter who drew fake money by hand and was caught when his ink ran on a wet bar top.
> **When and where:** New Jersey and Manhattan, 1883 to 1896. Lived 1845 to 1924.

**He drew each fake bill by hand, and one took weeks.**

Emanuel Ninger drew fake banknotes by hand for thirteen years, until his ink smudged on a wet bar. He was a sign painter, born in Germany, who lived in New Jersey. He drew United States banknotes, mostly $50 and $100 bills, with a pen, a pencil and a brush made of camel's hair.

To make a bill, Ninger laid a real note against glass and traced over it. He used colored inks for the parts printed in color. One bill took him weeks. From 1883 to 1896 he spent his fakes in Manhattan.

Ninger laid one of his bills on a wet saloon bar on March 28, 1896, and the ink smudged. A man working for the bartender followed him and had him arrested. The tellings of the story differ a little on how the arrest happened, but all of them include the smudged ink on the wet bar.

Ninger pleaded guilty in May 1896, and a judge sentenced him to six years in prison. Newspaper writers and experts at the time marveled that each fake had cost him more work than honest sign painting would have paid.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emanuel-ninger" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Freedman's Savings Bank, 1865 to 1874" -->
The Freedman's Savings Bank held the savings of people freed from slavery. It failed in 1874, and about 31,000 of its depositors never got any of their money back.

President Abraham Lincoln signed the bank's charter into law on March 3, 1865. The full name was the Freedman's Savings and Trust Company. By 1871 it had about 37 branches in 17 states and Washington, D.C. About 70,000 people opened accounts over the bank's life. Accounts ranged from about $5 to $50, and deposits over those years added up to more than $57 million.

At first the bank could hold only safe government bonds. In 1870 members of Congress changed its charter to let it make loans on land and buildings, and other loans.

**Henry Cooke.** Henry D. Cooke sat on the bank's board and also served his family's banking business. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the government office that oversees national banks, published a history of the bank. It states that Cooke used the Freedman's Bank's money and property "to benefit the latter," meaning his family's business. The bank's headquarters, on Lafayette Square across from the White House, cost more than $200,000.

Charles Meigs, an examiner who inspected banks for the Comptroller's office, reported the bank's problems in February 1873. In the Panic of 1873, many of the Freedman's Bank's loans went bad. Meigs reported the bank's problems again in 1874.

Frederick Douglass became the bank's president in March 1874. He put $10,000 of his own money into the bank to restore trust in it. Then he saw that it could not be saved. The bank closed on June 29, 1874.

When the bank closed, it owed 61,144 depositors nearly $3 million, by the count in the Comptroller's history. Between 1875 and 1883 the commissioners who closed out the bank's business paid back 62 percent of what was owed, in five payments.

Only about half the depositors ever collected. Of the 61,131 who could claim, 29,996 turned in their passbooks, the small books that recorded their savings. About 31,000 never collected anything, and the records do not say why.

The bank's signature books and deposit records from 29 branches survive at the National Archives. They list depositors' names, ages, jobs and family members. Black families today use them to trace their history.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bank runs of 1873 and 1893" -->
There was no deposit insurance in the 1800s, no promise backed by the government to repay people's savings if their bank failed. Every depositor in a failed bank waited for whatever the receiver, the official who took over the failed bank, could collect from the bank's leftover property.

At least 100 banks failed in the Panic of 1873, in Washington, D.C., Pennsylvania, New York, Virginia, Georgia, Indiana, Illinois and Ohio. On September 20, 1873, the New York Stock Exchange, the market in New York City where people buy and sell shares of companies, closed for ten days. It was the first time it had ever closed.

The Treasury's gold fell to about $100 million in 1893, from $190 million in 1890. Writers for the Federal Reserve's history website count more than 100 banks that suspended in June 1893, meaning they shut their doors or stopped paying out cash. Between mid-July and mid-August, 340 more suspended. Some of them later reopened, so counts differ, and an Encyclopedia.com article puts the banks that failed in 1893 at about 500.

No surviving record counts how many depositors lost savings in 1873 or 1893, or how much they lost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold against silver" -->
Americans argued over whether gold alone or gold and silver should stand behind the dollar. Farmers and others who owed money were on one side, and bankers on the other.

With the dollar tied to gold, the supply of money grew slowly. Prices fell through most of the years from 1865 to 1896. A farmer's crop brought fewer dollars each year, but his mortgage payment, the money he owed each year on a loan against his farm, stayed the same.

Supporters of "free silver" wanted the government to coin all the silver brought to it, at 16 ounces of silver to 1 ounce of gold. That would have put more money in use and raised prices, which would have made debts easier to pay. Debtors and many people in the states that mined silver wanted silver. Bankers and bondholders, people who had lent money by buying bonds, wanted gold alone.

Members of Congress dropped the standard silver dollar from the list of coins in the Coinage Act of February 12, 1873. After that, in practice, the dollar was tied to gold alone and not to silver, just as miners in the West were digging more and more silver. People on the silver side called the act "the Crime of '73."

Two later laws required the government to buy some silver. The Bland-Allison Act of 1878 required the Treasury to buy $2 million to $4 million of silver each month and coin it into silver dollars. Lawmakers passed it over President Hayes's veto.

Under the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890, the purchases rose to 4.5 million ounces a month. Treasury officials paid for the silver in notes that could be traded for gold. As holders traded the notes in, the government's gold shrank. In 1893, during the panic, members of Congress canceled the Sherman Act because President Cleveland insisted on it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-jennings-bryan" name="William Jennings Bryan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Jennings Bryan

> **Who:** A 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska who became the Democratic Party's candidate for president with a speech for silver.
> **When and where:** The Democratic convention in Chicago, July 9, 1896.

**"You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."**

William Jennings Bryan became the Democratic Party's candidate for president in 1896 with a speech for silver. On July 9, 1896, at the Democratic convention in Chicago, he gave the last speech in the debate over the party's platform, its list of positions.

He ended the speech with words aimed at the supporters of gold. "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns," he said. Then he said, "you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold," crucify meaning to put someone to death by nailing them to a cross.

Both pictures come from the Bible's account of the death of Jesus. Bryan was comparing that suffering to the gold standard, a dollar backed by gold alone. Under it prices fell, and farmers and working people found their debts harder to pay each year.

After the speech, the delegates, the party members sent to the convention, chose him as their candidate for president on the fifth round of voting. Republicans wrote support for the gold standard into their platform. Their candidate was William McKinley. Bryan's votes came mostly from the South, the rural Midwest and the Rocky Mountain states. McKinley was strongest in the cities and in the Northeast, the Upper Midwest and the Pacific Coast.

Each state had a number of electoral votes, set by its seats in Congress, and a candidate needed more than half of them to become president. McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176.

In votes from the people, McKinley won 51.0 percent and Bryan 46.7 percent, 7,112,138 votes to 6,510,807. Bryan ran against McKinley again in 1900 and lost again.
