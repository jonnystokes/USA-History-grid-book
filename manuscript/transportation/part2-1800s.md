## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Steamboats, canals and railroads cut the time and the cost of moving people and goods in the first half of the 1800s. In 1807 Robert Fulton's steamboat went from New York City to Albany in 32 hours. A sailing boat took about four days. After the Erie Canal opened in 1825, hauling a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City cost under $10, against about $100 by wagon.

American-born laborers and Irish immigrants dug the Erie Canal. In the South, enslaved Black men dug the canals and built most of the railroads. Their owners rented them out to the builders and kept the men's pay, and the men running Southern railroad companies also bought enslaved men outright.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steamboats on the rivers" -->
On August 17, 1807, Robert Fulton's North River Steamboat left New York City for Albany, 150 miles up the Hudson River. It arrived 32 hours later. Sailing boats called sloops took about four days to make the same trip. Fulton's boat began carrying passengers on a regular schedule right away. People later called it the *Clermont*.

Many later accounts say that people who doubted the boat called it "Fulton's Folly." A folly is a foolish project. Few records from 1807 show anyone using that name. It comes from stories told afterward.

Within a few years, steamboats were running on the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers. New Orleans recorded 21 steamboats arriving in 1814 and 2,770 arriving in 1846. In the 1810s steamboats covered about 50 miles a day, and by the 1820s about 100.

Much of what the boats carried was cotton. In Mississippi, cotton output rose from 20 million pounds in 1820 to 535 million pounds in 1860. Before the Civil War, more than half of all the cotton grown in the United States passed through New Orleans. Enslaved people grew and picked that cotton, forced to work by the people who held them in slavery.

A steamboat's boiler is the tank where water is heated into steam under pressure. Steamboat boilers on the Mississippi and the rivers that flow into it exploded again and again in these years. Thousands of people died in those explosions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-fulton" name="Robert Fulton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Fulton

> **Who:** The man who made the steamboat a business that lasted.
> **When and where:** The Hudson River in New York, from August 17, 1807.

Robert Fulton put a steam engine, a boat and a paying route together into a business that kept running. John Fitch had carried paying passengers by steamboat 17 years earlier, in 1790, but his service failed.

Fulton's partner was Robert Livingston, who paid for the work. Livingston held a monopoly on steamboats in New York. A monopoly is a legal right to be the only seller of something. Under it, only Livingston and his partner Fulton could run steamboats on New York's waters.

Fulton and Livingston paid for a second boat, the *New Orleans*, built at Pittsburgh. It left Pittsburgh on October 20, 1811. It traveled more than 1,800 miles down the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers and reached New Orleans on January 10, 1812. It was the first steamboat on the western rivers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-fulton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The National Road" -->
The National Road was the first highway in the United States built entirely with money from the federal government, the national government in Washington. Members of Congress approved it in 1806, while Thomas Jefferson was president. Work began at Cumberland, Maryland, in 1811. The road reached Wheeling, on the Ohio River, in 1818. By the 1830s it went on to Vandalia, Illinois, where the federal money stopped.

West of Cumberland, the road followed an older path. A Delaware chief named Nemacolin and a colonist named Thomas Cresap marked and cleared it in 1749 or 1750. Their path ran between the Potomac and Monongahela Rivers. It went from where Cumberland now stands to the mouth of Redstone Creek, where Brownsville, Pennsylvania, is today. George Washington used it on his first trip to the west, and General Edward Braddock's army used it in the French and Indian War. It was called Braddock's Road until the National Road was built on the same route.

Irish laborers built sections of the National Road. They were paid six dollars a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Erie Canal" -->
A canal is a waterway that people dig so boats can travel where no river runs. Workers dug the Erie Canal from 1817 to 1825. It ran 363 miles, from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo on Lake Erie. It opened on October 26, 1825, and cost about $7.1 million. While it was being dug, people who mocked it called it "Clinton's Ditch" after DeWitt Clinton, who pushed for it. The tolls that boats paid to use the canal paid back the cost of building it in about nine years.

Moving goods by canal cost far less than moving them by wagon. Hauling a ton from Buffalo to New York City cost about $100 by wagon. By canal it cost under $10. Some accounts put the cost by canal at about $6 a ton.

The boys who drove the mules that pulled the canal boats were called hoggees. Many of them were about 10 years old. A hoggee walked 18 to 20 miles a day, six hours on and six hours off. He earned about $8 to $10 a month, and many hoggees were cheated out of their pay. The accounts do not name the men who cheated them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who dug the canals, and whose land they crossed" -->
American-born workers and Irish immigrants dug the Erie Canal. In 1818 between 2,000 and 3,000 laborers were working on it, with a horse for every second man. Ads placed in Ireland to find workers promised wages of 8, 10 or 12 dollars a month, with meat three times a day and liquor. Many men worked for 75 cents to $1.50 a day, and less as more workers arrived. They got no pay on rainy days, but they still had to pay for their lodging. Some contractors, the men who ran sections of the work, started fights among the workers to get out of paying them. At Rochester, a contractor named William Brittin brought about 30 convicts, people serving prison sentences, from Auburn State Prison to work. Many escaped, and Irish workers took their places.

In 1819 the digging reached the Montezuma Marshes, wet ground full of mosquitoes. The mosquitoes spread malaria, a disease that brings high fevers and chills again and again. The canal commissioners, the state officials in charge of the canal, reported that about 1,000 workers fell sick between the middle of July and October 1, 1819. Many died. No one kept a count of the dead.

The Erie Canal ran through the homelands of the Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations. The Oneida Indian Nation's history of the canal says New York State officials used a series of 27 treaties to get Oneida land for it, and it calls all 27 illegal. From 1858 to 1871 New York State officials took parts of the Seneca Oil Spring Reservation for a canal reservoir, a lake made to store water. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation.

In the South, owners rented out enslaved men to dig canals, and the owners kept the men's pay. Enslaved men dug most of the Dismal Swamp Canal, which runs 22 miles from Deep Creek, Virginia, to South Mills, North Carolina. They dug it from 1793 to 1805. Before the canal, George Washington and his brother John Augustine Washington ran a business called the Dismal Swamp Company. Through it they bought 54 enslaved men, women and children to dig ditches and cut timber in the swamp.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" name="DeWitt Clinton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DeWitt Clinton

> **Who:** The New York governor who pushed the Erie Canal through while people mocked it.
> **When and where:** New York State, 1811 to 1825.

DeWitt Clinton pushed for the Erie Canal while critics called it "Clinton's Ditch" and "Clinton's Folly." In 1811 he was the mayor of New York City and a new canal commissioner, one of the state officials in charge of planning the canal. That year, in a speech to the New-York Historical Society, he predicted that "before the passing away of the present generation, not a single Iroquois will be seen in this state." The canal ran through Iroquois land. His prediction was wrong. Today more people of the Six Nations, the Iroquois nations, live in New York than lived there at the end of the American Revolution.

As governor, Clinton rode on the first boat to travel the whole length of the canal in 1825. The boat was named the *Seneca Chief*. At the opening, Clinton poured water from Lake Erie into New York harbor. People called this the "wedding of the waters."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-garfield-transportation" name="James Garfield, canal boy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Garfield, canal boy

> **Who:** A 16-year-old who worked on an Ohio canal boat. He later became president of the United States.
> **When and where:** The Ohio and Erie Canal, 1848, on the boat *Evening Star*.

In 1848, when he was 16, James Garfield ran away to work on a canal boat called the *Evening Star*. The boat carried freight on the Ohio and Erie Canal. For about six weeks he helped haul coal, copper ore and wood.

Garfield could not swim. One night he fell overboard. He later caught malaria and went home. Writers of biographies for his campaign for president claimed he fell into the canal fourteen times. That number comes from those later stories and not from the record of his time on the boat.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-garfield-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="moses-grandy" name="Moses Grandy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Moses Grandy

> **Who:** An enslaved man who ran boats through the Dismal Swamp canals, bought his own freedom three times, and described the men who dug the canals.
> **When and where:** Camden County, North Carolina, and the canals to Norfolk, Virginia, from the early 1800s. His narrative was published in London in 1843.

Moses Grandy was born into slavery in Camden County, North Carolina, around 1786. His owners hired him out each year to other men. He worked canal boats that carried shingles, boards and other freight through the Dismal Swamp canals to Norfolk.

Grandy paid for his own freedom three times. First he paid $600 to his owner, James Grandy. James Grandy kept the money and sold him to a man named Trewitt. Over two and a half years, Moses Grandy earned $600 more and paid it to Trewitt. Then Trewitt's business failed, and a man named Mews claimed Grandy to cover Trewitt's debt. Grandy's next owner, a Mr. Sawyer, put him to work in the fields. A ship captain from Deep Creek, Edward Minner, lent Grandy the third price, $650, and Sawyer set him free. Grandy paid Minner back over three years.

While Grandy worked on the canal, his wife's owner sold her. A group of enslaved people passed along the road beside the canal, and she called out to him. The man who had bought her, Mr. Rogerson, pulled out a pistol. He said he would shoot Grandy if he came near the wagon. Grandy wrote: "I have never seen or heard of her from that day to this."

Grandy also described the enslaved men who dug the canals. They worked "up to the middle or much deeper in mud and water, cutting away roots and baling out mud." Baling out means scooping out. They slept in open huts, still covered in mud, with no bedding. Once a month their owners came to collect the men's pay.

An overseer named Wiley M'Pherson gave every man the same amount of digging each day. An overseer was the man in charge of the workers. When men had not finished the day before, M'Pherson tied them up and flogged them. Flogging means whipping a person's bare back until the skin splits. Then he had "pork or beef brine," the salt water used to keep meat, put "on their bleeding backs, to increase the pain." He left men tied all day with their feet barely touching the ground, while flies and mosquitoes settled on the cuts.

Grandy wrote: "I have seen him flog slaves with his own hands, till their entrails were visible." Entrails are the organs inside the body. Grandy went on: "I have seen the sufferers dead when they were taken down. He never was called to account in any way for it."

Grandy later married again. He lived in Boston, sawed wood, worked in coal yards, loaded and unloaded ships, and went on more voyages. He saved enough to buy his second wife out of slavery in Virginia, and she joined him in Boston. He raised $450 to buy his son's freedom. His daughters in New Orleans paid for their own freedom, $1,200 for each of them. His book was sold to raise money for his relatives who were still enslaved.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="moses-grandy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first railroads" -->
The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad was the first railroad in the United States to carry both passengers and freight on a schedule for anyone who paid. Its builders laid the first stone on July 4, 1828. Its first 13 miles of track, from Baltimore to Ellicott's Mills, opened in 1830.

In a well-known story, Peter Cooper's steam engine, the *Tom Thumb*, raced a horse pulling a railroad car on August 28, 1830. In the story, the engine lost when a belt on it slipped. The story comes from a talk that John H. B. Latrobe gave in 1868, 38 years later. No record from 1830 shows that the race took place. The B&O Railroad Museum tells it as a legend.

On August 9, 1831, a locomotive named the *DeWitt Clinton* pulled the first steam passenger train in New York State. A locomotive is the engine that pulls a train. The train ran 16 miles from Albany to Schenectady in about an hour, on the Mohawk and Hudson line.

City transit began in these years too. From 1827, Abraham Brower ran a horse-drawn coach called an omnibus on the streets of New York City. On November 14, 1832, the *John Mason*, a horse-drawn car that rolled on rails, began carrying passengers in lower Manhattan for the New York and Harlem Railroad. It was the first street railway in the country.

In the South, enslaved people built most of the railroads. As early as 1841, the men running Southern railroad companies bought hundreds of enslaved men between the ages of 16 and 35. In the 1850s they bought or rented enslaved workers in groups of hundreds. The historian Theodore Kornweibel Jr. found enslaved workers on 85 of the 113 railroads in the Confederate states, the Southern states that later fought the United States in the Civil War. In 1860 Southern railroad companies owned about 15,000 enslaved people, and women and children were among them. In 1855 the president of the Mississippi Central Railroad told the people who owned shares in the company that "the slave is preferable to free labor, and far better adapted to the construction of railways in the south." Free labor meant workers who were not enslaved.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Railroad track spread across the United States in the second half of the 1800s. In 1850 the country had about 9,000 miles of railroad track. By 1900 it had more than 190,000 miles. Counts for 1900 run from about 193,000 to 215,000, depending on whether side tracks are counted.

In 1869 the first railroad across the country cut the trip from one coast to the other from about four months to about a week. Central Pacific managers hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers to build its western part. Its tracks crossed the lands of the Cheyenne, Lakota, Arapaho, Shoshone and Paiute nations. By the end of the century, the companies that ran city streetcars were switching from horse-drawn cars to electric ones, and many Americans, women among them, were riding bicycles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The railroad across the continent" -->
Two companies built the first railroad across the country. Union Pacific crews built west from Omaha, Nebraska, while Central Pacific crews built east from Sacramento, California. The two lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869, and officials marked the meeting with a golden spike.

Members of Congress voted to give the two companies land and loans. On July 1, 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railroad Act. Under it, each company got 6,400 acres of public land for every mile of track it built. Each company also got up to $48,000 in government bonds for every mile. A bond is a kind of loan. The top rate of $48,000 was for miles built through mountains.

The act also said: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." To extinguish a title means to end someone's ownership of land. With those words, the lawmakers directed federal officials to end Native nations' ownership of the land along the railroad as fast as they could. From 1850 to 1871, members of Congress voted to give railroads about 130 million acres of federal land. Some counts run as high as 175 million acres. State lawmakers gave railroads about 51 million acres more.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the railroad crossed" -->
Many Native nations were harmed and driven from their homes while the two lines were built, among them the Cheyenne, Lakota (also called Sioux), Arapaho, Shoshone and Paiute. The companies' builders and US officials took Native land for the lines. The builders also changed the natural resources these nations lived on: the land, water, plants and animals. Some Paiute people worked for the Central Pacific. Union Pacific managers and US officials hired hundreds of Pawnee, who guarded the work crews alongside the US Army. On January 13, 1865, four companies of Pawnee soldiers joined the Army under Major Frank J. North.

On November 29, 1864, at Sand Creek, US soldiers on horseback led by Colonel John Chivington killed about 150 Cheyenne and Arapaho people. Most of them were women and children. The people killed were not armed. The count of 150 comes from PBS, and other counts differ.

Cheyenne, Arapaho and Lakota fighters struck back for Sand Creek. On January 7, 1865, they burned the town of Julesburg, Colorado, and cut the telegraph line. On August 27, 1867, at Plum Creek, Nebraska, Cheyenne men pulled up railroad track, wrecked a work train and killed its crew. One man survived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chinese workers" -->
Central Pacific managers hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers from 1865 to 1869. Most came from Guangdong, a province in southern China. The Chinese workers were paid about $35 a month, and they had to buy their own food and housing. White workers got about $42 a month, plus housing.

The Central Pacific's managers kept no record of the Chinese workers who died, so no one knows how many there were. Avalanches, masses of snow sliding down the mountains, killed workers in the winter of 1866 to 1867. There were 44 storms that winter. In one, snow carried away the camp at Strong's Canyon. Blasts of black powder and nitroglycerin, both explosives used to cut through rock, killed others. Smallpox, a disease that spreads from person to person, killed more in 1868 and 1869.

On June 30, 1870, writers at two Sacramento newspapers printed guesses of the dead. The writer at the *Sacramento Union* reported "about fifty." The writer at the *Sacramento Reporter* told of "the accumulated bones of perhaps 1,200," about 20,000 pounds of bones being shipped home to China. Some researchers today estimate 100 to 150 dead. Other writers say hundreds or thousands.

On June 25, 1867, Chinese workers in the Sierra Nevada mountains went on strike, which means they stopped work to demand better terms. Estimates of how many struck run from 2,000 to 5,000. They asked for $40 a month instead of $35, for workdays of no more than ten hours, and for shorter shifts in the cramped tunnels. The construction boss, Charles Crocker, and his foreman, James Harvey Strobridge, cut off food and supplies to the Chinese camps. After a week with little food, Crocker told the strikers that men who went back at once would only be fined. Men who stayed out would lose all their pay for June. Most men, weak from lack of food, went back. The men who refused faced a group of armed white men, and work started again. Crocker gave the strikers no raise, though pay for experienced men later rose a little, according to the Library of Congress. It was the largest strike by workers in the country up to that time.

On April 28, 1869, crews laid 10 miles and 56 feet of track in one day. Eight Irish workers carried the rails, and several thousand Chinese workers did the rest of the work. Twelve days later, at Promontory Summit, eight Chinese workers laid the last track joining the two lines. Three of them, Ging Cui, Wong Fook and Lee Shao, rode on a float at the celebration of the railroad's 50th anniversary on May 10, 1919.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hung-wah" name="Hung Wah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hung Wah

> **Who:** The leading Chinese headman on the Central Pacific. He handled pay and living arrangements for hundreds of Chinese workers, at times thousands, and dealt with the company for them.
> **When and where:** The Central Pacific Railroad, from January 1864.

Hung Wah is one of the few Chinese railroad workers whose names appear in the records. The company's payroll clerks usually listed only headmen, the men who led the Chinese work gangs. So the names of most of the thousands of Chinese laborers are not in the records.

Hung Wah first appears on the Central Pacific's payroll in January 1864. With another headman, Ah Toy, he supervised the railroad's first Chinese crew, about 23 men. He became the leading Chinese headman on the line. He handled the workers' pay and their living arrangements, and he dealt with the company for them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hung-wah" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" name="Cornelius Vanderbilt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cornelius Vanderbilt

> **Who:** A ferryman who moved his money from steamboats into railroads and left the largest American fortune of his time.
> **When and where:** New York, 1810 to 1877.

Cornelius Vanderbilt began as a ferryman and died in 1877 with about $100 million, the largest American fortune of his time. In 1810, at 16, he began carrying freight and passengers between Staten Island and Manhattan. His boat was a periauger, a shallow sailboat with two masts. Accounts of how he got the boat differ. One says he borrowed $100 from his mother and named the boat *Swiftsure*. The first account of his life, printed in 1853, says the boat was his father's and he kept half the profit. The website Biography.com describes the story of the young ferryman as a legend. Other boat captains called him "the Commodore," at first as a joke. A commodore is a navy officer who commands a group of ships.

Vanderbilt owned steamboats and moved his money into railroads in the early 1860s. In 1867 he took control of the New York Central Railroad. On November 1, 1869, he joined it with his Hudson River Railroad. In 1871 he opened Grand Central Depot, a train station in Manhattan.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mark-twain-transportation" name="Mark Twain, steamboat pilot" movie="Mark Twain (2001)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mark Twain, steamboat pilot

> **Who:** Samuel Clemens, who steered steamboats on the Mississippi River before he wrote books as Mark Twain.
> **When and where:** The Mississippi River. He got his pilot's license on April 9, 1859.
> **Movie:** *Mark Twain* (2001), a documentary by Ken Burns about his whole life. PBS showed it in January 2002.

Samuel Clemens was a licensed steamboat pilot on the Mississippi River. A pilot is the person who steers a boat. He got his license on April 9, 1859, when he was 23. He piloted boats until 1861, when the Civil War began and traffic on the river stopped.

He took his pen name from a call used on the river. A leadsman was the crew member who measured the depth of the water and called it out to the pilot. "Mark twain" meant two fathoms. A fathom is six feet, so two fathoms is twelve feet of water. In 1883 he published *Life on the Mississippi*, a book about his years as a pilot.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mark-twain-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Clocks, sleeping cars and danger on the railroads" -->
On November 18, 1883, railroad managers in the United States and Canada began using standard time zones. A time zone is a region where all clocks show the same time. Before then, each railroad company ran its trains by its own clocks. William F. Allen, the editor of the *Traveler's Official Railway Guide*, a book of train schedules, drew up the plan. On the day of the switch, railroad workers reset every railroad clock to the new time, even clocks that had already passed noon. In those places noon came twice, so people called it "the day of two noons." Members of Congress did not vote time zones into law until the Standard Time Act of March 19, 1918.

In 1865 George Pullman built the *Pioneer*, his first sleeping car built for luxury. It had upper beds that folded away and seats that pulled out. His company, the Pullman Palace Car Company, began in 1867.

Railroad work was dangerous. In 1889, about 8.5 of every 1,000 American trainmen, the workers who ran the trains, died on the job. That was twice the rate for trainmen in Britain. That year 1.73 of every 1,000 trainmen died while coupling, the job of joining railroad cars together. With the old link-and-pin coupler, a brakeman, the crew member who set the brakes and joined the cars, had to stand between two moving cars to join them. Mangled hands were known as "the badge of the experienced worker."

Safer equipment existed for years before lawmakers required railroad owners to use it. In 1873 Eli Janney got a patent, the legal right to be the only one to make and sell an invention, for an automatic coupler. It joined cars without a worker standing between them. George Westinghouse's air brake existed from 1869. In 1893 members of Congress passed the Safety Appliance Act. It required automatic couplers and air brakes on trains that crossed state lines, starting in 1900. Accidents while coupling cars fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902, while the number of railroad workers grew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Separate cars by race" -->
Train crews and lawmakers decided where Black passengers could sit. In 1884 Ida B. Wells had a first-class ticket on a train in Tennessee. The train crew ordered her into the car for Black passengers. She refused, and they forced her off the train. She sued the railroad and won $500 in a Tennessee court. In 1887 the judges of the Tennessee Supreme Court threw out that decision.

On June 10, 1890, Louisiana lawmakers passed the Separate Car Act. It required railroads to provide "equal but separate" cars for Black and white passengers. A passenger who broke it could be fined $25 or jailed for 20 days. Homer Plessy, a shoemaker in New Orleans, offered to test the law. To test it, he would break it on purpose so that judges would have to decide whether the law was allowed. On June 7, 1892, he bought a ticket on the East Louisiana Railroad and sat in the car for white passengers. The railroad's officials agreed to the test, because separate cars cost the company money. A private detective hired by the group planning the test, the Citizens' Committee, arrested him. Judge John H. Ferguson ruled against Plessy. On May 18, 1896, the justices of the US Supreme Court ruled against him too, 7 to 1, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*. Their ruling let states keep separating Black and white passengers by law. It stood for nearly sixty years, until 1954.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Electric streetcars and bicycles" -->
Electric streetcars replaced horse-drawn ones in American cities between 1888 and 1902. Electric streetcars ran earlier in East Cleveland in 1884, in Baltimore in 1885 and in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1886. The Dictionary of American History describes the system that Frank Sprague built in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888 as the first successful one. By 1891, about 200 streetcar systems had switched to electricity or planned to. Street railway track grew from about 8,000 miles in 1890 to 22,500 miles in 1902. In those same years, the share of streetcar service pulled by animals fell from 75 percent to 1 percent. The last horse-drawn streetcar in paid service in New York City ran on July 26, 1917.

In the 1890s cycling became a craze, something that very many people took up at the same time. Riders used the safety bicycle, which had two wheels of the same size, a chain drive and air-filled tires. Women rode as well as men. On February 2, 1896, Susan B. Anthony told the reporter Nellie Bly in the *New York World* that bicycling "has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world." To emancipate means to set free. She said: "I stand and rejoice every time I see a woman ride by on a wheel."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-sprague" name="Frank Sprague" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frank Sprague

> **Who:** The engineer whose streetcar system in Richmond showed that electric streetcars could work in a city.
> **When and where:** Richmond, Virginia. Regular service began February 2, 1888.

Frank Sprague built the Richmond Union Passenger Railway, which began regular electric streetcar service on February 2, 1888. It started with 10 cars. By May 1888 it had about 12 miles of track and 23 cars. Other electric streetcars had run before, in East Cleveland, Baltimore and Montgomery. Richmond's was the first large system that worked well. Within about two years, about 110 electric railways had been built or ordered using Sprague's equipment. The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, a society of engineers, has named his Richmond railway a milestone in the history of engineering.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-sprague" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->
