## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 13 minutes**

Moving people and goods got much faster and cheaper in the first half of the 1800s, on steamboats, canals and the first railroads. Robert Fulton's steamboat went from New York City to Albany in 32 hours in 1807, a trip that took a sailing boat about four days. After the Erie Canal opened in 1825, hauling a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City cost under $10, against about $100 by wagon.

**Who did the work.** American-born laborers and Irish immigrants dug the Erie Canal. In the South, enslaved Black men dug most of the Dismal Swamp Canal, and enslaved people built most of the railroads. Owners rented the men out to the builders and kept their pay, and the men running Southern railroad companies also bought enslaved people outright.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steamboats on the rivers" -->
Robert Fulton's North River Steamboat left New York City on August 17, 1807, and reached Albany, 150 miles up the Hudson River, 32 hours later. Sailing boats called sloops took about four days to make the same trip. Fulton's boat began carrying passengers on a regular schedule right away. People later called it the *Clermont*.

Many later accounts say that people who doubted the boat called it "Fulton's Folly" (a folly is a foolish project). Few records from 1807 show anyone using that name. It comes from stories told afterward.

**The western rivers.** Within a few years, steamboats were running on the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers. Twenty-one steamboats arrived at New Orleans in 1814, and 2,770 arrived in 1846, more than 130 times as many. A steamboat covered about 50 miles a day in the 1810s and about 100 by the 1820s.

Much of what the boats carried was cotton, grown and picked by enslaved people whom their enslavers forced to work. Farms in the state of Mississippi grew 20 million pounds of cotton in 1820 and 535 million pounds in 1860. Before the Civil War, more than half of all the cotton grown in the United States passed through New Orleans.

**Boilers.** Steamboat boilers, the tanks where water was heated into steam under pressure, exploded again and again on the Mississippi and the rivers that flow into it in these years. The explosions killed thousands of people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-fulton" name="Robert Fulton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Fulton

> **Who:** The man who made the steamboat a business that lasted.
> **When and where:** The Hudson River in New York, from August 17, 1807.

**His second boat steamed 1,800 miles from Pittsburgh to New Orleans.**

Robert Fulton's partner, Robert Livingston, paid for the building of his boats. Livingston held a monopoly on steamboats in New York, which meant that by law no one else could run them there.

Fulton and Livingston paid for a second boat, the *New Orleans*, built at Pittsburgh. It left Pittsburgh on October 20, 1811, traveled more than 1,800 miles down the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers, and reached New Orleans on January 10, 1812. It was the first steamboat on the western rivers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-fulton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The National Road" -->
The National Road was the first highway in the United States built entirely with money from the federal government, the national government in Washington. Members of Congress approved it in 1806, while Thomas Jefferson was president. Work began at Cumberland, Maryland, in 1811. The road reached Wheeling, on the Ohio River, in 1818, and by the 1830s it went on to Vandalia, Illinois, where the federal money stopped.

**An older path.** West of Cumberland, the road followed a path that a Delaware chief named Nemacolin and a colonist named Thomas Cresap marked and cleared in 1749 or 1750. It ran between the Potomac and Monongahela Rivers, from the site of Cumberland to the mouth of Redstone Creek, where Brownsville, Pennsylvania, now stands.

George Washington used the path on his first trip to the west, and General Edward Braddock's army used it in the French and Indian War. People called it Braddock's Road until the National Road was built on the same route.

**Who built it.** Irish laborers built sections of the National Road for six dollars a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much did the Erie Canal save?" -->
Hauling a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City cost about $100 by wagon. On the Erie Canal it cost under $10, and some accounts put it at about $6 a ton.

The Erie Canal was a waterway that people dug so boats could travel where no river ran. Workers dug it from 1817 to 1825, and it stretched 363 miles, from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo on Lake Erie. While it was being dug, people who mocked it called it "Clinton's Ditch," after DeWitt Clinton, who pushed for it.

The canal opened on October 26, 1825, and cost about $7.1 million. The tolls that boats paid to use it covered the cost of building it in about nine years.

**The hoggees.** Boys drove the mules that pulled the canal boats, and people called them hoggees. Many were about 10 years old. A hoggee walked 18 to 20 miles a day, six hours on and six hours off, for about $8 to $10 a month. Many hoggees were cheated out of their pay. Columns on the canal boys in the Lockport *Union-Sun & Journal*, a newspaper in a canal town, tell of the cheating but do not name the men who did it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who dug the canals, and whose land they crossed" -->
American-born workers and Irish immigrants dug the Erie Canal. Between 2,000 and 3,000 laborers were working on it in 1818, with a horse for every second man. Ads in Ireland that called for workers promised wages of 8, 10 or 12 dollars a month, and meals came on top of that pay: meat three times a day, and liquor.

**The pay.** Many men were paid by the day instead, 75 cents to $1.50 a day, and less as more workers arrived. They got no pay on rainy days, but they still had to pay for their lodging. Some contractors, the men who ran sections of the work, started fights among the workers to get out of paying them.

At Rochester, a contractor named William Brittin brought about 30 convicts, people serving prison sentences, from Auburn State Prison to work. Many escaped, and Irish workers took their places.

**Malaria.** The canal crews reached the Montezuma Marshes, wet ground full of mosquitoes, in 1819. The mosquitoes spread malaria, a disease that brings high fevers and chills again and again. The canal commissioners, the state officials in charge of the canal, reported that about 1,000 workers fell sick between the middle of July and October 1, 1819. Many died, and no one kept a count of the dead.

**Whose land.** The Erie Canal ran through the homelands of the Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations. The Oneida Indian Nation's history of the canal states that the Oneida lost the land for it through a series of 27 treaties with the State of New York, and it describes all 27 as illegal. New York State officials took parts of the Seneca Oil Spring Reservation, land set aside for the Seneca, from 1858 to 1871 for a canal reservoir, a lake made to store water.

**In the South.** Owners rented out enslaved men to dig canals and kept the men's pay. Enslaved men dug most of the Dismal Swamp Canal, which runs 22 miles from Deep Creek, Virginia, to South Mills, North Carolina, from 1793 to 1805.

Before the canal, George Washington and his brother John Augustine Washington ran a business called the Dismal Swamp Company. Through it they bought 54 enslaved men, women and children to dig ditches and cut timber in the swamp.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" name="DeWitt Clinton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DeWitt Clinton

> **Who:** The New York governor who pushed the Erie Canal through while people mocked it.
> **When and where:** New York State, 1811 to 1825.

**He predicted the Iroquois would be gone from New York.**

DeWitt Clinton pushed for the Erie Canal while critics called it "Clinton's Ditch" and "Clinton's Folly." In 1811 he was the mayor of New York City and a new canal commissioner, one of the state officials planning the canal. Its planned route ran through the land of the Iroquois, six Native nations joined in one league and also called the Six Nations.

**The speech.** That year Clinton spoke to the New-York Historical Society. He predicted that "before the passing away of the present generation, not a single Iroquois will be seen in this state." He was wrong. More people of the Six Nations live in New York now than lived there at the end of the American Revolution.

**The wedding of the waters.** As governor, Clinton rode on the *Seneca Chief*, the first boat to travel the whole length of the canal, in 1825. At the opening he poured water from Lake Erie into New York harbor, and people called it the "wedding of the waters."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dewitt-clinton-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-garfield-transportation" name="James Garfield, canal boy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### James Garfield, canal boy

> **Who:** A 16-year-old who worked on an Ohio canal boat. He later became president of the United States.
> **When and where:** The Ohio and Erie Canal, 1848, on the boat *Evening Star*.

**At 16 he ran away to drive a canal boat's mules.**

James Garfield was 16 when he ran away in 1848 to work on a canal boat called the *Evening Star*. The only job open was driving the mules that pulled the boat. The boat carried freight, goods being shipped, on the Ohio and Erie Canal, and for about six weeks he helped haul coal, copper ore and wood.

Later he caught malaria and went home. His six weeks on the canal made a strong picture for his campaign for president in 1880. Writers of biographies for that campaign told stories of him falling into the canal, fourteen times by their count. That number comes from those later stories, not from the record of his time on the boat.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-garfield-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="moses-grandy" name="Moses Grandy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Moses Grandy

> **Who:** An enslaved man who ran boats through the Dismal Swamp canals, bought his own freedom three times, and described the men who dug the canals.
> **When and where:** Camden County, North Carolina, and the canals to Norfolk, Virginia, from the early 1800s. His book about his life was published in London in 1843.

**He paid for his own freedom three times.**

Moses Grandy was born into slavery in Camden County, North Carolina, around 1786. His owners hired him out each year to other men. He worked canal boats that carried shingles, boards and other freight through the Dismal Swamp canals to Norfolk.

**Three prices.** The first time, Grandy paid $600 to his owner, who kept the money and sold him to a man named Trewitt.

The second time, Grandy paid Trewitt $600, money he earned over two and a half years. But Trewitt had pledged Grandy as security for a loan, which meant the lender could take Grandy if the loan was not paid back. When Trewitt's business failed, the lender took Grandy and sold him to Enoch Sawyer, who put him to work in the fields.

The third time, a captain from Deep Creek, Edward Minner, lent Grandy $650, and Sawyer set him free for that price. Grandy paid Minner back over three years.

**His wife.** While Grandy worked on the canal, his wife's owner sold her. A group of enslaved people passed along the road beside the canal, and she called out to him. The man who had bought her, Mr. Rogerson, pulled out a pistol and said he would shoot Grandy if he came near the wagon. Grandy wrote: "I have never seen or heard of her from that day to this."

**The diggers.** Grandy also described the enslaved men who dug the canals. They worked "up to the middle or much deeper in mud and water, cutting away roots and baling out mud" (baling out means scooping out). They slept in open huts, still covered in mud, with no bedding. Once a month their owners came to collect the men's pay.

An overseer named Wiley M'Pherson, the man in charge of the workers, gave every man the same amount of digging each day. When men had not finished the day before, M'Pherson tied them up and flogged them. Flogging means whipping a person's bare back until the skin splits.

Then he had "pork or beef brine," the salt water used to keep meat, put "on their bleeding backs, to increase the pain." He left men tied all day with their feet barely touching the ground, while flies and mosquitoes settled on the cuts.

Grandy wrote: "I have seen him flog slaves with his own hands, till their entrails were visible" (entrails are the organs inside the body). He went on: "I have seen the sufferers dead when they were taken down. He never was called to account in any way for it."

**After freedom.** Grandy later married again. He lived in Boston, sawed wood, worked in coal yards, loaded and unloaded ships, and went on more voyages. He saved enough to buy his second wife out of slavery in Virginia, and she joined him in Boston.

He raised $450 to buy his son's freedom. His daughters in New Orleans paid for their own freedom, $1,200 for each of them. His book was sold to raise money for his relatives who were still enslaved.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="moses-grandy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first railroads" -->
The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad was the first railroad in the United States to carry both passengers and freight on a schedule for anyone who paid. Its builders laid the first stone on July 4, 1828. Its first 13 miles of track, from Baltimore to Ellicott's Mills, opened in 1830.

**A legend.** In a well-known story, Peter Cooper's steam engine, the *Tom Thumb*, raced a horse pulling a railroad car on August 28, 1830, and lost when a belt on the engine slipped. The story comes from a talk that John H. B. Latrobe gave in 1868, 38 years later. No record from 1830 shows that the race took place, and the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad Museum's page on the *Tom Thumb* describes the race as a legend.

New York State's first steam passenger train ran on August 9, 1831, behind a locomotive, or train engine, named the *DeWitt Clinton*. It went 16 miles from Albany to Schenectady in about an hour, on the Mohawk and Hudson line.

**In the city.** Abraham Brower ran a horse-drawn coach called an omnibus on the streets of New York City from 1827. The *John Mason*, a horse-drawn car that rolled on rails, began carrying passengers in lower Manhattan for the New York and Harlem Railroad on November 14, 1832. It was the first street railway in the country.

**Who built the Southern railroads.** In the South, enslaved people built most of the railroads. The men running Southern railroad companies bought hundreds of enslaved men between the ages of 16 and 35 as early as 1841, and in the 1850s they bought or rented enslaved workers in groups of hundreds.

The historian Theodore Kornweibel Jr. found enslaved workers on 85 of the 113 railroads in the Confederate states, the Southern states that later fought the United States in the Civil War. Southern railroad companies owned about 15,000 enslaved people in 1860, and women and children were among them.

The president of the Mississippi Central Railroad told the people who owned shares in the company in 1855 that "the slave is preferable to free labor, and far better adapted to the construction of railways in the south." By free labor he meant workers who were not enslaved.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 14 minutes**

Railroad builders laid track across the whole country in the second half of the 1800s. The country had about 9,000 miles of railroad track in 1850 and more than 190,000 by 1900, more than twenty times as much. Counts for 1900 run from about 193,000 to 215,000 miles, depending on whether side tracks are counted.

**Coast to coast.** The first railroad across the country was finished in 1869. Central Pacific managers hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers to build its western part. The tracks crossed the lands of the Cheyenne, Lakota, Arapaho, Shoshone and Paiute nations.

In the cities, streetcars ran on rails laid in the streets, and by the end of the century the companies that ran them were switching from horse-drawn cars to electric ones. Many Americans, women among them, were riding bicycles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The railroad across the continent" -->
Two companies built the first railroad across the country. Union Pacific crews built west from Omaha, Nebraska, while Central Pacific crews built east from Sacramento, California. The two lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869.

**Land and loans.** Members of Congress voted to give the two companies land and loans, and President Abraham Lincoln signed their Pacific Railroad Act on July 1, 1862. Under it, each company got 6,400 acres of public land, ten square miles, for every mile of track it built. Each also got up to $48,000 in government bonds, a kind of loan, for every mile. The top rate was for miles built through mountains.

The act also stated: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act" (to extinguish a title means to end someone's ownership of land). With those words, the lawmakers directed federal officials to end Native nations' ownership of the land along the railroad as fast as they could.

Members of Congress voted to give railroads about 130 million acres of federal land from 1850 to 1871, and some counts run as high as 175 million acres. State lawmakers gave railroads about 51 million acres more.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the railroad crossed" -->
The companies' builders and US officials took land from many Native nations while the two lines were built, and they drove people from their homes. Among those nations were the Cheyenne, Lakota (also called Sioux), Arapaho, Shoshone and Paiute. Historians at the Utah Division of State History count 15 Native homelands that the track crossed.

**The bison.** The Lakota, Arapaho and Cheyenne hunted bison, also called buffalo, for food, shelter and trade. As the railroad crews pushed west, soldiers, railroad workers and travelers hunted the bison too. National Park Service writers state that the number of bison fell from tens of millions in the early 1800s until they had almost died out.

**Sand Creek.** The railroad crews worked in the middle of a war between the US Army and the Cheyenne, Arapaho and Lakota nations. US soldiers on horseback led by Colonel John Chivington attacked a camp of Cheyenne and Arapaho people at Sand Creek, in Colorado, on November 29, 1864. The camp flew a US flag and a white flag.

The soldiers killed about 230 people, most of them women and children, and the people they killed were not armed. Britannica and the National Park Service give that count, and PBS writers give 150. Chapter 2 tells Sand Creek in full.

**Striking back.** Cheyenne, Arapaho and Lakota fighters struck back for Sand Creek. They burned the town of Julesburg, Colorado, on January 7, 1865, and cut the telegraph line, the wire that carried messages by electric signals.

**Guards for the crews.** Six days later, on January 13, 1865, four army units of Pawnee soldiers, called companies, joined the US Army under Major Frank J. North. Union Pacific managers and US officials hired hundreds of Pawnee, who guarded the work crews alongside the Army. Some Paiute people worked for the Central Pacific.

The fighting reached the tracks. At Plum Creek, Nebraska, on August 27, 1867, Cheyenne men pulled up railroad track, wrecked a work train and killed all but one of its crew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chinese workers" -->
Central Pacific managers hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers from 1865 to 1869. Most came from Guangdong, a province in southern China. The Chinese workers were paid about $35 a month, and they had to buy their own food and housing. White workers got about $42 a month, plus housing.

**The dead.** The Central Pacific's managers kept no record of the Chinese workers who died, so no one knows how many there were. Avalanches, masses of snow sliding down the mountains, killed workers in the winter of 1866 to 1867, when 44 storms hit. In one, snow carried away the camp at Strong's Canyon.

Blasts of black powder and nitroglycerin, explosives used to cut through rock, killed others. Smallpox, a disease that spreads from person to person, killed more in 1868 and 1869.

Guesses of the number run from about 50 into the thousands. On June 30, 1870, a writer at the *Sacramento Union* reported "about fifty." The same day, a writer at the *Sacramento Reporter* told of "the accumulated bones of perhaps 1,200," about 20,000 pounds of bones being shipped home to China. Researchers on the online forum of the Central Pacific Railroad Photographic History Museum estimate 100 to 150 dead, and other estimates run to hundreds or thousands.

**The strike.** Chinese workers in the Sierra Nevada mountains went on strike on June 25, 1867, which means they stopped work to demand better terms. Estimates of how many struck run from 2,000 to 5,000. They asked for $40 a month instead of $35, for workdays of no more than ten hours, and for shorter shifts in the cramped tunnels. It was the largest strike by workers in the country up to that time.

The construction boss, Charles Crocker, and his foreman, James Harvey Strobridge, who ran the work crews, cut off food and supplies to the Chinese camps. After a week with little food, Crocker told the strikers that men who went back at once would only be fined. Men who stayed out would lose all their pay for June.

Most men, weak from lack of food, went back. The men who refused faced a group of armed white men, and work started again. Crocker gave the strikers no raise, though pay for experienced men later rose a little, according to the Library of Congress.

**Ten miles in a day.** Crews laid 10 miles and 56 feet of track in one day on April 28, 1869. Eight Irish workers carried the rails, and several thousand Chinese workers did the rest of the work.

**The last track.** Twelve days later, on May 10, 1869, a different crew of eight, all of them Chinese, laid the last track at Promontory Summit, joining the two lines, and a golden spike marked the place. Three of those eight, Ging Cui, Wong Fook and Lee Shao, rode on a float at the celebration of the railroad's 50th anniversary on May 10, 1919.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hung-wah" name="Hung Wah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hung Wah

> **Who:** The leading Chinese headman on the Central Pacific.
> **When and where:** The Central Pacific Railroad, from January 1864.

**The payroll names him, and not most of his workers.**

Hung Wah is one of the few Chinese railroad workers whose names appear in the records. The company's payroll, its list of the people it paid, usually named only headmen, the men who led the Chinese work gangs. The names of most of the thousands of Chinese laborers are not in the records.

Hung Wah first appears on the Central Pacific's payroll in January 1864. With another headman, Ah Toy, he supervised the railroad's first Chinese crew, about 23 men. He became the leading Chinese headman on the line, over hundreds of Chinese workers and at times thousands. He handled the workers' pay and their living arrangements, and he dealt with the company for them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hung-wah" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" name="Cornelius Vanderbilt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cornelius Vanderbilt

> **Who:** A ferryman who moved his money from steamboats into railroads and left the largest American fortune of his time.
> **When and where:** New York, 1810 to 1877.

**The ferryman of 1810 died in 1877 with about $100 million.**

Cornelius Vanderbilt took control of the New York Central Railroad, in the East, in 1867, the same year Chinese crews were building the Central Pacific through the Sierra Nevada. He had owned steamboats, and early in the 1860s he moved his money into railroads. He joined the New York Central with his Hudson River Railroad on November 1, 1869, and in 1871 he opened Grand Central Depot, a train station in Manhattan.

**The ferryman.** Vanderbilt had started at 16, in 1810, carrying freight and passengers between Staten Island and Manhattan in a periauger, a shallow sailboat with two masts. Other boat captains called him "the Commodore," the title of a navy officer who commands a group of ships, at first as a joke.

**Two stories.** Accounts of how he got the boat differ. One says he borrowed $100 from his mother and named the boat *Swiftsure*. The first account of his life, printed in 1853, says the boat was his father's and he kept half the profit. The website Biography.com describes the story of the young ferryman as a legend.

When he died in 1877, he left the largest American fortune of his time.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cornelius-vanderbilt-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mark-twain-transportation" name="Mark Twain, steamboat pilot" movie="Mark Twain (2001)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mark Twain, steamboat pilot

> **Who:** Samuel Clemens, who steered steamboats on the Mississippi River before he wrote books as Mark Twain.
> **When and where:** The Mississippi River. He got his pilot's license on April 9, 1859.
> **Movie:** *Mark Twain* (2001), a documentary by Ken Burns about his whole life. PBS showed it in January 2002.

**"Mark twain" was a river call meaning twelve feet of water.**

Steamboats still ran on the Mississippi River while the railroads spread. Samuel Clemens was a licensed steamboat pilot, the person who steers the boat, on the Mississippi. He got his license on April 9, 1859, when he was 23. He piloted boats until 1861, when the Civil War began and traffic on the river stopped.

He took his pen name from a call used on the river. A leadsman, the crew member who measured the depth of the water, called the depth out to the pilot. "Mark twain" meant two fathoms, and a fathom is six feet, so the call meant twelve feet of water. In 1883 he published *Life on the Mississippi*, a book about his years as a pilot.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mark-twain-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Clocks, sleeping cars and danger on the railroads" -->
**Time zones.** Railroad managers in the United States and Canada began using standard time zones, regions where all clocks show the same time, on November 18, 1883. Before then, each railroad company ran its trains by its own clocks. William F. Allen, the editor of the *Traveler's Official Railway Guide*, a book of train schedules, drew up the plan.

On the day of the switch, railroad workers reset every railroad clock to the new time, even clocks that had already passed noon. In those places noon came twice, so people called it "the day of two noons." Members of Congress did not vote time zones into law until the Standard Time Act of March 19, 1918.

**Sleeping cars.** George Pullman built the *Pioneer*, his first sleeping car built for luxury, in 1865. It had upper beds that folded away and seats that pulled out. His company, the Pullman Palace Car Company, began in 1867.

**Danger.** About 8.5 of every 1,000 American trainmen, the workers who ran the trains, were killed on the job in 1889, twice the rate in Britain.

In the same year, 1.73 of every 1,000 trainmen died while coupling, the job of joining railroad cars together.

With the old link-and-pin coupler, a brakeman, the crew member who set the brakes and joined the cars, had to stand between two moving cars to join them. Mangled hands were known as "the badge of the experienced worker."

**Safer equipment.** Safer couplers and brakes existed for years before lawmakers required railroad owners to use them. Eli Janney got a patent, the legal right to be the only one to make and sell an invention, for an automatic coupler in 1873. It joined cars without a worker standing between them. George Westinghouse's air brake existed from 1869. A pump on the locomotive pushed air through hoses to every car, and when the engineer opened a valve, the air set the brakes on all the cars at once.

Members of Congress passed the Safety Appliance Act in 1893. Under it, railroad owners had to put automatic couplers and air brakes on trains that crossed state lines, starting in 1900. Accidents while coupling cars fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902, while the number of railroad workers grew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who decided where Black passengers sat?" -->
Train crews and lawmakers decided where Black passengers could sit. Ida B. Wells had a first-class ticket on a train in Tennessee in 1884, but the train crew ordered her into the car for Black passengers. She refused, and they forced her off the train. She sued the railroad and won $500 in a Tennessee court. The judges of the Tennessee Supreme Court threw out that decision in 1887.

**Plessy.** Louisiana lawmakers passed the Separate Car Act on June 10, 1890. Under it, railroads had to provide "equal but separate" cars for Black and white passengers. A passenger who broke the law could be fined $25 or jailed for 20 days.

Homer Plessy, a shoemaker in New Orleans, offered to test the law. To test it, he would break it on purpose so that judges would have to decide whether the law was allowed. He bought a ticket on the East Louisiana Railroad on June 7, 1892, and sat in the car for white passengers. The railroad's officials agreed to the test, because separate cars cost the company money. A private detective hired by the group planning the test, the Citizens' Committee, arrested him.

Judge John H. Ferguson ruled against Plessy. On May 18, 1896, the justices of the US Supreme Court ruled against him too, 7 to 1, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*. With that ruling, the justices allowed states to keep separating Black and white passengers by law. Nearly sixty years later, in 1954, the justices of the Supreme Court threw out that ruling's reasoning in *Brown v. Board of Education*.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Electric streetcars and bicycles" -->
Electric streetcars replaced horse-drawn ones in American cities between 1888 and 1902. Electric streetcars had run earlier in East Cleveland in 1884, in Baltimore in 1885 and in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1886. The Dictionary of American History describes the system that Frank Sprague built in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888 as the first successful one.

The owners of about 200 streetcar systems had switched to electricity or planned to by 1891. Street railway track grew from about 8,000 miles in 1890 to 22,500 miles in 1902. In those same years, the share of streetcar service pulled by animals fell from 75 percent to 1 percent. The last horse-drawn streetcar in paid service in New York City ran on July 26, 1917.

**Bicycles.** Cycling became a craze in the 1890s, something that very many people took up at the same time. Riders used the safety bicycle, which had two wheels of the same size, a chain drive and air-filled tires. Women rode as well as men.

Susan B. Anthony told the reporter Nellie Bly in the *New York World* on February 2, 1896, that bicycling "has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world" (to emancipate is to set free). She said: "I stand and rejoice every time I see a woman ride by on a wheel."

### Frank Sprague

> **Who:** The engineer whose streetcar system in Richmond showed that electric streetcars could work in a city.
> **When and where:** Richmond, Virginia. Regular service began February 2, 1888.

**Ten electric cars began running in Richmond on February 2, 1888.**

Frank Sprague built the Richmond Union Passenger Railway, which began regular electric streetcar service that day with 10 cars. By May 1888 it had about 12 miles of track and 23 cars.

Within about two years, about 110 electric railways had been built or ordered using Sprague's equipment. Members of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, a society of engineers, have named his Richmond railway a milestone in the history of engineering. Chapter 6 tells how builders put up new neighborhoods along the electric lines.
