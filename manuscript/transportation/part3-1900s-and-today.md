## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 10 minutes**

Orville and Wilbur Wright flew a powered airplane in 1903, and Ford's Model T car went on sale five years later, for $825. Workers at Ford built 15 million Model Ts by 1927. The first airline to fly on a schedule began in 1914, across Tampa Bay in Florida, and by 1927 airplanes were carrying mail and paying passengers on schedules.

Trains carried about 43.7 million members of the armed forces in World War II, from 1941 to 1945. In Virginia, bus drivers had the power of police officers to make Black passengers sit apart from white passengers and to arrest riders who refused.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Model T and the new roads" -->
The Ford Model T went on sale on October 1, 1908, at $825, and its price fell to $260 by 1925. From 1913 Ford's workers built it on a moving assembly line, and the time it took to build a car's frame fell from more than 12 hours to about 93 minutes. Chapter 8 tells how the line worked. The 15 millionth Model T left the factory on May 26, 1927, the last day Ford's workers made the car.

**The Lincoln Highway.** A group of private backers, among them Carl Fisher and Henry Joy, laid out the Lincoln Highway in 1913. It was the first named road for cars across the whole country, from Times Square in New York City to San Francisco. After 1926 highway officials made parts of it into sections of numbered routes.

**Route 66.** State and federal highway officials gave the main highways of the United States numbers on November 11, 1926, and Route 66 got its number that day. It ran 2,448 miles, from Chicago to Santa Monica, California, and people called it the "Main Street of America." It was fully paved only by the late 1930s.

**Crashes.** Car crashes killed about 4,100 to 4,200 people in the United States in 1913. In 1937 they killed about 37,800 to 39,600, about nine times as many, which was about 30 of every 100,000 Americans. The counts differ because the National Safety Council, a private safety group, and the federal officials who kept the national tables counted deaths in different ways.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-transportation" name="The Wright brothers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers

> **Who:** Orville and Wilbur Wright, the brothers who flew their powered airplane four times in one day.
> **When and where:** Near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, December 17, 1903.

**The first flight lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet.**

Orville and Wilbur Wright made four flights in a powered airplane near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, on December 17, 1903. Orville flew first. Wilbur made the fourth and last flight of the day, 852 feet in 59 seconds. Five people watched, and one of them, John T. Daniels, took the photograph. He belonged to a lifesaving station, a crew that rescued people from wrecked ships.

About ten years after those flights, an airplane in Florida was carrying paying passengers on a schedule. Chapter 8 tells how the brothers built their airplane.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the airlines began" -->
The St. Petersburg-Tampa Airboat Line in Florida was the first airline in the world to fly on a schedule. Its first flight left on January 1, 1914. The pilot, Tony Jannus, flew a Benoist flying boat, a plane that takes off from and lands on water. The trip across Tampa Bay took about 23 minutes, low over the water.

Abram C. Pheil, a former mayor of St. Petersburg, paid $400 at an auction for the first round-trip ticket. The airline ran for about four months.

**Airmail.** Army pilots began flying mail on a regular route on May 15, 1918, 218 miles from Washington to Philadelphia to New York. Post Office pilots took over in August 1918. Members of Congress passed the Kelly Act in 1925, and under it private companies flew the airmail on contract. The airline companies began with those mail contracts.

**After Lindbergh.** Charles Lindbergh, an airmail pilot, flew the *Spirit of St. Louis* alone and without stopping from New York to Paris on May 20 and 21, 1927, about 3,600 miles in 33 hours and 30 minutes. Chapter 1 tells the flight in full. Airline passengers in the United States rose from 5,782 in 1926 to 173,405 in 1928, about 30 times as many. Applications for pilot licenses tripled in 1927.

**The DC-3.** The Douglas DC-3 airliner first flew passengers in 1936. It carried 21 or more passengers at up to 207 miles an hour. In the words of van der Linden, a curator, or collection keeper, at the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum, the DC-3 is "widely considered to be the first airliner capable of making money just by carrying passengers." By 1939 DC-3s carried about 90 percent of the world's airline travel.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-knight" name="Jack Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Knight

> **Who:** An airmail pilot who flew through the night and a snowstorm to finish a test that kept the airmail going.
> **When and where:** North Platte, Nebraska, to Chicago, the night of February 22 to 23, 1921.

**The next pilot would not fly in the storm, so Knight did.**

Post Office officials ran a test in February 1921 to show that airplanes could carry mail across the country faster than trains. Pilots flew the mail in a relay, day and night, each pilot handing it to the next. Jack Knight flew his part, from North Platte, Nebraska, to Omaha.

At Omaha the next pilot refused to take off in the storm. Knight flew the mail on to Chicago himself, at night, through snow and fog, over ground he had never flown before. The mail crossed the country, 2,629 miles, in 33 hours and 20 minutes. After the test, members of Congress voted money to keep the airmail flying.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-knight" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The sleeping-car porters" -->
Pullman Company managers hired Black men as the porters who served passengers in the company's sleeping cars. After the Civil War, George Pullman looked for men who had been enslaved to hire as his porters. By the early 1900s the Pullman Company was the largest single employer of Black men in the United States.

**400 hours.** Porters often had to work at least 400 hours a month to earn a full month's pay. A porter who worked every day of a 30-day month would still need more than 13 hours a day to reach 400. Their pay was among the lowest of all the workers on the train. Company managers built tips from passengers into the porters' pay, which saved the company money. To earn those tips, porters had to do whatever passengers asked.

**"George."** Passengers called porters "George," whatever their real names were, after George Pullman. Calling an enslaved person by the owner's name was a practice from slavery. Some passengers called the porters "boy."

The porters formed a union, a group of workers who bargain together with their employer. The union's leaders won a contract in 1937 that limited the porters' work to 240 hours a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" name="The porters of Miles of Smiles" movie="Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle (1982)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The porters of Miles of Smiles

> **Who:** C. L. Dellums, E. D. Nixon and six other retired Pullman porters, with Rosina Tucker, a porter's wife, who described the porters' work in their own words.
> **When and where:** Pullman sleeping cars across the country, from the 1910s to the 1940s. Filmed in 1982.
> **Movie:** *Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle* (1982), a documentary by Jack Santino and Paul Wagner, made from the porters' own accounts.

**On the job, the porters had to smile for the passengers.**

Eight retired Pullman porters told two filmmakers in 1982 what their job had been like. Rosina Tucker, a porter's wife who worked to build the porters' union, tells the story in the film. The porters describe the smile Pullman managers required them to wear and the nights they worked without sleep.

Two of the men in the film are C. L. Dellums and E. D. Nixon. Pullman managers fired Dellums in October 1927 for organizing porters into a union. Nixon later called the meeting that organized the Montgomery bus boycott. From December 1955 to December 1956, most of the 40,000 Black people of Montgomery, Alabama, stayed off the city's buses, until federal judges ended bus segregation there. Chapter 25 tells the boycott in full.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trains and Jeeps in World War II" -->
Trains carried about 43.7 million members of the armed forces from December 1941 to August 1945, more than 97 percent of all the troops moved in organized groups. About 100,000 of them rode troop trains on an average day, and half of all Pullman sleeping cars were in use carrying troops.

**The Jeep.** About 640,000 Jeeps, small army cars, were built in the war. About 363,000 of them were the Willys MB, and about 280,000 were the Ford GPW.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drivers with the power to arrest" -->
Under Virginia law, buses had to seat Black and white passengers apart, and bus drivers were "special policemen." A driver could decide a passenger's race, order the passenger to another seat, throw the passenger off the bus and make an arrest. Refusing a driver was a crime with a fine of $5 to $25. Greyhound's own rules stated that the company had "full control and discretion as to the seating of passengers" (discretion means the power to decide).

**Riders thrown off.** Bessie Nelson and Mamie Kinchlow were thrown off a Greyhound bus in Alexandria, Virginia, in August 1932 and jailed for more than a week. Mildred Turpin and Caroline Johnson, clerks for the Navy Department, were jailed at Clarendon, Virginia, on January 16, 1943. Tonito Holmes was beaten, removed from a Greyhound bus and fined $14.25 in Johnson City, Tennessee, on February 19, 1945. The Encyclopedia Virginia account of these cases does not name the drivers and officers who threw the riders off, jailed them or beat them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="irene-morgan" name="Irene Morgan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Irene Morgan

> **Who:** A Baltimore war worker who refused to give up her bus seat in Virginia and won her case in the US Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** A Greyhound bus near Saluda, Virginia, July 16, 1944. The Supreme Court decided her case on June 3, 1946.

**She tore up the sheriff's warrant and threw it out the window.**

Irene Morgan, of Baltimore, was 27 and built B-26 bombers in a wartime factory. She had been visiting her mother in Gloucester County, Virginia, and on July 16, 1944, she took a Greyhound bus home to her husband and to see a doctor. Near Saluda, two white passengers got on the crowded bus. The driver told Morgan and the woman next to her, a Black woman holding a baby, to move to the back seat.

"I refused to move," Morgan said years later. The driver drove the bus to the jail in Saluda, where the sheriff showed her a warrant, a paper that allows an arrest. She "just took it and tore it up and just threw it out the window." "So then he put his hands on me," she said, and she kicked him. The sheriff and a deputy forced her off the bus.

**The courts.** Her mother paid $500 to get her out of jail. Morgan paid a $100 fine for resisting arrest on October 18, 1944. She would not admit breaking the seating law. A judge found her guilty of it anyway, and she refused to pay the $10 fine. The Encyclopedia Virginia account of her case does not name the judge.

Her lawyer was Spottswood W. Robinson III, and the lawyers Thurgood Marshall and William H. Hastie of the NAACP, a civil rights group, joined him. The judges of the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals ruled against her on June 6, 1945.

The justices of the US Supreme Court ruled for her on June 3, 1946. Justice Stanley Reed wrote that a passenger crossing several states could meet a different seating rule in each one, and under Virginia's law might have to change seats again and again inside Virginia. Seating on trips across state lines, he wrote, needed "a single, uniform rule," so Virginia's law could not apply to those buses.

The ruling covered only trips between states. Reporters at the *Afro-American* newspaper wrote that bus companies' seating rules did not change right away.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="irene-morgan" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 11 minutes**

Three new ways of moving people and goods over long distances began between 1956 and 1958: Interstate highways, shipping containers and American jet airliners across the Atlantic. President Dwight Eisenhower signed the law for the Interstates, the big highways that join the states, in 1956, the same year a ship called the *Ideal X* carried 58 shipping containers from New Jersey to Texas.

Pan Am's jets crossed the Atlantic about twice as fast as the older planes. Private railroads were losing money on passenger trains, and in 1971 a new company, Amtrak, took over most of them.

**Homes taken.** Highway officials forced the people of more than 475,000 households, more than a million people, out of their homes to make room for roads paid for by the national government. That number is the US Department of Transportation's estimate, a figure worked out rather than an exact count.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Interstate highways" -->
President Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act on June 29, 1956. The law allowed for 41,000 miles of new highways, called the Interstate Highway System. It was the largest public works program, a set of projects that the government pays for and the public uses, in the history of the United States up to then. The system is about 48,900 miles long today, and in 1990 members of Congress added Eisenhower's name to it.

The new Interstates went around Route 66. Officials of AASHTO, the association of state highway officials, took Route 66 off the list of numbered US highways on June 27, 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" name="Dwight Eisenhower" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Dwight Eisenhower

> **Who:** The president who signed the Interstate highway law, 37 years after he crossed the country in an Army motor convoy.
> **When and where:** The 1919 convoy from Washington, D.C., to San Francisco. The highway law, June 29, 1956.

**In 1919 an Army convoy needed 62 days to cross the country.**

The Army's first convoy of motor vehicles, a group of 81 vehicles traveling together, crossed the country in 1919. It went 3,251 miles, from Washington, D.C., to San Francisco. Its vehicles sank in mud and broke down, and bridges failed under them.

Dwight Eisenhower was a young Army officer, a lieutenant colonel, and he volunteered to go. He wrote a report on the trip. At the end of World War II, in 1945, he saw the autobahns, Germany's highways. After those two experiences, he argued for a system of highways across the United States, and as president he signed the law for it on June 29, 1956.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The homes the highways took" -->
The people who planned the Interstates ran many of them through city neighborhoods, and they took the homes that stood in the way. By the US Department of Transportation's estimate, highway officials took the homes of more than 475,000 households, more than a million people, for federal roads.

The planning scholar Alan Altshuler estimated in those years that the new system would push out up to a million people, "a disproportionate number of them Black," meaning more than their share. The law professor Deborah N. Archer says the neighborhoods chosen "were largely Black and poor."

**Montgomery.** Interstate 85 ran south of Montgomery, Alabama, in the plans drawn up in 1955. Sam Engelhardt became head of the Alabama State Highway Department in 1959. His business cards read "I Stand for White Supremacy and Segregation," a stand for white rule and for keeping Black and white people apart by law.

Under Engelhardt, state highway officials chose a final route through Montgomery's only middle-class Black neighborhood. Ralph Abernathy, a minister and civil rights leader, lived there, and Alabama State University stood next to it. An engineering firm had suggested a route through Oak Park, which city officials had closed rather than let Black people use it.

Highway officials took about 356 homes in the Centennial Hill, Bel Air and Bottoms neighborhoods and paid owners an average of $3,300 a house. Abernathy sent a telegram, a short message sent over electric wires, to President John F. Kennedy in 1961. He wrote that the route "will destroy one of the best negro neighborhoods in the south" ("negro" was the common word for Black people in his time). Rebecca Retzlaff, a researcher at Auburn University, told this history in a 2019 study.

**Rondo.** Highway officials built Interstate 94 through Rondo, a Black neighborhood in St. Paul, Minnesota, from 1956 to 1968. Counts of what was lost differ: about 600 families by one count, about 700 homes and 300 businesses by another. Chapter 6 tells Rondo's story in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jet airliners, a crash and cheaper fares" -->
**The Grand Canyon crash.** Two airliners, TWA Flight 2 and United Air Lines Flight 718, left Los Angeles three minutes apart on June 30, 1956, and flew into each other over the Grand Canyon in Arizona. The crash killed all 128 people on the two planes, the deadliest airplane crash in the world up to that time. It was a major reason the Federal Aviation Agency was set up in 1958.

**The jet.** A Pan Am Boeing 707 jet flew from New York to Paris with 111 passengers and 12 crew members on October 26, 1958, in about 8 hours. It was the first jet service across the Atlantic by an American airline. The 707 flew about twice as fast as the older propeller airliners and carried about twice as many people.

**Fares.** Until 1978 the members of a federal board, the Civil Aeronautics Board, or CAB, decided which airlines could start or stop flying and what they could charge. President Jimmy Carter signed the Airline Deregulation Act on October 24, 1978, and under that law the board lost its control of fares and routes. Under the CAB Sunset Act, a part of that law, lawmakers set dates for the board to lose its powers one by one, and the board shut down on December 31, 1984.

Average fares fell about 40 to 45 percent in the decades after 1978, counted in dollars adjusted for rising prices, by government and industry figures. Two economists, experts on money, prices and trade, studied the change for a research group, the Brookings Institution, and found that fares ended up lower than they would have been under the old rules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-dusty-roads" name="Barbara 'Dusty' Roads" movie="Fly With Me (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara "Dusty" Roads

> **Who:** An American Airlines stewardess who fought the airline's rule that fired women at age 32 or when they married.
> **When and where:** American Airlines, from 1950. She lived from 1928 to 2023.
> **Movie:** *Fly With Me* (2024), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about flight attendants, directed by Sarah Colt and Helen Dobrowski. Roads appears in it.

**American's managers fired stewardesses at 32 or when they married.**

Barbara Roads, called Dusty, joined American Airlines in 1950 as a stewardess, a woman who served passengers on an airplane. The job is now called flight attendant. In a 1953 contract, American's managers put in a rule that any stewardess hired after November 1953 would be fired at age 32. A stewardess who married was fired too.

In a radio interview, Roads gave her view of the reason: "It was cheaper to have someone that'll never get married, never have babies, never get pensions, never get the highest rate of pay."

**Fighting the rule.** Roads lobbied members of Congress, trying to talk them into acting, on her own flights to Washington, D.C. In 1963 she held a news conference that made the papers. At news conferences she also asked reporters to pick the 32-year-old stewardesses out of a lineup. Representative Martha Griffiths helped her.

The stewardesses came close to a strike, a refusal to work until their demands were met, in August 1968. By Roads's account, American's managers dropped the age rule the day after someone asked Griffiths to press the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the federal office that handles complaints of unfair treatment at work.

Roads helped start the Association of Professional Flight Attendants, a union, in 1977. She worked for American Airlines for 44 years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-dusty-roads" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Freedom Rides" -->
Black and white riders traveled together by bus into the South in May 1961, and people called them Freedom Riders. They were testing a court order that banned separating Black and white travelers in bus stations used for trips between states. Two groups left Washington, D.C., on May 4 on a Greyhound bus and a Trailways bus, headed for New Orleans. Among the Black riders was John Lewis, who later led the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, a civil rights group.

**Anniston.** A white mob with clubs and bats attacked the Greyhound at Anniston, Alabama, on May 14. They broke its windows and slashed its tires, then chased the bus until the flat tires stopped it several miles out of town. One of the attackers threw a firebomb, a bomb that starts a fire, through a window. The riders and other passengers barely got out of the flames.

A state officer riding the bus in plain clothes held the attackers back with his gun. The mob beat the riders while the bus burned, until police arrived. Later that day white men got on the Trailways bus at Anniston and beat the riders on it. Their beating left one rider, Walter Bergman, a white man, with permanent brain damage.

**Birmingham.** The same day, a crowd met the Trailways bus in Birmingham with pipes, chains and baseball bats. Many in the crowd belonged to the Ku Klux Klan, a group that used violence against Black people and their supporters. Eugene "Bull" Connor, the city official in charge of the police, had told Klan leaders that the police would hold back from the bus station for 15 minutes, and he encouraged the Klan to attack the riders.

With no police there, Klansmen beat the riders in the waiting room and in a dark hallway. They beat James Peck, a white rider, until he was unconscious, and he needed more than 50 stitches. Walter Bergman was among the riders they beat, for the second time that day.

**Montgomery.** A white crowd in Montgomery attacked a new group of riders arriving by Greyhound on May 20. They beat John Seigenthaler, a Justice Department official, until he was unconscious. Alabama's public safety director had arranged protection for the buses up to Montgomery, but Montgomery police did not take it over, and the director went into the fighting himself. Governor John Patterson blamed the riders for the violence.

**The ban.** The rides went on, and over the summer of 1961 the number of Freedom Riders grew to more than 400. Justice Department officials asked the members of the Interstate Commerce Commission, the federal officials in charge of trade and travel between states, to act. The commissioners banned keeping Black and white passengers apart in bus and train stations. They issued the ban on September 22, 1961, and it took effect on November 1, 1961. People in many Southern towns kept ignoring it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Amtrak takes over the passenger trains" -->
Private railroad companies were losing money on their passenger trains. Members of Congress passed the Rail Passenger Service Act in 1970 and created a new company called Amtrak. Amtrak began running most of the passenger trains between American cities on May 1, 1971, after the private railroads gave them up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much did a box save?" -->
Loading a ton of cargo onto a ship by hand cost about $5.86 in 1956. Loading it in containers cost about 16 cents, less than a thirtieth as much, by the count of the writer Marc Levinson in his book *The Box*.

Before containers, dockworkers loaded cargo onto ships by hand. A ship called the *Ideal X* left Port Newark, New Jersey, on April 26, 1956, carrying 58 metal boxes, each 35 feet long, the size of a truck trailer. These boxes are called shipping containers. The ship reached Houston, Texas, five days later.

Members of an international standards group set standard sizes for containers in the late 1960s, and the same boxes came into use around the world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="malcom-mclean" name="Malcom McLean" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Malcom McLean

> **Who:** A North Carolina trucking owner who sold his trucking company to start shipping truck-sized boxes by sea.
> **When and where:** Port Newark, New Jersey, April 26, 1956.

**His trucking company began with one used truck in 1934.**

Malcom McLean built that one truck into a trucking company in North Carolina. Then he sold the trucking company and put the money into carrying containers by sea, and in 1956 he sent the *Ideal X* to sea with them.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="malcom-mclean" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The deadliest year on the roads" -->
Car crashes killed 54,589 people in the United States in 1972, the most in any single year before or since. By 1975 the yearly count had fallen to 44,525.

Federal safety officials have given part of the credit to a lower speed limit. During the oil shortage of 1973 and 1974, which Chapter 10 tells, members of Congress set a national speed limit of 55 miles an hour to save fuel, and by March 1974 every state had lowered its limit to match. A federal task force later estimated that the 55-mile limit saved 20,000 to 30,000 lives from 1974 to 1978.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The minivan" -->
Workers in Windsor, Ontario, in Canada, built the first Dodge Caravan and Plymouth Voyager minivans on November 2, 1983. The minivans went on sale as 1984 models.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 12 minutes**

With a phone in hand, Americans after 2000 could find their way and hire a ride, and some of them bought electric cars. About 8 of every 100 new cars sold in 2024 ran on batteries alone, according to Kelley Blue Book. About 7 of every 100 bridges in the country were rated in poor condition in federal records in 2025, by the count of a road builders' group.

**Deaths.** Federal safety officials counted 43,230 deaths in car crashes in 2021, and their early estimate for 2025, published in April 2026, is about 36,640. A container ship that had lost power hit a bridge in Baltimore in 2024, and the bridge's fall killed six road workers. An Army helicopter and a passenger jet collided near Washington, D.C., in 2025, and the crash killed all 67 people aboard.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maps and rides on a phone" -->
GPS, the Global Positioning System, uses signals from satellites to show a GPS device where it is. Until 2000 a setting called Selective Availability made GPS less exact for ordinary users. Federal officials switched the setting off a few minutes after midnight at the end of May 1, 2000, and the error of an ordinary GPS device shrank from about 100 meters to about 20, according to the government's GPS website.

**Ride apps.** People also began hiring rides through apps on their phones. Uber began in March 2009 as a company called UberCab and started its app service in San Francisco on July 5, 2010. In October 2010, officials of San Francisco's city transit agency and of California's Public Utilities Commission ordered UberCab to stop operating. Its owners changed the company's name to Uber that month, so that it would not look like a taxi business. Lyft started its service in June 2012.

In a ride-hailing app, a rider asks for a ride, and a driver using a personal car takes the job. The companies' managers called their drivers independent contractors, people in business for themselves rather than employees. One driver, Barbara Ann Berwick, asked the state of California to decide whether she was Uber's employee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-berwick" name="Barbara Ann Berwick" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara Ann Berwick

> **Who:** A San Francisco Uber driver who asked the state of California to decide whether she was Uber's employee, and won.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, July to September 2014. The decision came on June 3, 2015.

**A hearing officer ruled that Uber owed her 56 cents a mile.**

Barbara Ann Berwick drove for Uber in San Francisco, in her own car, for about eight weeks in the summer of 2014, and she quit on September 18. Uber's managers called her an independent contractor, and the company did not pay her back for the costs of her car. She took a claim to the California Labor Commissioner, the state office that handles pay disputes.

**The hearing.** Berwick argued her case herself on March 10, 2015, at a hearing, a meeting where each side tells its story to an official who decides. Uber sent two lawyers and a product manager, Brian Tolkin.

The hearing officer, Stephanie Barrett, listed the rules Uber's managers set for its drivers. The car could be no more than 10 years old, counted by its model year, and the company had to approve it. Drivers needed $1,000,000 in insurance. They had to keep a rating from riders of 4.6 stars or higher, or the company turned off their app. The agreement said riders did not tip. Barrett wrote that Uber was "involved in every aspect of the operation."

**The ruling.** Barrett ruled on June 3, 2015, that Berwick was Uber's employee, and she ordered Uber to pay Berwick $4,152.20. That was 56 cents a mile for 6,468 miles, plus $256 in bridge tolls and $274.12 in interest, extra money owed for the time she had waited to be paid. Barrett turned down Berwick's claim for unpaid wages, because Berwick agreed that Uber had paid her.

Uber's lawyers asked a court in San Francisco to undo the decision. In a statement, Uber's spokespeople said the ruling applied to Berwick alone, and Uber's managers kept treating the company's other drivers as contractors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-berwick" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fight over drivers' pay" -->
**What was at stake.** If app drivers were employees, the companies would have to pay back the costs of their cars, as the hearing officer ordered Uber to do for Berwick. As contractors, drivers paid those costs themselves.

California voters decided the question for app drivers in their state in November 2020. They passed Proposition 22, a law put to the voters directly, 58.6 percent to 41.4 percent. Under it, app drivers stayed contractors, but the companies had to pay them at least 120 percent of the minimum wage for the time they spent working on a ride request, plus 30 cents a mile.

**The money.** Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart and Postmates paid for the campaign for the law. It raised $205.7 million, the most for any California ballot question up to then, by the count of Ballotpedia, which tracks state ballot questions.

**In court.** Four drivers and a union, the Service Employees International Union, went to court on January 12, 2021, to overturn the law. The justices of the California Supreme Court ruled on July 25, 2024, that the law could stand.

**Massachusetts.** In June 2024 Uber and Lyft settled a lawsuit brought by Massachusetts, agreeing to pay $175 million and to pay drivers there at least $32.50 an hour for time spent driving to a rider or carrying one, while the drivers stayed contractors.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bigger vehicles and electric ones" -->
Americans bought more light trucks, meaning pickups, sport utility vehicles (SUVs) and minivans, than cars for the first time in 2001. Light trucks made up 50.8 percent of new vehicle sales that year, against 49.2 percent for cars, according to trade magazines that follow the car business. The gap grew wider in the years after.

**Tesla.** The company Tesla delivered its first Model S electric cars to buyers in Fremont, California, on June 22, 2012. It is named after the inventor Nikola Tesla, who died in 1943 and had no connection to it.

Americans bought about 1.3 million battery electric cars, which run on electricity alone, in 2024 and almost as many in 2025, by the counts of Kelley Blue Book and Cox Automotive, car-market research companies, published in January 2026. They made up 8.1 percent of new car sales in 2024 and 7.8 percent in 2025. Chapter 10 gives larger shares that also count hybrids, which have both a gasoline engine and an electric motor. Tesla sold nearly half of all the battery electric cars bought in 2025.

**The tax credit.** Members of Congress passed a budget law in July 2025, and President Donald Trump signed it on July 4. Under it, the federal tax credits for buying electric cars, money taken off what a buyer owes in taxes, ended for cars bought after September 30, 2025. In the last three months of 2025, Americans bought 234,000 electric cars, 46 percent fewer than in the three months before.

**Scooters.** A company called Bird put electric scooters for rent on the streets of Santa Monica, California, in September 2017. Another company, Lime, followed in 2018, and city officials began trial programs for scooters.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ports, trains and bridges" -->
**Ports.** Ships carrying containers backed up outside American ports in 2021. Staff at the Marine Exchange of Southern California counted a record 109 container ships waiting off the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach on January 9, 2022. By October 2022 the line was down to about 4 ships.

**A fast train.** California voters approved $9.95 billion in bonds, a kind of loan, for high-speed trains from San Francisco to Anaheim in November 2008. The state's high-speed rail officials then put the cost of the whole system at $33.6 billion. Workers broke ground in Fresno on January 6, 2015, and the first section planned is 171 miles, from Merced to Bakersfield.

US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy canceled about $4 billion in federal grants, money the national government had promised, for the California line in July 2025. He said the state's rail agency had "no viable plan," meaning no plan that could work. California officials sued, then dropped the case in December 2025.

The project now gets $1 billion a year through 2045 from cap-and-trade, a state program that makes polluters pay for permits to pollute. Associated Press reporters wrote in December 2025 that cost estimates are now above $100 billion, about three times the $33.6 billion estimate. No passenger had ridden a California high-speed train as of 2026.

**Bridges.** Researchers at ARTBA, a group for companies that build roads, counted the federal bridge records for 2025. The United States had 624,167 bridges, and 41,677 of them were rated in poor condition, about 7 of every 100. Drivers crossed those poor bridges 163 million times a day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaths on the roads and in the air" -->
Car crashes killed 43,230 people in the United States in 2021, the most in recent years, and the count then fell four years in a row. In April 2026 officials at the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration published an early estimate of 36,640 deaths in 2025. That is 1.10 deaths for every 100 million miles driven, and the only lower rate on record was 1.08, in 2014.

**The Key Bridge.** The container ship *Dali* was leaving Baltimore for Sri Lanka at about 1:30 in the morning on March 26, 2024, when it lost power and steering and hit a support of the Francis Scott Key Bridge. The bridge fell into the Patapsco River. The fall killed six men who were filling potholes on the bridge on an overnight shift.

Maryland Governor Wes Moore named them in a statement: Dorlian Ronial Castillo Cabrera, Carlos Daniel Hernandez Estrella, Alejandro Hernandez Fuentes, Miguel Angel Luna Gonzalez, Jose Mynor Lopez and Maynor Yassir Suazo Sandoval. They were immigrants from Mexico and Central America.

**The cause.** Members of the National Transportation Safety Board, the federal board that investigates crashes, reported the cause in November 2025. A label band had been put on a wire the wrong way. The band kept the wire from going all the way into its connector, so the connection came loose and cut the ship's power.

The board's press release does not say who put the band on or when. The board's members sent safety advice to several groups, among them the ship's builder, HD Hyundai Heavy Industries of South Korea.

**The charges.** Federal prosecutors, the government lawyers who bring criminal cases, charged the two Synergy Marine companies that ran the ship with crimes on May 12, 2026. They also charged the *Dali*'s technical superintendent, Radhakrishnan Karthik Nair, the manager in charge of the ship's machinery and upkeep. The charges include causing the deaths of the six workers.

A charge is an accusation, not proof. Under the law, the companies and Nair are innocent unless a judge or jury finds them guilty. The same day, Attorney General Anthony Brown, Maryland's top lawyer, announced a $2.25 billion settlement, a deal that ends a legal claim, with the ship's owner, Grace Ocean, and with Synergy.

**Near Washington.** An Army Black Hawk helicopter collided with an American Eagle passenger jet that was landing at Reagan National Airport, near Washington, D.C., on January 29, 2025. The crash killed all 67 people on the two aircraft, 64 on the jet and three soldiers on the helicopter. It was the deadliest crash of an American passenger airline in more than 20 years.

The head of the National Transportation Safety Board, Jennifer Homendy, said on January 27, 2026: "This was preventable. This was 100% preventable." CNN reported that the board's members blamed Federal Aviation Administration officials for helicopter routes that ran close to the path planes took down to the runway, and for failing to act on years of reports of near misses.

**The three questions, answered**
- How much faster and cheaper did travel get? On the Erie Canal a ton of goods went from Buffalo to New York City for under $10 instead of about $100 by wagon, and on the first railroad across the country a coast-to-coast trip took a week instead of four months. By 1956, loading a ton of cargo onto a ship cost about 16 cents in containers, against about $5.86 by hand.
- Who built the new routes, and whose land and homes did they take? Irish and American-born laborers dug the Erie Canal, enslaved people dug the Dismal Swamp Canal and built most of the Southern railroads, and Chinese workers built the western part of the first railroad across the country. Builders and US officials took Native nations' land, and highway officials building federal roads took the homes of more than 475,000 households, many in Black neighborhoods.
- How did Black travelers fight the laws that made them sit apart? They refused to move, went to court and stayed off the buses: Ida B. Wells sued a railroad in 1884, Homer Plessy tested a Louisiana law and lost in 1896, Irene Morgan won in the Supreme Court in 1946, and Black riders in Montgomery, Alabama, stayed off the city buses for more than a year in 1955 and 1956. After white mobs and Klansmen beat the Freedom Riders in Alabama in 1961, federal officials banned separating passengers in bus and train stations.

**What changed:** People here went from moving on foot, by canoe and with dogs before 1500 to railroads, highways, jets and container ships today, each faster or cheaper than what came before.

**Five dates to keep**
- January 1673: the first post rider leaves New York for Boston.
- October 26, 1825: the Erie Canal opens.
- May 10, 1869: Chinese workers lay the last track of the first railroad across the country.
- May 18, 1896: the Supreme Court rules against Homer Plessy.
- June 29, 1956: Eisenhower signs the Interstate highway law.

**Not known:** Nobody knows how many Chinese workers died building the Central Pacific, because its managers kept no record of their deaths.

**Where to go:** Golden Spike National Historical Park, at Promontory Summit in Utah, marks where the last track was laid, and from May to October you can see working copies of the engines Jupiter and No. 119 there.
