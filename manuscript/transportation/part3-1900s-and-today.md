## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1927 Ford's workers had built 15 million Model T cars, and airplanes were carrying mail and paying passengers on schedules. The Model T first went on sale in 1908 for $825. The first airline to fly on a schedule began in 1914, across Tampa Bay in Florida.

Trains moved about 43.7 million members of the armed forces in World War II, from 1941 to 1945. In Virginia, bus drivers had the power of police officers to make Black passengers sit apart from white passengers, and to arrest riders who refused.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Model T and the new roads" -->
The Ford Model T went on sale on October 1, 1908, at $825. Its price fell to $260 by 1925. On May 26, 1927, the 15 millionth Model T left the factory, and that was the last day Ford made the car.

In 1913 a group of private backers, among them Carl Fisher and Henry Joy, laid out the Lincoln Highway. It was the first named road for cars across the whole country, from Times Square in New York City to San Francisco. After 1926 its parts became sections of numbered routes.

In 1926 the main highways of the United States got numbers. Route 66 got its number on November 11, 1926. It ran 2,448 miles, from Chicago to Santa Monica, California. People called it the "Main Street of America." It was fully paved only by the late 1930s.

In 1913 car crashes killed about 4,100 to 4,200 people in the United States. In 1937 they killed about 37,800 to 39,600, which was about 30 of every 100,000 Americans. The counts differ because the National Safety Council, a private safety group, and the federal tables, kept by the national government, count crashes in different ways.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wright-brothers-transportation" name="The Wright brothers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Wright brothers

> **Who:** Orville and Wilbur Wright, the brothers who flew their powered airplane four times in one day.
> **When and where:** Near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, December 17, 1903.

On December 17, 1903, Orville and Wilbur Wright made four flights in a powered airplane near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina. Orville flew first. He stayed in the air for 12 seconds and went 120 feet. Wilbur made the fourth and longest flight, 852 feet in 59 seconds. Five people watched. One of them, John T. Daniels, a member of a lifesaving station, a crew that rescued people from wrecked ships, took the photograph.

About ten years after those flights, an airplane in Florida was carrying paying passengers on a schedule.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wright-brothers-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Airplanes start carrying mail and passengers" -->
The St. Petersburg-Tampa Airboat Line in Florida was the first airline in the world to fly on a schedule. Its first flight left on January 1, 1914. The pilot, Tony Jannus, flew a Benoist flying boat, a plane that takes off from and lands on water. The trip across Tampa Bay took about 23 minutes, low over the water. Abram C. Pheil, a former mayor of St. Petersburg, paid $400 at an auction for the first round-trip ticket. The airline ran for about four months.

Airplanes began carrying mail on a regular route on May 15, 1918. The route ran 218 miles, from Washington to Philadelphia to New York. Army pilots flew it first, and Post Office pilots took over in August 1918. In 1925 members of Congress passed the Kelly Act. Under it, private companies flew the airmail on contract. The airline companies grew out of those mail contracts.

On May 20 and 21, 1927, Charles Lindbergh, an airmail pilot, flew the *Spirit of St. Louis* alone and without stopping from New York to Paris. He covered about 3,600 miles in 33 hours and 30 minutes. Airline passengers in the United States rose from 5,782 in 1926 to 173,405 in 1928. Applications for pilot licenses tripled in 1927.

The Douglas DC-3 airliner first flew passengers in 1936. It carried 21 or more passengers at up to 207 miles an hour. Van der Linden is a curator at the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum. In van der Linden's words, the DC-3 is "widely considered to be the first airliner capable of making money just by carrying passengers." By 1939 DC-3s carried about 90 percent of the world's airline travel.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-knight" name="Jack Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Knight

> **Who:** An airmail pilot who flew through the night and a snowstorm to finish a test that kept the airmail going.
> **When and where:** North Platte, Nebraska, to Chicago, the night of February 22 to 23, 1921.

In February 1921 Post Office officials ran a test to show that airplanes could carry mail across the country faster than trains. Pilots flew the mail in a relay, day and night, each pilot handing it to the next. Jack Knight flew his part, from North Platte, Nebraska, to Omaha.

At Omaha the next pilot refused to take off in the storm. Knight flew on to Chicago himself, at night, through snow and fog, over ground he had never flown before. The mail crossed the country, 2,629 miles, in 33 hours and 20 minutes. After the test, members of Congress voted money to keep the airmail flying.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-knight" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The sleeping-car porters" -->
The Pullman Company hired Black men as the porters who served passengers in its sleeping cars. After the Civil War, George Pullman set out to hire men who had been enslaved as his porters. By the early 1900s the Pullman Company was the largest single employer of Black men in the United States.

Porters often had to work at least 400 hours a month to earn a full month's pay. That is about 13 hours a day, every day of the month. Their pay was among the lowest of all the workers on the train. Company managers built tips from passengers into the porters' pay, which saved the company money. To earn those tips, porters had to do whatever passengers asked.

Passengers called porters "George," whatever their real names were, after George Pullman. Calling an enslaved person by the owner's name was a practice from slavery. Some passengers called the porters "boy." In 1937 the porters' union leaders won a contract that limited their work to 240 hours a month.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" name="The porters of Miles of Smiles" movie="Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle (1982)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The porters of Miles of Smiles

> **Who:** Eight retired Pullman porters and Rosina Tucker, a porter's wife, who described the porters' work in their own words.
> **When and where:** Pullman sleeping cars across the country, from the 1910s to the 1940s. Filmed in 1982.
> **Movie:** *Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle* (1982), a documentary by Jack Santino and Paul Wagner, made from the porters' own accounts.

In 1982 eight retired Pullman porters told the filmmakers Jack Santino and Paul Wagner what their job had been like. Rosina Tucker, the wife of a porter and a union organizer, narrates the film. The porters describe the smile the company required them to wear for passengers. They talk about living on tips, working through the night without sleep, and being called "George" instead of their own names.

Two of the men in the film are C. L. Dellums and E. D. Nixon. Pullman managers fired Dellums in October 1927 for organizing porters into a union. Nixon later called the meeting for the bus boycott in Montgomery, Alabama.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="miles-of-smiles-porters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trains and Jeeps in World War II" -->
From December 1941 to August 1945, trains carried about 43.7 million members of the armed forces. That was more than 97 percent of all the troops moved in organized groups. About 100,000 members of the armed forces rode troop trains on an average day. Half of all Pullman sleeping cars were in use carrying troops.

The Jeep was a small army car. About 640,000 were built in the war. About 363,000 of them were the Willys MB, and about 280,000 were the Ford GPW.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Separate seats on the buses" -->
In Virginia, the law required buses to seat Black and white passengers apart. The same law made bus drivers "special policemen." A driver could decide a passenger's race, order the passenger to another seat, throw the passenger off the bus and make an arrest. Refusing a driver was a crime with a fine of $5 to $25. Greyhound's own rules stated that the company had "full control and discretion as to the seating of passengers." Discretion means the power to decide.

In August 1932, Bessie Nelson and Mamie Kinchlow were thrown off a Greyhound bus in Alexandria, Virginia, and jailed for more than a week. On January 16, 1943, Mildred Turpin and Caroline Johnson, clerks for the Navy Department, were jailed at Clarendon, Virginia. On February 19, 1945, in Johnson City, Tennessee, Tonito Holmes was beaten, removed from a Greyhound bus and fined $14.25. The records of these cases do not name the drivers and officers who threw the riders off, jailed them or beat them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="irene-morgan" name="Irene Morgan" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Irene Morgan

> **Who:** A Baltimore war worker who refused to give up her bus seat in Virginia and won her case in the US Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** A Greyhound bus near Saluda, Virginia, July 16, 1944. The Supreme Court decided her case on June 3, 1946.

Irene Morgan, of Baltimore, was 27 and built B-26 bombers in a wartime factory. On July 16, 1944, she had been visiting her mother in Gloucester County, Virginia, and took a Greyhound bus home. She was going back to her husband and to see a doctor. Near Saluda, two white passengers got on the crowded bus. The driver told Morgan and the woman next to her, a Black woman holding a baby, to move to the back seat.

"I refused to move," Morgan said years later. The driver drove the bus to the jail in Saluda. The sheriff showed her a warrant, a paper that allows an arrest. She "just took it and tore it up and just threw it out the window." The sheriff grabbed her, and she kicked him. The sheriff and a deputy pulled her off the bus. Her mother paid $500 to get her out of jail.

On October 18, 1944, Morgan paid a $100 fine for resisting arrest. She would not admit breaking the seating law. She was found guilty of it anyway, and she refused to pay the $10 fine. The records do not name the judge. Her lawyer was Spottswood W. Robinson III. The lawyers Thurgood Marshall and William H. Hastie of the NAACP, a civil rights group, joined him. The judges of the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals ruled against her on June 6, 1945.

On June 3, 1946, the justices of the US Supreme Court ruled for her. Justice Stanley Reed wrote that Virginia's law put a burden on travel between states, so it could not apply to buses crossing state lines. The ruling covered only trips between states. Reporters at the *Afro-American* newspaper wrote that bus companies' seating rules did not change right away.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="irene-morgan" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950, Americans moved people and goods over long distances on three new systems: Interstate highways, shipping containers and jet airliners. In 1956 President Dwight Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act, the law for the Interstate highways. That year a ship called the *Ideal X* carried 58 shipping containers from New Jersey to Texas. By the writer Marc Levinson's count, loading cargo in containers in 1956 cost about 16 cents a ton, against about $5.86 a ton by hand. In 1958 Pan Am began flying jet airliners across the Atlantic Ocean, about twice as fast as the older planes.

Highway officials forced the people of more than 475,000 households, more than a million people, out of their homes to make room for federal roads. A household is the people who live in one home, and federal roads are roads paid for by the national government. That number is the US Department of Transportation's estimate, a careful guess from its records. Private railroads were losing money on passenger trains, and in 1971 a new company, Amtrak, took over most of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Interstate highways" -->
On June 29, 1956, President Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act. The law allowed for 41,000 miles of new highways, called the Interstate Highway System. It was the largest public works program in the history of the United States up to then. Public works are projects that the government pays for and the public uses, such as roads and bridges. The system is about 48,900 miles long today. In 1990 members of Congress added Eisenhower's name to it.

Interstate highways went around Route 66. Officials of AASHTO, the association of state highway officials, took Route 66 off the list of numbered U.S. highways on June 27, 1985.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" name="Dwight Eisenhower" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Dwight Eisenhower

> **Who:** The president who signed the Interstate highway law, 37 years after he crossed the country in an Army truck convoy.
> **When and where:** The 1919 convoy from Washington, D.C., to San Francisco. The highway law, June 29, 1956.

In 1919 the Army's first convoy of motor vehicles crossed the country. A convoy is a group of vehicles traveling together. It had 81 vehicles and went 3,251 miles, from Washington, D.C., to San Francisco. The trip took 62 days. Its vehicles sank in mud and broke down, and bridges failed under them.

Dwight Eisenhower was a young Army officer, a lieutenant colonel, and he volunteered to go. He wrote a report on the trip. In 1945, at the end of World War II, he saw the autobahns, Germany's highways. Those two experiences formed his case for a system of highways across the United States. As president he signed the law for it on June 29, 1956.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dwight-eisenhower-transportation" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The homes the highways took" -->
The people who planned the Interstates ran many of them through city neighborhoods, and they took the homes that stood in the way. By the US Department of Transportation's estimate, highway officials took the homes of more than 475,000 households, more than a million people, for federal roads. The planning scholar Alan Altshuler made an estimate in those years. He said the new system would push out up to a million people, "a disproportionate number of them Black." Disproportionate means more than their share. The law professor Deborah N. Archer says the neighborhoods chosen "were largely Black and poor."

In Montgomery, Alabama, Interstate 85 ran south of the city in the plans drawn up in 1955. Sam Engelhardt became head of the Alabama State Highway Department in 1959. His business cards read "I Stand for White Supremacy and Segregation." Under Engelhardt, state highway officials chose a final route through Montgomery's only middle-class Black neighborhood. Ralph Abernathy, a minister and civil rights leader, lived there, and Alabama State University stood next to it. An engineering firm had suggested a route through Oak Park, a city park that city officials had closed rather than let Black people use it.

Highway officials took about 356 homes in the Centennial Hill, Bel Air and Bottoms neighborhoods. They paid owners an average of $3,300 a house. In 1961 Abernathy sent a telegram to President John F. Kennedy. He wrote that the route "will destroy one of the best negro neighborhoods in the south." Rebecca Retzlaff, a researcher at Auburn University, told this history in a 2019 study.

In St. Paul, Minnesota, highway officials built Interstate 94 through the Black neighborhood of Rondo from 1956 to 1968. Counts of what was lost differ. One puts it at about 600 families. Another puts it at about 700 homes and 300 businesses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jets in the sky" -->
Two airliners left Los Angeles three minutes apart on June 30, 1956. One was TWA Flight 2, and the other was United Air Lines Flight 718. Over the Grand Canyon in Arizona, they flew into each other. All 128 people on the two planes died. It was the deadliest airplane crash in the world up to that time. The crash was a major reason the Federal Aviation Agency was set up in 1958.

Pan Am flew a Boeing 707 jet from New York to Paris with 111 passengers and 12 crew members on October 26, 1958. It was the first jet service across the Atlantic by an American airline, and the trip took about 8 hours. The older airliners were propeller planes. The 707 flew about twice as fast and carried about twice as many people.

Before 1978 the members of a federal board, the Civil Aeronautics Board, decided which airlines could start or stop flying and what they could charge. President Jimmy Carter signed the Airline Deregulation Act on October 24, 1978. Under it, the board's control ended step by step, and the board closed at the end of 1984. Two economists, Morrison and Winston, studied the change for the Brookings Institution, a research group. Economists study money, prices and trade. Morrison and Winston worked out that fares ended up about 22 percent lower than they would have been under the old rules. Prices for most other things rose over those years. Measured against them, average fares fell about 40 to 45 percent in the decades after 1978, by government and industry figures.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-dusty-roads" name="Barbara 'Dusty' Roads" movie="Fly With Me (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara "Dusty" Roads

> **Who:** An American Airlines stewardess who fought the airline's rule that fired women at age 32 or when they married.
> **When and where:** American Airlines, from 1950. She lived from 1928 to 2023.
> **Movie:** *Fly With Me* (2024), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about flight attendants, directed by Sarah Colt and Helen Dobrowski. Roads appears in it.

Barbara Roads, called Dusty, joined American Airlines as a stewardess in 1950. A stewardess was a woman who served passengers on an airplane. Today the job is called flight attendant. In 1953, American's managers put a rule into a contract that any stewardess hired after November 1953 would be fired at age 32. A stewardess who married was fired too. In a radio interview, Roads gave her view of the reason: "It was cheaper to have someone that'll never get married, never have babies, never get pensions, never get the highest rate of pay."

Roads lobbied members of Congress on her own flights to Washington, D.C. To lobby is to try to talk lawmakers into doing something. In 1963 she held a news conference and asked reporters to pick the 32-year-old stewardesses out of a lineup. Representative Martha Griffiths helped her. In August 1968 the stewardesses came close to a strike. By Roads's account, American's managers dropped the age rule the day after someone asked Griffiths to press the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. That is the federal office that handles complaints of unfair treatment at work.

In 1977 Roads helped start the Association of Professional Flight Attendants, a union. She worked for American Airlines for 44 years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-dusty-roads" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Freedom Rides" -->
In May 1961 Black and white riders traveled together by bus into the South. They were called Freedom Riders. They were testing a court order that banned separating Black and white travelers in bus stations used for trips between states. On May 4, two groups, John Lewis among them, left Washington, D.C., on a Greyhound bus and a Trailways bus, headed for New Orleans.

On May 14, at Anniston, Alabama, a white mob with clubs and bats attacked the Greyhound. They broke its windows and slashed its tires, then chased the bus until the flat tires stopped it several miles out of town. One of the attackers threw a firebomb, a bomb that starts a fire, through a window. The riders and other passengers barely got out of the flames. E. L. Cowling, a state officer riding the bus in plain clothes, held the attackers back with his gun. The mob beat the riders while the bus burned, until police arrived. Later that day white men got on the Trailways bus at Anniston and beat the riders on it. Their beating left one rider, Walter Bergman, a white man, with permanent brain damage.

The same day, a crowd met the Trailways bus in Birmingham with pipes, chains and baseball bats. Many in the crowd belonged to the Ku Klux Klan, a group that used violence against Black people and their supporters. Eugene "Bull" Connor, the city official in charge of the police, had told Klan leaders that the police would hold back from the bus station for 15 minutes. He encouraged the Klan to attack the riders.

On May 20, a white crowd in Montgomery attacked a new group of riders arriving by Greyhound. They beat John Seigenthaler, a Justice Department official, until he was unconscious. Alabama's public safety director, Floyd Mann, had arranged protection for the buses up to Montgomery. Montgomery police did not take over the protection, and Mann went into the fighting himself. Governor John Patterson blamed the riders for the violence.

Justice Department officials asked the Interstate Commerce Commission to act. Its members were federal officials in charge of trade and travel between states. They banned keeping Black and white passengers apart in bus and train stations. The ban was issued on September 22, 1961, and took effect on November 1, 1961. People in many Southern towns went on defying it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Amtrak takes over the passenger trains" -->
Private railroad companies were losing money on their passenger trains. In 1970 members of Congress passed the Rail Passenger Service Act, which set up a new company called Amtrak. On May 1, 1971, Amtrak began running most of the passenger trains between American cities, which the private railroads gave up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The shipping container" -->
Before containers, dockworkers loaded cargo onto ships by hand. On April 26, 1956, a ship called the *Ideal X* left Port Newark, New Jersey, carrying 58 metal boxes. Each box was 35 feet long, the size of a truck trailer. These boxes are called shipping containers. The ship reached Houston, Texas, five days later.

Marc Levinson worked out what the change saved in his book *The Box*. In 1956 loading cargo by hand cost about $5.86 a ton. Loading it in containers cost about 16 cents a ton. In the late 1960s, an international standards group set standard sizes for containers, and the same boxes came into use around the world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="malcom-mclean" name="Malcom McLean" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Malcom McLean

> **Who:** A North Carolina trucking owner who sold his trucking company to start shipping truck-sized boxes by sea.
> **When and where:** Port Newark, New Jersey, April 26, 1956.

Malcom McLean sent the *Ideal X* to sea with its 58 containers on April 26, 1956. He had started in 1934 in North Carolina with one used truck and built it into a trucking company. Then he sold the trucking company and put the money into shipping containers by sea.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="malcom-mclean" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaths in car crashes" -->
Car crashes killed 54,589 people in the United States in 1972. That is the most in any single year, before or since. By 1975 the yearly count had fallen to 44,525.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The minivan" -->
Workers in Windsor, Ontario, in Canada, built the first Dodge Caravan and Plymouth Voyager minivans on November 2, 1983. A minivan is a tall, boxy car with room for a family and its bags. They went on sale as 1984 models.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have used phones to find their way and to hire rides, and some of them drive electric cars. In 2024 about 8 of every 100 new cars sold were electric, according to Kelley Blue Book. In 2025 about 7 of every 100 bridges in the country were rated in poor condition in federal records, by the count of a road builders' group.

Federal safety officials counted 43,230 deaths in car crashes in 2021. Their early estimate for 2025, published in April 2026, is about 36,640. A ship knocked down a bridge in Baltimore in 2024 and killed six road workers. In 2025 an Army helicopter and a passenger jet collided near Washington, D.C., and all 67 people aboard died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maps and rides on a phone" -->
GPS, the Global Positioning System, uses signals from satellites to show a GPS device where it is. Until 2000 a setting called Selective Availability made GPS less exact for ordinary users. The setting was switched off just after midnight at the end of May 1, 2000. According to the government's GPS website, the error of an ordinary GPS device shrank from about 100 meters to about 20 meters.

People also began hiring rides through apps on their phones. According to Britannica and news reports, Uber began in March 2009 as a company called UberCab. It started its app service in San Francisco on July 5, 2010. In October 2010, after receiving an official order to stop, the company's owners changed its name to Uber. Lyft started its service in June 2012.

In a ride-hailing app, a rider asks for a ride, and a driver using a personal car takes the job. The companies' managers called their drivers independent contractors, meaning people in business for themselves rather than employees. In 2015 a California state officer found that Uber did not pay its drivers back for the costs of their cars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="barbara-berwick" name="Barbara Ann Berwick" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Barbara Ann Berwick

> **Who:** A San Francisco Uber driver who asked the state of California to decide whether she was Uber's employee, and won.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, July to September 2014. The decision came on June 3, 2015.

Barbara Ann Berwick drove for Uber in San Francisco, in her own car, for about eight weeks in the summer of 2014. She quit on September 18, 2014. Uber called her an independent contractor, so the company paid nothing toward the costs of her car. She took a claim to the California Labor Commissioner, the state office that handles pay disputes. At a hearing on March 10, 2015, she argued her case herself. Uber sent two lawyers and a product manager, Brian Tolkin.

The hearing officer, Stephanie Barrett, listed the rules Uber set for its drivers. The car could be no more than 10 years old, counted by its model year, and Uber had to approve it. Drivers needed $1,000,000 in insurance. They had to keep a rating from riders of 4.6 stars or higher, or Uber turned off their app. The agreement said riders did not tip. Barrett wrote that Uber was "involved in every aspect of the operation."

On June 3, 2015, Barrett ruled that Berwick was Uber's employee. She ordered Uber to pay Berwick $4,152.20. That was 56 cents a mile for 6,468 miles, plus $256 in bridge tolls and $274.12 in interest. Barrett turned down Berwick's claim for unpaid wages, because Berwick agreed that Uber had paid her.

Uber's lawyers asked a court in San Francisco to undo the decision. The company said the ruling applied to Berwick alone, and it kept treating its other drivers as contractors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="barbara-berwick" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fight over drivers' pay" -->
In November 2020, California voters decided the question for app drivers in their state. They passed Proposition 22, a law put to the voters directly, 58.6 percent to 41.4 percent. The law kept app drivers as contractors. It also set a minimum for their pay: 120 percent of the minimum wage for the time spent on a trip, plus 30 cents a mile. Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart and Postmates paid for the campaign for it, which raised $205.7 million. Ballotpedia, which tracks state ballot questions from official vote counts and money reports, lists that as the most raised for any California ballot question up to then.

On January 12, 2021, drivers Hector Castellanos, Joseph Delgado, Saori Okawa and Michael Robinson went to court to overturn the law, along with a union, the Service Employees International Union. On July 25, 2024, the justices of the California Supreme Court ruled that the law could stand.

In Massachusetts, the state's top lawyer, Attorney General Maura Healey, sued Uber and Lyft in 2020. On June 27, 2024, the next attorney general, Andrea Campbell, ended the case with a deal, as WBUR, a Boston public radio station, reported. Uber and Lyft agreed to pay $175 million. They also agreed to pay drivers in the state at least $32.50 an hour for time spent driving to a rider or carrying one. The drivers stayed contractors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bigger vehicles and electric ones" -->
In 2001, Americans bought more light trucks than cars for the first time. Light trucks are pickups, SUVs and minivans. They made up 50.8 percent of new vehicle sales that year, against 49.2 percent for cars, according to trade magazines that follow the business. The gap grew wider in the years after.

Tesla delivered its first Model S electric cars to buyers on June 22, 2012, in Fremont, California, according to the company and news reports. The company is named after the inventor Nikola Tesla, who died in 1943 and had nothing to do with it.

Americans bought about 1.3 million electric cars in 2024 and almost as many in 2025, by the count that Kelley Blue Book and Cox Automotive, car-market research companies, published in January 2026. Electric cars made up 8.1 percent of new car sales in 2024 and 7.8 percent in 2025. Tesla sold nearly half of all the electric cars bought in 2025. At the start of October 2025, the federal tax credits for buying electric cars ended. A tax credit is money taken off what a buyer owes in taxes. In the last three months of 2025, Americans bought 234,000 electric cars, 46 percent fewer than in the three months before.

In September 2017, a company called Bird put electric scooters for rent on the streets of Santa Monica, California. In 2018 another company, Lime, followed, and city officials began trial programs for scooters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ports, trains and bridges" -->
In 2021 ships carrying containers backed up outside American ports. On January 9, 2022, the Marine Exchange of Southern California counted a record 109 container ships waiting off the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. By October 2022 the line was down to about 4 ships.

In November 2008 California voters approved $9.95 billion in bonds, a kind of loan, for high-speed trains from San Francisco to Anaheim. The state's high-speed rail officials then put the cost of the whole system at $33.6 billion. Workers broke ground in Fresno on January 6, 2015. The first section planned is 171 miles, from Merced to Bakersfield.

In July 2025, US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy canceled about $4 billion in federal grants, money the national government had promised, for the California line. He said the state's rail agency had "no viable plan," meaning no plan that could work. California officials sued, then dropped the case in December 2025. The project now gets $1 billion a year through 2045 from cap-and-trade, a state program that makes polluters pay for permits to pollute. Associated Press reporters wrote in December 2025 that cost estimates are now above $100 billion. No passenger had ridden a California high-speed train as of 2026.

ARTBA, a trade group for companies that build roads, counted the federal bridge records for 2025. The United States had 624,167 bridges, and 41,677 of them were rated in poor condition, about 7 of every 100. Drivers crossed those poor bridges 163 million times a day.

Car crashes killed 43,230 people in the United States in 2021, the most in recent years. The count then fell four years in a row. In April 2026 the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration published an early estimate of 36,640 deaths in 2025. That is 1.10 deaths for every 100 million miles driven. The only lower rate on record was 1.08, in 2014.

At about 1:30 in the morning on March 26, 2024, the container ship *Dali* was leaving Baltimore for Sri Lanka. It lost power and steering and hit a support of the Francis Scott Key Bridge. The bridge fell into the Patapsco River. Six men filling potholes on the bridge on an overnight shift died. They were Dorlian Ronial Castillo Cabrera, Carlos Daniel Hernandez Estrella, Alejandro Hernandez Fuentes, Miguel Angel Luna Gonzalez, Jose Mynor Lopez and Maynor Yassir Suazo Sandoval. They were immigrants from Mexico and Central America. Maryland Governor Wes Moore named them in a statement.

In November 2025, members of the National Transportation Safety Board, the federal board that investigates crashes, reported the cause. A wire connection had come loose because a label band on the wire was put on wrongly, and the loose connection cut the ship's power. On May 12, 2026, federal prosecutors, the government lawyers who bring criminal cases, charged the two Synergy Marine companies that ran the ship with crimes. They also charged the *Dali*'s technical superintendent, the manager in charge of the ship's machinery and upkeep, Radhakrishnan Karthik Nair. The charges include causing the deaths of the six workers. A charge is an accusation, not proof. The law treats the companies and Nair as innocent unless a judge or jury finds them guilty. The same day, Maryland's top lawyer, Attorney General Anthony Brown, announced a $2.25 billion settlement, a deal that ends a legal claim, with the ship's owner, Grace Ocean, and with Synergy.

On January 29, 2025, an Army Black Hawk helicopter collided with an American Eagle passenger jet that was landing at Reagan National Airport near Washington, D.C. All 67 people on the two aircraft died, 64 on the jet and three soldiers on the helicopter. It was the deadliest crash of an American passenger airline in more than 20 years. On January 27, 2026, the head of the National Transportation Safety Board, Jennifer Homendy, said: "This was preventable. This was 100% preventable." CNN reported that the board's members blamed Federal Aviation Administration officials for helicopter routes that ran close to the path planes took down to the runway. The board's members also blamed those officials for failing to act on years of reports of near misses.
