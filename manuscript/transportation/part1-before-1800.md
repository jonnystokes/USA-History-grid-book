## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 11 of 37 · Transportation · 10 eras · about 70 minutes**

**Each new way of moving people and goods made trips faster or cheaper, and enslaved people and immigrant workers did much of the building, on land taken from Native nations.**

Eight Chinese workers laid the last stretch of track at Promontory Summit, in Utah, on May 10, 1869. It joined two railroad lines into the first one across the country, and a golden spike marked the spot. With it, a trip from coast to coast took about a week instead of about four months. Chinese workers had built the line's western part for less pay than white workers got. Fifty years later, three of the eight, Ging Cui, Wong Fook and Lee Shao, rode on a float at the anniversary.

Era by era, this chapter describes how people and goods moved, from foot trails and canoes before 1500 to jets, container ships and ride apps after 2000. Watch two numbers fall as the eras go by: how long a trip took and what it cost. Look for old routes under new ones, too. Many colonial roads began as Native paths, and so did part of the first federal highway. And keep an eye on who did the digging and the track-laying, whose land and homes the routes went through, and who was told where to sit.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How much faster and cheaper did travel get?
- Whose land and homes did the new routes take?
- How did Black travelers fight the laws that made them sit apart?

**Five minutes?** Read "The Chinese workers" in 1850 to 1900.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

Nobody in North America hauled loads on wheels before 1500, and nobody rode a horse. People moved themselves and their goods on foot and by water, over trails and rivers that crossed the whole continent. The horses of the Americas had died out after the ice age. North of Mexico, no large animal was left that could pull a cart.

With no animal to pull a cart, people here had no use for one. In Mesoamerica, the region that is now Mexico and Central America, people made toys with wheels, but they did not use wheels to haul loads.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trails that became highways" -->
Native people built and kept up long trails across the continent, and many of those trails later became colonial roads. Some of those roads became highways. Many trails had names. The Great Trail, also called the Great Path, was a network of footpaths that linked New England, the middle of the Atlantic coast and the Great Lakes. People who spoke Algonquian and Iroquoian languages, two families of related languages, used it.

**The Natchez Trace.** This route began as paths worn by bison walking to salt licks, places where animals lick salt from the ground, near present-day Nashville. Natchez, Choctaw and Chickasaw people followed the paths, widened them and kept them clear. They used them for trade, travel, war and talks with other nations. People have used the route for about 8,000 years.

People traded over these trails and rivers for thousands of years. As early as 6000 BC, they carried seashells and native copper, copper found pure in the ground, between the Great Lakes and the coast of the Gulf of Mexico.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Canoes, portages and the travois" -->
On the rivers and lakes, people traveled in two kinds of canoe. A dugout was one log that builders hollowed out with fire and tools. For thousands of years dugouts were the main boats of the Southeast and of the Eastern Woodlands, the forests that covered the eastern part of the continent. Some dugouts found in Wisconsin lakes are more than 4,000 years old.

**Birchbark.** In the Northeast and around the Great Lakes, people built canoes from the bark of birch trees. One or two people could carry one. It floated in shallow water, and its owners repaired it with materials from the forest. Builders made them in sizes from one-person boats to big canoes that carried freight, goods being shipped.

Where one river or lake ended, travelers lifted their canoes and carried them over land to the next, a crossing called a portage. Portages and trails linked the waterways into one network across the continent.

On the Great Plains, dogs were the only animals people used to haul loads. A dog dragged a travois, two long poles with the load slung between them. The travois was the usual way to move goods on the Plains before horses came.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

Spanish soldiers and colonists brought horses and wagons into what is now the United States in the 1500s, and they built a port in Florida, all on land where Native nations already lived. The horses came north first, with Coronado's soldiers in 1540. The port, St. Augustine, began inside a Timucua town, where Spanish colonists took over the chief's council house in 1565. Juan de Oñate's colonists followed in 1598 with 83 wagons and carts, bound for Pueblo land in New Mexico.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses, a port and 83 wagons" -->
**Horses.** Christopher Columbus carried horses to the Caribbean islands on his second voyage, in 1493. Almost fifty years later a Spanish commander, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, led a long march north from the west coast of Mexico, from 1540 to 1542. His soldiers brought more than a thousand horses, the first in what is now Arizona. The march went on into New Mexico and the Great Plains.

**A port.** Spanish colonists built St. Augustine, in what is now Florida, in 1565, and it became a port. Spanish ships sailing between Spain and the Caribbean stopped there for supplies and for protection. People have lived there without a break ever since. It is the oldest European town and port on the mainland of the United States.

The colonists built it inside a Timucua town. Their leader, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, landed at Seloy on September 8, 1565. Seloy was also the name of the town's chief, who answered to a greater chief. One count gives Menéndez about 1,500 soldiers and colonists, and another gives 800 colonists, including 24 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans.

The priest with the Spanish ships wrote that the Timucua received the Spanish well and gave them their chief's large house on the riverbank. It was the chief's council house, the building where the town's leaders met. The Spanish took it over. Two of their captains had a ditch, a moat and a wall of earth built around it, and it became their first fort there. Chapter 1 tells what happened between the colonists and the Timucua after that.

**Wagons.** Juan de Oñate led a Spanish colony north from Santa Bárbara, in northern New Spain, the Spanish colony that included Mexico, in January 1598. About 400 men came, about 130 of them with families. They brought 83 wagons and carts and more than 7,000 farm animals. In late April the long line of wagons, people and animals reached the Rio Grande near El Paso del Norte.

The land the colony moved into was the homeland of the Pueblo peoples. Oñate claimed New Mexico for Spain on April 30, 1598, in a ceremony the Spanish called La Toma, "the taking." In July he set up the colony's headquarters at San Juan Pueblo, called Ohkay Owingeh, on the upper Rio Grande. The route the wagons followed became El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, Spanish for "the royal road of the interior." It ran from Mexico City to northern New Mexico.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 3 minutes**

Boats carried most of the goods in the English colonies of the 1600s, from canoes and small open boats called shallops to ships that sailed along the coast from one colony to another. The leaders of the Massachusetts colony licensed a ferry across Boston's harbor in 1631. On land, colonists followed paths that Native people had made long before, and a rider carrying mail began using them between New York and Boston in 1673.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston Post Road, on Native paths" -->
The first post rider, a man on horseback who carried letters, left New York for Boston in January 1673. The governor of New York, Francis Lovelace, had pushed for the route along with John Winthrop Jr. of Connecticut. The rider went by way of New Haven, Hartford, Springfield and Worcester. The trip was about 250 miles and took about two weeks. The path that he and later riders used grew into the Boston Post Road.

Much of that road followed Native paths. Its lower part followed the Pequot Path, which Narragansett and Pequot people had traveled for hundreds of years. The path ran about 69 miles near the shore, from Providence to New London, Connecticut. English settlers kept using it until it became the lower part of the Boston Post Road.

**What Roger Williams saw.** The English colonist Roger Williams walked the Pequot Path in 1636. Along 20 miles of it he passed about a dozen Narragansett villages. He wrote: "It is admirable to see what paths their naked hardened feet have made in the wilderness in the most stony and rockie places" (by admirable he meant worth wondering at).

The road's upper part followed the Old Connecticut Path and the Bay Path, Native trails from Massachusetts Bay to the Connecticut River. Nipmuc people carried corn along them to Massachusetts Bay in the early 1630s and traded it there for copper, iron goods and wool cloth. Thomas Hooker led the members of his church along the same path in 1636 to found the town of Hartford.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edward-converse" name="Edward Converse" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Edward Converse

> **Who:** A Puritan settler and church deacon who ran the first ferry between Charlestown and Boston.
> **When and where:** Boston harbor, licensed June 14, 1631. Lived 1589 to 1663.

**The colony's rulers let him charge two pence a passenger.**

Edward Converse ran a ferry across the harbor between Charlestown and Boston, and the record that licensed it still exists. The members of the Massachusetts Court of Assistants, the colony's ruling council, wrote it on June 14, 1631: "Edw: Converse hath vndertaken to sett vpp a fferry betwixte Charlton & Boston." That is how people spelled English then. In modern spelling, Converse had taken on the job of setting up a ferry between Charlestown and Boston.

**The fares.** The same men set his prices. A passenger crossing alone paid two pence, or two of the small English coins called pennies. When two or more crossed together, each paid one penny. Their license was a charter, a written permission from a government.

**Two firsts.** A history page from the West End Museum in Boston states that Converse started his ferry in 1630, the first on that crossing, and ran it for about ten years. At the time it was the only way across the river there. The history page of Boston's transit agency, the MBTA, names a different first. It states that Thomas Williams opened the first chartered transit service in the United States in 1631, with his own ferry to Charlestown.

Converse was a Puritan, one of the English Christians who started the Massachusetts colony on land where Native people already lived. He was also a deacon, a church officer who helps the minister. The Charlestown ferry earned 50 pounds in English money in 1639. The next year the colony's leaders gave the ferry's earnings to Harvard College.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 5 minutes**

Stage lines for passengers and a big freight wagon called the Conestoga came into use in the English colonies between 1700 and 1750. Pennsylvania wagon builders made the Conestoga to haul freight to Philadelphia, and New Jersey officials licensed one of the first stage lines in 1706. The roads were still rough. Riding from Boston to New York in 1704, Sarah Kemble Knight crossed some rivers by canoe in the dark.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wagon and the people it was named for" -->
The Conestoga wagon was a big freight wagon from Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. Its name first shows up in writing on December 31, 1717. That day James Logan, who had been William Penn's secretary, wrote in his account book that he had bought a "Conestogoe Waggon."

German and Swiss wagon builders along the Conestoga River worked out the design. The wagon's bed curved down toward the middle, so the load settled toward the center, and a white cloth cover stretched over hoops above it. Drivers hitched teams of four to six horses to it and hauled loads more than 60 miles to Philadelphia. People in the East used it as their main freight wagon well into the age of railroads.

**The name.** The wagon took its name from the Conestoga River and from the Conestoga people who lived in Lancaster County. In December 1763 a mob of men from Paxton township, known as the Paxton Boys, killed the last of the Conestoga people. First they killed the six people at home at Conestoga Manor, a settlement under the colony's protection: three men, two women and a boy.

Lancaster officials then put the survivors in the county workhouse, a locked building where people who broke small laws were held and made to work, to protect them. On December 27 the mob broke in and killed them, 14 or 16 men, women and children by different counts. The mob killed about 20 Conestoga people in the two attacks, and no one was ever put on trial for it. Chapter 2 tells the killings in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stage lines, log roads and the Great Wagon Road" -->
A stage line carried passengers in a wagon or coach along a fixed route between towns. New Jersey officials gave Hugh Huddy a patent, a legal right, to run one between Burlington and Perth Amboy in 1706. Coaches ran between New York and Philadelphia from 1732, though not on a fixed schedule.

New lines opened from Trenton to New Brunswick in 1738 and from Bordentown to New York in 1740. By 1756 several regular coach services ran between New York and Philadelphia, and the trip took about three days.

Where a road crossed swampy ground, builders laid logs side by side across it. People called it a corduroy road, after a cloth with raised ribs.

**The Great Wagon Road.** It ran from Philadelphia down the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia toward Augusta, Georgia, and colonists used it heavily from the 1720s on. Accounts give its length as about 730 miles or almost 800, depending on where the count ends.

South of Lancaster and York, in Pennsylvania, the road roughly followed a route that Europeans called the Great Warriors' Path. Native people had made that path to travel through the Shenandoah Valley, and they still valued it.

**The Treaty of Lancaster.** Leaders of the Six Nations of the Iroquois, six Native nations joined in one league, met officials of Pennsylvania, Virginia and Maryland in the town of Lancaster in June 1744. There the two sides made a treaty, a written agreement between nations. In it the Iroquois leaders gave up their use of the path and their claims to land in the Shenandoah Valley.

The two sides later disagreed about how much land the treaty covered. Virginia's leaders claimed land as far as the Ohio River and beyond it. The Iroquois said they had given up much less.

**Who built the roads.** In Virginia, colonists built and repaired the roads themselves, under road laws that the colony's lawmakers passed in 1632 and 1657 and added to in 1661. Each year the members of each county court chose an overseer of the highways, a man put in charge of the roads. He was usually given all the "Labouring Male Titheables" who lived on or near the road.

Titheables, or tithables, were the people counted for a tax on each person: all free men 16 and older, and all enslaved people 16 and older, women as well as men. The road orders required the working men among them to bring their own tools, wagons and teams of animals and to work six days a year on the roads. The surviving road orders do not say how many of these men were enslaved.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-kemble-knight-transportation" name="Sarah Kemble Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Kemble Knight

> **Who:** A Boston businesswoman who rode from Boston to New York and wrote down what the road was like.
> **When and where:** October 1704, by road from Boston to New Haven and on to New York. Her journal was published in 1825.

**She crossed rivers by canoe in the dark, riding with the mail.**

Sarah Kemble Knight, a businesswoman in Boston, rode from Boston to New Haven and on to New York in October 1704, and she kept a journal of the trip. She traveled with post riders, the men who carried the mail, and hired guides from one town to the next.

Her journal is an eyewitness record of the road itself. She crossed some rivers by canoe in the dark and others at fords, shallow places where people and horses can wade across. She wrote down which bridges she was afraid to cross. The journal was published in 1825, more than a hundred years after her ride.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 3 minutes**

Around Philadelphia, between 1750 and 1800, investors paid for a road and charged travelers a fee to use it, and an inventor ran a boat pushed by a steam engine. The investors, people in and around Philadelphia and Lancaster, put their money into the road company in 1792. Their Philadelphia and Lancaster Turnpike, finished in the mid-1790s, was the first long-distance road of its kind in the country.

John Fitch's steamboat carried paying passengers on the Delaware River on a schedule in 1790, seventeen years before Robert Fulton's steamboat ran on the Hudson.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first turnpike" -->
The Philadelphia and Lancaster Turnpike Company got its charter in 1792 to build a turnpike, a road whose owners charge travelers a fee, called a toll, at gates along the way. Work on the road began that year. People in and around Lancaster and Philadelphia bought 1,000 shares of the company's stock, pieces of ownership in the company, at $300 each. A Philadelphia merchant, William Bingham, led the company.

The road ran 62 miles between Philadelphia and Lancaster, mostly along the older Philadelphia-Lancaster Road, the first stretch of the Great Wagon Road. Its surface was broken stone and gravel. Some accounts say it was in use in 1794, and most say 1795.

**Known:** The records name the people who bought shares and the company's president.
**Not known:** who broke the stone, or how many men did the work.

The Lancaster Turnpike was the country's first long-distance engineered toll road, planned and built to a design, with a prepared surface. After it succeeded, people built many more toll roads, the historian Charlene Mires writes. By the 1830s the country had almost 12,000 miles of turnpikes, nearly 200 times the length of the first one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A steamboat before Fulton's" -->
John Fitch tested a steamboat on the Delaware River on August 22, 1787, while delegates to the Constitutional Convention watched. They were the men meeting in Philadelphia that summer to write the Constitution of the United States. A steam engine on Fitch's boat worked rows of oars that pushed the boat through the water.

Fitch's boat, the *Experiment*, carried passengers on a schedule in the summer of 1790. It was the first steamboat service anywhere in the world to carry paying passengers on a schedule. The boat carried up to about 30 passengers at a time on the Delaware, between Philadelphia and New Jersey towns such as Trenton, Burlington and Bordentown, and also to Wilmington.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-fitch" name="John Fitch" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Fitch

> **Who:** The builder of a working steamboat in 1787 and of the first steamboat service anywhere to carry paying passengers on a schedule.
> **When and where:** The Delaware River, 1787 to 1790. Died 1798.

**Men writing the Constitution watched his steamboat in 1787.**

Fitch's steamboat service of 1790 did not last. Too few people bought tickets, the machinery broke down, and the people who had put money into his boats stopped paying for them.

Fitch died in 1798. Nine years later Robert Fulton's boat began running on the Hudson River, and Fulton built the first steamboat business that lasted.
