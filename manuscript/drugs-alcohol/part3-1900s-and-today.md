## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 19 minutes**

Drugs and alcohol came under federal control in the first half of the 1900s, first through labels and then through bans. From 1906, drug makers had to list opium, morphine and alcohol on their labels. From 1915, everyone who sold opium or cocaine had to register and keep records, and Treasury officials, who worked in the department that handles the government's money and taxes, took doctors to court for giving addicts their drug. In 1937 members of Congress taxed marijuana.

**Prohibition.** Under federal law, making, selling and carrying alcoholic drinks was banned anywhere in the United States from 1920 to 1933. This ban is called Prohibition. People kept drinking in hidden bars and made liquor at home, and federal agents made more than half a million arrests a year. Treasury officials had more poison added to factory alcohol. Bootleggers, people who made or sold liquor against the law, stole that alcohol and sold it as drink, and the poison killed thousands of drinkers.

**Cigarettes.** Americans smoked an average of 54 cigarettes a year each in 1900, and by 1963 the figure was 4,345.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carry-nation-drugs-alcohol" name="Carry Nation" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carry Nation

> **Who:** A Kansas woman who smashed saloons to force officials to enforce the state's ban on liquor.
> **When and where:** Kiowa, Wichita and Topeka, Kansas, 1900 to 1901, and Kansas City, Missouri, 1901. Arrested more than 30 times through 1910.

**She walked into Dobson's Saloon carrying rocks.**

Carry Nation smashed saloons in Kansas because officials there did not enforce the state's ban on liquor. Kansas voters had put the ban into their state constitution in 1880, and it took effect in 1881, but saloons stayed open.

**Her name.** She was born Caroline Amelia Moore in Garrard County, Kentucky, in 1846. Official records spell her name Carrie. Later she spelled it Carry, and she said her name meant "Carry A Nation for Prohibition."

**Two husbands.** In 1867 she married Charles Gloyd, a doctor who drank heavily. They separated before their daughter, Charlien, was born in 1868, and Gloyd died of alcoholism, the illness of being addicted to alcohol, in 1869. In 1874 she married David A. Nation, a lawyer and minister. In Medicine Lodge, Kansas, she started a branch of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union and pressed officials to enforce the ban. She kept at it for about five years.

**Kiowa.** On June 5, 1900, she wrote later, she heard a voice telling her, "GO TO KIOWA." Two days later she walked into Dobson's Saloon in Kiowa, Kansas, carrying rocks. She said, "Men, I have come to save you from a drunkard's fate," and smashed the saloon's stock of liquor. She smashed two more saloons in Kiowa.

**The hatchet.** She wrecked a saloon in Wichita in January 1901 and spent two weeks in jail. Her husband joked that she should use a hatchet next time. "That is the most sensible thing you have said since I married you," she answered. In February 1901, in Topeka, she met the governor and spoke to Kansas lawmakers.

She was arrested more than 30 times between 1900 and 1910. She paid her fines with money from her speeches and by selling small pins shaped like hatchets. In 1901 she opened a home in Kansas City, Missouri, for the wives and children of men who drank. Kansas officials did not really enforce the ban until 1907. She died on June 9, 1911.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carry-nation-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the label had to say morphine" -->
The reporter Samuel Hopkins Adams wrote 11 articles about patent medicines, the brand-name medicines whose makers did not have to say what was in them, for *Collier's* magazine in 1905. He wrote that Americans would spend about $75 million that year on medicines that held large amounts of alcohol and opium.

By official tests, Hostetter's Bitters was 44 percent alcohol. One bottle of a medicine called Peruna, he wrote, held as much alcohol as five bottles of beer. He called the opium syrups sold for babies medicines "which stunt or kill helpless infants."

**The 1906 law.** President Theodore Roosevelt signed the Pure Food and Drug Act on June 30, 1906. Under it, drug makers had to print on the label whether a medicine held any of 10 drugs. They included alcohol, morphine, opium and cannabis, the plant marijuana comes from. The law did not ban any of these drugs. Chemists led by Harvey W. Wiley, at the Department of Agriculture, enforced it.

**What changed.** After the law passed, the makers of Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup took out the morphine. The historian David T. Courtwright writes that after 1900 fewer people became addicted through medicine. Doctors had grown more careful, and after the 1906 law and the articles of reporters like Adams, more people were wary of dosing themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Harrison Act, 1914: doctors and addicts" -->
Some officials and newspaper writers in these years blamed drug use on Black and Chinese people. A citizens' panel called the Committee on the Acquirement of the Drug Habit wrote in 1903 that if a Chinese man "cannot get along without his dope we can get along without him." In place of "a Chinese man," the panel used an insulting name for Chinese people.

**Hamilton Wright.** Theodore Roosevelt named Dr. Hamilton Wright the first United States opium commissioner, the official in charge of the country's work against opium, in 1908. Wright told members of Congress that cocaine was often "the direct incentive to the crime of rape of white women by Negroes." He was claiming that cocaine drove Black men (Negroes, in the word of the time) to rape white women, that is, to force them to have sex.

A *New York Times* headline of 1914 read "Negro Cocaine 'Fiends' Are a New Southern Menace."

**The dispute.** Historians disagree about how much these claims shaped the drug laws. David T. Courtwright writes that cocaine became linked in the public mind with crime sprees, especially by Black men. George Fisher of Stanford Law School points out that the first state cocaine bans, from 1887 to 1898, came before these newspaper stories. He argues that racial hatred came later, in the way police and other officers enforced the laws.

**The law.** Representative Francis Burton Harrison of New York proposed a new federal drug law, and President Woodrow Wilson signed it as the Harrison Narcotics Act on December 17, 1914. It took effect on March 1, 1915. In the law, narcotics meant drugs made from opium or from coca, the plant that cocaine comes from. Everyone who sold or handed out these drugs, including doctors and druggists, had to register, pay a small tax and keep records.

**Doctors in court.** Treasury officials read the law to mean that a doctor could not give an addict regular doses of a drug, and they took doctors who did to court. The justices of the Supreme Court agreed with them on March 3, 1919, in the case of *Webb v. United States*. A doctor could not write a prescription, a doctor's written order for a drug, for an addict just to keep the addict comfortable. Dr. Webb had written more than 80 morphine prescriptions a week, at 50 cents each.

**The clinics.** City officials then opened 35 public clinics that sold morphine cheaply to addicts who registered. Treasury officials closed every one of them. The last, in Shreveport, Louisiana, closed on February 10, 1923. After that, an addict could get drugs only from dealers, or go to prison.

**Heroin.** Heroin is a drug made from morphine. Young men in the poor immigrant neighborhoods of cities began sniffing small packets of heroin powder in the 1910s and early 1920s. President Calvin Coolidge signed a law on June 7, 1924, that banned bringing opium into the country to make heroin. Representative Stephen G. Porter of Pennsylvania had introduced it.

**The narcotic farm.** Officials of the Public Health Service, the federal health agency, opened a hospital for addicts in Lexington, Kentucky, in 1935. It was called a "narcotic farm," and prisoners could be sent there. One doctor remembered it as "more like a prison than a hospital and more like a hospital than a prison." Courtwright writes that several studies found that 90 percent or more of the people released from Lexington soon went back to drugs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cigarettes become an everyday habit" -->
Americans smoked an average of 54 cigarettes a year each in 1900, by the count of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. By 1963 the figure was 4,345. The justices of the Supreme Court ordered James B. Duke's American Tobacco Company broken into four smaller companies in 1911.

**"Tobacco as much as bullets."** General John Pershing led the American army sent to Europe in World War I. Asked what America needed to win the war, he answered, "Tobacco as much as bullets." People in community groups, businesses and relief groups such as the Red Cross, the Salvation Army and the Young Men's Christian Association set up "tobacco funds" to send cigarettes to soldiers. Editors at the *New York Sun* asked readers to give as a way of "proving your patriotism."

**The first cancer studies.** The scientist Ernst Wynder and others began studies in the 1940s that linked smoking cigarettes to lung cancer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition: the law and the people who wrote it" -->
Wayne Wheeler of the Anti-Saloon League played a major part in winning a national ban on liquor. Wheeler and other prohibitionists gained support from the fight to win the vote for women. In World War I, League leaders also used Americans' anger at Germany to win support.

**The amendment.** Members of Congress approved the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution on December 18, 1917. An amendment is a change added to the Constitution. Lawmakers in the 36th state voted for it on January 16, 1919, which made it part of the Constitution. Only the lawmakers of Connecticut and Rhode Island never voted for it.

**What it banned.** The amendment banned making, selling and carrying alcoholic drinks. It did not ban owning them or drinking them. Wheeler helped draft the Volstead Act, the law that set out the rules for the ban. It is named for Andrew Volstead of Minnesota, who led the House committee in charge of laws about courts and crimes. President Wilson refused to sign it and sent it back, and members of Congress passed it anyway on October 28, 1919. The ban began on January 17, 1920.

**The gaps.** A family could make up to 200 gallons of wine and cider a year at home. Doctors could prescribe whiskey as medicine, and they wrote about 11 million prescriptions for it a year through the 1920s. Officials caught 1,000 doctors and 400 druggists selling signed prescription forms to bootleggers in 1931. Twelve of the doctors and 13 of the druggists were charged with crimes.

**Speakeasies.** People drank in speakeasies, hidden bars that sold liquor against the law. By the end of the 1920s New York City alone had about 30,000 of them, by the count of the writer Deborah Blum. Officials in some states, among them Maryland and New York, refused to enforce the federal ban.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="izzy-einstein-drugs-alcohol" name="Izzy Einstein" movie="Izzy and Moe (1985)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Izzy Einstein

> **Who:** A Jewish immigrant and former postal clerk who became a federal Prohibition agent. He arrested people while wearing disguises.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1920 to 1925.
> **Movie:** *Izzy and Moe* (1985), a television film directed by Jackie Cooper, with Jackie Gleason as Izzy Einstein and Art Carney as Moe Smith. It is about the two agents.

**One Sunday he and his partner made 71 raids in 12 hours.**

Izzy Einstein and his partner Moe Smith arrested thousands of people for breaking the Prohibition law, and most of those people were found guilty. Einstein caught many of them while wearing a disguise.

**Eight languages.** Isidor Einstein was born in 1880 to a Jewish family in Austria-Hungary. He came to the United States around 1901, speaking Yiddish, Hungarian, Polish and German, and some Russian, French, Spanish and Italian. He worked as a salesman and then as a postal clerk, and he struggled to support his family on a clerk's pay.

**More than 100 disguises.** With no police training, he joined the new federal Prohibition service in 1920. He brought in his friend Moe Smith, who owned a cigar store. The two men said they used more than 100 disguises, among them a streetcar conductor, a gravedigger, a fisherman, an iceman and an opera singer. They usually carried no guns. Sometimes Einstein told a doorman that he was a Prohibition agent. The doorman laughed and let him in.

**The count.** One Sunday, with reporters following them, they made 71 raids in a little more than 12 hours. Together they made 4,932 arrests by one count, and more than 5,000 by the count of the National Archives. Of the people they arrested, 95 percent were found guilty. Thousands of bartenders, bootleggers and speakeasy owners went to jail because of their work.

**Fired.** Their boss, General Lincoln C. Andrews, fired them along with 36 other men in November 1925, when he reorganized the New York office. Writers at *Time* magazine suggested that their fame in the newspapers had annoyed their bosses. Both men went on to sell insurance. In 1932 Einstein published a book, *Prohibition Agent No. 1*. He died on February 17, 1938.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="izzy-einstein-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition agents: who enforced the ban" -->
The federal government had 1,450 front-line Prohibition agents in 1930. From 1921 to 1930 the agents made more than half a million arrests a year and seized more than 45,000 cars, by the count of the historian Richard F. Hamm.

**Political jobs.** Politicians handed out many of the agents' jobs. Judge William S. Kenyon said in 1927 that "three-fourths of the 2,500 dry agents are ward heelers and sycophants named by the politicians." He meant that most agents were political helpers and flatterers. When agents had to pass a civil service test, the kind of test used to give out many government jobs, 60 percent failed it.

**Fired for bribes.** In the six years from 1920, 752 Prohibition officials were fired, most for drunkenness or for taking bribes. Mabel Walker Willebrandt was the Assistant Attorney General, a top lawyer in the Justice Department, in charge of Prohibition cases from 1921 to 1929. She said the agents were "as devoid of honesty and integrity" as the lawbreakers, meaning they were just as dishonest.

**The dead.** By their own count, Prohibition agents killed 89 people. The Association Against the Prohibition Amendment, a group working to end the ban, counted about 1,000 people killed in enforcing it. Near the Capitol in Washington in 1924, an agent shooting at a bootlegger's car hit Senator Frank L. Greene of Vermont in the head. Greene never got back the full use of one arm.

The writer Daniel Okrent describes the first agent to kill a suspected bootlegger. That agent had joined under a false name. He had killed a man at the age of 14, and he got his badge while he was still locked up in Dannemora State Prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poisoned alcohol and jake leg" -->
Treasury Department officials under President Calvin Coolidge ordered more poison put into factory alcohol in 1926 and 1927, and the poison killed thousands of people who drank it. Factory alcohol, used for fuel and in factories, had chemicals added to it so that no one could drink it. This is called denatured alcohol. Bootleggers stole it and paid chemists to take the chemicals out.

**More poison.** The officials then ordered makers to add far more poison. By 1927 the mixes included kerosene, gasoline, benzene and mercury salts, and up to 10 percent methyl alcohol, also called wood alcohol, which blinds and kills people who drink it. *Time* magazine reported in January 1927 that three ordinary drinks of the new mix could cause blindness. The records do not name the official who signed the order.

**Christmas 1926.** More than 60 people had come to Bellevue Hospital in New York sick from poisoned liquor by dusk on Christmas Day 1926, and eight of them had died. In the next two days, 23 more people died in the city.

**Known:** the poisoned alcohol killed thousands of Americans.
**Disputed:** how many. Deborah Blum gives 400 dead in New York City in 1926 and 700 in 1927, and another count gives 585 in the city in 1926. For the whole country, one estimate gives more than 5,000 dead, and Blum gives an estimate of at least 10,000 by 1933.

**What officials said.** Charles Norris was New York City's chief medical examiner, the doctor in charge of finding out why people in the city died. He said, "The government knows it is not stopping drinking by putting poison in alcohol." The government, he said, "must be charged with the moral responsibility for the deaths that poisoned liquor causes, although it cannot be held legally responsible."

Seymour Lowman, the Treasury official in charge of Prohibition from 1927 as Assistant Secretary of the Treasury, said drinkers were "dying off fast from poison 'hooch.'" If the result was a sober America, he said, "a good job will have been done." Wayne Wheeler said that a person who drank this alcohol "is a deliberate suicide." Senator Edward I. Edwards of New Jersey called the program "legalized murder."

**Jake leg.** Thousands of people were poisoned in 1930 after they drank Jamaica ginger, a medicine that was 70 to 80 percent alcohol. The poison came from Harry Gross, the president of Hub Products Corporation in Boston, who wanted a cheaper ingredient for his Jamaica ginger. He chose a chemical called TOCP, used in making lacquer, a shiny coating for wood and metal. People at the Celluloid Corporation, which made TOCP, told him it was not poisonous.

TOCP destroys nerve cells in the spinal cord, the bundle of nerves inside the backbone that carries signals from the brain to the body. People who drank Gross's Jamaica ginger lost the use of their hands and feet. They walked with their toes flopping down. People called the illness "jake leg" and the walk the "jake walk." For most of them the damage never healed.

**Not known:** the exact number harmed. The figure most often given is between 30,000 and 50,000. Most were poor, and many were immigrants.

**Gross's sentence.** A judge fined Gross and his partner Max Reisman $1,000 each. The judge also gave each of them a two-year jail sentence that they did not have to serve. Blues singers recorded songs about jake leg in 1930.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alcoholics Anonymous, 1935" -->
Two heavy drinkers named Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith met in Akron, Ohio, in 1935, through a Christian group called the Oxford Group. Together they started Alcoholics Anonymous, or AA (anonymous means without names), where drinkers help each other stop drinking. At meetings, members share their own stories of drinking and of getting sober.

AA members published the group's Twelve Steps, a list of steps for staying sober, in 1939, in a book that members call the Big Book. Wilson and Smith had never heard of the Washingtonians, the Baltimore drinkers of 1840 who also helped each other stay sober by telling their stories.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Did drinking fall under Prohibition?" -->
Drinking fell under Prohibition, and then much of it came back. The economists Miron and Zwiebel found in a 1991 study that drinking fell at first to about 30 percent of what it had been before the ban. Over the next several years it rose again, to about 60 to 70 percent. Deaths from cirrhosis, damage to the liver from heavy drinking, fell by nearly two-thirds. Blum writes that insurance companies charted a rise in alcoholism of more than 300 percent in the 1920s.

**Known:** drinking fell sharply when the ban began.
**Not known:** how much drinking changed over the whole ban, because the counts point different ways.

**Pauline Sabin.** Pauline Sabin, a leader of the Republican Party in New York, first backed the ban. "I felt I should approve of it because it would help my two sons," she said. She changed her mind. By 1929 she said that "children are growing up with a total lack of respect for the Constitution and for the law." In May 1929, in Chicago, she founded the Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform to end the ban.

**Repeal.** President Franklin Roosevelt signed a law on March 22, 1933, that allowed beer with 3.2 percent alcohol by weight. Delegates in Utah made it the 36th state to approve the Twenty-first Amendment, which ended national Prohibition, on December 5, 1933. After that, lawmakers in each state and town made their own rules about when, where and to whom alcohol could be sold. Liquor stayed banned in Kansas until 1948.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marijuana and the 1937 tax" -->
Marijuana is made from the dried leaves and flowers of the cannabis plant. Harry J. Anslinger ran the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, the federal drug police, from 1930 to 1962. "The best cure for addiction? Never let it happen," he said.

**Anslinger's stories.** His articles against marijuana in the 1930s often told stories that blamed the drug for crimes by Black and Mexican people. One story in his files began, "Two Negroes took a girl fourteen years old..." The historian Courtwright writes that Anslinger played on Americans' fear of immigrants and of other races. Anslinger told the story of Victor Licata, who killed his family with an axe in Florida in 1933, as a marijuana crime. Doctors had found that Licata had a severe mental illness when he was young.

**The tax.** Anslinger drafted the Marihuana Tax Act, and doctors of the American Medical Association objected to it. President Franklin Roosevelt signed it on August 2, 1937, and it took effect on October 1. Selling marijuana without a federal tax stamp, which cost $1 an ounce, became a crime.

**The first arrests.** Federal narcotics agents and Denver police arrested Samuel R. Caldwell, a 57-year-old laborer, for selling marijuana on October 2, 1937. They arrested Moses Baca, 26, for having it. The tax stamps had not yet been printed. Judge J. Foster Symes sentenced Caldwell to four years of hard labor at Leavenworth prison and a $1,000 fine, and Baca to 18 months. "Under its influence men become beasts," Symes said of marijuana. Caldwell was released in 1940 and died in 1941.

**A study.** Mayor Fiorello La Guardia of New York named a committee to study marijuana in 1938. In their report of 1944, the committee's members disputed Anslinger's claims.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor laws and Native nations" -->
Federal bans on liquor on Native land stayed in force through all of these years. Members of Congress made it a crime in 1910 to bring alcohol onto the land of the Pueblo nations of New Mexico, as a condition of New Mexico becoming a state. That same year Attorney General George Wickersham asked for lighter punishments for Native liquor crimes.

The Native American Church was organized in Oklahoma in 1918. Its members take peyote as a sacred act of worship.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1950" -->
**If you remember one thing:** In these fifty years federal lawmakers took charge of drugs and alcohol, with labels in 1906, a drug law in 1914, a national ban on alcohol from 1920 to 1933 and a tax on marijuana in 1937.
**One number:** Americans smoked 54 cigarettes a year each in 1900 and 4,345 in 1963, about 80 times as many.
**Next:** After 1950 scientists showed that cigarettes cause cancer, and a president declared a war on drugs.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 15 minutes**

Americans learned in these fifty years that smoking kills, and the federal fight against drugs grew from $81 million a year in 1969 to $18.4 billion in 2000. About 42 of every 100 American adults smoked cigarettes in 1965, and by 1997 the number was about 25. In 1996 a fired tobacco scientist told a national television audience that his company changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine. Drinking rose until 1981 and then fell.

**The war on drugs.** President Richard Nixon called drug abuse "public enemy number one" on June 17, 1971, and reporters named his program the war on drugs. Candy Lightner started Mothers Against Drunk Driving in 1980, after a drunk driver killed her daughter, and by 1988 lawmakers in every state had set the drinking age at 21.

Under a 1986 law passed by members of Congress and signed by President Ronald Reagan, 5 grams of crack, a kind of cocaine that can be smoked, carried the same prison term as 500 grams of powder cocaine. A company owned by the Sackler family began selling a strong painkiller called OxyContin in 1996.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much people drank and smoked" -->
Americans aged 15 and over drank an average of 2.04 gallons of pure alcohol each in 1950. From 1970 the count covers people aged 14 and over, and by that count the figure rose to 2.76 gallons in 1981, the highest since Prohibition. By 1995 it had fallen to 2.15 gallons.

Scientists at the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism work these figures out from sales of alcohol, not from asking people what they drank. Between 1974 and 1981, 68 to 71 of every 100 adults told the Gallup company, which asks adults questions about what they think and do, that they drank.

**Cigarettes.** Cigarette smoking peaked in 1963, at 4,345 cigarettes a year for each person. By 1998 the figure was 2,261. In the late 1990s the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) counted about 430,000 deaths a year from tobacco, one of every five deaths in the country.

**The Surgeon General's report.** The studies that Ernst Wynder and others began in the 1940s went on into the 1950s. In 1964 a committee of experts working for the Surgeon General, the nation's top public health doctor, concluded that smoking causes lung cancer and cancer of the voice box. Nixon signed a law on April 1, 1970, that banned cigarette ads on radio and television from January 2, 1971.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Billie Holiday and the narcotics agents" -->
The singer Billie Holiday was arrested for drugs three times. Federal narcotics agents made the last two arrests, the last one in her hospital bed.

**New York, 1947.** Holiday was arrested on May 16, 1947, for having narcotics in her New York apartment. In court she said, "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt." She pleaded guilty, and a judge sent her to Alderson Federal Prison Camp in West Virginia.

After she was found guilty, she lost the police license she needed to sing in New York City clubs that sold alcohol. The federal agent George Hunter White arrested her again in a San Francisco hotel on January 22, 1949.

**The hospital bed.** Holiday was in Metropolitan Hospital in New York in 1959, being treated for liver and heart disease. On May 31, Harry Anslinger ordered agents of his Federal Bureau of Narcotics to arrest her there for having drugs. The agents handcuffed her to her hospital bed. A police guard stayed at her room until a judge ordered the handcuffs taken off.

She died in that room on July 17, 1959, at the age of 44. The cause was heart failure and fluid in her lungs, which came from cirrhosis, scarring of the liver. She had 70 cents in the bank.

**Disputed:** why the agents went after her. The writer Johann Hari has argued that Anslinger's agents targeted Holiday from 1939 because of her song "Strange Fruit," which is about lynching, killing by a mob. The jazz writer Lewis Porter disputes this. He points out that the claim first appears in Hari's own book, in 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native nations and the end of the federal ban, 1953" -->
Federal law stopped barring Native people from buying alcohol off their nations' land in 1953. Members of Congress passed a law on August 15 of that year "to eliminate certain discriminatory legislation against Indians," meaning laws that treated Native people worse than others. The ban on liquor on Native land had lasted 20 years longer than national Prohibition.

**Each nation's choice.** On a nation's own land, a sale of alcohol now had to follow both state law and a law passed by that nation's government, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, the head of the federal department that handles dealings with Native nations.

Members of Congress gave each nation the choice of keeping its ban, and some nations kept it. The Oglala Sioux Tribe, on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota, was one, according to the National Alcohol Beverage Control Association, a group of state alcohol officials.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="LSD and marijuana" -->
LSD is a drug that makes people see and hear things that are not there, and in the early 1960s it was legal. Timothy Leary, a teacher at Harvard University who studied how the mind works, ran experiments from 1960 in which he gave people psilocybin, a drug from certain mushrooms that causes visions. He also used LSD.

Harvard officials stopped paying him on April 30, 1963, and Harvard's president, Nathan Pusey, said he had "failed to keep his classroom appointments." Nixon later called Leary "the most dangerous man in America."

**Marijuana.** A man named Lowell Eggemeier lit a marijuana cigarette in San Francisco's Hall of Justice in 1964 and asked to be arrested. The writer Martin A. Lee dates the start of the modern campaign to make marijuana legal to that act. Oregon lawmakers made having a small amount of marijuana punishable by a fine instead of jail in 1973. In November 1996, California voters approved Proposition 215, 56 percent to 44 percent, and California became the first state to allow marijuana as a medicine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nixon declares the war on drugs, 1970 and 1971" -->
Nixon signed a law on October 27, 1970, that sorted drugs into five lists, called schedules. The lawmakers put marijuana and heroin on Schedule I, the list for drugs with a high risk of abuse and no accepted use as medicine. Under the same law, having a small amount of a drug became a lesser crime, with at most one year in jail for a first offense. Selling drugs carried harsher punishments than before.

**Heroin in Vietnam.** Two members of Congress, Morgan Murphy and Robert Steele, came back from the war in Vietnam in May 1971. They reported that "as many as 10 to 15 percent of our servicemen are addicted to heroin."

**"Public enemy number one."** Nixon spoke at the White House on June 17, 1971. "America's public enemy number one in the United States is drug abuse," he said. He asked for $155 million in new money, for a total of "over $350 million" a year to fight drugs. When he took office in 1969, the federal drug budget had been $81 million. While Nixon was president, about 70 of every 100 federal dollars spent against drugs went to treatment and prevention.

**Arrests.** Agents of Nixon's new Office of Drug Abuse Law Enforcement made 6,000 drug arrests in 18 months, from 1972 to 1973, and most of the people arrested were Black. In 1973 Governor Nelson Rockefeller of New York signed laws that set 15 years to life in prison for selling two ounces, or holding four ounces, of heroin, cocaine or marijuana.

**The Ehrlichman quote.** The journalist Dan Baum published notes in 2016 of a 1994 interview with John Ehrlichman, one of Nixon's top advisers. In Baum's notes, Ehrlichman said that "by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and blacks with heroin," and then making both a crime and punishing them heavily ("criminalizing both heavily"), "we could disrupt those communities." Ehrlichman had died in 1999. His children and other officials who had worked for Nixon disputed the quote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="betty-ford-drugs-alcohol" name="Betty Ford" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Betty Ford

> **Who:** The wife of President Gerald Ford. She told the country she was addicted to pills and alcohol, and then she founded a treatment center.
> **When and where:** Long Beach and Rancho Mirage, California, 1978 to 1982.

**At times she took as many as 20 pills a day.**

Betty Ford told the whole country in 1978 that she was addicted to pills and to alcohol. Four years later she founded a center to treat people with the same addictions.

**The pills.** A pinched nerve in her neck put her in the hospital for two weeks in 1964. Doctors gave her drugs for the pain, including strong painkillers and Valium, a drug that calms people. At times she took as many as 20 pills a day. She also drank. Her father and her brother Bob had both been addicted to alcohol.

**The intervention.** Her family held an intervention, a meeting to ask her to go into treatment, on April 1, 1978, after the Fords had left the White House. On April 11 she checked into the Naval hospital in Long Beach, California. She had arthritis, a disease that makes the joints swell and hurt, and a family spokesman read her statement: "I have found I am not only addicted to the medication I have been taking for my arthritis, but also to alcohol."

She later wrote, "I liked alcohol, it made me feel warm. And I loved pills. They took away my tension and my pain."

**The center.** She and Leonard Firestone, a former ambassador, founded the Betty Ford Center in Rancho Mirage, California, in 1982, for people recovering from addiction to alcohol and other drugs. It opened on October 4, 1982. She died on July 8, 2011.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="betty-ford-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the drinking age is 21" -->
Clarence Busch was drunk when he hit and killed 13-year-old Cari Lightner with his car on May 3, 1980. She was walking to a church festival in Fair Oaks, California. He drove away and left her body there. He had a long record of arrests for drunk driving.

**The sentence.** A judge ruled that Busch's alcoholism had made it harder for him to control his decisions. The judge gave him two years in prison for involuntary manslaughter, the crime of killing someone without meaning to. He entered prison in December 1980 and was paroled nine months later, which means he was let out early under watch.

He went back to prison in 1982 for breaking the rules of his parole, and he was freed again in February 1983. Police arrested him again in April 1985, drunk and driving without a license, after a crash.

**MADD.** Cari's mother, Candy Lightner, founded Mothers Against Drunk Driving, or MADD, in 1980. By MADD's count, nearly 25,000 people were killed in crashes involving alcohol in 1980. That was half of all traffic deaths.

**The drinking age.** Lawmakers in 29 states had lowered the drinking age to 18, 19 or 20 between 1970 and 1975, after the voting age was lowered to 18. Senator Frank Lautenberg of New Jersey wrote a law to raise the drinking age again, and Reagan signed it on July 17, 1984. Under it, a state that let people under 21 buy alcohol lost part of its federal money for highways. By 1988 every state had set the age at 21.

**Disputed:** how many lives the higher drinking age saved. A review for the Institute of Medicine, a national group of health experts, found strong evidence that it worked. The economists Jeffrey Miron and Elina Tetelbaum found in 2009 that the effect faded after a year or two.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crack cocaine and the 1986 law" -->
Nancy Reagan, the president's wife, began a campaign in the early 1980s, with help from an advertising company, in which she told young people to "Just Say No" to drugs. Later studies found little or no change in how many young people used drugs after the campaign. The FBI's budget for fighting drugs rose from $8 million in 1980 to $95 million in 1984.

**Crack.** Crack is cocaine that dealers cooked with water and baking soda and dried into small rocks that can be smoked. Smoked crack reaches the brain in 6 to 8 seconds, and the high lasts 5 to 15 minutes. Around 1984 a dose cost as little as $2.50 in some cities. Hospital emergencies from cocaine more than doubled in one year, from 26,300 in 1985 to 55,200 in 1986.

**The 1986 law.** The basketball player Len Bias died from cocaine on June 19, 1986. Afterward, Speaker of the House Tip O'Neill had Democratic leaders write a new drug law, and Reagan signed it on October 27, 1986. In it, members of Congress added $1.7 billion to the fight against drugs and set 29 new minimum prison terms that judges had to give. The law set five years in federal prison for 5 grams of crack, the same as for 500 grams of powder cocaine.

**Who went to prison.** Under the law, a person caught with crack got the same prison term as a person caught with 100 times as much powder cocaine. Most of the people sentenced for crack were Black. In 1992, 91 of every 100 people sentenced in federal court for crack were Black, by the count of the US Sentencing Commission.

Among people who entered treatment for crack in 2004, 53 of every 100 were Black and 38 were white. The historian Elizabeth Hinton writes that Reagan "led Congress in criminalizing drug users, especially African American drug users."

**The bag on television.** President George H. W. Bush held up a bag of crack on television on September 5, 1989. He said it had been "seized a few days ago in a park across the street from the White House." Agents of the Drug Enforcement Administration had lured the seller to that park to make the arrest. By 2000 the federal government spent $18.4 billion a year against drugs. Nearly half went to law enforcement, the police and agents who catch lawbreakers, and one-sixth went to treatment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeffrey-wigand-drugs-alcohol" name="Jeffrey Wigand" movie="The Insider (1999)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jeffrey Wigand

> **Who:** A scientist who ran research at a tobacco company and then told the public that the company changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine.
> **When and where:** Brown & Williamson Tobacco, Louisville, Kentucky, 1989 to 1996.
> **Movie:** *The Insider* (1999), directed by Michael Mann, with Russell Crowe as Jeffrey Wigand. It is about Wigand.

**He said his company added ammonia to boost nicotine's effect.**

Jeffrey Wigand told the public in 1996 that his former company had changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine, the drug in tobacco that makes people addicted.

**Fired.** Wigand trained as a biochemist, a scientist who studies the chemistry of living things. He became vice president for research at Brown & Williamson Tobacco in Louisville, Kentucky, in January 1989. Brown & Williamson fired him on March 24, 1993. That September the company sued him and cut off his health insurance. In November 1993 he signed an agreement not to talk about his work.

**Testimony.** Under a judge's order, Wigand gave sworn testimony in Pascagoula, Mississippi, in late November 1995, for the state's lawsuit against the tobacco companies. The company's lawyers got a Kentucky court order against him and sued him for "theft, fraud, breach of contract," which means they said he stole, cheated and broke his agreement with the company. Lawyers for the CBS television network stopped the news program *60 Minutes* from showing an interview with him that November.

**On the air.** *60 Minutes* showed the interview on February 4, 1996. Wigand said the company added chemicals such as ammonia to its tobacco to increase the effect of nicotine. He said he had been harassed and had received death threats from people who did not give their names. The company hired investigators, who wrote a 500-page report attacking him and gave it to reporters. The reporters checked it and found that many of its charges were unproven or small.

After he was fired, Wigand taught science and Japanese at a high school in Louisville. He was named Kentucky's Teacher of the Year for 1996.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeffrey-wigand-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="OxyContin goes on sale, 1996" -->
Opioids are strong painkillers made from the opium poppy, or from chemicals that act the same way in the body. Too much of an opioid slows a person's breathing until it stops.

**The Sacklers.** Three doctors, the brothers Arthur, Mortimer and Raymond Sackler, bought a drug company called Purdue Frederick in 1952. Their company, later named Purdue Pharma, began selling OxyContin in 1996. OxyContin is a pill of oxycodone, an opioid, made to release the drug slowly. Purdue sold it to be taken every 12 hours, but most patients found that it wore off after eight hours or less.

**The approval.** Dr. Curtis Wright, of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), reviewed OxyContin before it went on sale. He approved a label that said the slow release "is believed to reduce the abuse liability of a drug," meaning the risk that people would misuse it. A year later Wright left the FDA and went to work for Purdue.

**The sales push.** Purdue's managers trained its salespeople to tell doctors that the risk of addiction was "less than one percent." More than 5,000 doctors, pharmacists and nurses went to Purdue meetings with all their costs paid. Richard Sackler, Raymond's son, ran the company's sales and marketing, and he became its president in 1999.

Prescriptions of OxyContin for pain not caused by cancer rose from about 670,000 in 1997 to about 6.2 million in 2002. From 1995 to 2001, Purdue took in $2.8 billion from sales of OxyContin. David Kessler, who led the FDA when the pill was approved, later called the approval "one of the worst medical mistakes."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

**If you remember one thing:** Smokers fell from about 42 of every 100 adults in 1965 to about 25 in 1997, while the federal fight against drugs grew from $81 million a year to $18.4 billion and the prison terms for crack fell most heavily on Black Americans.
**The question this era leaves open:** What would happen to the people taking OxyContin, now that doctors were writing about 6.2 million prescriptions for it a year?

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 14 minutes**

Opioids, first pain pills such as OxyContin, then heroin, then fentanyl, killed about 806,000 Americans from 1999 to 2023, by the count of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). An overdose is too much of a drug at once, enough to make a person very sick or kill them.

Most of the deaths from 2013 on came from fentanyl made outside the law. Overdoses of all drugs killed 107,941 people in 2022, the peak year in the CDC's final figures. By the CDC's early count, the yearly total had fallen to about 67,000 in the 12 months ending in April 2026.

**The rest of the story.** A federal judge sentenced Purdue Pharma, the maker of OxyContin, in 2026, and the company shut down. No member of the Sackler family, who owned it, has been charged with a crime. Over the same years, voters and lawmakers in 24 states made marijuana legal for adults. In July 2026, 54 of every 100 adults told Gallup pollsters that they drink, the lowest share since Gallup began asking in 1939. About 9 of every 100 adults smoked cigarettes in 2025, by the CDC's count.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Overdose deaths, 1999 to 2026" -->
CDC scientists describe the opioid deaths as coming in three waves. The first wave began in 1999, after doctors wrote more and more prescriptions for opioid pills in the 1990s. Deaths from heroin rose from 2010. From 2013, deaths rose from opioids made in labs, mostly fentanyl made and sold outside the law.

**The death rate.** About 6 of every 100,000 Americans died of a drug overdose in 1999, by the CDC's count. The rate rose to about 33 of every 100,000 in 2022 and fell to about 23 in 2024.

**The counts.** The CDC's final counts of overdose deaths from all drugs rose from 70,237 in 2017 to 91,799 in 2020 and 107,941 in 2022. That last figure is about 300 deaths a day. By the CDC's early count, deaths peaked at 112,418 in the 12 months ending in August 2023.

The final count fell to 105,007 in 2023, and opioids were involved in 79,358 of those deaths. In 2024 it fell to 79,384. The CDC's early count for the 12 months ending in April 2026 is 66,937, and opioids were involved in 41,755 of those. CDC scientists warn that their early counts are often incomplete.

**Who dies.** Some groups die at far higher rates than others. American Indian and Alaska Native people died of overdoses at a rate of 51.6 of every 100,000 in 2024, by the CDC's count. The rate was 33.8 for Black Americans and 24.7 for white Americans. By age, people aged 35 to 44 died at the highest rate, 44.2 of every 100,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fentanyl" -->
Fentanyl is an opioid made in a lab. By the CDC's count, it is 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine. Too much of it slows a person's breathing until the breathing stops. A drug called naloxone can undo the overdose and save the person if someone gives it fast enough. Officials of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a naloxone spray for the nose that anyone can buy without a doctor's order on March 29, 2023.

**The rise and fall.** Deaths from fentanyl and similar lab-made opioids rose from 1 of every 100,000 Americans in 2013 to 22.7 in 2022, by the CDC's count. In 2024 the rate fell to 14.3.

**Who makes it.** Most of the illegal fentanyl sold in the United States has come from Mexico since about 2019. A 2025 report by the Congressional Research Service, which studies problems for members of Congress, names the makers. Mexican crime groups called cartels, especially the Sinaloa Cartel and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, make it outside the law. Companies in China are the largest source of the chemicals they need. The cartels' members use machines to press fentanyl into fake pills made to look like real medicine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why no Sackler went to prison" -->
No member of the Sackler family has gone to prison. When Judge Madeline Cox Arleo sentenced Purdue Pharma in 2026, she said she could not send its leaders or owners to prison because Justice Department lawyers had charged only the company.

**The first guilty plea.** Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty in May 2007 to misleading the public about OxyContin. The company admitted that it had sold OxyContin as less addictive and less likely to be abused than other painkillers. It paid $600 million. Three of its leaders also pleaded guilty: its president, Michael Friedman, its top lawyer, Howard Udell, and its former chief medical officer, Paul Goldenheim. Together they paid $34.5 million. In 2009 Purdue changed OxyContin into a form that was harder to crush and snort or inject.

**Bankruptcy.** Purdue filed for bankruptcy, a court process for a business that cannot pay its debts, on September 15, 2019. Purdue hired a firm called AlixPartners to check the company's money records. In December 2019 the firm reported that members of the Sackler family had taken $10.7 billion out of the company after lawsuits and investigations began.

**The second guilty plea.** Purdue pleaded guilty to three federal crimes on November 24, 2020. One was a plot to cheat the United States. The other two were plots to pay doctors and others to push its pills. In that case, no company leader and no Sackler was charged.

**The settlement.** Five justices of the Supreme Court threw out a bankruptcy plan on June 27, 2024, because it would have protected the Sacklers from lawsuits. A year later, on June 16, 2025, 55 attorneys general, the top lawyers of states, agreed to a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sacklers, a deal that ended lawsuits in return for money.

**The sentence.** Judge Arleo, in Newark, New Jersey, sentenced Purdue to $5.5 billion in fines and penalties for its 2020 guilty plea on April 28, 2026. For nearly seven hours she heard from people harmed by the crisis. More than 40 spoke in court, and more than 200 sent letters.

Ed Bisch's son Eddie died of an overdose in 2001. "Punishment by a fine means 'legal for a price,'" Bisch said. Alexis Pleus said her son Jeff had first been prescribed OxyContin for a football injury, before he died of an overdose. "We still deserve justice, and this isn't it," she said.

**"Your government failed you."** That is what Arleo told the families. Most of the $5.5 billion will never be paid. Federal officials collect $225 million, and Purdue's other money goes to the states and towns it owes. The settlement includes $865 million for people harmed one by one. Many victims said they could not qualify because they could not find their old prescription records.

**Purdue shuts down.** New York Attorney General Letitia James announced three days after the sentence, on May 1, 2026, that Purdue had shut down. A new company, Knoa Pharma, owned by a charity, took over. Under the settlement the Sacklers must pay $2.9 billion by 2029, and none of them may sell opioids in the United States. The Sackler family has always said it did nothing wrong.

**Other companies.** The three largest companies that ship drugs to pharmacies, McKesson, Cardinal Health and AmerisourceBergen, agreed in 2021 to pay up to $21 billion over 18 years. The drug maker Johnson & Johnson agreed to pay up to $5 billion. In 2022 the drug makers Teva and Allergan and the pharmacy chains CVS, Walgreens and Walmart agreed to pay up to about $18.5 billion more among them. Under the settlements, states and towns must spend at least 85 of every 100 dollars they get on the overdose crisis.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nan-goldin-drugs-alcohol" name="Nan Goldin" movie="All the Beauty and the Bloodshed (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nan Goldin

> **Who:** A photographer who became addicted to OxyContin after surgery, overdosed on fentanyl, went through treatment, and led protests against the Sackler family.
> **When and where:** Berlin and New York, the 2010s to 2021.
> **Movie:** *All the Beauty and the Bloodshed* (2022), a documentary by Laura Poitras about Goldin's life and her protests.

**She threw OxyContin bottles into a museum's temple pool.**

Nan Goldin became addicted to OxyContin after a doctor prescribed it, and she overdosed on fentanyl. After treatment, she led protests against the Sackler family, who owned the company that made OxyContin. She told her own story in the magazine *Artforum* in January 2018.

**Addicted.** Goldin is a photographer known for her book of photographs *The Ballad of Sexual Dependency* (1986). A doctor in Berlin prescribed OxyContin for her after surgery on her wrist. "Though I took it as directed I got addicted overnight," she wrote. "I went from three pills a day, as prescribed, to eighteen."

**The overdose.** When her doctors stopped prescribing it, she bought pills from a dealer in New York. "When I ran out of money for Oxy I copped dope," she wrote, which means she bought illegal drugs from dealers. "I ended up snorting fentanyl and I overdosed." She waited a year before she went into treatment, because she was afraid of withdrawal, the sickness that comes when the body loses a drug it depends on. Then she spent two and a half months in treatment.

**The protest.** Goldin started a group called P.A.I.N., which stands for Prescription Addiction Intervention Now, in 2018. On March 10, 2018, she and other members protested in the Sackler Wing of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. They threw pill bottles labeled OxyContin into the pool around the Temple of Dendur, an Egyptian temple about 2,000 years old that stands in the wing. Then they lay on the floor and chanted, "Sacklers lie, people die."

**The names come down.** Officials at the National Portrait Gallery in London turned down a £1 million gift from the Sacklers in 2019, after Goldin threatened to pull her show. Leaders of the Tate galleries in London said they would take no more Sackler gifts. Officials of the Metropolitan Museum and members of the Sackler families announced on December 9, 2021, that seven of the museum's spaces would no longer carry the Sackler name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nan-goldin-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marijuana becomes legal in 24 states" -->
Voters in Colorado and Washington made marijuana legal for adults on November 6, 2012, the first two states to do so. Washington's law took effect first, on December 6, 2012. In 2018 Vermont's lawmakers became the first to make it legal without a vote of the public.

**The count.** By the count of the National Conference of State Legislatures, a group that serves state lawmakers, 24 states and the District of Columbia allow adults to use marijuana. The group's page was last updated in September 2026.

The 24 states are Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Ohio, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia and Washington. By the same count, 41 states allow marijuana as medicine. In November 2024, voters in Florida, North Dakota and South Dakota voted against making it legal.

**Federal law.** Under federal law, marijuana was still on Schedule I, the list for drugs with a high risk of abuse and no accepted use as medicine. President Donald Trump ordered officials on December 18, 2025, to speed up moving it to Schedule III, a list of drugs that doctors may order as medicine.

The Acting Attorney General moved marijuana sold under state medical licenses to Schedule III on April 28, 2026. All other marijuana stayed on Schedule I. In June 2026 officials of the Drug Enforcement Administration began hearings on moving the rest. As of late September 2026, they had not decided.

**Crack sentences.** Punishments for crack also changed. President Barack Obama signed the Fair Sentencing Act, written by Senator Dick Durbin, in 2010. Under it, the difference between crack and powder cocaine sentences dropped from 100 to 1 to 18 to 1. Under the First Step Act, which President Trump signed in 2018, the change also covered people already in prison. In its first year, more than 2,000 federal prisoners had their sentences shortened.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vaping and smoking" -->
An e-cigarette heats a liquid, usually one with nicotine in it, into a mist that the user breathes in. Using one is called vaping. About 27.5 of every 100 high school students vaped in 2019, by the count of a national survey released by the CDC.

**The lung injuries.** A lung injury from vaping put 2,807 people in the hospital or killed them in 2019 and early 2020, by the CDC's count. Of them, 68 died. CDC scientists found that vitamin E acetate, a chemical added to illegal marijuana vaping liquids, was strongly linked to the injuries. They linked most cases to those liquids, which most of the sick people had bought from friends, family or dealers. The records do not name who made them.

**Age 21.** President Trump signed a law on December 20, 2019, that raised the age to buy any tobacco product, including e-cigarettes, from 18 to 21. About 5.2 of every 100 middle and high school students vaped in 2025, by the count of a national survey released by the FDA in June 2026.

**Cigarettes.** About 9.1 of every 100 adults smoked cigarettes in 2025, by the CDC's count, against 42.4 in 1965. Smoking and secondhand smoke, the smoke other people breathe in, still kill more than 480,000 Americans a year, by the CDC's newest count, from 2024.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinking today" -->
Americans aged 14 and over drank an average of 2.48 gallons of pure alcohol each in 2023, by the count of the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism. That is below the peak of 2.76 gallons in 1981. Americans now drink more liquor and less beer than in 2000. Pure alcohol from beer fell from 1.23 gallons a person in 2000 to 0.99 in 2023, and pure alcohol from liquor rose from 0.64 gallons to 1.08.

**Fewer drinkers.** Gallup pollsters asked adults about drinking in July 2026, and 54 of every 100 said they drink, the lowest share since Gallup began asking in 1939. In 2023 the figure was 62. In the same 2026 poll, 51 of every 100 adults said that one or two drinks a day is bad for health.

**The harm.** The CDC counts about 178,000 deaths a year from drinking too much, from data for 2020 and 2021. On average, each of those people lost 24 years of life. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy reported on January 3, 2025, that alcohol causes about 100,000 cases of cancer and 20,000 cancer deaths a year in the United States. He asked Congress to put a cancer warning on alcohol labels.

Federal health officials dropped their old advice in January 2026, which had been to drink no more than one drink a day for women and two for men. The new advice says only, "Consume less alcohol for better health."

**Young people and AA.** About 41 of every 100 high school seniors said in 2025 that they had drunk alcohol in the past year, by the count of the Monitoring the Future survey. Alcoholics Anonymous estimates that it has more than two million members in more than 123,000 groups around the world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- How long have people here used tobacco, and what did they use it for? For at least 12,300 years, since the fire at the Wishbone site. Native people offered it in prayer, burning it or smoking it in pipes, and the Algonquian people at Roanoke smoked it to keep the body healthy.
- What happened when the whole country banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933? Drinking fell at first to about 30 percent of what it had been, then rose to about 60 to 70 percent. Poisoned factory alcohol killed thousands of drinkers before delegates in Utah ended the ban on December 5, 1933.
- Who has been punished for drugs, and who has not? Robert Coles with his red D, the laborer Samuel Caldwell with four years of hard labor, Billie Holiday handcuffed to her hospital bed, and the people sentenced for crack, 91 of every 100 of them Black in 1992, were all punished. Harry Gross never served his jail sentence, and no member of the Sackler family has been charged with a crime.

**What changed:** Tobacco went from an offering in prayer to a habit that kills more than 480,000 Americans a year, and opium, cocaine and liquor went from open sale in the 1800s to bans in the 1900s, with liquor made legal again in 1933.

**Five dates to keep**
- 1634: Massachusetts judges order Robert Coles to wear a red D for a year.
- February 13, 1826: Reformers in Boston found the American Temperance Society.
- January 17, 1920: National Prohibition begins.
- June 17, 1971: President Nixon calls drug abuse "public enemy number one."
- 1996: Purdue Pharma begins selling OxyContin.

**Not known:** The number of people killed by poisoned alcohol during Prohibition is not known, and the estimates run from more than 5,000 to at least 10,000.

**Where to go:** The Temple of Dendur stands in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, in the wing where Nan Goldin's group threw pill bottles into the pool in 2018 and that no longer carries the Sackler name.
