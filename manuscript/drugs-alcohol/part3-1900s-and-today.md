## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1920 to 1933, federal law banned making, selling and carrying alcoholic drinks anywhere in the United States. This ban is called Prohibition. People kept drinking in hidden bars and made liquor at home. Federal agents made more than half a million arrests a year. Treasury officials, who worked in the department that handles the government's money and taxes, had more poison added to factory alcohol. Bootleggers, people who made or sold liquor against the law, stole that alcohol and sold it as drink, and the poison killed thousands of drinkers.

From 1906, drug makers had to list opium, morphine and alcohol on their labels. Opium is a painkilling drug from the poppy plant, and morphine is a stronger painkiller made from opium. From 1915, everyone who sold opium or cocaine, a drug made from coca leaves, had to register and keep records. Treasury officials took doctors to court for giving addicts their drug. An addict is a person whose body has come to need a drug. In 1937 members of Congress taxed marijuana. Americans smoked an average of 54 cigarettes a year each in 1900, and by 1963 the figure was 4,345.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carry-nation-drugs-alcohol" name="Carry Nation" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carry Nation

> **Who:** A Kansas woman who smashed saloons to force officials to enforce the state's ban on liquor.
> **When and where:** Kiowa, Wichita and Topeka, Kansas, 1900 to 1901, and Kansas City, Missouri, 1901. Arrested more than 30 times through 1910.

Carry Nation smashed saloons in Kansas because officials there did not enforce the state's ban on liquor. Kansas voters had added a ban on liquor to the state constitution, the state's highest set of laws, in 1880, and the ban took effect in 1881, but saloons stayed open.

She was born Caroline Amelia Moore in Garrard County, Kentucky, on 25 November 1846. Official records spell her name Carrie. Later she spelled it Carry, and she said her name meant "Carry A Nation for Prohibition." In 1867 she married Charles Gloyd, a doctor who drank heavily. They separated before their daughter, Charlien, was born in 1868. Gloyd died of alcoholism in 1869. Alcoholism is the illness of being addicted to alcohol.

In 1874 she married David A. Nation, a lawyer and minister. In Medicine Lodge, Kansas, she started a branch of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, a women's group working to end drinking, and pressed officials to enforce the ban. She kept at it for about five years.

On 5 June 1900, she wrote later, she heard a voice telling her, "GO TO KIOWA." On 7 June she walked into Dobson's Saloon in Kiowa, Kansas, carrying rocks. She said, "Men, I have come to save you from a drunkard's fate," and smashed the saloon's stock of liquor. She smashed two more saloons in Kiowa.

In January 1901 she wrecked a saloon in Wichita and spent two weeks in jail. Her husband joked that she should use a hatchet next time. "That is the most sensible thing you have said since I married you," she answered. In February 1901, in Topeka, she met the governor and spoke to Kansas lawmakers.

Between 1900 and 1910 she was arrested more than 30 times. She paid her fines with money from her speeches and by selling small pins shaped like hatchets. In 1901 she opened a home in Kansas City, Missouri, for the wives and children of men who drank. Kansas officials did not really enforce the ban until 1907. She died on 9 June 1911.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carry-nation-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Labels on the bottle, 1906" -->
In 1905 the reporter Samuel Hopkins Adams wrote 11 articles about patent medicines for *Collier's* magazine. Patent medicines were medicines sold under brand names, and their makers did not have to say what was in them. He wrote that Americans would spend about $75 million that year on medicines that held large amounts of alcohol and opium. By official tests, Hostetter's Bitters was 44 percent alcohol. He wrote that one bottle of a medicine called Peruna held as much alcohol as five bottles of beer. He called the opium syrups sold for babies medicines "which stunt or kill helpless infants."

On 30 June 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt signed the Pure Food and Drug Act. Under it, drug makers had to print on the label whether a medicine held any of 10 drugs. They included alcohol, morphine, opium and cannabis, the plant marijuana comes from. The law did not ban any of these drugs. Chemists led by Harvey W. Wiley, at the Department of Agriculture, enforced the law.

After the law passed, the makers of Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup took out the morphine. The historian David T. Courtwright writes that after 1900 fewer people became addicted through medicine. Doctors had grown more careful. After the 1906 law and the articles of reporters like Adams, more people were wary of dosing themselves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Harrison Act, 1914: doctors and addicts" -->
In these years, some officials and newspaper writers blamed drug use on Black and Chinese people. In 1903 a citizens' panel called the Committee on the Acquirement of the Drug Habit wrote that if a Chinese man "cannot get along without his dope we can get along without him." In place of "a Chinese man," the panel used an insulting name for Chinese people. In 1908 Theodore Roosevelt named Dr. Hamilton Wright the first United States opium commissioner, the official in charge of the country's work against opium. Wright told members of Congress that cocaine was often "the direct incentive to the crime of rape of white women by Negroes." Rape means forcing a person to have sex. Negro was a word used then for a Black person. In 1914 a *New York Times* headline read "Negro Cocaine 'Fiends' Are a New Southern Menace."

Historians disagree about how much these claims shaped the drug laws. David T. Courtwright writes that cocaine became linked in the public mind with crime sprees, especially by Black men. George Fisher of Stanford Law School points out that the first state cocaine bans, from 1887 to 1898, came before these newspaper stories. He argues that racial hatred came later, in the way police and other officers enforced the laws.

Representative Francis Burton Harrison of New York proposed a new federal drug law. On 17 December 1914, President Woodrow Wilson signed it as the Harrison Narcotics Act. It took effect on 1 March 1915. In the law, narcotics meant drugs made from opium or from coca, the plant that cocaine comes from. Everyone who sold or handed out these drugs, including doctors and druggists, had to register, pay a small tax and keep records.

Treasury officials read the law to mean that a doctor could not give an addict regular doses of a drug. They took doctors who did to court. On 3 March 1919, in the case of *Webb v. United States*, the justices of the Supreme Court agreed with them. A doctor could not write a prescription, a doctor's written order for a drug, for an addict just to keep the addict comfortable. Dr. Webb had written more than 80 morphine prescriptions a week, at 50 cents each.

City officials then opened 35 public clinics that sold morphine cheaply to addicts who registered. Treasury officials closed every one of them. The last, in Shreveport, Louisiana, closed on 10 February 1923. After that, an addict could get drugs only from dealers, or go to prison.

Heroin is a drug made from morphine. In the 1910s and early 1920s, young men in the poor immigrant neighborhoods of cities began sniffing small packets of heroin powder. On 7 June 1924, President Calvin Coolidge signed a law that banned bringing opium into the country to make heroin. Representative Stephen G. Porter of Pennsylvania had introduced it.

In 1935 officials of the Public Health Service, the federal health agency, opened a hospital for addicts in Lexington, Kentucky. It was called a "narcotic farm." Prisoners could be sent there. One doctor remembered it as "more like a prison than a hospital and more like a hospital than a prison." Courtwright writes that several studies found that 90 percent or more of the people released from Lexington soon went back to drugs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cigarettes become an everyday habit" -->
In 1900 Americans smoked an average of 54 cigarettes a year each, by the count of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. By 1963 the figure was 4,345. In 1911 the justices of the Supreme Court ordered James B. Duke's American Tobacco Company broken into four smaller companies.

In World War I, General John Pershing led the American army sent to Europe. Asked what America needed to win the war, he answered, "Tobacco as much as bullets." People in community groups, businesses and relief groups such as the Red Cross, the Salvation Army and the Young Men's Christian Association set up "tobacco funds" to send cigarettes to soldiers. Editors at the *New York Sun* asked readers to give as a way of "proving your patriotism."

In the 1940s the scientist Ernst Wynder and others began studies that linked smoking cigarettes to lung cancer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition: the law and the people who wrote it" -->
Wayne Wheeler of the Anti-Saloon League played a major part in winning a national ban on liquor. Wheeler and other prohibitionists gained support from the fight to win the vote for women. In World War I, League leaders also used Americans' anger at Germany to win support. On 18 December 1917, members of Congress approved the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution. An amendment is a change added to the Constitution. Lawmakers in the 36th state voted for it on 16 January 1919, which made it part of the Constitution. Only the lawmakers of Connecticut and Rhode Island never voted for it.

The amendment banned making, selling and carrying alcoholic drinks. It did not ban owning them or drinking them. Wheeler helped draft the Volstead Act, the law that set out the rules for the ban. It is named for Andrew Volstead of Minnesota, who led the House committee in charge of laws about courts and crimes. President Wilson refused to sign it and sent it back, and on 28 October 1919 members of Congress passed it anyway. The ban began on 17 January 1920.

A family could make up to 200 gallons of wine and cider a year at home. Doctors could prescribe whiskey as medicine, and they wrote about 11 million prescriptions for it a year through the 1920s. In 1931 officials caught 1,000 doctors and 400 druggists selling signed prescription forms to bootleggers. Twelve of the doctors and 13 of the druggists were charged with crimes.

People drank in speakeasies, hidden bars that sold liquor against the law. By the end of the 1920s New York City alone had about 30,000 of them, by the count of the writer Deborah Blum. Officials in some states, among them Maryland and New York, refused to enforce the federal ban.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="izzy-einstein-drugs-alcohol" name="Izzy Einstein" movie="Izzy and Moe (1985)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Izzy Einstein

> **Who:** A Jewish immigrant and former postal clerk who became a federal Prohibition agent. He arrested people while wearing disguises.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1920 to 1925.
> **Movie:** *Izzy and Moe* (1985), a television film directed by Jackie Cooper, with Jackie Gleason as Izzy Einstein and Art Carney as Moe Smith. It is about the two agents.

Izzy Einstein and his partner Moe Smith arrested thousands of people for breaking the Prohibition law, and most of those people were found guilty. Einstein caught many of them while wearing a disguise.

Isidor Einstein was born in 1880 to a Jewish family in Austria-Hungary. He came to the United States around 1901, speaking Yiddish, Hungarian, Polish and German, and some Russian, French, Spanish and Italian. Einstein worked as a salesman and then as a postal clerk, and he struggled to support his family on a clerk's pay.

In 1920, with no police training, he joined the new federal Prohibition service. He brought in his friend Moe Smith, who owned a cigar store. The two men said they used more than 100 disguises. Among them were a streetcar conductor, a gravedigger, a fisherman, an iceman and an opera singer. They usually carried no guns. Sometimes Einstein told a doorman that he was a Prohibition agent. The doorman laughed and let him in.

One Sunday, with reporters following them, they made 71 raids in a little more than 12 hours. Together they made 4,932 arrests by one count, and more than 5,000 by the count of the National Archives. Of the people they arrested, 95 percent were found guilty. Thousands of bartenders, bootleggers and speakeasy owners went to jail because of their work.

In November 1925 their boss, General Lincoln C. Andrews, fired them along with 36 other men when he reorganized the New York office. Writers at *Time* magazine suggested that their fame in the newspapers had annoyed their bosses. Both men went on to sell insurance. In 1932 Einstein published a book, *Prohibition Agent No. 1*. He died on 17 February 1938.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="izzy-einstein-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition agents: who enforced the ban" -->
In 1930 the federal government had 1,450 front-line Prohibition agents. From 1921 to 1930 the agents made more than half a million arrests a year and seized more than 45,000 cars. Richard F. Hamm, a professor at the University at Albany, gives these figures in a 2026 article in *The Conversation*.

Politicians handed out many of the agents' jobs. In 1927 Judge William S. Kenyon said that "three-fourths of the 2,500 dry agents are ward heelers and sycophants named by the politicians." He meant that most agents were political helpers and flatterers. When agents had to pass a civil service test, the kind of test used to give out many government jobs, 60 percent failed it. In the six years from 1920, 752 Prohibition officials were fired, most for drunkenness or for taking bribes. Mabel Walker Willebrandt was the Assistant Attorney General, a top lawyer in the Justice Department, in charge of Prohibition cases from 1921 to 1929. She said the agents were "as devoid of honesty and integrity" as the lawbreakers. She meant they were just as dishonest.

By their own count, Prohibition agents killed 89 people. The Association Against the Prohibition Amendment, a group working to end the ban, counted about 1,000 people killed in enforcing it. In 1924, near the Capitol in Washington, an agent shooting at a bootlegger's car hit Senator Frank L. Greene of Vermont in the head. Greene never got back the full use of one arm.

The writer Daniel Okrent describes the first agent to kill a suspected bootlegger. That agent had joined under a false name. He had killed a man at the age of 14, and he got his badge while he was still locked up in Dannemora State Prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poisoned alcohol and jake leg" -->
Treasury Department officials under President Calvin Coolidge ordered more poison put into factory alcohol in 1926 and 1927. The poison killed thousands of people who drank it. Factory alcohol, used for fuel and in factories, had chemicals added to it so that no one could drink it. This is called denatured alcohol. Bootleggers stole it and paid chemists to take the chemicals out. The officials then ordered makers to add far more poison. By 1927 the mixes included kerosene, gasoline, benzene and mercury salts, and up to 10 percent methyl alcohol, also called wood alcohol. Methyl alcohol blinds and kills people who drink it. *Time* magazine reported in January 1927 that three ordinary drinks of the new mix could cause blindness. The records do not name the official who signed the order.

By dusk on Christmas Day 1926, more than 60 people had come to Bellevue Hospital in New York sick from poisoned liquor, and eight of them had died. In the next two days, 23 more people died in the city. Counts of all the deaths differ. Deborah Blum gives 400 dead in New York City in 1926 and 700 in 1927. Another count gives 585 dead in the city in 1926. For the whole country, one estimate gives more than 5,000 dead, and Blum gives an estimate of at least 10,000 by 1933.

Charles Norris was New York City's chief medical examiner, the doctor in charge of finding out why people in the city died. He said, "The government knows it is not stopping drinking by putting poison in alcohol." The government, he said, "must be charged with the moral responsibility for the deaths that poisoned liquor causes, although it cannot be held legally responsible." Seymour Lowman, the Treasury official in charge of Prohibition from 1927 as Assistant Secretary of the Treasury, said drinkers were "dying off fast from poison 'hooch.'" If the result was a sober America, he said, "a good job will have been done." Wayne Wheeler said that a person who drank this alcohol "is a deliberate suicide." Senator Edward I. Edwards of New Jersey called the program "legalized murder."

In 1930 thousands of people were poisoned after they drank Jamaica ginger, a medicine that was 70 to 80 percent alcohol. The poison came from Harry Gross, the president of Hub Products Corporation in Boston. He wanted a cheaper ingredient for his Jamaica ginger. He chose a chemical called TOCP, used in making lacquer, a shiny coating for wood and metal. People at the Celluloid Corporation, which made TOCP, told him it was not poisonous. TOCP destroys nerve cells in the spinal cord, the bundle of nerves inside the backbone that carries signals from the brain to the body. People who drank Gross's Jamaica ginger lost the use of their hands and feet. They walked with their toes flopping down. People called the illness "jake leg" and the walk the "jake walk." For most of them the damage never healed.

Nobody made an exact count of the people harmed. The figure most often given is between 30,000 and 50,000. Most were poor, and many were immigrants. A judge fined Gross and his partner Max Reisman $1,000 each. The judge also gave each of them a two-year jail sentence that they did not have to serve. Blues singers recorded songs about jake leg in 1930.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alcoholics Anonymous, 1935" -->
In 1935, in Akron, Ohio, two heavy drinkers named Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith met through a Christian group called the Oxford Group. Together they started Alcoholics Anonymous, or AA, where drinkers help each other stop drinking. Anonymous means without names. At meetings, members share their own stories of drinking and of getting sober. In 1939 AA members published the group's Twelve Steps, a list of steps for staying sober, in a book that members call the Big Book. Wilson and Smith had never heard of the Washingtonians, a group of drinkers in Baltimore in 1840 who also helped each other stay sober by telling their stories.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much people drank, and repeal" -->
Drinking fell under Prohibition, and then much of it came back. A 1991 study by the economists Miron and Zwiebel found that drinking fell at first to about 30 percent of what it had been before the ban. Over the next several years it rose again, to about 60 to 70 percent. Deaths from cirrhosis, damage to the liver from heavy drinking, fell by nearly two-thirds. Blum writes that insurance companies charted a rise in alcoholism of more than 300 percent in the 1920s. The counts point different ways, so how much drinking changed is not settled.

Pauline Sabin, a leader of the Republican Party in New York, first backed the ban. "I felt I should approve of it because it would help my two sons," she said. She changed her mind. By 1929 she said that "children are growing up with a total lack of respect for the Constitution and for the law." In May 1929, in Chicago, she founded the Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform to end the ban.

On 22 March 1933, President Franklin Roosevelt signed a law that allowed beer with 3.2 percent alcohol by weight. On 5 December 1933, delegates in Utah made it the 36th state to approve the Twenty-first Amendment, which ended national Prohibition. After that, lawmakers in each state and town made their own rules about when, where and to whom alcohol could be sold. Liquor stayed banned in Kansas until 1948.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marijuana and the 1937 tax" -->
Marijuana is made from the dried leaves and flowers of the cannabis plant. Harry J. Anslinger ran the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, the federal drug police, from 1930 to 1962. "The best cure for addiction? Never let it happen," he said. His articles against marijuana in the 1930s often told stories that blamed the drug for crimes by Black and Mexican people. One story in his files began, "Two Negroes took a girl fourteen years old..." The historian Courtwright writes that Anslinger played on Americans' fear of immigrants and of other races.

Anslinger told the story of Victor Licata, who killed his family with an axe in Florida in 1933, as a marijuana crime. Doctors had found that Licata had a severe mental illness when he was young.

Anslinger drafted the Marihuana Tax Act. Doctors of the American Medical Association objected to it. President Franklin Roosevelt signed it on 2 August 1937, and it took effect on 1 October. Selling marijuana without a federal tax stamp, which cost $1 an ounce, became a crime.

On 2 October 1937, federal narcotics agents and Denver police arrested Samuel R. Caldwell, a 57-year-old laborer, for selling marijuana. They arrested Moses Baca, 26, for having it. The tax stamps had not yet been printed. Judge J. Foster Symes sentenced Caldwell to four years of hard labor at Leavenworth prison and a $1,000 fine. He sentenced Baca to 18 months. "Under its influence men become beasts," Symes said of marijuana. Caldwell was released in 1940 and died in 1941.

In 1938 Mayor Fiorello La Guardia of New York named a committee to study marijuana. In their report of 1944, the committee's members disputed Anslinger's claims.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor laws and Native nations" -->
Federal bans on liquor on Native land stayed in force through all of these years. In 1910 members of Congress made it a crime to bring alcohol onto the land of the Pueblo nations of New Mexico, as a condition of New Mexico becoming a state. That same year Attorney General George Wickersham asked for lighter punishments for Native liquor crimes.

In 1918 the Native American Church was organized in Oklahoma. Its members take peyote, a small cactus that causes visions, as a sacred act of worship.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
About 42 of every 100 American adults smoked cigarettes in 1965. By 1997 the number was about 25. Scientists had shown that smoking causes lung cancer, and in 1996 a fired tobacco scientist told a national television audience that his company changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine. Drinking rose until 1981 and then fell.

On 17 June 1971 President Richard Nixon called drug abuse "public enemy number one," and reporters named his program the war on drugs. Candy Lightner started Mothers Against Drunk Driving in 1980, after a drunk driver killed her daughter, and by 1988 lawmakers in every state had set the drinking age at 21. In 1986 members of Congress and President Ronald Reagan set the same prison term for 5 grams of crack, a kind of cocaine that can be smoked, as for 500 grams of powder cocaine. A company owned by the Sackler family began selling a strong painkiller called OxyContin in 1996.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much people drank and smoked" -->
In 1950 Americans aged 15 and over drank an average of 2.04 gallons of pure alcohol each. From 1970 the count covers people aged 14 and over. By that count the figure rose to 2.76 gallons in 1981, the highest since Prohibition. By 1995 it had fallen to 2.15 gallons. Scientists at the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism work these figures out from sales of alcohol, not from asking people what they drank. Between 1974 and 1981, 68 to 71 of every 100 adults told the Gallup company, which asks adults questions about what they think and do, that they drank.

Cigarette smoking peaked in 1963, at 4,345 cigarettes a year for each person. By 1998 the figure was 2,261. In the late 1990s the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) counted about 430,000 deaths a year from tobacco, one of every five deaths in the country.

In the 1940s and 1950s, Ernst Wynder and other scientists linked cigarettes to lung cancer. In 1964 a committee of experts working for the Surgeon General, the nation's top public health doctor, concluded that smoking causes lung cancer and cancer of the voice box. Members of Congress passed a law, and Nixon signed it on 1 April 1970, that banned cigarette ads on radio and television from 2 January 1971.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Billie Holiday and the narcotics agents" -->
The singer Billie Holiday was arrested for drugs three times. Federal narcotics agents made the last two arrests, the last one in her hospital bed. On 16 May 1947 she was arrested for having narcotics in her New York apartment. In court she said, "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt." She pleaded guilty, and a judge sent her to Alderson Federal Prison Camp in West Virginia. After she was found guilty, she lost the police license she needed to sing in New York City clubs that sold alcohol. On 22 January 1949, the federal agent George Hunter White arrested her again in a San Francisco hotel.

In 1959 Holiday was in Metropolitan Hospital in New York, being treated for liver and heart disease. On 31 May, Harry Anslinger ordered agents of his Federal Bureau of Narcotics to arrest her there for having drugs. The agents handcuffed her to her hospital bed. A police guard stayed at her room until a judge ordered the handcuffs taken off. She died in that room on 17 July 1959, at the age of 44. The cause was heart failure and fluid in her lungs, which came from cirrhosis, scarring of the liver. She had 70 cents in the bank.

The writer Johann Hari has argued that Anslinger's agents went after Holiday from 1939 because of her song "Strange Fruit," which is about lynching, the killing of a person by a mob without any trial. The jazz writer Lewis Porter disputes this. He points out that the claim first appears in Hari's own book, in 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native nations and the end of the federal ban, 1953" -->
On 15 August 1953, members of Congress passed a law "to eliminate certain discriminatory legislation against Indians." Discriminatory means treating one group of people worse than others. After this law, federal law no longer barred Native people from buying alcohol off their nations' land. On a nation's own land, a sale of alcohol had to follow both state law and a law passed by that nation's government, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, the head of the federal department that handles dealings with Native nations. The ban on liquor on Native land had lasted 20 years longer than national Prohibition. Members of Congress gave each nation the choice of keeping its ban. Some nations kept it. The Oglala Sioux Tribe, on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota, was one, according to the National Alcohol Beverage Control Association, a group of state alcohol officials.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="LSD and marijuana" -->
LSD is a drug that makes people see and hear things that are not there. In the early 1960s it was legal. From 1960 Timothy Leary, a teacher at Harvard University who studied how the mind works, ran experiments in which he gave people psilocybin, a drug from certain mushrooms that causes visions. He also used LSD. On 30 April 1963 Harvard officials stopped paying him, and Harvard's president, Nathan Pusey, said he had "failed to keep his classroom appointments." Nixon later called Leary "the most dangerous man in America."

In 1964 a man named Lowell Eggemeier lit a marijuana cigarette in San Francisco's Hall of Justice and asked to be arrested. The writer Martin A. Lee dates the start of the modern campaign to make marijuana legal to that act. In 1973 Oregon lawmakers made having a small amount of marijuana punishable by a fine instead of jail. In November 1996, California voters approved Proposition 215, 56 percent to 44 percent. California was the first state to allow marijuana as a medicine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nixon declares the war on drugs, 1970 and 1971" -->
On 27 October 1970, Nixon signed a law that sorted drugs into five lists, called schedules. The lawmakers put marijuana and heroin on Schedule I, the list for drugs with a high risk of abuse and no accepted use as medicine. Under the same law, having a small amount of a drug became a lesser crime, with at most one year in jail for a first offense. Selling drugs carried harsher punishments than before.

In May 1971 two members of Congress, Morgan Murphy and Robert Steele, came back from the war in Vietnam. They reported that "as many as 10 to 15 percent of our servicemen are addicted to heroin."

On 17 June 1971 Nixon spoke at the White House. "America's public enemy number one in the United States is drug abuse," he said. He asked for $155 million in new money, for a total of "over $350 million" a year to fight drugs. When he took office in 1969, the federal drug budget had been $81 million. While Nixon was president, about 70 of every 100 federal dollars spent against drugs went to treatment and prevention.

From 1972 to 1973, agents of Nixon's new Office of Drug Abuse Law Enforcement made 6,000 drug arrests in 18 months, and most of the people arrested were Black. In 1973 Governor Nelson Rockefeller of New York signed laws that set 15 years to life in prison for selling two ounces, or holding four ounces, of heroin, cocaine or marijuana.

In 2016 the journalist Dan Baum published notes of a 1994 interview with John Ehrlichman, one of Nixon's top advisers. In Baum's notes, Ehrlichman said that "by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we could disrupt those communities." Criminalizing means making something a crime. Ehrlichman had died in 1999. His children and other officials who had worked for Nixon disputed the quote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="betty-ford-drugs-alcohol" name="Betty Ford" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Betty Ford

> **Who:** The wife of President Gerald Ford. She told the country she was addicted to pills and alcohol, and then she founded a treatment center.
> **When and where:** Long Beach and Rancho Mirage, California, 1978 to 1982.

Betty Ford told the whole country in 1978 that she was addicted to pills and to alcohol. Four years later she founded a center to treat people with the same addictions.

In 1964 a pinched nerve in her neck put her in the hospital for two weeks. Doctors gave her drugs for the pain, including strong painkillers and Valium, a drug that calms people. At times she took as many as 20 pills a day. She also drank. Her father and her brother Bob had both been addicted to alcohol.

On 1 April 1978, after the Fords had left the White House, her family met with her and asked her to go into treatment. A meeting like this is called an intervention. On 11 April she checked into the Naval hospital in Long Beach, California. A family spokesman read her statement: "I have found I am not only addicted to the medication I have been taking for my arthritis, but also to alcohol." Arthritis is a disease that makes the joints swell and hurt.

She later wrote, "I liked alcohol, it made me feel warm. And I loved pills. They took away my tension and my pain."

In 1982 she and Leonard Firestone, a former ambassador, founded the Betty Ford Center in Rancho Mirage, California, for people recovering from addiction to alcohol and other drugs. It opened on 4 October 1982. She died on 8 July 2011.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="betty-ford-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drunk driving and the drinking age of 21" -->
On 3 May 1980, Clarence Busch hit and killed 13-year-old Cari Lightner with his car while he was drunk. She was walking to a church festival in Fair Oaks, California. He drove away and left her body there. He had a long record of arrests for drunk driving.

A judge ruled that Busch's alcoholism had made it harder for him to control his decisions. The judge gave him two years in prison for involuntary manslaughter, the crime of killing someone without meaning to. He entered prison in December 1980 and was paroled nine months later, which means he was let out early under watch.

In 1982 he went back to prison for breaking the rules of his parole, and he was freed again in February 1983. In April 1985 police arrested him again, drunk and driving without a license, after a crash.

Her mother, Candy Lightner, founded Mothers Against Drunk Driving, or MADD, in 1980. By MADD's count, nearly 25,000 people were killed in crashes involving alcohol in 1980. That was half of all traffic deaths.

Between 1970 and 1975, lawmakers in 29 states had lowered the drinking age to 18, 19 or 20. They did it after the voting age was lowered to 18. Senator Frank Lautenberg of New Jersey wrote a law to raise the drinking age again, and Reagan signed it on 17 July 1984. Under it, a state that let people under 21 buy alcohol lost part of its federal money for highways. By 1988 every state had set the age at 21.

Researchers disagree about how many lives the higher drinking age saved. A review for the Institute of Medicine, a national group of health experts, found strong evidence that it worked. The economists Jeffrey Miron and Elina Tetelbaum found in 2009 that the effect faded after a year or two.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crack cocaine and the 1986 law" -->
In the early 1980s, Nancy Reagan, the president's wife, began a campaign, with help from an advertising company, in which she told young people to "Just Say No" to drugs. Later studies found little or no change in how many young people used drugs after the campaign. From 1980 to 1984, the FBI's budget for fighting drugs rose from $8 million to $95 million.

Crack is cocaine that dealers cooked with water and baking soda and dried into small rocks that can be smoked. Smoked crack reaches the brain in 6 to 8 seconds, and the high lasts 5 to 15 minutes. Around 1984 a dose cost as little as $2.50 in some cities. Hospital emergencies from cocaine more than doubled in one year, from 26,300 in 1985 to 55,200 in 1986.

On 19 June 1986 the basketball player Len Bias died from cocaine. Afterward, Speaker of the House Tip O'Neill had Democratic leaders write a new drug law, and Reagan signed it on 27 October 1986. In it, members of Congress added $1.7 billion to the fight against drugs and set 29 new minimum prison terms that judges had to give. The law set five years in federal prison for 5 grams of crack, the same as for 500 grams of powder cocaine.

Under the law, a person caught with crack got the same prison term as a person caught with 100 times as much powder cocaine. Most of the people sentenced for crack were Black. In 1992, 91 of every 100 people sentenced in federal court for crack were Black, by the count of the US Sentencing Commission. Among people who entered treatment for crack in 2004, 53 of every 100 were Black and 38 were white. The historian Elizabeth Hinton writes that Reagan "led Congress in criminalizing drug users, especially African American drug users," which means treating them as criminals.

On 5 September 1989 President George H. W. Bush held up a bag of crack on television. He said it had been "seized a few days ago in a park across the street from the White House." Agents of the Drug Enforcement Administration had lured the seller to that park to make the arrest. By 2000 the federal government spent $18.4 billion a year against drugs. Nearly half went to law enforcement, the police and agents who catch lawbreakers, and one-sixth went to treatment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jeffrey-wigand-drugs-alcohol" name="Jeffrey Wigand" movie="The Insider (1999)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jeffrey Wigand

> **Who:** A scientist who ran research at a tobacco company and then told the public that the company changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine.
> **When and where:** Brown & Williamson Tobacco, Louisville, Kentucky, 1989 to 1996.
> **Movie:** *The Insider* (1999), directed by Michael Mann, with Russell Crowe as Jeffrey Wigand. It is about Wigand.

Jeffrey Wigand told the public in 1996 that his former company had changed its tobacco to strengthen the effect of nicotine. Nicotine is the drug in tobacco that makes people addicted.

Wigand was born in New York City on 17 December 1942. He trained as a biochemist, a scientist who studies the chemistry of living things. In January 1989 he became vice president for research at Brown & Williamson Tobacco in Louisville, Kentucky. Brown & Williamson fired him on 24 March 1993. That September the company sued him and cut off his health insurance. In November 1993 he signed an agreement not to talk about his work.

In late November 1995, under a judge's order, Wigand gave sworn testimony in Pascagoula, Mississippi, for the state's lawsuit against the tobacco companies. The company's lawyers got a Kentucky court order against him and sued him for "theft, fraud, breach of contract." That means they said he stole, cheated and broke his agreement with the company. Lawyers for the CBS television network stopped the news program *60 Minutes* from showing an interview with him that November.

On 4 February 1996, *60 Minutes* showed the interview. Wigand said the company added chemicals such as ammonia to its tobacco to increase the effect of nicotine. He said he had been harassed and had received death threats from people who did not give their names. The company hired investigators, who wrote a 500-page report attacking him and gave it to reporters. The reporters checked it and found that many of its charges were unproven or small.

After he was fired, Wigand taught science and Japanese at a high school in Louisville. He was named Kentucky's Teacher of the Year for 1996.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jeffrey-wigand-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="OxyContin goes on sale, 1996" -->
Opioids are strong painkillers made from the opium poppy, or from chemicals that act the same way in the body. Too much of an opioid slows a person's breathing until it stops.

In 1952 three doctors, the brothers Arthur, Mortimer and Raymond Sackler, bought a drug company called Purdue Frederick. Their company, later named Purdue Pharma, began selling OxyContin in 1996. OxyContin is a pill of oxycodone, an opioid. It is made to release the drug slowly. Purdue sold it to be taken every 12 hours, but most patients found that it wore off after eight hours or less.

Dr. Curtis Wright, of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), reviewed OxyContin before it went on sale. He approved a label that said the slow release "is believed to reduce the abuse liability of a drug," meaning the risk that people would misuse it. A year later Wright left the FDA and went to work for Purdue.

Purdue trained its salespeople to tell doctors that the risk of addiction was "less than one percent." More than 5,000 doctors, pharmacists and nurses went to Purdue meetings with all their costs paid. Richard Sackler, Raymond's son, ran the company's sales and marketing, and he became its president in 1999. Prescriptions of OxyContin for pain not caused by cancer rose from about 670,000 in 1997 to about 6.2 million in 2002. From 1995 to 2001, Purdue took in $2.8 billion from sales of OxyContin. David Kessler, who led the FDA when the pill was approved, later called the approval "one of the worst medical mistakes."

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1999 to 2023, about 806,000 Americans died of opioid overdoses, by the count of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). An overdose is too much of a drug at once, enough to make a person very sick or kill them. The deaths came first from pain pills such as OxyContin, then from heroin, and from 2013 mostly from fentanyl made outside the law. Overdoses of all drugs killed 107,941 people in 2022, the year the death rate peaked in the CDC's final figures. By the CDC's early count, deaths peaked at 112,418 in the 12 months ending in August 2023. By the same early count, the yearly total had fallen to about 67,000 in the 12 months ending in April 2026.

In 2026 a federal judge sentenced Purdue Pharma, the maker of OxyContin, and the company shut down. No member of the Sackler family, who owned it, has been charged with a crime. Over the same years, voters and lawmakers in 24 states made marijuana legal for adults. In July 2026, 54 of every 100 adults told Gallup pollsters that they drink, the lowest share since Gallup began asking in 1939. In 2025, by the CDC's count, about 9 of every 100 adults smoked cigarettes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Overdose deaths, 1999 to 2026" -->
CDC scientists describe the opioid deaths as coming in three waves. The first wave began in 1999, after doctors wrote more and more prescriptions for opioid pills in the 1990s. Deaths from heroin rose from 2010. From 2013, deaths rose from opioids made in labs, mostly fentanyl made and sold outside the law.

In 1999, about 6 of every 100,000 Americans died of a drug overdose, by the CDC's count. The rate rose to about 33 of every 100,000 in 2022 and fell to about 23 in 2024.

The CDC's final counts of overdose deaths from all drugs are 70,237 in 2017, 91,799 in 2020 and 107,941 in 2022. The count was 105,007 in 2023, and opioids were involved in 79,358 of those deaths. In 2024 it fell to 79,384. The CDC's early count for the 12 months ending in April 2026 is 66,937, and opioids were involved in 41,755 of those. CDC scientists warn that their early counts are often incomplete.

Some groups die at far higher rates than others. In 2024 American Indian and Alaska Native people died of overdoses at a rate of 51.6 of every 100,000, by the CDC's count. The rate was 33.8 for Black Americans and 24.7 for white Americans. By age, people aged 35 to 44 died at the highest rate, 44.2 of every 100,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fentanyl" -->
Fentanyl is an opioid made in a lab. By the CDC's count, it is 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine. Too much of it slows a person's breathing until the breathing stops. A drug called naloxone can undo the overdose and save the person if someone gives it fast enough. On 29 March 2023, officials of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a naloxone spray for the nose that anyone can buy without a doctor's order.

Deaths from fentanyl and similar lab-made opioids rose from 1 of every 100,000 Americans in 2013 to 22.7 in 2022, by the CDC's count. In 2024 the rate fell to 14.3.

Since about 2019, most of the illegal fentanyl sold in the United States has come from Mexico. A 2025 report by the Congressional Research Service, which studies problems for members of Congress, names the makers. Mexican crime groups called cartels, especially the Sinaloa Cartel and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, make it outside the law. Companies in China are the largest source of the chemicals they need. The cartels' members use machines to press fentanyl into fake pills made to look like real medicine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Purdue Pharma and the Sacklers in court" -->
In May 2007 Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to misleading the public about OxyContin. The company admitted that it had sold OxyContin as less addictive and less likely to be abused than other painkillers. It paid $600 million. Three of its leaders also pleaded guilty: its president, Michael Friedman, its top lawyer, Howard Udell, and its former chief medical officer, Paul Goldenheim. Together they paid $34.5 million. In 2009 Purdue changed OxyContin into a form that was harder to crush and snort or inject.

On 15 September 2019, Purdue filed for bankruptcy, a court process for a business that cannot pay its debts. Purdue hired a firm called AlixPartners to check the company's money records. In December 2019 the firm reported that members of the Sackler family had taken $10.7 billion out of the company after lawsuits and investigations began. On 24 November 2020, Purdue pleaded guilty to three federal crimes. One was a plot to cheat the United States. The other two were plots to pay doctors and others to push its pills. In that case, no company leader and no Sackler was charged.

Five justices of the Supreme Court threw out a bankruptcy plan on 27 June 2024, because it would have protected the Sacklers from lawsuits. A year later, on 16 June 2025, 55 attorneys general agreed to a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sacklers. A settlement is a deal that ends lawsuits in return for money. An attorney general is the top lawyer of a state.

On 28 April 2026, Judge Madeline Cox Arleo in Newark, New Jersey, sentenced Purdue to $5.5 billion in fines and penalties for its 2020 guilty plea. For nearly seven hours she heard from people harmed by the crisis. More than 40 spoke in court, and more than 200 sent letters. Ed Bisch's son Eddie died of an overdose in 2001. "Punishment by a fine means 'legal for a price,'" Bisch said. Alexis Pleus said her son Jeff had first been prescribed OxyContin for a football injury, before he died of an overdose. "We still deserve justice, and this isn't it," she said.

Arleo said she could not send Purdue's leaders or owners to prison because Justice Department lawyers had charged only the company. "Your government failed you," she told the families. Most of the $5.5 billion will never be paid. Federal officials collect $225 million, and Purdue's other money goes to the states and towns it owes. The settlement includes $865 million for people harmed one by one. Many victims said they could not qualify because they could not find their old prescription records.

Three days after the sentence, on 1 May 2026, New York Attorney General Letitia James announced that Purdue had shut down. A new company, Knoa Pharma, owned by a charity, took over. Under the settlement the Sacklers must pay $2.9 billion by 2029, and none of them may sell opioids in the United States. The Sackler family has always said it did nothing wrong.

Other companies paid too. In 2021 the three largest companies that ship drugs to pharmacies agreed to pay up to $21 billion over 18 years. They are McKesson, Cardinal Health and AmerisourceBergen. The drug maker Johnson & Johnson agreed to pay up to $5 billion. In 2022 the drug makers Teva and Allergan and the pharmacy chains CVS, Walgreens and Walmart agreed to pay up to about $18.5 billion more among them. Under the settlements, states and towns must spend at least 85 of every 100 dollars they get on the overdose crisis.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nan-goldin-drugs-alcohol" name="Nan Goldin" movie="All the Beauty and the Bloodshed (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nan Goldin

> **Who:** A photographer who became addicted to OxyContin after surgery, overdosed on fentanyl, went through treatment, and led protests against the Sackler family.
> **When and where:** Berlin and New York, the 2010s to 2021.
> **Movie:** *All the Beauty and the Bloodshed* (2022), a documentary by Laura Poitras about Goldin's life and her protests.

Nan Goldin became addicted to OxyContin after a doctor prescribed it, and she overdosed on fentanyl. After treatment, she led protests against the Sackler family, who owned the company that made OxyContin. She told her own story in the magazine *Artforum* in January 2018.

Goldin was born in Washington, D.C., in 1953. She is a photographer known for her book of photographs *The Ballad of Sexual Dependency* (1986). A doctor in Berlin prescribed OxyContin for her after surgery on her wrist. "Though I took it as directed I got addicted overnight," she wrote. "I went from three pills a day, as prescribed, to eighteen."

When her doctors stopped prescribing it, she bought pills from a dealer in New York. "When I ran out of money for Oxy I copped dope," she wrote, which means she bought illegal drugs from dealers. "I ended up snorting fentanyl and I overdosed." She waited a year before she went into treatment, because she was afraid of withdrawal, the sickness that comes when the body loses a drug it depends on. Then she spent two and a half months in treatment.

In 2018 Goldin started a group called P.A.I.N., which stands for Prescription Addiction Intervention Now. On 10 March 2018, she and other members protested in the Sackler Wing of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. They threw pill bottles labeled OxyContin into the pool around the Temple of Dendur, an Egyptian temple about 2,000 years old that stands in the wing. Then they lay on the floor and chanted, "Sacklers lie, people die."

In 2019 officials at the National Portrait Gallery in London turned down a £1 million gift from the Sacklers after Goldin threatened to pull her show. Leaders of the Tate galleries in London said they would take no more Sackler gifts. On 9 December 2021, officials of the Metropolitan Museum and members of the Sackler families announced that seven of the museum's spaces would no longer carry the Sackler name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nan-goldin-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marijuana becomes legal in 24 states" -->
On 6 November 2012, voters in Colorado and Washington made marijuana legal for adults. They were the first two states to do so. Washington's law took effect first, on 6 December 2012. In 2018 Vermont's lawmakers became the first to make it legal without a vote of the public.

By the count of the National Conference of State Legislatures, a group that serves state lawmakers, 24 states and the District of Columbia allow adults to use marijuana. The group's page was last updated in September 2026. The 24 states are Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Ohio, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia and Washington. By the same count, 41 states allow marijuana as medicine. In November 2024, voters in Florida, North Dakota and South Dakota voted against making it legal.

Under federal law, marijuana was still on Schedule I, the list for drugs with a high risk of abuse and no accepted use as medicine. On 18 December 2025, President Donald Trump ordered officials to speed up moving it to Schedule III. Schedule III is a list of drugs that doctors may order as medicine. On 28 April 2026, the Acting Attorney General moved marijuana sold under state medical licenses to Schedule III. All other marijuana stayed on Schedule I. In June 2026 officials of the Drug Enforcement Administration began hearings on moving the rest. As of late September 2026, they had not decided.

Punishments for crack also changed. In 2010 President Barack Obama signed the Fair Sentencing Act, written by Senator Dick Durbin. Under it, the difference between crack and powder cocaine sentences dropped from 100 to 1 to 18 to 1. Under the First Step Act, which President Trump signed in 2018, the change also covered people already in prison. In its first year, more than 2,000 federal prisoners had their sentences shortened.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vaping and smoking" -->
An e-cigarette heats a liquid, usually one with nicotine in it, into a mist that the user breathes in. Using one is called vaping. In 2019, 27.5 of every 100 high school students vaped, by the count of a national survey released by the CDC.

In 2019 and early 2020, 2,807 people were put in the hospital or died with a lung injury from vaping, by the CDC's count. Of them, 68 died. CDC scientists found that vitamin E acetate, a chemical added to illegal marijuana vaping liquids, was strongly linked to the injuries. They linked most cases to those liquids, which most of the sick people had bought from friends, family or dealers. The records do not name who made them.

On 20 December 2019, President Trump signed a law that raised the age to buy any tobacco product, including e-cigarettes, from 18 to 21. In 2025, 5.2 of every 100 middle and high school students vaped, by the count of a national survey released by the FDA in June 2026.

In 2025, 9.1 of every 100 adults smoked cigarettes, by the CDC's count. In 1965 the figure had been 42.4. Smoking and secondhand smoke, the smoke other people breathe in, still kill more than 480,000 Americans a year, by the CDC's newest count, from 2024.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinking today" -->
In 2023 Americans aged 14 and over drank an average of 2.48 gallons of pure alcohol each, by the count of the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism. That is below the peak of 2.76 gallons in 1981. Americans now drink more liquor and less beer than in 2000. Pure alcohol from beer fell from 1.23 gallons a person in 2000 to 0.99 in 2023. Pure alcohol from liquor rose from 0.64 gallons to 1.08.

In July 2026, 54 of every 100 adults told Gallup pollsters that they drink, the lowest share since Gallup began asking in 1939. In 2023 the figure was 62. In the same 2026 poll, 51 of every 100 adults said that one or two drinks a day is bad for health.

The CDC counts about 178,000 deaths a year from drinking too much, from data for 2020 and 2021. On average, each of those people lost 24 years of life. On 3 January 2025, Surgeon General Vivek Murthy reported that alcohol causes about 100,000 cases of cancer and 20,000 cancer deaths a year in the United States. He asked Congress to put a cancer warning on alcohol labels. In January 2026, federal health officials dropped their old advice to drink no more than one drink a day for women and two for men. The new advice says only, "Consume less alcohol for better health."

In 2025, 41 of every 100 high school seniors said they had drunk alcohol in the past year, by the count of the Monitoring the Future survey. Alcoholics Anonymous estimates that it has more than two million members in more than 123,000 groups around the world.
