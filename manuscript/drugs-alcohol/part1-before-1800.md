## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 28 of 37 · Drugs and Alcohol · 10 eras · about 110 minutes**

**People in this land have used tobacco for at least 12,000 years, and when Americans turned against a drug, the punishment fell hardest on the people who had the least power.**

Judges in the Massachusetts Bay Colony ordered a colonist named Robert Coles in 1634 to wear a letter D for a year. The D was made of red cloth, set on a white background, and it hung from his neck over his clothes whenever he was among other people. It was his fourth punishment for drunkenness in three years. Governor John Winthrop wrote in his journal that Coles, "having been oft (often) punished for drunkenness, was now ordered to wear a red D about his neck for a year."

This chapter follows tobacco, alcohol and other drugs through all ten eras, from a fire pit 12,300 years old to the fentanyl deaths of the 2020s. Again and again a drug is sold freely, then feared, then taxed or banned, and sometimes allowed again.

Each time, look at who gets punished. Coles was a free colonist with the right to vote. The laws that came later fell on Native people, Chinese immigrants, Black Americans and poor drinkers. No member of the Sackler family, which owned the company that made the painkiller OxyContin, has been charged with a crime. The businessman whose ginger medicine left tens of thousands of people unable to use their hands and feet never served a day in jail. Look, too, for the people who quit and then helped others quit.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How long have people here used tobacco, and what did they use it for?
- What happened when the whole country banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933?
- Who has been punished for drugs, and who has not?

**Five minutes?** Read "Poisoned alcohol and jake leg" in 1900 to 1950.
**Can skip:** "Drinks for voters" and "The tavern as the town's meeting place" in 1700 to 1750, and "Chewing tobacco" in 1800 to 1850. The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 6 minutes**

People in this land used plants that change how the body and the mind feel for thousands of years before Europeans came. The oldest one found so far is tobacco. In the records written later, many of these uses were part of prayer and ceremony. Native people offered tobacco in prayer, men in the Southeast drank Black Drink to make themselves pure, and the O'odham drank cactus wine at a rain ceremony. The Apache drank their corn beer mostly at everyday social times.

**Beer and wine, but no liquor.** Before Europeans came, people made beer and wine mainly in the Southwest, and people elsewhere used alcohol only here and there. Nobody in this land made distilled liquor, such as rum or whiskey. To distill a drink, people heat it and catch the steam, which cools into a liquor stronger than beer or wine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How old is tobacco use here?" -->
People in North America were using tobacco about 12,300 years ago. Scientists found burned tobacco in a hearth, an old fire pit, at the Wishbone site, in the desert of western North America. The find is 9,000 years older than any earlier evidence of tobacco use, and it was first published online in October 2021. How the people at that fire used the tobacco is not known.

A pipe found in central Washington State is about 1,430 years old. Chemists found nicotine, the drug in tobacco, inside it. The pipe also held traces of a wild tobacco that grows here, mixed with a plant called smooth sumac.

**Tobacco as an offering.** The "Keep It Sacred" program of the Inter-Tribal Council of Michigan describes tobacco used as an offering, which it calls traditional tobacco. People grow or gather it for ceremony or for medicine. It is the plant known by its Latin name, *Nicotiana rustica*. People offer it to the Creator, or to another person, place or being, by burning it in a fire or smoking it in a pipe.

In the program's words, the smoke carries "thoughts and prayers to the spirit world." The smoke is generally not breathed into the lungs. The program's writers state that traditional tobacco used this way is not linked to addiction, a need for a drug so strong that stopping is very hard, or to harm to health. They set it apart from commercial tobacco, the kind sold in stores. The ways people used tobacco differed from one nation to another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Drink, cacao, peyote and datura" -->
Black Drink was a tea made from the leaves of yaupon holly or dahoon holly, two kinds of holly bush. It contains caffeine, the drug in coffee that keeps people awake. Chemists found traces of it inside beaker cups from Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, dated from 1050 to 1250. These hollies grow far to the south of Cahokia, so people carried the leaves north. Records from later centuries describe men in the Southeast drinking Black Drink in a ceremony to make themselves pure.

**Chocolate from the south.** Cacao is the bean that chocolate is made from, and it grows in Mexico and Central America. Jars at Pueblo Bonito, in Chaco Canyon in New Mexico, held traces of a cacao drink. They date from about 1000 to 1125, and the beans came north by trade. It is the first known use of cacao drinks north of the Mexican border. Drinks made from cacao or holly turn up at 18 sites in the Southwest and northern Mexico, from about 750 to 1400.

**Peyote.** Peyote is a small cactus whose top holds mescaline, a drug that causes visions. Two dried peyote tops kept at the Witte Museum in San Antonio are about 5,700 years old. They were probably found in Shumla Cave, on the Rio Grande in Texas. When chemists tested them, they still held mescaline. The chemists wrote that Native North Americans knew what peyote does as long as 5,700 years ago.

**Datura.** Datura is a flowering plant that holds two drugs, scopolamine and atropine. They cause hallucinations, which means seeing and hearing things that are not there. In Pinwheel Cave in California, people chewed wads of datura and pressed them into the painted ceiling. Records from later times show Native Californians using datura to enter a trance, a state like a dream while awake. People used the cave from before 1500 into the years after Europeans arrived, so the wads have no exact date.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who made alcohol before 1500?" -->
Native nations in the Southwest made beer and wine before Europeans came, and other nations used alcohol only here and there. Patrick J. Abbott, a doctor, found this in a 1996 study of how Native nations used alcohol before Europeans came. Most Native people first met alcohol when Europeans brought it in the 1500s and 1600s.

**Cactus wine.** The Akimel O'odham and the Tohono O'odham made wine from a cactus. Older books call them the Pima and the Papago. Each late summer the women gathered the red fruit of the saguaro cactus. They made most of it into jam and the last of the harvest into wine. People drank the wine at the rain ceremony held at their New Year, late in the summer. When Abbott wrote, many Tohono O'odham villages still held that ceremony.

**Corn beer.** The Apache made a corn beer called tiswin, which they learned to make from peoples of northern Mexico. They drank it mostly in everyday dealings with one another, and only sometimes in ceremony. The Apache reached the Southwest sometime between 1000 and 1550. For that reason, nobody knows whether they made tiswin here before 1500.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 4 minutes**

Europeans learned to use tobacco from the peoples of the Americas in the 1500s and carried the habit home. A French official sent tobacco seeds to the queen of France in 1560, and English colonists at Roanoke smoked it the Algonquian way in 1585. Spanish colonists brought wine with them to Florida.

Most Native people in this land first met alcohol in the 1500s and 1600s, when Europeans arrived. A regular trade in alcohol with Native nations began in the 1600s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why nicotine is named after a Frenchman" -->
Jean Nicot was the French ambassador to Portugal, the official who spoke for the king of France there. In 1560 he sent tobacco seeds to Queen Catherine de Médicis of France, with a letter about the plant's uses as a medicine. The next year he gave her tobacco leaves and showed her how to grind them into a powder to sniff for headaches. The people around the French king and queen took up the powder and sniffed it too.

The word nicotine comes from Nicot's name. So does *Nicotiana*, the name the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus gave the tobacco plants in 1753.

Tobacco reached England in 1565, perhaps brought from Florida by Sir John Hawkins. By 1588 English men and women of high rank were using it, and so were some well-trained doctors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" name="Thomas Hariot" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hariot

> **Who:** An English mathematician who smoked tobacco the Algonquian way at Roanoke and wrote down how the Algonquian people used it.
> **When and where:** Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, 1585 to 1586. London until his death in 1621.

**He smoked the Algonquian plant uppowoc and died of nose cancer.**

Thomas Hariot studied mathematics and the stars. In 1585 he sailed to Roanoke Island with a group of English ships and men sent to start a colony. Most of the colonists were soldiers. Hariot and the artist John White were the best known of the few who were not.

**Whose island it was.** Roanoke Island was the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples. Their leading werowance, or chief, was Wingina, and he let the English build a fort on the north end of the island. Hariot learned the Algonquian language from two Native men of the region, Manteo and Wanchese.

**The plant.** The people called it uppowoc. They planted it in plots of its own, dried the leaves, ground them to powder and drew the smoke through clay pipes. They believed the smoke opened the passages of the body and kept them healthy. As an offering, they threw the powder into sacred fires, and onto the water in storms and after escaping a danger.

Hariot wrote that the English "vsed to suck it after their maner" on the island. In today's words, they smoked it the way the Algonquian people did, and they went on smoking it after they came home.

**The killing of Wingina.** Wingina had let the English build their fort in 1585, and the next year they killed him. On June 1, 1586, men under the English commander Ralph Lane attacked his village. They shot Wingina and chased him down, and an Irishman named Edward Nugent cut off his head. A week later the English sailed home, and Hariot went with them.

Hariot described uppowoc in a book printed in 1588, *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia*. In it he also wrote that near the end of the colony's year, some of the English had been "too fierce, in slaying some of the people." In the same sentence he wrote that the people had "iustly deserued" (justly deserved) it. He did not name the dead.

Hariot died in London on July 2, 1621, of cancer of the nose. The writers of Encyclopedia Virginia state that the cancer was "probably caused by heavy tobacco use."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish wine in Florida" -->
Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine, in Florida, on September 8, 1565. They built it at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people.

Wine and olive oil came by ship in big Spanish storage jars called olive jars. Historians at the Florida Museum of Natural History write that the two were perhaps even more important to the Spanish than wheat and beef. They also write that the Spanish soon turned to the Timucua for food, cooking pots and wives. Their account does not say whether the Timucua women chose these marriages.

**Alcohol as a gift.** The historian Peter C. Mancall writes that some Europeans surely offered alcohol to Native people when they first met, though no record from this century names a case. No record from the 1500s describes the Spanish trading wine to Native nations as a regular business.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 9 minutes**

Drinking came over on the first English ships, and colony leaders spent the 1600s trying to keep it in bounds. The colonists' first drinks were cider and beer, and they believed alcohol kept the body warm and strong. Their leaders licensed taverns and punished drunks with fines, iron shackles, the stocks and public shame. In Virginia, planters grew tobacco to sell in England, and the smoking habit spread.

**Liquor for furs.** English traders sold rum and French traders sold brandy to Native nations in exchange for furs. Colony leaders passed laws against these sales, and traders kept breaking them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beer, cider and bad water" -->
The first drinks the colonists made were cider and beer. They brewed beer from corn, wheat, oats, persimmons and green cornstalks. They tried to grow grapes for wine, but the vines they brought from Europe died of American pests and diseases.

Colonists believed that alcohol warmed the body, helped digestion and made people strong. The land here had plenty of clean water, but some colonists drank from dirty sources and got sick. At Jamestown, George Percy wrote that the settlers drank river water that was salty at high tide and "full of slime and filth" at low tide. He called it "the destruction of many of our men." In Europe, where rivers were often dirty, people drank alcohol in place of water, and the colonists kept that habit.

Officials of the Virginia Company of London complained about it. In a letter to Governor Francis Wyatt in 1622, they wrote that the colonists' drinking was harming the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bolts, fines and the stocks" -->
Virginia's first lawmakers passed a law against drunkenness in 1619, at the colony's first General Assembly. The first time a man was found drunk, the minister scolded him in private. The second time, the minister scolded him in public. For a third offense he had to lie for 12 hours in bolts, iron shackles, in the house of an officer called the provost marshal, and pay the marshal's fees.

If he still kept drinking, the governor and his council, the men who advised him, could give him whatever harsh punishment they chose.

**Massachusetts.** The lawmakers of Massachusetts passed a set of drinking laws between 1633 and 1647. Nobody could run a tavern without a license from the county court. A tavern keeper could not serve one person more than half a pint of wine at a time. Nobody could sit drinking for more than half an hour, or stay after nine at night.

**The fine.** A person found drunk in Massachusetts paid 10 shillings, half a pound in English money. The law defined drunk as unable to think clearly, which showed in the way the person talked or moved. A person who could not pay was to be jailed, or set for up to three hours in the stocks, a wooden frame that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place.

**Prices.** The lawmakers also set what an innkeeper could charge. In Boston in 1634 a meal cost sixpence and a quart of beer a penny, with a fine of 10 shillings for charging more. A traveller named John Josselyn reported that a constable, a town officer who kept order, would stop a stranger in a Boston tavern from ordering more drink than the constable thought right.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" name="Samuel Cole" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Cole

> **Who:** The keeper of the earliest tavern in Boston known to have had a license. He is not Robert Coles, whom Massachusetts judges punished for drunkenness.
> **When and where:** Boston, in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1634 to 1638.

**About twenty Narragansett men ate their peace-treaty meal at his inn.**

Samuel Adams Drake, a historian of Boston's taverns, dates the opening of Samuel Cole's tavern to March 4, 1634. A tavern was then called an ordinary.

Cole was a comfit-maker, a maker of sugar-coated sweets. He came to Boston in 1630, in the fleet of ships that brought Governor John Winthrop. His ordinary stood on Washington Street, and the house had a brew-house, a building for making beer.

**The Narragansett visit.** The English and the Narragansett people made a peace agreement in 1636, and Governor Henry Vane hosted Narragansett leaders in Boston. The Narragansett leader Miantonomi and two sons of the leader Canonicus ate at the governor's house. About twenty other Narragansett men ate their meal at Cole's inn.

The colony's judges let Cole sell his house as an inn in 1638, and he sold it to Robert Sedgwick. Cole died in 1666.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" name="Robert Coles" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Robert Coles

> **Who:** A colonist whom judges in Massachusetts punished four times for drunkenness, the last time by making him wear a red letter D. He is not Samuel Cole, the Boston tavern keeper.
> **When and where:** Roxbury and Agawam, now Ipswich, in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1631 to 1634.

**A paper on his back said DRUNKARD in large letters.**

**The first fine.** Robert Coles came to Massachusetts in 1630 with Governor John Winthrop's fleet. In 1631 the colony's judges fined him five marks, about 3 pounds, 6 shillings and 8 pence, for being drunk on the ship *Friendship* and at Winnisimmet, now Chelsea. The *Friendship* was carrying a spiced wine made from honey.

**The paper.** At Agawam in 1633 he was charged with drunkenness for the third time. He had been drunk, had encouraged John Shatswell's wife to drink, and had urged her to be unfaithful to her husband. The judges fined him 10 pounds and made him stand in public with a paper on his back. On it the word drunkard was written in large letters.

**The red D.** He was drunk again at Roxbury in 1634. This time the judges ordered him to wear the red D for a year, and they took away his right to vote.

**Afterward.** Coles got back his right to vote about two months later, on May 14, 1634. The records show no later charge of drunkenness against him, though the Roxbury church later put him out.

By 1637 he had moved to Providence, a town built on land that Roger Williams got in 1636 from the Narragansett leaders Canonicus and Miantonomi. Coles was one of the 13 men who first owned land there, and in 1640 he helped write the Providence Combination, a written plan for governing the town. He died in Warwick, Rhode Island, in 1655.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor for furs" -->
Henry Hudson used liquor to test a group of Mahican leaders in September 1609. His ship lay near what is now Albany, in Mahican land. Hudson and his mate took the leaders into the ship's cabin and gave them "so much Wine and Aqua vitae," a strong liquor like brandy, "that they were all merrie."

**Why Hudson did it.** Robert Juet, who kept a journal on the ship, wrote that Hudson and the mate wanted to find out whether the leaders were planning "treacherie," a plan to betray them. In the end one of the men got drunk. Juet wrote that this was strange to the Mahican, "for they could not tell how to take it." The next day the people came back with tobacco and beads for Hudson, and a platter of venison.

**The regular trade.** The historian Peter C. Mancall dates the start of a regular trade in alcohol to the middle of the 1600s. From 1650 on, he writes, liquor was a common item in the fur trade. English traders sold rum to Native nations, and French traders sold brandy, in exchange for furs. Mancall writes that young men who controlled the furs from a season's hunt traded them for liquor, and more families became poor.

**What the liquor did.** Native and colonial witnesses both wrote down what happened when Native people drank the trade liquor. Drinkers fought each other and members of their own families. Some fell into fires or off cliffs, some drowned, and some killed other people. No one counted the deaths.

**The bans.** Colony leaders passed laws against the trade. In New Netherland, the Dutch colony that is now New York, Director General Willem Kieft, the colony's governor, and his council banned selling liquor to Native people on June 18, 1643. Their order said that "large quantities of strong liquors are daily sold to the Indians." The fine was 25 guilders, a guilder being a Dutch coin.

They passed the ban again in 1645. The next Director General, Petrus Stuyvesant, and his council passed it a third time in 1647, and raised the fine to 500 guilders plus the cost of any damage. The laws of the colonies name the officials who banned the trade. They rarely name the traders who broke the bans.

**A false belief.** Mancall writes that Native people organized against the trade themselves. Some colonists came to believe that Native people were born more likely to become alcoholics, people who cannot stop drinking. Mancall states that no evidence supports that belief.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco and rum" -->
King James I of England attacked smoking in 1604, in a pamphlet called *A Counterblaste to Tobacco*. He called it "lothsome (disgusting) to the eye, hatefull to the Nose, harmefull to the braine, dangerous to the Lungs." In the same pamphlet he insulted Native people. He mocked the English for copying "the barbarous and beastly maners of the wilde, godlesse, and slauish Indians."

**A milder leaf.** The tobacco that the Powhatan people grew tasted dark and bitter to the English. John Rolfe, a colonist at Jamestown who smoked heavily, got seeds of a milder Spanish tobacco from Trinidad and Caracas. He was growing it by July 1612. The colonists shipped 20,000 pounds of it to England in 1617 and grew 60,000 pounds in 1622. After 1622 English planters took over land that the Powhatan had already cleared, to grow more tobacco.

**Smoking rules in Massachusetts.** Massachusetts officials told constables in 1633 to report "tobacco takers" as idle people. By 1638 the lawmakers had canceled their earlier laws against tobacco, and that year they wrote that since then smoking was "more abused than before." So they banned smoking near any house, barn, corn or haystack, because a pipe that close might set them on fire.

They also banned smoking outdoors in the streets and fields, except on a journey or at a meal, and in the public rooms of an inn. They passed these rules again in 1647, and that version put the distance from buildings and crops at about 330 feet.

**Rum.** Rum is a strong liquor made from molasses, the thick syrup left over when sugar is made from sugarcane. The first known name for rum comes from the island of Barbados, in about 1651. A planter there called it "Kill-Divill" and "a hott hellish and terrible liquor." In the 1600s New England merchants began shipping fish and lumber to the islands of the West Indies. They traded them for molasses and made the molasses into rum at home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 7 minutes**

Colonists drank more rum than any other strong drink between 1700 and 1750, and it was cheap. A gallon of rum made in the colonies cost 1 shilling 8 pence in Philadelphia in 1740. People drank in taverns, and they also met there to do the town's business. Colony leaders controlled drinking with tavern licenses and with rules for elections.

**Georgia's ban.** A Yamacraw leader named Tomochichi asked Georgia's leaders to stop the sale of rum, and they banned strong liquor for seven years, from 1735 to 1742. Nobody counted how much colonists drank in these years. The first estimates come from the 1770s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rum, molasses and tobacco" -->
Colonial distillers made rum from molasses shipped in from the sugar plantations of the Caribbean. Some of the molasses came in legally, and some was smuggled, which means merchants brought it in secretly without paying the tax on it. In Philadelphia in 1740, a gallon of colonial rum cost 1 shilling 8 pence. A gallon of rum from the Caribbean cost 2 shillings 5 pence.

**The Molasses Act.** At the urging of large sugar planters on the British islands in the Caribbean, the members of the British Parliament, the lawmakers of Britain, passed the Molasses Act in 1733. Under it, importers owed sixpence a gallon on molasses from the French islands. Colonial merchants kept smuggling French molasses anyway, much of it from Saint Domingue, now Haiti. The tax should have brought in 25,000 pounds in British money each year. British officials collected 259 pounds in 1735.

**Rum for people.** Rum was part of a trade in people. New England ships carried rum to Africa, enslaved Africans to the West Indies, and molasses back to New England. Distillers made an extra-strong rum for this trade, so that ships could carry more of it in less space. When the ships reached Africa, the captains added water to the rum. They traded it for people to sell into slavery.

**Tobacco and slavery.** Enslaved Africans outnumbered white indentured servants, workers bound to a master for a set number of years, on Virginia's tobacco farms by 1700. Virginia planters grew 29 million pounds of tobacco in 1709, the most the colony ever grew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinks for voters" -->
Only white men aged 21 or older who owned land could vote in colonial Virginia. Enslaved people, free Black people, Native people, servants, white men with no land and all women could not. Each voter said his choice out loud in front of the crowd. Voters expected the men running for office to give them liquor and food.

**The dry weeks.** Virginia's lawmakers limited these gifts, called treats, in a law of 1705. Candidates could give treats before the sheriff read out the notice of an election, and again after the voting. The weeks in between were supposed to be "dry," with no treats. Some candidates got around the rule. They stayed away and had a friend, a servant or even a wife hand out the drinks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tavern as the town's meeting place" -->
Boston men used taverns as meeting places for a lodge, for the colony's lawmakers, and for signing up sailors in wartime. Francis Holmes kept the Bunch of Grapes on King Street, now State Street, from 1712. One traveller called it the best punch house in Boston. Punch was a mixed drink made with liquor.

**The first grand lodge.** Henry Price, a Boston tailor, organized a grand lodge of Freemasons at the Bunch of Grapes on July 30, 1733. The Freemasons were a society of men who met in lodges, and a grand lodge was a head lodge over the others.

**Vardy's.** Luke Vardy kept the Royal Exchange tavern. When Boston's town-house burned in the winter of 1747, the colony's lawmakers finished their meetings at Vardy's.

**A duel.** Two young men quarrelled at Vardy's in July 1728, over their cards or their wine. They fought with swords on Boston Common, and one of them killed the other, a man named Woodbridge. The killer's friends put him on a ship that sailed at once. The historian Samuel Adams Drake, who told the story in 1886, does not give the killer's name.

**Signing up sailors.** During a war between Britain and France in 1745, notices posted in Boston told sailors who wanted to join a privateer to report to the Widow Gray's Crown Tavern. A privateer was a privately owned ship allowed to attack enemy ships. Women kept several of Boston's taverns. Drake's list includes the Widow Gray, the Widow Anna Swords, a Mrs. Wardwell and a Mrs. Glover.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->


<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Georgia bans rum, 1735 to 1742" -->
James Oglethorpe and the first colonists of Georgia settled on Yamacraw Bluff, where Savannah stands today, in February 1733. Tomochichi, the leader of a Yamacraw town of about 200 Creek and Yamasee people, gave them the land on the bluff.

**Tomochichi's request.** Tomochichi saw more of his people drinking heavily. In 1734 he asked Oglethorpe and the Trustees, a board of men who met in London and ran the colony from there, to ban the sale of rum.

The Trustees banned rum and brandy in Georgia in 1735, and King George II and his council approved the law. It called these liquors "hurtful and pernicious (harmful) to Man's Body." The Trustees named an officer to enforce the ban and set fines that grew larger with each offense. Taverns could still sell beer and wine with a license.

**The Malcontents.** Colonists soon accused the officials who ran the ban of being dishonest, and they protested the ban. A group known as the Malcontents, a word for people who are unhappy and complain, argued that the ban hurt trade. Georgia merchants, they said, needed rum to trade for timber and other goods, which they then shipped to the British islands in the Caribbean. The Trustees ended the ban on September 29, 1742, and let rum in again for use in the lumber trade. Oglethorpe disagreed with their decision.

Traders from South Carolina had kept carrying rum up the Savannah River all along, against the Trustees' order. In a letter of 1737, Oglethorpe wrote that the Creek had learned drunkenness "from our Traders."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-physician-drugs-alcohol" name="Dr. Alexander Hamilton" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Dr. Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** A Scottish-born doctor from Annapolis, Maryland, who kept a diary of the taverns and drinking clubs he saw on a long ride north. He is not the Alexander Hamilton who later ran the Treasury.
> **When and where:** From Maryland to York, Maine, and back, May 30 to September 27, 1744.

**He drank three "bumpers" with the Hungarian Club and left at ten.**

Dr. Alexander Hamilton had moved to Annapolis in 1738 to work as a doctor, and in 1743 he was very sick. Mainly to get his health back, he set out on horseback on May 30, 1744. He rode 1,624 miles, from Maryland to Maine and back, and reached home on September 27.

**Crooked riders.** On May 31, at Tradaway's inn in Maryland, he met "a drunken Club" leaving. Most of the men had climbed onto their horses and sat on them crooked. At an inn near Philadelphia on June 5, the landlady did all the work because the landlord was drunk. Hamilton wrote that the liquor had taken away "the use of his tongue." The landlord sat without moving in a corner, smoking his pipe.

**The Hungarian Club.** On June 15 he ate supper in New York with the Hungarian Club at Todd's tavern. After supper the men drank "bumpers," glasses filled to the brim and drunk off at once, at each round. Hamilton did not want to drink and drank only three. He left at ten, "pretty well flushed (red in the face) with my three bumpers."

Some of the men, he wrote, thought nothing was finer than being able "to pour down seas of liquor, and remain unconquered, while others sank under the table." He wrote that some members were "very fond of making a stranger drunk."

**The doctors' club.** In Boston he met with a club of doctors at the Sun Tavern. "We drank punch, smoaked tobacco, and talked of sundry physical matters," he wrote, meaning various matters of medicine. In 1745 he started a club of his own in Annapolis, the Tuesday Club. He died on May 11, 1756.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 13 minutes**

Rum made fortunes in the colonies before the Revolution, and whiskey took its place after. By 1770 the colonies had more than 140 rum distilleries, making about 4.8 million gallons a year. Ship captains from Rhode Island carried rum to Africa and traded it for African people to sell into slavery. After the war, Americans switched to whiskey made from their own corn and rye.

**Who pushed back.** Native leaders asked colonists from the 1750s on to stop selling them liquor. The Philadelphia doctor Benjamin Rush argued in 1785 that heavy drinking was a disease. When members of Congress taxed whiskey in 1791, armed men in western Pennsylvania attacked the tax collectors, and President George Washington called up nearly 13,000 militiamen, part-time citizen soldiers, against them. In 1799 Handsome Lake, a Seneca religious leader, stopped drinking and preached against it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much did the colonists drink?" -->
Nobody measured how much colonists drank before the 1770s, and the estimates made since then each measure something different. The writer Ed Crews, in the *Colonial Williamsburg Journal*, reports an estimate that the average grown man in the 1770s may have drunk as much as three pints a week. The writer Ian Williams gives 3.7 gallons of rum a year for each colonist by the time of the Revolution. The historian Peter C. Mancall cites an estimate of perhaps seven shots of liquor a day by 1770.

**The first government count.** Government figures for 1790 showed that the average American over the age of 15 drank 34 gallons of beer and cider, 5 gallons of liquor and 1 gallon of wine in a year.

**Drink as medicine.** People believed alcohol was good for the body. They took whiskey for stomach pain and for a sore throat, and hot brandy punch for cholera, a disease that causes severe diarrhea. Pregnant women and women in labor drank a shot of liquor to ease the pain. Benjamin Franklin collected more than 200 words that meant drunk.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rum and the slave trade" -->
Rhode Island had about 30 rum distilleries by 1764, 22 of them in the town of Newport. Rhode Island merchants traded the rum on the coast of West Africa for African people to enslave. In the hundred years before 1807, Rhode Islanders made at least 1,000 voyages to buy and carry enslaved people. They carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery.

**The *Sally*.** Chapter 16 tells the voyage of the slave ship *Sally* stage by stage. Here is the rum's part in it. In 1764 the brothers Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown of Providence sent the ship to West Africa with 17,274 gallons of rum to trade, along with chains, shackles and guns. Its captain, Esek Hopkins, used the rum to buy 196 people.

At least 109 of the 196 died, by the count in Brown University's report on the voyage. Several were children, and one woman hanged herself below deck. When the captives rose up against the crew at sea, Hopkins's men fired on them. The ship's account book records that the crew "Destroyed 8," which means they killed eight people. After that, the account book says, "Some Drowned themselves Some Starved and others Sickened & Dyed."

**The Sugar Act.** Rum was made from molasses. Since the Molasses Act of 1733, colonial merchants had smuggled molasses from the French islands to avoid its tax of sixpence a gallon. In 1764, the year the *Sally* sailed, members of the British Parliament cut the tax on foreign molasses to threepence a gallon, in a law called the Sugar Act. They also pushed the British navy to stop the smuggling, and navy officers who caught smugglers could seize half of the smuggled goods they found.

**From rum to whiskey.** During the Revolution, British warships stopped much of the shipping, and molasses became costly and hard to get. Scotch-Irish farmers in Pennsylvania, Maryland, western Virginia and western North Carolina made whiskey from rye and corn instead. Before 1800, distillers in Kentucky were shipping whiskey down the rivers to New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native leaders against the liquor trade" -->
Hagler, a leader of the Catawba people in the hill country of the Carolinas, spoke to officials sent by North Carolina in 1754. He told them, "You Rot Your grain in Tubs, out of which you take and make Strong Spirits." Hagler and other Native leaders asked the colonists to stop the liquor trade, because it brought violence and despair.

**What the British official wanted.** Sir William Johnson, the British superintendent of Indian affairs for the northern colonies, handled dealings with Native nations, and he wanted the trade to go on. In 1764 he wrote to his bosses in London, the Lords of Trade, that rum could cause problems. He also wrote that "the Trade will never be so extensive" without rum. After the Revolution, traders carried liquor farther west, and they added water to it so they had more to sell.

**Handsome Lake.** United States troops burned the villages of the Haudenosaunee, also called the Iroquois, in New York in 1779. After the war, at Fort Stanwix in 1784, American negotiators backed by soldiers took huge sections of land from the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee. By 1799 the Haudenosaunee had lost most of their land in New York and Pennsylvania, and many of them drank heavily.

One was Handsome Lake, a Seneca man from the Genesee River. In 1799, sick after many years of heavy drinking, he had visions. Soon after, he stopped drinking and began preaching against drunkenness. His teaching became the Longhouse Religion, which Haudenosaunee people still practice.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-rush-drugs-alcohol" name="Benjamin Rush" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Rush

> **Who:** A Philadelphia doctor who signed the Declaration of Independence and wrote that heavy drinking was a disease.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1785 to 1790.

**His chart put water at the top and the gallows at the bottom.**

Before Benjamin Rush, most Americans thought a drunkard simply lacked the will to stop. Rush argued that heavy drinking was an addiction.

**The book.** In 1785 he published a book about ardent spirits, meaning strong liquor such as rum, gin and whiskey. Its title was *An Inquiry into the Effects of Ardent Spirits Upon the Human Body and Mind*, and some records date the first printing to 1784.

Rush wrote that a person begins drinking by free choice, and the habit then becomes a need. In his words: "The use of strong drink is at first the effect of free agency. From habit it takes place from necessity." He said the only cure was to stop drinking completely. His advice to heavy drinkers was "Taste not, handle not."

**The thermometer.** An edition of 1790 added a chart that Rush called "A Moral and Physical Thermometer." Water and milk stood at the top. Lower down, punch led to "idleness, sickness and debt." At the bottom, gin, brandy and rum taken every day led to "murder, madness" and the gallows.

Few people changed their drinking at the time. The historian Mark Lender says that "the conception we have of addiction today can generally be traced back to Benjamin Rush."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-rush-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why farmers went to war over a whiskey tax" -->
Farmers in western Pennsylvania turned their grain into whiskey and used the whiskey as money. From 1791 some of them fought a new federal tax on whiskey, and in 1794 hundreds of them took up guns against it. The fight is called the Whiskey Rebellion.

**Why whiskey.** The roads over the mountains to the east were bad, and grain spoiled on the way. Whiskey kept, and it was easier to carry. Coins were scarce on the frontier, so people paid for goods and paid their workers in whiskey.

**Whose land it was.** The whiskey farmers worked land that the Shawnee, Delaware and Mingo peoples had used. Representatives of the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee had signed that land over to Britain in 1768, at Fort Stanwix in New York. That was an earlier treaty than the one made at the same fort in 1784. The Shawnee and Delaware, who lived on much of the land, were at the meeting but did not sign.

**The tax.** Alexander Hamilton, the Treasury Secretary in charge of the country's money, asked for a tax on whiskey made in the United States in January 1791, to pay the debt from the Revolutionary War. Members of Congress voted it into law. The law set the tax at 7 cents a gallon, but big distillers paid less than that and small ones paid more.

A big distiller could instead pay a flat fee for each still, the equipment used to distill liquor, and that came to as little as 6 cents a gallon. A farmer with a small still paid about 9 cents a gallon. The law required payment in coin, which the farmers rarely had.

**Two men tarred and feathered.** A gang of armed men in disguise caught Robert Johnson, a new tax collector, at Pigeon Creek in Washington County, Pennsylvania, on September 6, 1791. Some records give the date as September 11. They tarred and feathered him, which meant pouring hot tar on his skin and sticking feathers to the tar. They cut off his hair and took his horse, so he had to walk a long way. Hamilton's 1794 report on the attacks names none of the men.

Officials sent a man named John Connor to serve court papers on Johnson's attackers. Men seized Connor, whipped him, and tarred and feathered him too. They took his money and his horse, blindfolded him and tied him up in the woods, where he stayed for five hours. No surviving record names who did it.

**The fight at Bower Hill.** John Neville, the regional tax inspector, went with a United States marshal to serve court papers on July 15, 1794. A marshal was an officer who carried out the orders of the national courts. At a farm about 10 miles south of Pittsburgh, men fired warning shots at them.

The next day, at least 30 men from the Mingo Creek militia surrounded Neville's house, called Bower Hill. Neville shot and killed one of them, Oliver Miller. Neville had the people he enslaved help him defend the house. They could not refuse.

The men came back on July 17, this time between 400 and 600 of them, led by Major James McFarlane, who had fought in the Revolution. By then 10 United States soldiers from Pittsburgh had come to defend the house, and Neville had left to hide nearby.

After about an hour of shooting, McFarlane called a stop. When he stepped into the open, someone in the house shot and killed him. The records do not say who fired. The attackers burned Neville's house and the quarters where the enslaved people lived. The soldiers surrendered, and the attackers sent them away.

**Known:** The dead at Bower Hill included McFarlane and one or two other militiamen.
**Not known:** How many men died there in all, and whether one of the soldiers later died of his wounds.

**The army.** Washington called up 12,950 militiamen from New Jersey, Maryland, Virginia and Pennsylvania. He went with them as far as Bedford, Pennsylvania, and Fort Cumberland, Maryland, then went back to Philadelphia. He left Henry Lee, the governor of Virginia, in command, and Hamilton went along as an adviser. The rebels scattered without a battle. About 12 soldiers died of illness or accidents.

**The Dreadful Night.** Soldiers under Lee broke into houses on the night of November 13, 1794, and seized people, rebels and witnesses alike. The soldiers drove the prisoners barefoot, in their nightclothes, over muddy roads and through streams, and locked them in animal pens and in cellars with no floors. The soldiers ruined the health of some of the prisoners, and at least one prisoner died. In all, the soldiers arrested about 150 men during the march west.

**The trials.** Soldiers marched twenty of the prisoners to Philadelphia and paraded them down Broad Street on Christmas Day 1794, as trophies of the march. Ten were tried for treason, the crime of making war against one's own country, and two were found guilty. Members of Congress voted to end the whiskey tax in 1802.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-vigol-drugs-alcohol" name="Philip Vigol" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Vigol

> **Who:** A man from western Pennsylvania, one of the first two Americans found guilty of treason against the United States, for attacking whiskey-tax officers.
> **When and where:** Westmoreland and Fayette counties, Pennsylvania, 1794. Philadelphia, 1795.

**His party carried guns, beat drums and burned a tax officer's house.**

The court report spells Philip Vigol's name this way. Other records spell it Wigle, Weigel or Wigal.

**Reigan.** During the rebellion, Vigol went with an armed party, carrying guns and beating drums, to the house of Reigan, a tax officer in Westmoreland County. They forced Reigan to hand over his official papers and made him swear never to collect the tax again.

**Wells.** The same party then rode to the house of Wells, the tax officer in Fayette County, and swore to destroy Wells and his house. Wells had run and hidden. The men ransacked his house, which means they tore through it and wrecked it. Then they burned it, with everything inside, including the tax records. Later they found Wells, locked him up and made him swear to quit his job.

**The trial.** A jury in the United States court in Philadelphia found Vigol guilty of treason in 1795, and the judges sentenced him to death by hanging. At the trial, Justice William Paterson told the jury that fear excused a crime only when a person's life was in real danger at that moment. John Mitchell, the other man found guilty, was also sentenced to hang.

**The pardon.** President Washington pardoned Vigol and Mitchell in November 1795, which means he freed them from their punishment. The records differ on the day, November 2 or November 14. In his yearly message to Congress, Washington wrote, "The misled have abandoned their errors."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-vigol-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="George Washington's rum and whiskey" -->
George Washington used alcohol to win votes and to keep soldiers working, and he handed it out to the people he enslaved on the days they worked. At the end of his life he owned one of the largest whiskey distilleries in the country.

**Votes.** Washington ran for the Virginia House of Burgesses, the colony's elected lawmaking body, in Frederick County in 1758. He gave the voters 28 gallons of rum, 50 gallons of rum punch and 82 gallons of wine, beer and cider. He won the seat.

**Soldiers and the enslaved.** As commander of the Continental Army, he wrote in June 1779 that he wanted "a pretty good stock of rum" at the forts for soldiers on work duty.

At his plantation, Mount Vernon, he gave the people he enslaved an allowance of liquor for each day's work. They could not refuse the work. Once he accused his overseer, James Butler, the man who ran their work, of giving the enslaved people 36 pints of rum. Butler answered that he had given "no more than a pint of rum & a pound of Meat to each man."

**The distillery.** His farm manager, James Anderson, advised him to build a whiskey distillery at Mount Vernon in 1797. It made 600 gallons that first year. In 1799, the year Washington died, it made almost 11,000 gallons, worth $7,500, when the average distillery in Virginia made about 650 gallons. Anderson's son, John, ran it with an assistant distiller and six enslaved workers. Their names were Hanson, Peter, Nat, Daniel, James and Timothy.
