## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
For thousands of years before 1500, people in this land used plants that change how the body and the mind feel. The oldest one found so far is tobacco. Most of the use that the records describe was part of prayer and ceremony.

Alcohol was much rarer here. Before Europeans came, people brewed beer and wine mainly in the Southwest, and only a few scattered nations made it anywhere else. Nobody in this land made distilled liquor, such as rum or whiskey, which is much stronger than beer or wine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco for prayer" -->
The oldest known sign of people using tobacco in the Americas is about 12,300 years old. Scientists found burned tobacco in a hearth, a place where people made a fire, at the Wishbone site in the desert west of North America. It is 9,000 years older than any earlier evidence of tobacco use. The scientists published the find in 2021. How the people at the hearth used the tobacco is not known.

A pipe found in central Washington State is about 1,430 years old. Chemists found nicotine inside it. Nicotine is the drug in tobacco. The pipe also held traces of a wild tobacco that grows here, mixed with the leaves of the sumac plant.

Many Native nations used tobacco as an offering. Today the "Keep It Sacred" program of the Inter-Tribal Council of Michigan calls this traditional tobacco. It is tobacco grown or gathered for ceremony or for medicine, today mostly the plant *Nicotiana rustica*. People offer it to the Creator, or to another person, place or being. They burn it in a fire or smoke it in a pipe, and the smoke carries their thoughts and prayers to the spirit world. The smoke is generally not breathed into the lungs.

The program's writers state that traditional tobacco used in this way is not linked to addiction or to harm to health. Addiction means that a person's body and mind come to need a drug, so that stopping is very hard. Native health programs today keep traditional tobacco separate from store-bought cigarettes. The ways people used tobacco differed from one nation to another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Drink, cacao, peyote and datura" -->
Black Drink was a tea made from the leaves of yaupon holly or dahoon holly, two kinds of holly bush. It is full of caffeine, the drug in coffee that keeps people awake. Chemists found traces of it inside beaker cups from Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, dated from 1050 to 1250. These hollies grow far to the south of Cahokia, so people carried the leaves north. Records from later centuries describe men in the Southeast drinking Black Drink in a ceremony to make themselves pure.

Cacao is the bean that chocolate is made from. It grows in Mexico and Central America. At Pueblo Bonito, in Chaco Canyon in New Mexico, jars from about 1000 to 1125 held traces of a cacao drink. The beans came north by trade. It is the first known use of cacao drinks north of the Mexican border. Drinks made from cacao or holly turn up at 18 sites in the Southwest and northern Mexico, from about 750 to 1400.

Peyote is a small cactus. Its top holds mescaline, a drug that causes visions. Two dried peyote tops kept at the Witte Museum in San Antonio are about 5,700 years old. They were probably found in Shumla Cave, on the Rio Grande in Texas. When chemists tested them, they still held mescaline. The find shows that people here knew what peyote does at least 5,700 years ago.

Datura is a flowering plant that holds two drugs, scopolamine and atropine. These drugs cause hallucinations, which means seeing and hearing things that are not there. In Pinwheel Cave in California, people chewed wads of datura and pressed them into the painted ceiling. Records from later times show Native Californians using datura to enter a trance, a state like a dream while awake. People used the cave from before 1500 into the time of the Spanish, so the wads have no exact date.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beer and wine in the Southwest" -->
In 1996 Patrick J. Abbott, a doctor, wrote a study of how Native nations used alcohol before Europeans came. He found that alcohol was used mainly by nations in the Southwest, with scattered use elsewhere. The drinks were beer and wine. Most Native people first met alcohol when Europeans brought it in the 1500s and 1600s.

The Akimel O'odham and the Tohono O'odham made wine from a cactus. Older books call them the Pima and the Papago. Each late summer the women gathered the red fruit of the saguaro cactus. They made most of it into jam and the last of the harvest into wine. People drank the wine at the rain ceremony held at their New Year, late in the summer. When Abbott wrote in 1996, many Tohono O'odham villages still held that ceremony.

The Apache made a corn beer called tiswin, which they learned to make from peoples of northern Mexico. People drank it mostly at social gatherings and only sometimes in ceremony. The Apache reached the Southwest sometime between 1000 and 1550. For that reason, nobody knows whether they made tiswin here before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s Europeans learned to use tobacco from the peoples of the Americas and carried it home. At first they took it as a medicine. Spanish colonists also brought wine with them to Florida.

Few Native nations in this land met alcohol in this century. Some Europeans offered it as a gift when they met Native people. A regular trade in alcohol with Native nations did not begin until about 1650.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco reaches Europe" -->
Jean Nicot was the French ambassador to Portugal, the official who spoke for the king of France there. In 1560 he sent tobacco seeds to Queen Catherine de Médicis of France. With them he sent a letter about the plant's uses as a medicine. In 1561 he gave her tobacco leaves and showed her how to grind them into a powder to sniff for headaches. Soon people at the French court were sniffing it too.

The word nicotine comes from Nicot's name. In 1753 the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus named the tobacco plants *Nicotiana*, also after Nicot.

Tobacco reached England in 1565, perhaps brought from Florida by Sir John Hawkins. By 1588 English men and women of high rank were taking it, and so were some learned doctors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" name="Thomas Hariot" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hariot

> **Who:** An English mathematician who learned to smoke tobacco from the Algonquian people at Roanoke and wrote down how they used it.
> **When and where:** Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina, 1585 to 1586. London until his death in 1621.

Thomas Hariot learned to smoke tobacco from the Algonquian people of the Outer Banks. In a book printed in 1588, he described how they used the plant.

Hariot was born in about 1560. In 1585 he sailed with an English expedition to Roanoke Island. The island was the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples. Their leading werowance, or chief, was Wingina. Wingina let the English build a fort on the north end of the island. Hariot learned the Algonquian language from two Algonquian men, Manteo and Wanchese.

In that book, *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia*, he described a plant the people called uppowoc. They planted it in plots of its own. They dried the leaves, ground them to powder, and drew the smoke through clay pipes. They believed the smoke opened the passages of the body and kept them healthy. They also threw the powder into sacred fires as an offering. In storms, and after they escaped a danger, they threw it on the water.

Hariot wrote that the English "vsed to suck it after their maner" while they were on the island. They went on smoking it after they came home.

On 1 June 1586, men under the English commander Ralph Lane attacked Wingina's village. They shot Wingina, chased him down, and an Irishman named Edward Nugent cut off his head. A week later the English sailed home.

Hariot died in London on 2 July 1621, of cancer of the nose. The writers of Encyclopedia Virginia state that the cancer was "probably caused by heavy tobacco use."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish wine in Florida" -->
On 8 September 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine in Florida. They built it at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. The colonists counted wine and olive oil as perhaps more important than wheat and beef. Ships brought both from Spain in clay jars called olive jars. The Spanish got food and cooking pots from the Timucua.

The historian Peter C. Mancall writes that some Europeans surely offered alcohol to Native people when they first met. He writes that the real trade in alcohol did not begin until about 1650. No record from the 1500s describes the Spanish trading wine to Native nations as a regular business.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English and Dutch colonists in the 1600s drank beer, cider and wine every day. They believed alcohol kept the body warm and strong. Colony leaders licensed taverns and punished people who got drunk with fines, shackles, the stocks and public shame. In Virginia, planters grew tobacco to sell in England, and the smoking habit spread.

From about 1650, English traders sold rum and French traders sold brandy to Native nations in exchange for furs. Colony leaders passed laws against these sales, and traders kept breaking them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beer, cider and bad water" -->
The first drinks the colonists made were cider and beer. They brewed beer from corn, wheat, oats, persimmons and green cornstalks. They tried to grow grapes for wine, but the vines they brought from Europe died of American pests and diseases. Their wine came by ship.

Colonists believed that alcohol warmed the body, helped digestion and made people strong. The land here had plenty of clean water, but some colonists drank from dirty sources and got sick. At Jamestown, George Percy wrote that the settlers drank river water that was salty at high tide and "full of slime and filth" at low tide. He called it "the destruction of many of our men." In Europe, where rivers were often dirty, people drank alcohol in place of water, and the colonists kept that habit.

In 1622 officials of the Virginia Company of London wrote to Governor Francis Wyatt. They complained that the colonists' drinking was harming the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Laws about drinking" -->
In 1619 the members of Virginia's first General Assembly, the colony's first lawmaking body, passed a law against drunkenness. The first time a man was found drunk, the minister scolded him in private. The second time, the minister scolded him in public. The third time, he was held for 12 hours in bolts, which were iron shackles. He was held in the house of an officer called the provost marshal, and he had to pay the marshal's fees.

In Massachusetts, the colony's lawmakers passed a set of drinking laws between 1633 and 1647. Nobody could run a tavern without a license from the county court. A tavern keeper could not serve one person more than half a pint of wine at a time. Nobody could sit drinking for more than half an hour, or stay after nine at night.

A person found drunk in Massachusetts paid a fine of 10 shillings. The law defined drunk as unable to think clearly, which showed in the way the person talked or moved. A person who could not pay the fine was put in the stocks for up to three hours. The stocks were a wooden frame that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place, where everyone passing could see.

The lawmakers also set prices. In 1634 an innkeeper in Boston could charge sixpence for a meal and a penny for a quart of beer. If he charged more, he paid a fine of 10 shillings. A traveller named John Josselyn reported that a constable in a Boston tavern would stop a stranger from ordering more drink than the constable thought right.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" name="Samuel Cole" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Cole

> **Who:** A maker of sugar-coated sweets who kept the earliest tavern in Boston known to have had a license.
> **When and where:** Boston, in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1634 to 1638.

Samuel Cole kept the earliest tavern in Boston known to have had a license. The historian Samuel Adams Drake, writing in 1886, dates its opening to 4 March 1634. A tavern was then called an ordinary.

Cole was a comfit-maker, a maker of sugar-coated sweets. He came to Boston in 1630, in the fleet of ships that brought Governor John Winthrop. His ordinary stood on Washington Street. The house had a brew-house, a building for making beer.

In 1636 Governor Henry Vane made a peace treaty with the Narragansett people at Boston. The Narragansett leader Miantonomi and two sons of the leader Canonicus ate at the governor's house. About twenty other Narragansett men ate their meal at Cole's inn.

In 1638 the colony's court gave Cole permission to sell his house as an inn. He sold it to Robert Sedgwick. Cole died in 1666.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" name="Robert Coles" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Robert Coles

> **Who:** A colonist whom the Massachusetts court punished again and again for drunkenness, the last time by making him wear a red letter D.
> **When and where:** Roxbury and Agawam, now Ipswich, in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1631 to 1634.

The Massachusetts court punished Robert Coles for drunkenness four times between 1631 and 1634. The last time, in 1634, the judges ordered him to wear a red letter D around his neck for a year.

Coles was born in England in about 1600. He came to Massachusetts in 1630 with Governor John Winthrop's fleet. In 1631 the colony's judges fined him five marks for being drunk on the ship *Friendship* and at Winnisimmet, now Chelsea. The *Friendship* was carrying a spiced wine made from honey.

In 1633, at Agawam, the judges fined him £10. He had been drunk, had gotten John Shatswell's wife to drink, and had urged her to be unfaithful to her husband. They also made him stand in public with a paper on his back. On it the word drunkard was written in large letters.

In 1634 he was drunk again, at Roxbury. The judges ordered him to wear a D made of red cloth, sewn onto a white patch, hanging from his neck over his clothes. He had to wear it for a year, whenever he was among other people. They also took away his right to vote. Governor Winthrop wrote in his journal that Coles, "having been oft punished for drunkenness, was now ordered to wear a red D about his neck for a year."

About two months later, the judges gave back his right to vote. No court charged him with drunkenness again. The Roxbury church later put him out of its membership.

Coles moved to Providence, a town built on land that Roger Williams got in 1636 from the Narragansett leaders Canonicus and Miantonomi. In 1640 Coles helped write the Providence Combination, a written plan for governing the town. He died in Warwick, Rhode Island, in 1655.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-coles-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Selling alcohol to Native nations" -->
In September 1609, Henry Hudson's ship lay near what is now Albany, in the land of the Mahican people. There Hudson and his mate took some Mahican leaders into the ship's cabin. They gave them "so much Wine and Aqua vitae," a strong liquor like brandy, that the men got drunk. Robert Juet, who kept a journal on the ship, wrote why. Hudson and the mate wanted to find out whether the leaders were planning "treacherie," a plan to betray them. One of the men got drunk and "could not tell how to take it."

A regular trade in alcohol began around 1650. English traders sold rum to Native nations, and French traders sold brandy, in exchange for furs. The historian Peter C. Mancall has studied this trade. He writes that young men who controlled the furs from a season's hunt traded them for liquor, and more families became poor.

Native and colonial witnesses both wrote down what happened when men drank the liquor. Drunken men fought each other and their own families. Some fell into fires or off cliffs, and some drowned. Some killed other people. No one counted the deaths.

The leaders of the colonies passed laws against the trade. In New Netherland, the Dutch colony that is now New York, Director General Willem Kieft and his council banned selling liquor to Native people on 18 June 1643. Their order said that "large quantities of strong liquors are daily sold to the Indians." They passed the ban again in 1645. In 1647 Director General Petrus Stuyvesant passed it a third time and raised the fine to 500 guilders. The laws of the colonies name the officials who banned the trade. They rarely name the traders who broke the bans.

Native people organized against the trade themselves. Mancall writes that they complained to colonial officials about the traders who sold it. Some colonists came to believe that Native people were born less able to handle alcohol than other people. Mancall states that no evidence supports that belief.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco and rum" -->
In 1604 King James I of England attacked smoking in *A Counterblaste to Tobacco*. He called it "lothsome to the eye, hatefull to the Nose, harmefull to the braine, dangerous to the Lungs." He also called Native Americans "barbarous and beastly" for starting the habit. Those words show the king's contempt for Native people.

The tobacco that the Powhatan people grew tasted bitter to the English. John Rolfe, a colonist at Jamestown who smoked heavily, got seeds of a milder Spanish tobacco from Trinidad and Caracas. He was growing it by July 1612. In 1617 the colonists shipped 20,000 pounds of it to England. In 1622 they grew 60,000 pounds. After 1622 English planters took over land that the Powhatan had already cleared, to grow more tobacco.

In 1633 Massachusetts officials told constables to report "tobacco takers" as idle people. Lawmakers there later banned smoking within about 330 feet of any house, barn or haystack, because a pipe could start a fire. They also banned smoking in the public rooms of an inn. They passed these rules in 1638 and again in 1647.

Rum is a strong liquor made from molasses. Molasses is the thick syrup left over when sugar is made from sugarcane. The first known name for rum comes from the island of Barbados, in about 1651. A planter there called it "Kill-Divill" and "a hott hellish and terrible liquor." In the 1600s New England merchants began shipping fish and lumber to the islands of the West Indies. They traded them for molasses and made the molasses into rum at home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, rum was the colonists' favorite strong drink, and it was cheap. In Philadelphia in 1740, a gallon of rum made in the colonies cost 1 shilling 8 pence. People drank in taverns, and they also met there to hear the news and do the town's business. Colony leaders controlled drinking with tavern licenses and with rules for elections.

In Georgia, the colony's leaders banned strong liquor for seven years, from 1735 to 1742. A Yamacraw leader named Tomochichi had asked them to stop the sale of rum. Nobody counted how much colonists drank in these years. The first estimates come from the 1770s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rum, molasses and tobacco" -->
Colonial distillers made rum from molasses shipped in from the sugar plantations of the Caribbean. Some of the molasses came in legally, and some was smuggled. In Philadelphia in 1740, a gallon of colonial rum cost 1 shilling 8 pence. A gallon of better rum from the Caribbean cost 2 shillings 5 pence.

At the urging of large sugar planters on the British islands in the Caribbean, members of the British Parliament passed the Molasses Act in 1733. It put a tax of sixpence a gallon on molasses from the French islands. Colonial merchants kept smuggling French molasses anyway, much of it from Saint Domingue, now Haiti. The tax should have brought in 25,000 pounds a year. In 1735 British officials collected 259 pounds.

Rum was part of a trade in people. New England ships carried rum to Africa, captive Africans to the West Indies, and molasses back to New England. Distillers made an extra-strong rum for this trade, so that ships could carry more of it in less space. When the ships reached Africa, the captains added water to the rum. They traded it for captive people.

In 1709 Virginia planters grew 29 million pounds of tobacco, the most the colony ever grew. By 1700, enslaved Africans outnumbered white indentured servants on Virginia's tobacco farms. An indentured servant was a worker bound to work for a master for a set number of years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinks for voters" -->
In colonial Virginia, only white men aged 21 or older who owned land could vote. Enslaved people, free Black people, Native people, servants, white men with no land and all women could not vote. Each voter said his choice out loud in front of the crowd. Voters expected the men running for office to give them liquor and food.

A Virginia law of 1705 limited these gifts, called treats. Candidates could give treats before the sheriff read out the notice of an election, and again after the voting. The weeks in between were supposed to be "dry," with no treats. Some candidates got around the rule. They stayed away and had a friend, a servant or even a wife hand out the drinks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tavern as the town's meeting place" -->
In Boston, much of the town's business happened in taverns. Francis Holmes kept the Bunch of Grapes on King Street, now State Street, from 1712. One traveller called it the best punch house in Boston. On 30 July 1733, Henry Price, a Boston tailor, organized a grand lodge of Freemasons there. The Freemasons were a society of men who met in lodges.

Luke Vardy kept the Royal Exchange tavern. In the winter of 1747, Boston's town-house burned. The members of the colony's legislature then finished their meetings at Vardy's.

A quarrel at Vardy's ended in a killing. In July 1728 two young men quarrelled at Vardy's over their cards or their wine. They fought with swords on Boston Common, and one of them killed the other, a man named Woodbridge. The killer's friends put him on a ship that sailed at once. The historian Samuel Adams Drake, who told the story in 1886, does not give the killer's name.

In 1745 Britain was at war with France. Notices posted in Boston told sailors who wanted to join a privateer, a privately owned ship allowed to attack enemy ships, to report to the Widow Gray's Crown Tavern. Women kept several of Boston's taverns. Drake's list includes the Widow Gray, the Widow Anna Swords, a Mrs. Wardwell and a Mrs. Glover.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->


<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Georgia bans rum, 1735 to 1742" -->
In February 1733 James Oglethorpe and the first colonists of Georgia settled on Yamacraw Bluff, where Savannah stands today. Tomochichi, the leader of a Yamacraw town of about 200 Creek and Yamasee people, gave them the land on the bluff.

Tomochichi saw more of his people drinking heavily. In 1734 he asked Oglethorpe and the Trustees to ban the sale of rum. The Trustees were a board of men in London who ran the colony. In 1735 the Trustees banned rum and brandy in Georgia, and King George II and his council approved the law. The law described these liquors as "hurtful and pernicious to Man's Body." Pernicious means harmful. The Trustees named an officer to enforce the ban and set fines that grew larger with each offense. Taverns could still sell beer and wine with a license.

Colonists soon accused the officials of corruption and protested the ban. A group called the Malcontents argued that the ban hurt trade. They said Georgia merchants needed rum to buy timber and other goods from the British islands in the Caribbean. On 29 September 1742 the Trustees ended the ban. Oglethorpe disagreed with their decision.

Traders from South Carolina kept carrying rum up the Savannah River into Native towns the whole time the ban lasted. In a letter of 1737, Oglethorpe wrote that the Creek had learned drunkenness "from our Traders."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-physician-drugs-alcohol" name="Dr. Alexander Hamilton" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Dr. Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** A Scottish-born doctor from Annapolis, Maryland, who kept a diary of the taverns and drinking clubs he saw on a long ride north. He is not the Alexander Hamilton who later ran the Treasury.
> **When and where:** From Maryland to York, Maine, and back, 30 May to 27 September 1744.

Dr. Alexander Hamilton kept a diary in 1744 that records how people drank in the taverns of the colonies. He rode 1,624 miles, from Maryland to Maine and back.

Hamilton was born near Edinburgh, Scotland, in 1712. In 1738 he moved to Maryland and worked as a doctor in Annapolis. He was very sick in 1743. On 30 May 1744 he set out on horseback, mainly to get his health back. He reached home on 27 September.

On 31 May, at Tradaway's inn in Maryland, he met "a drunken Club" leaving. Most of the men had climbed onto their horses and sat on them crooked. On 5 June, at an inn near Philadelphia, the landlady did all the work because the landlord was drunk. Hamilton wrote that the liquor had taken away "the use of his tongue." The landlord sat without moving in a corner, smoking his pipe.

On 15 June he ate supper in New York with the Hungarian Club at Todd's tavern. After supper the men drank "bumpers," glasses filled to the brim and drunk off at once, at each round. Hamilton did not want to drink and drank only three. He went home at ten, "pretty well flushed." Some of the men, he wrote, thought nothing was finer than being able "to pour down seas of liquor, and remain unconquered, while others sank under the table." He wrote that some members were "very fond of making a stranger drunk."

In Boston he met with a club of doctors at the Sun Tavern. "We drank punch, smoaked tobacco, and talked of sundry physical matters," he wrote. Physical matters meant medicine. In 1745 he started a club of his own in Annapolis, the Tuesday Club. He died on 11 May 1756.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1770 the colonies had more than 140 rum distilleries, making about 4.8 million gallons of rum a year. Ship captains from Rhode Island carried rum to Africa and traded it for captive people. After the Revolution, Americans switched from rum to whiskey made from their own corn and rye.

In 1791 members of Congress voted a tax on whiskey. In 1794 armed men in western Pennsylvania burned a tax inspector's house, and President George Washington called up nearly 13,000 militiamen against them. In 1785 the Philadelphia doctor Benjamin Rush argued that heavy drinking was a disease. Native leaders asked colonists to stop selling them liquor. In 1799 the Seneca prophet Handsome Lake stopped drinking and preached against it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much people drank" -->
Nobody measured how much colonists drank before the 1770s. Historians have made estimates since then, and each one measures something different. One historian estimates that in the 1770s a grown man drank up to three pints of rum a week. Another estimate gives 3.7 gallons of rum a year for each colonist by the time of the Revolution. The historian Peter C. Mancall cites an estimate of seven shots of liquor a day for each colonist by 1770.

The first government figures come from 1790. That year, the average American over the age of 15 drank 34 gallons of beer and cider, 5 gallons of liquor and 1 gallon of wine.

People believed alcohol was good for the body. They took whiskey for stomach pain and for a sore throat. They took hot brandy punch for cholera, a disease that causes severe diarrhea. Women in labor were given a drink of liquor to ease the pain. Benjamin Franklin collected more than 200 words that meant drunk.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rum and the slave trade" -->
By 1764 Rhode Island had about 30 rum distilleries, 22 of them in the town of Newport. Rhode Island merchants traded the rum on the coast of West Africa for captive people. In the hundred years before 1807, Rhode Islanders made at least 1,000 slaving voyages. They carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery.

In 1764 the brothers Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown of Providence sent their ship *Sally* to West Africa. Its captain was Esek Hopkins. The ship carried 17,274 gallons of rum to trade, along with chains, shackles and guns. Hopkins used the rum to buy 196 people. When the captives rose up against the crew, Hopkins's men fired on them. The ship's account book records that the crew "Destroyed 8," which means they killed eight people. At least 109 of the 196 captives died.

The same year, members of the British Parliament cut the tax on foreign molasses to threepence a gallon, in a law called the Sugar Act. Under it, British navy officers who caught smugglers could seize the goods and keep half.

During the Revolution, British warships stopped much of the shipping, and molasses became costly and hard to get. Scotch-Irish farmers in Pennsylvania, Maryland, western Virginia and western North Carolina made whiskey from rye and corn instead. Before 1800, distillers in Kentucky were shipping whiskey down the rivers to New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native leaders against the liquor trade" -->
In 1754 Hagler, a leader of the Catawba people in the Carolina piedmont, spoke to officials sent by North Carolina. He told them, "You Rot Your grain in Tubs, out of which you take and make Strong Spirits." Hagler and other Native leaders asked the colonists to stop the liquor trade, because it brought violence and despair to their towns.

The British official in charge wanted the trade to go on. Sir William Johnson was the British superintendent of Indian affairs for the northern colonies. In 1764 he wrote to his superiors in London that rum could cause problems. He also wrote that "the Trade will never be so extensive" without rum. After the Revolution, traders carried liquor farther west. They added water to it so they had more to sell.

In 1779 United States troops burned the villages of the Haudenosaunee, also called the Iroquois, in New York. After the war the Haudenosaunee lost most of their land in New York and Pennsylvania. Many of them drank heavily. One was Handsome Lake, a Seneca man from the Genesee River. In 1799, sick after many years of heavy drinking, he had visions. Soon after, he stopped drinking and began preaching against drunkenness. His teaching became the Longhouse Religion, which Haudenosaunee people still practice today.

### Benjamin Rush

> **Who:** A Philadelphia doctor who signed the Declaration of Independence and wrote that heavy drinking was a disease.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1785 to 1790.

Benjamin Rush argued that heavy drinking was a disease and an addiction. Before him, most Americans thought a drunkard simply lacked the will to stop. Rush was a Philadelphia doctor, and he had signed the Declaration of Independence.

In 1785 he published *An Inquiry into the Effects of Ardent Spirits Upon the Human Body and Mind*. Some sources date the first printing to 1784. Ardent spirits meant strong liquor such as rum, gin and whiskey.

Rush wrote that a person begins drinking by free choice, and the habit then becomes a need. In his words: "The use of strong drink is at first the effect of free agency. From habit it takes place from necessity." He said the only cure was to stop drinking completely. His advice to heavy drinkers was "Taste not, handle not."

An edition of 1790 added a chart that Rush called "A Moral and Physical Thermometer." Water and milk stood at the top. Lower down, punch led to "idleness, sickness and debt." At the bottom, gin, brandy and rum taken every day led to "murder, madness" and the gallows.

Few people changed their drinking at the time. The historian Mark Lender says that "the conception we have of addiction today can generally be traced back to Benjamin Rush."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whiskey as money, and the Whiskey Rebellion" -->
In western Pennsylvania, farmers turned their grain into whiskey, and they used the whiskey as money. They farmed land that the Shawnee, Delaware and Mingo peoples had used. In 1768, at Fort Stanwix in New York, representatives of the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee had signed that land over to Britain. The roads over the mountains to the east were bad, and grain spoiled on the way. Whiskey kept, and it was easier to carry. Coins were scarce on the frontier, so people paid for goods and paid their workers in whiskey.

In January 1791 Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton asked for a tax on whiskey made in the United States, to pay the debt from the Revolutionary War. Members of Congress voted it into law. The law specified a tax of 7 cents a gallon. Large distillers could instead pay a yearly fee for each still, which came to as little as 6 cents a gallon. A farmer with a small still paid about 9 cents a gallon. The tax had to be paid in coin, which the farmers rarely had.

On 6 September 1791, a gang in disguise tarred and feathered Robert Johnson, a new tax collector, in Washington County, Pennsylvania. Some records give the date as 11 September. Tarring and feathering meant pouring hot tar on a person's skin and then sticking feathers to the tar. The records do not describe Johnson's injuries. When officials sent a man to serve court papers on Johnson's attackers, another gang whipped him and tarred and feathered him too.

On 15 July 1794, John Neville, the regional tax inspector, went with a federal marshal, David Lenox, to serve court papers. At a farm about 10 miles south of Pittsburgh, men fired warning shots at them. The next day, about 30 men from the Mingo Creek militia surrounded Neville's house, called Bower Hill. Neville shot and killed one of them, Oliver Miller. Enslaved people on Neville's land helped him hold the house.

On 17 July the men came back, this time between 400 and 600 of them. Their leader was Major James McFarlane, who had fought in the Revolution. After about an hour of shooting, McFarlane called a stop. When he stepped into the open, someone in the house shot and killed him. The records do not say who fired. The attackers burned Neville's house and the quarters where the enslaved people lived. How many men died at Bower Hill is not clear. The dead included McFarlane, one or two other militiamen, and perhaps one soldier.

Washington called up 12,950 militiamen from New Jersey, Maryland, Virginia and Pennsylvania. He rode with them as far as Bedford, Pennsylvania, then went back to Philadelphia. He left Henry Lee, the governor of Virginia, in command, and Hamilton went along as an adviser. The rebels scattered without a battle. About 12 soldiers died of illness or accidents.

On the night of 13 November 1794, soldiers under Lee broke into houses and pulled people from their beds. They made no difference between rebels and witnesses. They drove the prisoners barefoot, in their nightclothes, over muddy roads and through streams. They held them in animal pens and in cellars with no floors. Some of the prisoners' health was ruined, and at least one prisoner died. People there called it "the Dreadful Night." About 150 men were arrested. The records do not name the officers who led each raid.

On Christmas Day 1794, soldiers paraded 20 of the prisoners down Broad Street in Philadelphia, as trophies of the march. Ten of them were tried for treason, the crime of making war against one's own country. Two were convicted. In 1802 members of Congress repealed the whiskey tax.

### Philip Vigol

> **Who:** A man from western Pennsylvania, one of the first two Americans convicted of treason against the United States, for attacking whiskey-tax officers.
> **When and where:** Westmoreland and Fayette counties, Pennsylvania, 1794. Philadelphia, 1795.

Philip Vigol and John Mitchell were the first two Americans convicted of treason against the United States. Vigol's crime was attacking two officers who collected the whiskey tax. The court report spells his name Vigol. Other records spell it Wigle, Weigel or Wigal.

In 1794 Vigol rode with an armed party, carrying guns and beating drums, to the house of Reigan, a tax officer in Westmoreland County. They forced Reigan to hand over his official papers. They made him swear never to collect the tax again.

The same party then rode to the house of Wells, the tax officer in Fayette County. They swore to destroy Wells and his house. Wells had run and hidden. The men searched his house and then burned it, with everything inside, including the tax records. Later they found Wells. They locked him up and made him swear to quit his job.

In 1795 a federal jury in Philadelphia found Vigol guilty of treason. He was sentenced to be hanged. At the trial, Justice William Paterson told the jury that fear excused a crime only when a person's life was in real danger at that moment.

In November 1795 President Washington pardoned Vigol and Mitchell. One source gives the date as 2 November and another as 14 November. In his yearly message to Congress, Washington wrote, "The misled have abandoned their errors."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="George Washington's rum and whiskey" -->
George Washington used alcohol to win votes, to keep soldiers working and to pay the people he enslaved. At the end of his life he owned one of the largest whiskey distilleries in the country.

In 1758 Washington ran for the Virginia House of Burgesses, the colony's elected lawmaking body, in Frederick County. He gave the voters 28 gallons of rum, 50 gallons of rum punch and 82 gallons of wine, beer and cider. He won the seat.

In June 1779, as commander of the Continental Army, he wrote that he wanted "a pretty good stock of rum" at the forts for soldiers on work duty. At his plantation, Mount Vernon, he gave the people he enslaved an allowance of liquor in exchange for a day's work. Once he accused his overseer, James Butler, of handing out 36 pints of rum. Butler answered that he had given "no more than a pint of rum & a pound of Meat to each man."

In 1797 his farm manager, James Anderson, talked him into building a whiskey distillery at Mount Vernon. It made 600 gallons that first year. In 1799 it made almost 11,000 gallons, worth $7,500. The average distillery in Virginia made about 650 gallons. Anderson's son, John, ran the distillery with an assistant distiller and six enslaved men. Their names were Hanson, Peter, Nat, Daniel, James and Timothy. Washington died in December 1799.
