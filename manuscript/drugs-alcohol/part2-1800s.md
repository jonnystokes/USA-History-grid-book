## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 9 minutes**

Americans of the early 1800s drank almost three times as much alcohol as Americans do now, and the first big campaign against drinking began. The average American of drinking age drank 7.1 gallons of pure alcohol in 1830, against 2.48 gallons in 2023. People drank at breakfast, in the fields and at work.

Ministers and reformers began asking people in 1826 to sign a pledge, a written promise not to drink, and more than a million signed. By the 1840s and 1850s drinking had fallen to about 3.5 gallons, half the 1830 figure.

**Other drugs.** Anyone could buy laudanum, a painkiller made of opium and alcohol, without a doctor's order, and doctors gave it to adults and to children. Among Native nations, leaders such as Little Turtle of the Miami asked members of Congress to stop the traders who sold liquor to their people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A dram with breakfast" -->
Men, women and even children in the early 1800s drank "a healthful dram" with breakfast. A dram was a small drink of liquor. Farmers took cider, beer or whiskey into the fields. Employers stopped work in the middle of the morning so their workers could drink. When people got sick, a doctor's first medicine was often a drink of alcohol.

**Counting the alcohol.** Beer, cider, whiskey and rum hold different amounts of alcohol, so the usual way to compare them is to count only the pure alcohol inside the drinks. By the count of the historian W. J. Rorabaugh, Americans of drinking age drank an average of 5.8 gallons of pure alcohol each in 1790 and 7.1 gallons in 1830. In 2023 Americans aged 14 and older drank an average of 2.48 gallons each, by the count of the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism.

Drinking fell to about 3.5 gallons of pure alcohol a person each year between 1840 and 1860. Those were the years when the temperance societies were most active.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Temperance and the pledge" -->
People in Connecticut, Virginia and New York had formed temperance societies, groups that urged drinking little or no alcohol, by 1808. Lyman Beecher, a minister in Connecticut, began giving talks against all drinking of liquor in 1825.

**The American Temperance Society.** Reformers in Boston founded the American Temperance Society on February 13, 1826. Its members signed a pledge not to drink liquor, such as whiskey and rum. The pledge still allowed beer, wine and alcohol taken as medicine. Within five years the society had 2,220 local groups and 170,000 members. By the late 1830s it had more than 8,000 groups and more than 1,250,000 members.

**No alcohol at all.** The reformers split in the late 1830s. Many moved to total abstinence, which means no alcohol at all, not even beer or wine. Some stopped trying to talk people out of drinking and began asking lawmakers to ban the sale of liquor.

**The first laws.** Massachusetts lawmakers banned selling hard liquor, such as whiskey and rum, in amounts of less than 15 gallons in 1838. Only people with money could buy 15 gallons at once, so under this law only the rich could buy hard liquor. The lawmakers canceled the law within two years. In the 1840s, lawmakers in many states let voters in each town or county decide on liquor licenses, the papers from the government that allowed a person to sell liquor.

**The Navy's ration.** Sailors in the Navy got a daily ration of liquor, a set share handed out each day. Almost 400 people signed a petition, a paper people sign to ask for something, to members of Congress in 1843, asking them to end it. The petition was more than 10 feet long.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Washingtonians, 1840" -->
Six drinkers met at Chase's Tavern on Liberty Street in Baltimore on April 2, 1840, and began keeping each other sober. They were William K. Mitchell, John F. Hoss, David Anderson, George Steers, James McCurley and Archibald Campbell. They called their group the Washington Temperance Society.

**Telling stories.** The members stayed sober by telling each other the stories of their own drinking. Then they went looking for other drinkers and told them the same stories. Estimates of how many people joined run from tens of thousands up to 600,000. Within a few years the group faded away.

**Lincoln's speech.** Abraham Lincoln, then 33 years old, spoke to the Washingtonians of Springfield, Illinois, on February 22, 1842. He said that people had blamed and scolded drinkers, and the men who sold them liquor, too much, and that this was both unwise and unfair. "If you would win a man to your cause," he said, "first convince him that you are his sincere friend." He also said that "a drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall," a bitter liquid.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-b-gough-drugs-alcohol" name="John B. Gough" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John B. Gough

> **Who:** An English-born bookbinder, a worker who sews and covers books, whose drinking cost him his work. His wife and baby died while he was drunk. He signed the temperance pledge and spent his life asking others to sign it.
> **When and where:** New York, and Worcester, Massachusetts, 1829 to 1845.

**Sent out to buy two quarts of rum, he drank them himself.**

**Drinking.** John B. Gough was born in Sandgate, England, in 1817, and came to America at the age of 12, landing in New York in August 1829. He learned bookbinding in New York City. After his mother died, he began drinking heavily. He lost one job after another and sang comic songs in theaters.

**Delirium tremens.** Once, when he stopped drinking all at once, he had delirium tremens, an illness of heavy drinkers who suddenly stop. The body shakes and the mind is confused, and the person sees things that are not there. The illness can kill. In *An Autobiography*, printed in 1845, Gough wrote: "Hideous faces appeared on the walls, and on the ceiling, and on the floors." He saw "millions of monstrous spiders" around him.

**His wife.** When his wife fell sick, the women nursing her sent him to buy two quarts of rum. He drank it himself. Ten days later his wife and their baby both died. Gough wrote that the whole town learned "that my wife and child were lying dead, and that I was drunk!"

**The pledge.** In Worcester, Massachusetts, in late October 1842, a stranger touched his shoulder and asked, "Why do you not sign the pledge?" The man was Joel Stratton, a waiter at the town's temperance hotel. The next evening Gough told his story to a meeting at the Worcester town hall. Then he signed the pledge.

About five months later he drank again, and then he signed the pledge a second time, in public. Between May 1843 and January 1845, he wrote, he traveled more than 12,000 miles and gave 605 talks. At those talks, 31,760 people signed the pledge.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-b-gough-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whiskey and enslaved people at Christmas" -->
Frederick Douglass, who was enslaved in Maryland in the 1830s, wrote in his book of 1845 that slaveholders used whiskey to control the people they enslaved. Enslaved people had the days between Christmas and New Year's off work. Slaveholders wanted them to spend those days drunk.

"It was deemed (thought to be) a disgrace not to get drunk at Christmas," Douglass wrote. Slaveholders made "bets on their slaves, as to who can drink the most whisky without getting drunk." In this way, Douglass wrote, they got "whole multitudes to drink to excess," which means far too much.

**Why they did it.** Douglass wrote that the slaveholders did this to keep enslaved people from rising up in an armed revolt. He wrote that he and the others felt "that we had almost as well be slaves to man as to rum."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor and Native nations" -->
Little Turtle, also called Michikinikwa, was a leader of the Miami people. He urged members of Congress to stop traders from selling liquor to Native nations. Congress members gave the president the power to stop those sales in 1802. War Department officials then made rules that banned traders from bringing liquor onto Native land, and did little to enforce them.

**The traders.** Traders kept selling because they made more money on liquor than on other goods. They also watered it down. Members of Congress made it a crime in 1834, with a fine of up to $300, to bring liquor onto Native land.

**Native leaders.** Native leaders worked against drinking among their own people. The Shawnee religious leader Tenskwatawa and a Delaware woman named Beate told their followers to give up alcohol. Pawnee leaders limited drinking in their towns.

Leaders of the Mohican-Stockbridge people wrote to Congress in 1827. They had been living on part of the Oneida Reservation in New York. They wrote that one reason they had agreed to move to what is now Wisconsin was to "flee from" liquor in their community. The land in Wisconsin was the homeland of the Menominee people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opium sold as medicine" -->
Opium is a drug made from the opium poppy. It stops pain and brings sleep, and a person who takes it every day comes to need it. Laudanum was opium dissolved in alcohol. Until the early 1900s, anyone could buy laudanum without a doctor's order.

**For colds and for babies.** Doctors gave laudanum for colds, for heart disease and for pain, to adults and to children. Nurses fed it to babies with a spoon. Patients having surgery in the late 1700s and early 1800s got laudanum and alcohol first. Then the surgeon's helpers held them down while the surgeon cut.

**Morphine.** A German druggist named Friedrich Sertürner took a drug called morphine out of opium in 1804. In 1817 he tested morphine on himself and three boys, and all four nearly died. A German pharmacy that later became the drug company Merck began making morphine for sale in 1827.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chewing tobacco" -->
The English writer Charles Dickens visited the United States in 1842. In his book *American Notes*, he complained about how common it was in Washington to chew tobacco and spit out the juice. In the courts of law, he wrote, "the judge has his spittoon, the crier his, the witness his, and the prisoner his." A spittoon is a bowl for spitting into, and the crier was the officer who called the court to order.

In hospitals, Dickens saw signs asking medical students to spit their tobacco juice into boxes and not onto the stairs. On a steamboat, he watched two young men chew and spit on the deck for a quarter of an hour before breakfast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 16 minutes**

Lawmakers in thirteen states banned liquor between 1851 and 1855, while drugstores went on selling opium, morphine and cocaine openly in medicine bottles. Maine's lawmakers were the first of the thirteen, in 1851. In Portland, Maine, in 1855, soldiers fired on a crowd at the mayor's order and killed a young sailor. In the Civil War, surgeons gave wounded soldiers morphine and opium, and thousands of veterans came home addicted.

**Medicine and the law.** Their medicines held opium, morphine, cocaine (a drug made from coca leaves) and alcohol, and the labels did not have to say so. City, state and federal lawmakers passed laws against opium. A federal law of 1887 named Chinese people outright, and accounts differ about whom San Francisco's opium law of 1875 was aimed at.

**Women against the saloon.** Women marched into saloons to pray, and in 1874 they founded the Woman's Christian Temperance Union. In 1880 Kansas voters approved a ban on liquor in their state constitution, the state's highest set of laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Maine Law and the Portland Rum Riot" -->
Lawmakers in Maine banned the sale of all alcoholic drinks on June 2, 1851. The law still allowed alcohol for medicine and for use in factories. Neal Dow, the mayor of Portland, had helped write it. By 1855, lawmakers in twelve more states had passed bans of their own. Judges threw out some of these laws, and lawmakers passed them again.

**Dow and the Irish.** For years Dow had ordered police raids on saloons in Portland. A saloon was a bar, mostly for men, that sold drinks by the glass. Irish immigrants ran many of the saloons Dow raided, and he gave speeches about "rum-swilling foreigners." About one in nine of Portland's 21,000 people was an Irish immigrant in 1850.

**The liquor at City Hall.** Dow was mayor again in 1855. He had city officials buy $1,600 worth of liquor for medicine and for use in workshops and factories, and he stored it at City Hall. The law required a city committee to make such a purchase, and no committee had been named.

Under the Maine Law, any three voters could ask a judge for a search warrant, a paper from a judge that allows a search. Three men got a warrant to search City Hall on June 2, 1855. The police would not let them in. By evening a crowd of between 1,000 and 3,000 people, most of them Irish, stood outside. Some threw stones at the door.

**The shooting.** Dow called out the militia, a force of part-time soldiers, and ordered them to fire. John Robbins, a 22-year-old sailor from Deer Isle, Maine, broke a hole in the door of the room where the liquor was kept. The soldiers shot him dead. He had planned to marry the next day. The soldiers kept firing as the crowd ran and wounded seven more people. The New England Historical Society's account of the riot does not name the soldiers who fired or their officers.

Dow was tried for buying the liquor improperly and found not guilty. Voters turned him out of office, and Maine lawmakers ended the ban in 1856.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the Civil War made thousands of addicts" -->
Soldiers in the Civil War were given whiskey as a "stimulant," a drink to give them energy. Members of Congress put a tax on liquor and tobacco in 1862 to help pay for the war. Navy officials ended the sailors' daily ration of liquor on September 1, 1862.

**The needle.** Doctors had begun using a new tool just before the war, between 1853 and 1855. The hypodermic syringe is a hollow needle for pushing a drug under the skin, and with it doctors gave more morphine. In the war, surgeons gave morphine shots and opium pills to soldiers with gunshot wounds and to soldiers whose arms or legs they had cut off. Diarrhea spread through the army camps, and doctors gave opium for that too.

**Withdrawal.** Morphine and opium stop pain, and a person who keeps taking them comes to need them. When the drug stops, the body goes into withdrawal, the sickness that comes when the body loses a drug it depends on. The person sweats, aches and cannot sleep. Later come stomach cramps, diarrhea, sick stomach and vomiting.

**"Opium slavery."** Thousands of veterans came home addicted, and they called it "opium slavery." Addicted veterans found little sympathy. Many of them could not get medical care, and many died of accidental overdoses. An overdose is too much of a drug at once, enough to make a person very sick or kill them.

**Not known:** how many veterans were addicted. The historian Jonathan S. Jones, who read their letters and records, says there were thousands.
**Disputed:** a claim that 400,000 veterans came home with a "soldier's disease" of morphine addiction. The earliest known use of the phrase "soldier's disease" is from 1915, fifty years after the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-tackett-goolrick-drugs-alcohol" name="John Tackett Goolrick" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Tackett Goolrick

> **Who:** A soldier for the Confederacy, the Southern states that broke away to keep slavery, in a unit that fired cannons, whose family's letters record his 30 years of morphine addiction.
> **When and where:** Petersburg and Richmond, Virginia, 1864. Fredericksburg, Virginia, 1865 to 1925.

**His family locked him in a farmhouse room for weeks.**

**The wound.** John Tackett Goolrick served in the Fredericksburg Artillery in Robert E. Lee's army, and he strongly supported the Confederacy's fight to keep slavery. In August 1864, at the siege of Petersburg, an attack in which an army surrounded the city to force it to give up, a bullet hit him in the left leg.

He was taken to Chimborazo, a hospital in Richmond. Jones, who read the family's letters, writes that surgeons there apparently gave him morphine for the pain. At Chimborazo, or in the years after, he became addicted. He stayed in the army until Lee surrendered at Appomattox in April 1865.

**The breakdown.** After the war he worked as a lawyer in Fredericksburg. His wife was Frances Bernard Goolrick. He took larger and larger doses of morphine. In 1896 he broke down and could no longer work, and the family ran short of money.

Frances told him that she would divorce him unless he gave up morphine completely. On February 5, 1896, she wrote to her brother, "I can bear neither for myself or the children, this life any longer."

**The locked room.** The family could have sent him to a state hospital for the mentally ill, which took addicted veterans. His brother wrote that sending him there would be "commented upon and asked about" in the newspapers. So the family took him to his brother's farmhouse outside Fredericksburg instead. They locked him in a room, and nurses they paid kept him there for several weeks while he went through withdrawal. His sister-in-law Nora wrote on March 10, 1896, "my heart aches to look at him."

Goolrick got well and went home. The family's letters suggest that he went back to morphine several times before he died in 1925.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-tackett-goolrick-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was in the medicine bottle?" -->
Many medicines sold in stores held opium, morphine, cocaine or alcohol, and some held mercury and lead, which are poisons. These were patent medicines, sold under a brand name. Their makers did not have to tell buyers what was in them.

**A syrup for babies.** Jeremiah Curtis and Benjamin A. Perkins began selling Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup in 1849, as a medicine for babies who were teething. Each ounce held about 65 milligrams of morphine. One teaspoon held enough morphine to kill a young child. Writers for the American Medical Association, a group of doctors, called it a "baby killer" in 1911. No record gives a count of the children who died after taking it.

**A medicine for women.** Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound was sold to women from the 1870s. It was 18 to 20 percent alcohol. The label said the alcohol was there only to dissolve the other ingredients and to keep the medicine from spoiling. Critics pointed out that women who had signed temperance pledges took it.

**Who was addicted.** The historian David T. Courtwright writes that people addicted to opium drugs in these years were most often middle-class or rich women who had started taking morphine for an illness. Surveys in the late 1800s found that two out of every three people addicted to laudanum or morphine were women. There was also a "hard core" of opium smokers, mainly Chinese workers and white criminals.

Courtwright writes that, for the size of the population, addiction to these drugs was at least as common in the late 1800s as in 1992. Most of the people who took these medicines left no record of what happened to them.

**Cocaine.** Cocaine came to America as a medicine in 1884, and doctors used it to numb the eye for surgery. Soon there were reports of doctors and druggists addicted to it. Oregon lawmakers banned cocaine in 1887, the first in any state to do so. Lawmakers in Montana, Colorado, Illinois and Massachusetts passed bans by 1898.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-stith-pemberton-drugs-alcohol" name="John Stith Pemberton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Stith Pemberton

> **Who:** A Georgia druggist, addicted to morphine after a war wound, who invented Coca-Cola.
> **When and where:** Columbus and Atlanta, Georgia, 1865 to 1888.

**The first Coca-Cola had cocaine in it and cost five cents.**

**The wound.** John Stith Pemberton had a medical degree and ran a drugstore in Columbus, Georgia. He fought for the Confederacy at the Battle of Columbus in April 1865 and took a sword wound to the chest. He took morphine for the pain and became addicted.

**The drink.** From 1866 he tried to make a painkiller that had no morphine in it. In 1885 he registered Pemberton's French Wine Coca, a wine mixed with coca, at his drugstore in Columbus. A ban on liquor took effect in Atlanta and Fulton County in 1886, so Pemberton made a version of his drink without wine and called it Coca-Cola. Jacob's Pharmacy in Atlanta first sold it on May 8, 1886, for five cents a glass. Pemberton claimed it cured headaches and morphine addiction.

The makers of Coca-Cola stopped using fresh coca leaves in 1903. They took out the last traces of cocaine by 1929.

**The end.** Pemberton was still addicted and poor when he sold his rights to the drink in 1888. He died of stomach cancer on August 16, 1888. His son Charles was addicted to opium. In June 1894 Charles was found unconscious with opium beside him, and he died ten days later, at the age of 40.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-stith-pemberton-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opium laws and Chinese immigrants" -->
Chinese immigrants came to California during the Gold Rush, and some of them brought the habit of smoking opium. Opium dens opened in San Francisco's Chinatown in the 1850s. An opium den was a room where people paid to lie down and smoke opium.

**San Francisco, 1875.** The members of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, the city's lawmakers, made it a crime in 1875 to keep or visit an opium den. Reporters for the *San Francisco Chronicle* wrote that the supervisors acted after they learned that Chinese owners were running dens for white customers. The police chief, Phillip Crowley, said his officers would "keep them from opening places where whites might resort to smoke." His officers often left alone the dens that sold only to Chinese people.

**Why they passed it.** Writers disagree about the reason for the 1875 law. George Fisher, of Stanford Law School, argues that the supervisors wanted to protect white opium smokers. Writers for the Drug Policy Alliance, a group that works to change drug laws, point out that the dens the law closed were mainly in Chinese immigrant neighborhoods.

**A false story.** Irish members of the Church of the Transfiguration, a Catholic church in New York, and its assistant pastor, a priest who helped lead the church, spread a false story in 1883. They said that Chinese men were luring girls as young as 11 and 12 into opium dens. Writers for the *New York Times* called the story "preposterous," which means too silly to believe. About a week later the story was shown to be a hoax, a lie made up on purpose.

**Laws that named race.** Later laws named race and nationality outright. Idaho lawmakers in 1887 set punishments for "every white person" who kept or used an opium den. Fisher calls the early drug laws "laws about whites." In his view, their makers cared only about what drugs did to white people.

In the same year as Idaho's law, members of Congress named Chinese people in a law of their own. They banned any "subject of the Emperor of China" from bringing opium into the United States. By 1896, lawmakers in 22 states had banned opium dens.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Praying in the saloons: the WCTU" -->
Eliza Jane Thompson led groups of women into the saloons of Hillsboro, Ohio, after she heard the lecturer Diocletian Lewis speak against drink on December 23, 1873. The women sang hymns and prayed until the owners closed. Women in 22 other states copied them. In Anoka, Minnesota, a saloon keeper and his wife poured cold water from an upstairs window onto the praying women.

**The WCTU.** Women met in Cleveland in November 1874 and founded the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, or WCTU. Its members worked to ban the making and selling of liquor. Frances Willard led the WCTU from 1879 until she died in 1898. She argued that women needed the vote to protect their homes from drunk men who beat women.

**Black women in the WCTU.** WCTU leaders set up a Department of Work Among Colored People in 1880. "Colored" was a word for Black people at the time. From 1883 Frances Watkins Harper, a Black poet and public speaker, ran it in the North.

Willard let the southern branches follow "states rights" on race, the idea that people in each state should settle such questions for themselves. In the 1880s Willard and the other national leaders let state and local branches form separate groups for Black women and white women. They did it to win over white women in the South, and Willard said women of both races wanted it. In practice, white members could use the separate groups to keep Black women out of their branches.

Harper called the split a short-term measure. She said it should last only until white members in the Deep South gave up "the spirit of caste," their belief that one race ranked above another.

**Ida B. Wells and Willard.** On speaking tours in Britain in 1893 and 1894, the journalist Ida B. Wells said that Willard had stayed silent about lynching, the killing of people by mobs without any trial. Wells said that the only public words Willard had spoken on the subject excused lynching. When people in Britain asked for proof, Wells sent them a copy of an interview Willard had given a New York newspaper in 1890, after a trip to the South.

In it, Willard said that "the colored race multiplies like the locusts of Egypt," comparing Black people to the swarms of insects sent on Egypt in the Bible. She said that "the grog shop (saloon) is its center of power" and that "the safety of woman, of childhood, of the home, is menaced (threatened)."

Defenders of lynching claimed that mobs killed Black men for raping white women, and Wells said Willard's words had been used to support that claim. Willard denied that she had stayed silent. She later spoke against lynching.

**The Anti-Saloon League.** Howard Hyde Russell, a minister, founded the Anti-Saloon League in Oberlin, Ohio, in 1893. Its leaders judged lawmakers only by how they voted on liquor, even if the lawmakers drank themselves. The league became a national group in 1895.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kansas goes dry, 1880" -->
Kansas voters were the first in any state to put a ban on liquor into their state constitution. They elected John St. John, who wanted a ban, as governor in 1878. Kansas lawmakers wrote a ban on making and selling liquor into a change to the constitution, and voters approved it in November 1880.

The ban began on May 1, 1881. Two years later Topeka, the state capital, still had 43 illegal bars. Their owners stayed open by paying a fine of $100 each month. The ban stayed in the Kansas constitution until 1948.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor laws and Native nations" -->
Members of Congress changed the 1834 law against bringing liquor onto Native land in 1854. The change stated that Native people themselves could not be charged under it. In 1895 they made it a crime to sell or give alcohol to anyone in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma, punished by a fine and one month to five years in prison.

**Allotments.** Members of Congress set a punishment of at least 60 days in prison, with no upper limit, in 1897 for giving alcohol to Native people who held allotments under the Dawes Act. Under that law of 1887, federal officials broke up land that a Native nation had held together into plots for single families, called allotments, and sold off the land that was left over. Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887 and about 48 million by 1934.

**Who was in prison.** By 1900, about one in every 20 people in federal prisons had been found guilty under these liquor laws. The count does not say how many of them were Native people. Native people could not be charged with bringing liquor onto Native land. Under the law of 1895, though, anyone who sold or gave alcohol in Indian Territory could be charged, Native people included.

**Native leaders' own rules.** Native religious leaders made their own rules about drink and drugs. The Paiute religious leader Wovoka told followers of the Ghost Dance, a religious movement he led, not to drink. On the Comanche reservation in Oklahoma, Quanah Parker led ceremonies in which people took peyote, the small cactus that causes visions, as a sacred act of worship. He argued for the right to use peyote in this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A gold cure and a cigarette machine" -->
Dr. Leslie Keeley opened a clinic for heavy drinkers in Dwight, Illinois, in 1879. He gave them shots of his medicine four times a day, and other patients got it by mail. He called it the "Gold Cure" and said the shots held "bichloride of gold," a chemical made with gold. By 1900 more than 300,000 people had taken it. Other doctors criticized it.

**The cigarette machine.** A skilled worker rolling by hand could make about four cigarettes a minute. In 1880 James Albert Bonsack of Virginia built a machine that rolled about 200 a minute. James Buchanan Duke signed a deal with Bonsack to use the machines, in 1884 or 1885 by different accounts. By 1890 Duke sold 40 percent of the cigarettes in the country. That year he joined his four biggest rivals with his own business to form the American Tobacco Company.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** By 1900 states were banning liquor and cities were banning opium dens, while anyone could still buy opium, morphine and cocaine in a medicine bottle that did not have to say so.
- 1851: Maine lawmakers ban the sale of all alcoholic drinks. Four years later soldiers in Portland shoot dead a sailor, John Robbins, at the mayor's order.
- 1874: Women in Cleveland found the Woman's Christian Temperance Union.
- 1887: Members of Congress ban any "subject of the Emperor of China" from bringing opium into the country.
**Next:** In 1906 drug makers had to start listing what was in the bottle, and in 1920 a ban on alcohol covered the whole country.
