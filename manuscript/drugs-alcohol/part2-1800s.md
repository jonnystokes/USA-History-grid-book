## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The average American of drinking age drank 7.1 gallons of pure alcohol in 1830. In 2023 the figure was 2.48 gallons. Americans drank at breakfast, in the fields and at work. From 1826, ministers and reformers asked people to sign a pledge, a written promise not to drink. More than a million people signed. Between 1840 and 1860, drinking fell by about half.

Anyone could buy laudanum, a medicine made of opium mixed with alcohol, without a doctor's order. Doctors gave it to adults and to children. Among Native nations, leaders such as Little Turtle of the Miami asked the United States to stop the traders who sold liquor to their people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much Americans drank" -->
Beer, cider, whiskey and rum hold different amounts of alcohol. To compare them, historians count only the pure alcohol inside the drinks. In 1790 Americans of drinking age drank an average of 5.8 gallons of pure alcohol each in a year. By 1830 the figure was 7.1 gallons. These figures come from the historian W. J. Rorabaugh and are printed by the National Archives. In 2023, by the count of the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, Americans aged 14 and older drank an average of 2.48 gallons each.

In the early 1800s, men, women and even children drank "a healthful dram" with breakfast. A dram was a small drink of liquor. Farmers took cider, beer or whiskey into the fields. Employers stopped work in the middle of the morning so their workers could drink. When people got sick, a doctor's first medicine was often a drink of alcohol.

Between 1840 and 1860 drinking fell to about 3.5 gallons of pure alcohol a person each year. Those were the years when the temperance societies were most active.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Temperance and the pledge" -->
Temperance means drinking little or no alcohol. By 1808, people in Connecticut, Virginia and New York had formed temperance societies. In 1825 Lyman Beecher, a minister in Connecticut, began preaching against all drinking of liquor.

On 13 February 1826, reformers in Boston founded the American Temperance Society. Its members signed a pledge not to drink liquor, such as whiskey and rum. The pledge still allowed beer, wine and alcohol taken as medicine. Within five years the society had 2,220 local groups and 170,000 members. By the late 1830s it had more than 8,000 groups and more than 1,250,000 members.

In the late 1830s the reformers split. Many moved to total abstinence, which means no alcohol at all, not even beer or wine. Some stopped relying on persuasion and began asking lawmakers to ban the sale of liquor.

In 1838 Massachusetts lawmakers banned selling liquor in amounts of less than 15 gallons. Only people with money could buy 15 gallons at once, so under this law only the rich could buy liquor. The lawmakers repealed it within two years. In the 1840s, lawmakers in many states let the voters of each town or county decide whether anyone there could get a license to sell liquor.

Navy officers gave every sailor a daily ration of liquor. In 1843 almost 400 people signed a petition to members of Congress asking them to end it. The petition was more than 10 feet long.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Washingtonians, 1840" -->
On 2 April 1840, six heavy drinkers met at Chase's Tavern on Liberty Street in Baltimore and promised each other to stop. They were William K. Mitchell, John F. Hoss, David Anderson, George Steers, James McCurley and Archibald Campbell. They called their group the Washington Temperance Society.

The members stayed sober by telling each other the stories of their own drinking. Then they went looking for other drunkards and told them the same stories. Estimates of how many people joined run from tens of thousands up to 600,000. Within a few years the group had broken apart.

On 22 February 1842, Abraham Lincoln, then a lawyer in Springfield, Illinois, spoke to the Washingtonians of that town. He told the reformers not to scold drinkers. "If you would win a man to your cause," he said, "first convince him that you are his sincere friend."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-b-gough-drugs-alcohol" name="John B. Gough" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John B. Gough

> **Who:** An English-born bookbinder whose drinking cost him his work and his family. He signed the temperance pledge and spent his life asking others to sign it.
> **When and where:** New York, and Worcester, Massachusetts, 1829 to 1845.

John B. Gough drank until he lost his work and his family. Then he signed the temperance pledge and spent the rest of his life asking others to sign it. He told his story in *An Autobiography*, printed in 1845.

Gough was born in Sandgate, England, on 22 August 1817. At the age of 12 he was sent alone to America. He arrived in New York in August 1829 and later learned bookbinding in New York City. After his mother died, he began drinking heavily. He lost one job after another and sang comic songs in theaters for money.

Once, when he stopped drinking all at once, he had delirium tremens. This is an illness of heavy drinkers who suddenly stop. The body shakes and the mind is confused, and the person sees things that are not there. The illness can kill. Gough wrote: "Hideous faces appeared on the walls." He saw "millions of monstrous spiders" around him.

When his wife fell sick, the women nursing her sent him to buy two quarts of rum. He drank it himself. Ten days later his wife and their newborn baby both died. Gough wrote that the whole town learned "that my wife and child were lying dead, and that I was drunk!"

In late October 1842, in Worcester, Massachusetts, a stranger touched his shoulder and asked, "Why do you not sign the pledge?" The man was Joel Stratton, a waiter at the town's temperance hotel. The next evening Gough told his story to a meeting at the Worcester town hall. Then he signed the pledge.

About five months later he drank again. Then he signed the pledge a second time, in public. Between May 1843 and January 1845, he wrote, he travelled more than 12,000 miles and gave 605 talks. At those talks, 31,760 people signed the pledge.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-b-gough-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whiskey and enslaved people at Christmas" -->
In the 1830s, Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. In his book of 1845 he wrote that slaveholders used whiskey to control the people they enslaved. Enslaved people had the days between Christmas and New Year's off work. Slaveholders wanted them to spend those days drunk.

"It was deemed a disgrace not to get drunk at Christmas," Douglass wrote. Slaveholders made bets on "who can drink the most whisky without getting drunk." In that way they got many people to drink far too much.

Douglass wrote that the slaveholders did this to keep enslaved people from rising up in an armed revolt. After a week of drinking, many felt, in his words, "that we had almost as well be slaves to man as to rum."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor and Native nations" -->
Little Turtle, also called Michikinikwa, was a leader of the Miami people. He urged members of Congress to stop traders from selling liquor to Native nations. In 1802 Congress members gave the president the power to stop those sales. War Department officials then made rules that banned traders from bringing liquor onto Native land. The officials did little to enforce the rules.

Traders kept selling because they made more money on liquor than on other goods. They added water to it so they had more to sell. In 1834 members of Congress made it a crime, with a fine of up to $300, to bring liquor onto Native land.

Native leaders worked against drinking among their own people. The Shawnee prophet Tenskwatawa and a Delaware woman named Beate told their followers to give up alcohol. Pawnee leaders limited drinking in their towns.

In 1827, leaders of the Mohican-Stockbridge people wrote to Congress. They had been living on part of the Oneida Reservation in New York. They wrote that one reason they had agreed to move to what is now Wisconsin was to "flee from" liquor in their community. The land in Wisconsin was the homeland of the Menominee people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opium sold as medicine" -->
Opium is a drug made from the opium poppy. It stops pain and brings sleep. A person who takes it every day comes to need it. Laudanum was opium dissolved in alcohol. Until the early 1900s, anyone could buy laudanum without a doctor's order.

Doctors gave laudanum for colds, for heart disease and for pain, to adults and to children. Nurses fed it to babies with a spoon. Patients having surgery were given laudanum and alcohol and then held down while the surgeon cut.

In 1804, in Germany, a pharmacist named Friedrich Sertürner took a stronger drug, morphine, out of opium. In 1817 he tested it on himself and three boys, and all four nearly died. A German drug company began selling morphine in 1827.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chewing tobacco" -->
In these years many American men chewed tobacco and spat out the juice. The English writer Charles Dickens visited the United States in 1842 and wrote about it in his book *American Notes*. In the courts of law, he wrote, "the judge has his spittoon, the crier his, the witness his, and the prisoner his." A spittoon is a bowl for spitting into.

In hospitals, Dickens saw signs asking medical students to spit their tobacco juice into boxes and not onto the stairs. On a steamboat, he watched two young men chew and spit on the deck for a quarter of an hour before breakfast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1851 lawmakers in Maine banned the sale of liquor, and by 1855 lawmakers in twelve more states had passed bans. In Portland, Maine, in 1855, soldiers fired on a crowd at the mayor's order and killed a young sailor. In the Civil War, surgeons gave wounded soldiers morphine and opium, and thousands of veterans came home addicted.

Drugstores sold medicines with opium, morphine, cocaine and alcohol in them, and the labels did not have to say so. City, state and federal lawmakers passed laws against opium, and several of those laws were aimed at Chinese immigrants. Women marched into saloons to pray, and in 1874 they founded the Woman's Christian Temperance Union. In 1880 Kansas voters wrote a ban on liquor into their state constitution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Maine Law and the Portland Rum Riot" -->
On 2 June 1851, lawmakers in Maine banned the sale of all alcoholic drinks. The law still allowed alcohol for medicine and for use in factories. Neal Dow, the mayor of Portland, had helped write it. By 1855, lawmakers in twelve more states had passed bans of their own. Judges struck down some of these laws, and lawmakers passed them again.

For years Dow had ordered police raids on saloons in Portland. A saloon was a bar, mostly for men, that sold drinks by the glass. Many of the saloons Dow raided were run by Irish immigrants, and he gave speeches about "rum-swilling foreigners." In 1850 about one in nine of Portland's 21,000 people was an Irish immigrant.

In 1855 Dow was mayor again. He had the city buy $1,600 worth of liquor for doctors and druggists, and he stored it at City Hall. The law required a city committee to make such a purchase, and no committee had been named. On 2 June 1855, three men got a warrant from a judge to search City Hall. The police would not let them in. By evening a crowd of between 1,000 and 3,000 people, most of them Irish, stood outside. Some threw stones at the door.

Dow called out the militia, a force of part-time soldiers, and ordered them to fire. John Robbins, a 22-year-old sailor from Deer Isle, Maine, broke a hole in the door of the room where the liquor was kept. The soldiers shot him dead. He was to have been married the next day. The soldiers kept firing as the crowd ran and wounded seven more people. The records do not name the soldiers who fired or their officers.

A jury found Dow not guilty of buying the liquor improperly. Voters turned him out of office. In 1856 Maine lawmakers repealed the ban.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Civil War: whiskey, taxes and morphine" -->
In the Civil War, army officers handed out whiskey to soldiers. In 1862 members of Congress put a tax on liquor and tobacco to help pay for the war. On 1 September 1862, Navy officials ended the daily ration of liquor for sailors.

A new tool had spread the use of morphine just before the war. The hypodermic syringe, a hollow needle for pushing a drug under the skin, came into use between 1853 and 1855. In the war, surgeons gave morphine shots and opium pills to soldiers with gunshot wounds and to soldiers whose arms or legs they had cut off. Diarrhea spread through the army camps, and doctors gave opium for that too.

Morphine and opium stop pain. A person who takes them for weeks comes to need them. When the drug stops, the body goes into withdrawal, the sickness that comes when the body loses a drug it depends on. The person sweats, aches and cannot sleep. Later come stomach cramps, diarrhea, sick stomach and vomiting.

Thousands of veterans came home addicted. They called it "opium slavery." Addicted veterans found little sympathy. Many of them could not get medical care, and many died of accidental overdoses. An overdose is too much of a drug at once, enough to make a person very sick or kill them. No one counted how many veterans were addicted. The historian Jonathan S. Jones, who read their letters and records, says there were thousands.

A claim often repeated today says that 400,000 veterans came home with a "soldier's disease" of morphine addiction. The claim does not hold up. The phrase "soldier's disease" first appears in print in 1915, fifty years after the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-tackett-goolrick-drugs-alcohol" name="John Tackett Goolrick" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Tackett Goolrick

> **Who:** A Confederate artillery soldier who became addicted to morphine after a wound and lived with the addiction for about 30 years. His family's letters record it.
> **When and where:** Petersburg and Richmond, Virginia, 1864. Fredericksburg, Virginia, 1865 to 1925.

John Tackett Goolrick became addicted to morphine after a war wound, and he took it for about 30 years. His family's letters record what the addiction did to him and to them. The historian Jonathan S. Jones read those letters and wrote his story.

Goolrick served in the Fredericksburg Artillery in Robert E. Lee's army. He strongly supported the Confederacy's fight to keep slavery. In August 1864, at the siege of Petersburg, a bullet hit him in the left leg. He was taken to Chimborazo, a large army hospital in Richmond. Jones writes that surgeons there apparently gave him morphine for the pain. At Chimborazo, or in the years after, he became addicted. He stayed in the army until Lee surrendered at Appomattox in April 1865.

After the war he worked as a lawyer in Fredericksburg. His wife was Frances Bernard Goolrick. He took larger and larger doses of morphine. In 1896 he broke down and could no longer work, and the family ran short of money.

Frances told him that she would divorce him unless he gave up morphine completely. On 5 February 1896 she wrote to her brother, "I can bear neither for myself or the children, this life any longer."

The family could have sent him to a state hospital for the mentally ill, which took addicted veterans. His brother wrote that sending him there would be "commented upon and asked about" in the newspapers. So the family took him to his brother's farmhouse outside Fredericksburg instead. They locked him in a room, and nurses they paid kept him there for several weeks while he went through withdrawal. His sister-in-law Nora wrote on 10 March 1896, "my heart aches to look at him."

Goolrick got well and went home. The family's letters suggest that he went back to morphine several times before he died in 1925.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-tackett-goolrick-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opium, cocaine and alcohol in the medicine bottle" -->
Patent medicines were medicines sold in stores under a brand name. Their makers did not have to tell buyers what was in them. Many of them contained opium, morphine, cocaine or alcohol. Some contained mercury and lead, which are poisons.

Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup was sold from 1849 as a medicine for babies who were teething. Each ounce held about 65 milligrams of morphine. One teaspoon held enough morphine to kill a young child. In 1911 the American Medical Association, a group of doctors, called it a "baby killer." Nobody counted the children who died after taking it.

Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound was sold to women from the 1870s. It was 18 to 20 percent alcohol. The label stated that the alcohol was there only to keep the medicine from spoiling. Women who had signed temperance pledges took it.

Most people addicted to opium in these years did not smoke it. They took it as medicine. Surveys in the late 1800s found that two out of every three people addicted to laudanum or morphine were women. Many of them were middle-class or rich women who had started taking morphine for an illness. The historian David T. Courtwright writes that addiction to these drugs was at least as common in the late 1800s as in 1992, for the size of the population. Most of the people who took these medicines left no record of what happened to them.

Cocaine is a drug made from the leaves of the coca plant. It came to America as a medicine in 1884. Doctors used it to numb the eye for surgery. Soon doctors and druggists were addicted to it. In 1887 Oregon lawmakers banned cocaine, the first in any state to do so. Lawmakers in Montana, Colorado, Illinois and Massachusetts passed bans by 1898.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-stith-pemberton-drugs-alcohol" name="John Stith Pemberton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Stith Pemberton

> **Who:** A Georgia druggist who became addicted to morphine after a war wound. He invented Coca-Cola, and the first Coca-Cola had cocaine in it.
> **When and where:** Columbus and Atlanta, Georgia, 1865 to 1888.

John Stith Pemberton invented Coca-Cola while he was addicted to morphine. His drink held cocaine from coca leaves, and he sold it as a medicine.

Pemberton was born in 1831 and earned a medical degree in 1850. He ran a drugstore in Columbus, Georgia. In April 1865 he fought for the Confederacy at the Battle of Columbus, and he took a sword wound to the chest. He took morphine for the pain and became addicted.

From 1866 he tried to make a painkiller that had no morphine in it. By 1885 he was selling a wine mixed with coca. In 1886 a ban on liquor took effect in Atlanta and Fulton County. Pemberton made a version of his drink without wine and called it Coca-Cola. Jacob's Pharmacy in Atlanta first sold it on 8 May 1886, for five cents a glass. Pemberton claimed it cured headaches and morphine addiction.

The makers of Coca-Cola stopped using fresh coca leaves in 1903. They took out the last traces of cocaine by 1929.

Pemberton was still addicted and poor when he sold his rights to the drink in 1888. He died that August. His son Charles was addicted to opium. In June 1894 Charles was found unconscious with opium beside him, and he died ten days later, at the age of 40.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-stith-pemberton-drugs-alcohol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Opium laws and Chinese immigrants" -->
Chinese immigrants came to California during the Gold Rush, and some of them brought the habit of smoking opium. In the 1850s opium dens opened in San Francisco's Chinatown. An opium den was a room where people paid to lie down and smoke opium.

In 1875 the members of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, the city's lawmakers, made it a crime to keep or visit an opium den. The *San Francisco Chronicle* reported that they acted after they learned that Chinese owners were running dens for white customers. The police chief, Phillip Crowley, said his officers would "keep them from opening places where whites might resort to smoke." His officers often left alone the dens that sold only to Chinese people.

Historians disagree about why the supervisors passed the 1875 law. George Fisher, of Stanford Law School, argues that they wanted to protect young white people. Other writers argue that hatred of the Chinese drove the supervisors.

Later laws named race and nationality outright. In 1887 Idaho lawmakers punished "every white person" who kept or used an opium den. That same year, members of Congress banned any "subject of the Emperor of China" from bringing opium into the United States. By 1896, lawmakers in 22 states had banned opium dens.

In 1883 Irish members of the Church of the Transfiguration, a Catholic church in New York, and its assistant pastor spread a false story. They said that Chinese men were luring girls as young as 11 and 12 into opium dens. Newspapers printed the story before it was shown to be false about a week later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Praying in the saloons: the WCTU" -->
On 23 December 1873, in Hillsboro, Ohio, Eliza Jane Thompson heard the lecturer Diocletian Lewis speak against drink. She then led groups of women into the town's saloons. The women sang hymns and prayed until the owners closed. Women in 22 other states copied them. In Anoka, Minnesota, a saloon keeper and his wife poured cold water from an upstairs window onto women praying on the sidewalk.

In November 1874 women met in Cleveland and founded the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, or WCTU. Its members worked to ban the making and selling of liquor. Frances Willard led the WCTU from 1879 until she died in 1898. She argued that women needed the vote to protect their homes from men who drank and beat their families.

In 1880 WCTU leaders set up a department for Black members. From 1883 Frances Watkins Harper, a Black lecturer, ran it in the North. Willard let the southern branches keep their own rules on race. In 1893 the journalist Ida B. Wells said that Willard had stayed silent about lynching, the killing of people by mobs without any trial. Wells quoted words Willard had spoken after a trip to the South: "The colored race multiplies like the locusts of Egypt," and "the grog shop is its center of power." A grog shop was a saloon. Willard denied the charge. She later spoke against lynching.

In 1893 the Reverend Howard Hyde Russell founded the Anti-Saloon League in Oberlin, Ohio. Its leaders cared how lawmakers voted on liquor, not whether the lawmakers drank.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kansas goes dry, 1880" -->
In 1878 Kansas voters elected John St. John as governor. He wanted to ban liquor. Kansas lawmakers wrote a ban on making and selling liquor into an amendment to the state constitution, and voters approved it in November 1880. Kansas was the first state to put such a ban in its constitution.

The ban began on 1 May 1881. In 1883 Topeka, the state capital, still had 43 illegal bars. Their owners stayed open by paying a fine of $100 each month. Kansas kept the ban until voters ended it in 1948.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Liquor laws and Native nations" -->
In 1854 members of Congress changed the 1834 law against bringing liquor onto Native land. The change stated that Native people themselves could not be charged under it. In 1895 they made it a crime to sell or give alcohol to anyone in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. The punishment was a fine and one month to five years in prison. In 1897 they set a punishment of at least 60 days in prison, with no upper limit, for giving alcohol to Native people who held land under the Dawes Act. By 1900, about one in every 20 people in federal prisons had been convicted of selling or giving alcohol to Native people.

Native leaders set their own rules. The Paiute prophet Wovoka told followers of the Ghost Dance, a religious movement he led, not to drink. On the Comanche reservation in Oklahoma, Quanah Parker led ceremonies in which people took peyote as a sacred act of worship. He argued for the right to use peyote in this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cures and cigarettes" -->
In 1879 Dr. Leslie Keeley opened a clinic in Dwight, Illinois, for heavy drinkers. He gave them shots of what he called "bichloride of gold," four times a day. Other patients got his medicine by mail. By 1900 more than 300,000 people had taken the Keeley Cure. Other doctors criticized it.

Before 1880 a skilled worker could roll about four cigarettes a minute by hand. In 1880 James Albert Bonsack of Virginia built a machine that rolled about 200 a minute. James Buchanan Duke rented Bonsack machines in 1884 or 1885. The sources differ by a year. By 1890 Duke sold 40 percent of the cigarettes in the country. That year he joined his four biggest rivals with his own business to form the American Tobacco Company.
