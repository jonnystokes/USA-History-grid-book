## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the children of the Native nations in this land learned from their own families and communities. There were no school buildings, no grades and no school year. Nobody taught as a full-time job. Children learned skills by working beside the adults who had them. They learned their nation's history and laws by listening to the people who kept them in memory.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Learning by doing and by listening" -->
The Blackfeet are one nation whose way of teaching is described in a National Park Service account. It states that "Blackfeet children learned by doing, by example, and through apprenticeship to their elders." An apprentice is a young person who learns a skill by working next to someone who already has it. Elders are the older people of a community. The same account states that on long winter evenings, adults passed on the nation's history and ways of life around a fire the whole group shared.

Teaching by the spoken word is called oral tradition. The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian states that "the oral tradition continues to be an integral part of Indigenous teaching and learning." Integral means a necessary part of the whole. Native families and teachers still teach this way today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Memory in place of writing: wampum" -->
No writing system was in use in this land before 1500. The first one made for a language spoken in what is now the United States was the Cherokee syllabary, in 1821. A syllabary is a set of written signs in which each sign stands for one syllable. Before that, everything a nation needed to keep, from its agreements to its history, was kept in people's memories. Some people were trained to hold those memories exactly.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is a union of five nations: the Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida and Mohawk. Its people record agreements and law in wampum. Wampum is beads cut from the white and purple parts of clam shells, strung on strings or woven into belts. The Onondaga were made the Wampum Keeper, the nation that looks after the belts, when the five nations joined under the Great Law of Peace. The Onondaga Nation's own account explains how a belt holds a record: "The speaker puts the words of the agreement into the wampum as the strings or belts are woven together." After that, "each speaker thereafter uses the wampum to remember the initial agreement and the history that has happened to date." The Onondaga call the belts "our living history."

So a person had to learn to read a belt and to recite what it held. When the Great Law of Peace was agreed is not settled. Haudenosaunee tradition places it between about 1000 and 1400. Scholars working from written accounts have put it at about 1450, and some argue for the late 1100s. No record settles which date is right.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="No names survive" -->
No pupil or teacher from this land before 1500 is known by name. With no writing system, no one made a written list of the people who taught or learned. No count exists either, of children taught or of people who could recite a record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
No schoolhouse stood in this land in the 1500s. The only European teaching was done by Spanish priests at missions in Florida, and at the very end of the century in New Mexico. A mission was a settlement where priests worked to make Native people into Catholics. The priests taught reading at some missions, but their purpose was conversion, which means changing a person's religion. Native families went on teaching their own children.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mission teaching in Florida" -->
In September 1565 Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and about 800 Spanish colonists landed at the Timucua town of a chief named Seloy. The colonists included 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. They made their first settlement in Seloy's town and used his council house as their first fort. That settlement became St. Augustine. Accounts of the landing do not say what happened to Seloy's people afterward.

Spanish priests began trying to convert Native people in Florida within a few years. By the 1580s, friars of the Franciscan order had missions across Florida. A friar is a member of a Catholic religious order who has promised to live in poverty. One of the largest missions, San Juan del Puerto, stood on Fort George Island among the Mocama, a coastal people who spoke Timucua. The National Park Service can date its start only to the 1570s.

At a mission the friars taught Catholic doctrine, meaning the beliefs of the church. They also taught European customs, farming, and reading and writing. They taught in Spanish and in Timucua, the local language.

Father Francisco Pareja, the friar at San Juan del Puerto, wrote that "many Indian men and women have learned to read in less than six months, and they write letters to one another in their own language." It is the teacher's own report of his own success.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate led a Spanish expedition of colonists to the upper Rio Grande in 1598. Franciscan missions in New Mexico, and the religious teaching at them, began that year, with two years of the century left.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Paquiquineo (Don Luís de Velasco)

> **Who:** A young man from the Chesapeake Bay country who was taught by Spanish priests for years and came home as a guide for a mission.
> **When and where:** 1561 to 1572. The Chesapeake, Spain, Mexico, and back.

Paquiquineo is the only person of the 1500s from this land whose schooling by Europeans is recorded by name. No surviving mission record from the 1500s names a single pupil.

He was about seventeen when he left the Chesapeake on a Spanish ship in late June 1561. Historians still argue over whether he went willingly or was kidnapped, and the record does not settle it. He came from the country of the Powhatan chiefdoms, possibly from a Paspahegh, Kiskiack or Kecoughtan family. Historians do not agree which.

He reached Seville, in Spain, on September 9, 1561, was in Madrid by the end of October, and met King Philip II. In 1562 he sailed to Mexico. He fell ill there and was baptized in Mexico City, in the words of the record "on the brink of death." Baptism is the Christian ceremony of joining the church. He took the name of the viceroy of New Spain, the king's ruler in Mexico: Don Luís de Velasco.

From 1566 to 1570 he studied in Seville with the Jesuits, an order of Catholic priests. No surviving record says what they taught him in those four years.

In September 1570 he landed on the James River with a party of Jesuits led by Father Juan Bautista de Segura. The priests brought him as their guide and interpreter, a person who translates between two languages. Paquiquineo left them and went back to his own family. On February 4, 1571, he killed three of the priests. Then he attacked the mission and killed Father Segura and the others. The only one left alive was the altar boy, Alonso de Olmos.

In August 1572 the Spanish governor of Florida, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, sent soldiers to the James River. They captured some of Paquiquineo's people in a fight. Spanish soldiers hanged several of them from the yardarms of their ship, the wooden beams that cross the masts. The Spanish records do not give the names of the men they hanged. The Powhatan people handed Alonso de Olmos back. The soldiers did not find Paquiquineo, and no European record mentions him again.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s the English colonists of Massachusetts made teaching children to read a duty under law. The men who wrote the laws gave a religious reason: children had to be able to read the Bible for themselves. Schooling was not free. Parents or masters paid the teacher, and the Latin schools took only boys. In the other colonies, children learned at home or as apprentices. Colonists also opened the first schools and a college building for Native students, with the aim of making them Christian.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston Latin School, 1635" -->
Boston Latin School opened on April 23, 1635, and it is still open. It is the oldest public school in the country. A public school is one that a town or a state runs for its children. Boston stood on the Shawmut peninsula, which the Massachusett people called Mushauwomuk, "the boat landing place." It was part of the land of the Massachusett nation, whose chief sachem, or leader, was Chickataubut. Between 1616 and 1618 an epidemic, a disease that spreads fast through a population, killed about 75,000 of the 100,000 people of the region, by the West End Museum's count. In 1630 John Winthrop and about 1,000 English settlers arrived on the peninsula.

On April 13, 1635, the townsmen of Boston met and agreed to ask Philemon Pormort to teach. The town record states that he "shall be intreated to become scholemaster for the teaching and nourtering of children with us." Intreated means asked, and nourtering means bringing up. The record dates the meeting to "the second month," which was April, because the English year then began on March 25. The first classes met in Pormort's own house. The boys learned Latin and Greek.

Collegiate School in New York City also claims to be the oldest school in the country. Dutch colonists founded it. In 1982 its leaders moved its founding date back from 1638 to 1628, the year the minister Jonas Michaelius began teaching in Manhattan. The two schools' claims to be oldest of all have never been settled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The two Massachusetts school laws" -->
In 1642 the men of the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmaking body, passed the first law in this land that ordered children to be taught to read. Under it, parents and masters had to teach their children and apprentices to "read the English tongue" and to know the colony's main laws. The lawmakers set up no school and hired no teacher. Selectmen, the men elected to run a town's business, visited homes to check. A family that failed could be fined. The selectmen could also take a child or an apprentice away and place the child with another master who would do the teaching.

Many families ignored that law, so the General Court passed a second one in 1647. Its opening words state the reason. It is "one of the chief projects of that old deluder Satan to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures." A deluder is someone who tricks people, and the Scriptures are the Bible. For that reason people call it the Old Deluder Satan Act. The lawmakers wanted every child able to read the Bible without help.

Under the 1647 law, every town of 50 households had to choose someone to teach children to read and write. The parents or masters paid that teacher's wages. Every town of 100 families also had to set up a grammar school, a school that taught Latin, to prepare boys "for the university." That meant Harvard College, founded in 1636. A town that did neither paid 5 pounds, in English money, every year to the nearest school. Lawmakers in the other New England colonies passed similar laws. Rhode Island's did not.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New England Primer" -->
A primer is a first reading book. The English printer Benjamin Harris made *The New England Primer* for buyers in the colonies sometime between 1687 and 1690. The American Antiquarian Society, which holds a copy printed in 1727, can date it no closer than that. The only evidence is advertisements, the first of them placed by Harris in 1690. No copy printed before 1727 survives anywhere.

The book contained lessons in reading, spelling and religious rules for behavior, all mixed together. Each letter of the alphabet had a small woodcut, a picture printed from a carved block of wood. After the alphabet, the 1727 edition gives this instruction: "let him learn these and such like Sentences by Heart." Children memorized the sentences and recited them aloud.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ezekiel-cheever" name="Ezekiel Cheever" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ezekiel Cheever

> **Who:** A schoolmaster who taught Latin and Greek for 70 years, the last 38 of them as head of the Boston Latin School.
> **When and where:** Born in London in 1615. Died in Boston on August 21, 1708. Taught at New Haven, Charlestown and Boston.

Ezekiel Cheever taught Latin and Greek for 70 years without a break. The Database of Classical Scholars, kept at Rutgers University, states that this is longer than anyone else in the history of this country.

He was born in London on January 25, 1615. Some sources give 1614, because the English calendar then began the year on March 25, so January fell in the old year. He ran the schools at New Haven from 1638 and taught at Charlestown from 1661. On December 29, 1670, the town of Boston asked him to head the Boston Latin School. He was still teaching there when he died at 93.

Accounts of his classroom describe a smoky fireplace, poor light, and "a bundle of birch rods beside his desk." A birch rod is a thin stick or bundle of twigs cut from a birch tree, used to beat a child.

Cotton Mather had been one of his pupils. He preached at Cheever's funeral and called him a teacher "than whom New England had known no better." Cheever's Latin textbook, *A Short Introduction to the Latin Tongue*, was printed in Boston in 1708, the year he died. Schools used it for nearly 200 years, longer than any other Latin textbook printed in this country.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ezekiel-cheever" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beaten at school: John Barnard" -->
In the 1600s the rod was standard equipment in a schoolroom. One of Ezekiel Cheever's pupils wrote down what it was like. John Barnard was born in Boston on November 6, 1681. In the spring of 1689, when he was seven, his family sent him to Cheever's school. He left after a few weeks and came back about a year and a half later. He stayed until he entered Harvard at fifteen.

Cheever told Barnard in front of the class that he played too much and kept his classmates from their lessons. Cheever said that "if any of them cannot perform their duty, I shall correct you for it." To correct a child, in the words of that time, meant to beat him. When one classmate did not know his lesson, "our master beat me," Barnard wrote. The other boy saw this and neglected his book on purpose, "for which I was still corrected, and that for several days." Barnard beat that boy after school, and the boy never came back.

Barnard wrote that he was "often beaten for my play." Once Cheever set the class to turn the stories called Aesop's fables into Latin verse. Barnard could not do it. Cheever beat him for it "two or three days going," until Barnard told him he had tried his hardest. Then Cheever stopped asking it of him. Barnard does not say what Cheever hit him with, or how many times. He wrote his account as an old man, in the late 1760s, and it was printed in 1836.

People often say that the saying "spare the rod and spoil the child" comes from the Bible. Proverbs 13:24 says that whoever spares the rod hates their children, but the words "spoil the child" are not in it. An English monk named Ælfric of Eynsham, who lived from about 950 to about 1010, wrote an older English version: "He who spareth his rod hateth his child." The wording people use now comes from a comic poem, *Hudibras*, by Samuel Butler: "Then spare the Rod, and spill the Child." Later speakers changed "spill" to "spoil." Sources give 1662, 1663 and 1664 for the part of the poem with that line.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Natick's school, and Deer Island" -->
In 1651 the English minister John Eliot and a group of Native leaders founded Natick, in Massachusetts, on a bend of the Charles River. Waban and Cutshamekin of the Massachusett nation and John Speen of the Nipmuc nation worked on it with him. Natick was the first of more than a dozen "praying towns," towns set up for Native people who had become Christians. The town built a meetinghouse with a school inside it. The schoolmaster was a Massachusett man named Monequassun.

Eliot printed schoolbooks in the Massachusett language: a catechism in 1653 and an *Indian Primer* in 1669. A catechism is a list of religious questions and answers that a pupil learns by heart. In every one of Eliot's books, the reading lessons were also lessons in Christian belief.

In 1675 war began between the English colonists and several Native nations led by Metacom, whom the English called King Philip. On October 13, 1675, the men who governed the Massachusetts colony ordered the Christian Native people of the praying towns moved to Deer Island in Boston Harbor. They started with the people of Natick. The surviving accounts do not name the men who voted for the order. The National Park Service counts about 500 to 1,100 people held on the island that winter, most of them women and children.

The people on Deer Island had little food and no proper shelter through the winter. The Park Service writes: "Due to starvation, freezing temperatures and lack of shelter, very few survived internment." Internment means being held prisoner in a camp. A Boston historical group estimates that more than half of them died. Colonists released the survivors in May 1676. An unknown number of people were smuggled off the island and sold into slavery in the West Indies or in Tangier, in North Africa. The records do not name the people who took and sold them. No record says what became of Monequassun, the Natick schoolmaster. Every year, descendants of the Native nations of that war hold a remembrance of their ancestors on Deer Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Harvard, the Indian College, and the people Harvard men enslaved" -->
Harvard's charter of 1650, its founding document, included a promise of "the education of the English and Indian youth of this Country." A building called the Indian College stood in Harvard Yard from 1655. Sources disagree on whether it came down in 1695 or in 1698. A printing press kept inside it printed the first Bible published in North America, in 1663, in the Algonquian language.

Only a handful of Native students came to the Indian College. Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck graduated in 1665. His classmate Joel Iacoomes drowned in a shipwreck just before the graduation ceremony. Two more students died of disease before they finished, and one left to become a sailor.

Harvard's leaders and staff also enslaved people. In April 2022 a Harvard committee led by Tomiko Brown-Nagin reported records of "at least 79 people who were enslaved by Harvard presidents, overseers, and faculty and staff members" before Massachusetts outlawed slavery in 1783. A woman named Jane was enslaved by the college steward Andrew Bordman, who enslaved at least eight people. A woman named Cicely was enslaved by William Brattle, a tutor and treasurer of the college. Titus, Venus, Juba and Bilhah lived and worked at Wadsworth House for two Harvard presidents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="caleb-cheeshahteaumuck" name="Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck

> **Who:** A Wampanoag student from Martha's Vineyard, the first Native American to graduate from Harvard.
> **When and where:** Graduated in 1665 in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Died in 1666.

Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck was the first Native American to graduate from Harvard. He took his degree in 1665. He came from a Wampanoag community on Martha's Vineyard, an island off the coast of Massachusetts.

One piece of his own writing survives. It is a short letter in Latin to the English donors who paid for the Indian College. It begins "Honoratissimi benefactores," which means "most honored benefactors." A benefactor is a person who gives money to help. The letter is often described as the earliest surviving writing by a Native person on this continent.

He died in 1666, within a year of his graduation, of what people then called consumption. One account says he died at Watertown, Massachusetts, when he was about twenty. Martha's Vineyard Magazine writes that the disease was "presumably" tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs. The word presumably means the writer judged it likely, because no record from the time names the disease as tuberculosis.

Of the Native students who came to the Indian College in his time, he was the only one who finished.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="caleb-cheeshahteaumuck" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Timucua in print" -->
In Florida, Father Francisco Pareja wrote the Timucua language down in books printed in the early 1600s. He published three catechisms, a confession book dated 1613, and a grammar. A grammar is a book that explains how a language works. Those books are now the only record of the Timucua language. Nobody speaks it today.

About 200,000 people who spoke Timucua lived in what is now northern Florida and southern Georgia in the 1500s, by the estimate of the historian Matthew Holt Jennings. Nobody counted them. By the 1650s about 2,000 were left. Most died in epidemics of diseases the Spanish had brought. After the English founded Charles Town in Carolina in 1670, Carolina slave traders and Native groups allied with them raided the Spanish missions again and again to capture people and sell them. In 1763 Spanish officials handed Florida to Britain, and they shipped the last Timucua people to Cuba.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1700-1750" order="04" chapter="education" label="1700 to 1750" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1740 the lawmakers of South Carolina made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to write. It was the first law in these colonies that punished someone for teaching. Most other children in these fifty years learned at home, at a small school in a neighbor's kitchen, or as apprentices. The schools that did exist mostly took boys whose families could pay. Church societies in London paid for a few charity schools. Two traders brought four captive Native boys to a college in Virginia to be its first Native pupils.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The law against teaching, 1740" -->
On May 10, 1740, South Carolina's lawmakers passed "An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing Negroes and Other Slaves in This Province." They passed it the year after the Stono Rebellion of 1739, an uprising by enslaved people. The act identifies the men who made it law: Lieutenant Governor William Bull, who was acting as the colony's head, his Council, and the Commons House of Assembly. It does not name the members who drafted it.

Section 45 of the act states its reason in these words: "the having of slaves taught to write, or suffering them to be employed in writing, may be attended with great inconveniences." It then states that anyone who teaches an enslaved person to write, or has one taught, or uses one as a scribe, "shall, for every such offense, forfeit the sum of one hundred pounds current money." A scribe is a person who writes documents for someone else. To forfeit means to lose as a punishment.

The teacher owed the fine, not the pupil. Under another section of the act, a person could collect the fine by suing the teacher in court. Half the money went to the colony. The other half went to whoever reported or sued. That gave anyone a reason to watch for a teacher and turn the teacher in.

Under the act, teaching writing was a crime and teaching reading was not. Historians explain the difference this way: a person who could write could forge a pass. A pass was a note from an enslaver that let an enslaved person travel. With a forged pass, a person could escape.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Harry and Andrew's school in Charleston" -->
Three years after the law, a school for Black pupils opened in Charleston, South Carolina. The historian Carter G. Woodson wrote in 1915 that the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, a Church of England society in London, bought two young Black men named Harry and Andrew. The Society's leaders bought them in order to train them as teachers "to their people." Alexander Garden, a Church of England official in Charleston, had a schoolhouse built for about 308 pounds. Harry and Andrew taught in it.

Accounts give 1743 or 1744 for the opening. The school began with about sixty young pupils. It also ran in the evenings for people who worked in the day. Woodson judged it "highly probable" that after the 1740 law only free Black people attended. The school closed in 1763. One of the two teachers had died, and the men who ran the Society judged that the other was not doing the job well.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where a small child started: the dame school and the hornbook" -->
In New England a small child usually learned to read first from a woman in her own home. This was called a dame school. Dame was an old word for a woman. The teacher took children from about three or four years old for a few hours a day, for a small fee. Often she was a widow or an unmarried woman who needed the money, and families paid her in eggs, firewood, or washing and ironing instead of cash. Her lessons covered the letters, reading, the catechism and the Lord's Prayer, and sewing for girls.

The town schools of New England would not take a boy until he could "stand up and read words of two syllables and keep their places." Somebody had to teach him that first, and that somebody was usually a woman in her kitchen. Women were already teaching, for very little pay, long before a town school would hire a woman.

A child's first reading tool was often a hornbook. A hornbook was a wooden paddle about the shape of a table-tennis bat, with one printed sheet tacked to it. A thin, clear sheet of animal horn covered the paper so that it would not wear out. A leather cord through the handle let a child hang it from a belt. The sheet showed a cross, then the alphabet in capital and small letters, then the vowels joined to consonants, then the Lord's Prayer. Because of the cross at the top, children called the alphabet row the "Christ's Cross row," later shortened to "crisscross row." The Smithsonian Institution holds a hornbook made in the 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charity schools" -->
Some schools in the colonies were paid for from London. King William III gave a charter to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts on June 16, 1701. The minister Thomas Bray pushed for it, along with Bishop Henry Compton and others. The Society's members raised money to send priests and schoolmasters to the colonies. Across the 1700s they paid for more than 300 of them to cross the Atlantic. Members of a separate group, the Associates of Dr. Bray, founded in 1724, set up schools of their own.

These were charity schools. A society in London paid the teacher. The men who ran the societies paid so that pupils could read the catechism and the Bible. The families did not pay, and the town did not run the school.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many people could read and write" -->
Nobody surveyed how many colonists could read. Historians count signatures on wills and deeds instead. A person who could write signed a name. A person who could not made a mark, such as an X. This method undercounts readers, because many children were taught to read and never taught to write. That was common for girls.

The historian Kenneth Lockridge counted signatures in New England. About 60 percent of white men could sign their names between 1650 and 1670. About 85 percent could sign between 1758 and 1762, and about 90 percent between 1787 and 1795. For women, the figures follow the years they were born. Of women born between 1650 and 1669, 27 percent could sign. The share rose to 48 percent for women born between 1690 and 1709, and 81 percent for women born between 1730 and 1749. More people could sign in New England and the middle colonies than in the South, and more in towns than in the countryside.

In Virginia, as many as 5 percent of enslaved people may have been able to read by 1775. One check on that number comes from enslavers themselves. Between 1736 and 1776 enslavers placed about 1,000 advertisements in the *Virginia Gazette*, a newspaper, to hunt for people who had run away. Fifty-five of the advertisements said the person could read or write.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Girls' schools: New Orleans and Pennsylvania" -->
In 1727 a group of Ursuline nuns came to New Orleans from France and started a school for girls. New Orleans was then a French colony. It became part of the United States in 1803. The school's own history states that it is the oldest Catholic school in the United States and the oldest school for girls that has run without stopping. It also states that the nuns taught the first classes for enslaved Black women, free women of color and Native women.

In May 1742 Countess Benigna von Zinzendorf started classes for twenty-five girls at Germantown, near Philadelphia. She belonged to the Moravian Church, a Protestant church. The school moved to Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, in 1745 and settled there in 1748. It took in mainly the daughters of traveling Moravian church workers. The Moravian schools' own histories call it the first Protestant boarding school for girls in what became the United States. A boarding school is one where pupils live as well as study.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Franklin's plan for an academy, 1749" -->
In October 1749 Benjamin Franklin published *Proposals Relating to the Education of Youth in Pensilvania*. He argued for a school that taught in English instead of Latin and Greek. He wanted it to teach geography, natural history and modern languages alongside the old subjects. This kind of school came to be called an academy. At an academy, older pupils studied more than at a town school and less than at a college.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a school day was like, and what is not known" -->
Many people picture an early school as a single room with a stove, slates to write on, and a bench where pupils stood to recite. That picture comes from records of the 1800s. For 1700 to 1750 the records contain less. A town appointed one man to teach, the parents or masters paid him, and children of every age sat in the same room because there was only one teacher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Indian school at William & Mary" -->
The College of William & Mary in Virginia received its royal charter in 1693. The charter lists three purposes for the college. The third was to teach Native boys "in the Christian Religion." Rents from a country estate in Yorkshire, England, bought in 1695 with 5,400 pounds from the estate of the scientist Robert Boyle, paid for this Indian school.

Governor Francis Nicholson tried to recruit Native pupils in 1700 and failed. So about 1702 two traders, Robert Hicks and John Evans, "purchased or acquired four young Indian captives from the Catawba Nation." William & Mary's own history states this. The traders got the boys along with deerskins and furs. The four boys became the school's first Native pupils. The college's published records do not give their names. The Meherrin Nation's account states that the college "resorted to buying their pupils."

By 1712 the college had twenty other Native pupils, plus the four captive boys. That was a third to a half of all the pupils in the college. In 1723 a building for the Indian school, called the Brafferton, was finished. Enslaved workers built it. More than 125 Native students from at least 26 nations lived and studied there between 1723 and 1776. They learned reading, writing, arithmetic and the prayers of the Church of England. The published records do not say how many of them died at the school.

The college's leaders also bought people. In 1718 they bought a tobacco plantation of 2,119 acres in Virginia for 150 pounds. They paid 476 pounds more for seventeen enslaved people to work the plantation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="christopher-dock" name="Christopher Dock" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Christopher Dock

> **Who:** A Mennonite schoolmaster in Pennsylvania who wrote the first book about how to teach written in this country.
> **When and where:** Skippack and Salford, Pennsylvania, from no later than 1718 until his death in 1771.

Christopher Dock wrote the first book about how to teach written in this country. Most of what he wrote down is about how he made children behave. He was a member of the Mennonite church, a Christian church.

He came to Pennsylvania between 1710 and 1714. His schools were on Lenape land. The Lenape homeland covered southeastern Pennsylvania, central and southern New Jersey and Delaware. Between 1682 and 1684 William Penn bought land for his colony in southeastern Pennsylvania from the Lenape, paying at least 1,200 pounds in goods.

Dock opened a school at Skippack no later than 1718. He taught for ten years, then farmed for about ten more. In 1738 he went back to teaching and taught for the last 33 years of his life. He ran two schools, at Skippack and at Salford, on alternate days.

On August 8, 1750, he finished a book called *Schul-Ordnung*, which is German for "school management." He did not want it printed while he was alive. The printer Christopher Saur printed it anyway. Sources disagree on the date. One gives 1769 for the first printing and 1770 for the second, and others give 1770 for the first. It was still being printed in English in the 1900s.

Dock sorted children by what worked on each one. A child who wanted praise got praise. A child who wanted money got a penny for studying hard. A child who wanted sweets got a sugar pretzel for learning well. In the words of the historian Martin Brumbaugh, "The ill-intentioned, who were not affected by the promise of a penny or a cooky, he threatened with whipping." Dock also put a yoke, a wooden frame worn over the neck and shoulders, on a boy who swore. He made some children sit alone. Whipping means hitting a person again and again with a whip, a rod or a strap. Dock was milder than the schoolmasters around him, and he still used whipping and the threat of it.

One evening in the autumn of 1771 Dock did not come home from school at the usual time. In Brumbaugh's account, people went to look for him, and "he was found in his schoolroom on his knees," dead.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="education" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The men who founded the United States said the new country would need citizens who could read. Several of them wrote plans for public schools. Lawmakers turned almost all of the plans down, and the fights were mostly about who would pay. The schools that opened in these fifty years were private academies, the first schools for girls with charters from a government, and church charity schools. At one of them, in Williamsburg, Virginia, Black children were taught to read and taught that slavery was God's will.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson's plan, and who was left out" -->
Thomas Jefferson wrote "A Bill for the More General Diffusion of Knowledge." Diffusion means spreading. The bill went to the Virginia House of Delegates, the lower house of the state's lawmakers, on June 18, 1779. Jefferson described the plan in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*. Each county would be divided into districts five or six miles square, called hundreds, with a school in each one. Every family could send its children for three years free, and longer if they paid. The district schools would teach reading, writing and arithmetic. Above them, grammar schools would teach Greek, Latin, geography and harder arithmetic.

Jefferson also set out how poor boys would be chosen for more schooling. Each year, officials called visitors would "chuse the boy, of best genius in the school, of those whose parents are too poor to give them further education." In this way, he wrote, "twenty of the best geniusses will be raked from the rubbish annually." Rubbish is Jefferson's word for the children of poor families.

The plan in *Notes on the State of Virginia* does not mention girls. Every reference is to boys. Enslaved children are not in it either, because it was a plan for free children only. Virginia's lawmakers never passed the bill. James Madison brought it forward again while Jefferson was in Paris, and it failed again. In 1796 the lawmakers passed a much weaker "Act to Establish Public Schools." Under that act each county's court chose whether to open schools, and in most counties the court did nothing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Land Ordinance of 1785: school money from Native land" -->
The first public money for American schools came from land that US officials had taken from Native nations. On May 20, 1785, the members of Congress passed the Land Ordinance. An ordinance is a law. Under it, surveyors were to cut the western land into townships six miles square. Each township was split into 36 numbered sections of one square mile. The law states: "There shall be reserved the lot N 16, of every township, for the maintenance of public schools, within the said township."

Section 16 sat near the middle of each township. Under the law it was not to be sold to a settler. The money it earned, from rent or from a later sale, paid for a school. US officials sold the rest of the land to settlers.

The first land surveyed under the Ordinance was in what is now eastern Ohio. Wyandot, Lenape, Ojibwe, Odawa and Shawnee people lived there. On January 21, 1785, at Fort McIntosh, US commissioners signed a treaty with Wyandot, Lenape, Ojibwe and Odawa leaders. The commissioners' terms confined most of the Ohio nations to a reserve, an area set aside for them, between the Cuyahoga and Maumee rivers. In September 1785 surveyors began measuring the land, guarded by troops from Fort McIntosh. The Ohio historian George Knepper writes that the Ohio nations, especially the Shawnee, "refused to abide by a treaty negotiated under duress by minor tribal chiefs who lacked authority to speak for their people." Under duress means under threat or pressure.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The academies" -->
Franklin's academy, the Academy and Charity School of Philadelphia, opened in 1751. Thomas and Richard Penn, the owners of the colony, granted it a charter in 1753. The College of Philadelphia was chartered two years later.

Samuel Phillips founded Phillips Academy at Andover, Massachusetts, on April 25, 1778. It opened five days later. Paul Revere cut its seal. John and Elizabeth Phillips signed the papers founding Phillips Exeter Academy in New Hampshire on April 3, 1781. That school opened on May 1, 1783, with one teacher and 56 students.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first schools for girls with charters" -->
John Poor founded the Young Ladies' Academy of Philadelphia on June 4, 1787. On January 7, 1792, it received a charter. That made it the first school for the higher education of women in the country to be recognized by a government. Its pupils studied reading, writing, English grammar, mathematics, geography, composition, chemistry and natural philosophy. Natural philosophy was the old name for physics and the other sciences of how nature works. Benjamin Rush was among the men who backed it.

Sarah Pierce, born in 1767, opened the Litchfield Female Academy in Connecticut in 1792. She ran it for 41 years. More than 2,000 girls attended over about 30 of those years, from 17 states and territories and from Canada and the West Indies. The Litchfield Historical Society has found 1,848 of them by name. More than 8 in 10 came from other towns and boarded with Litchfield families. They studied history, geography, arithmetic and composition, and later Latin, logic and natural philosophy. Girls who finished the whole course received diplomas.

Sarah Pierce and others who backed these schools argued that mothers gave children their first moral training and their first lessons, and that the new country depended on it. In this argument, girls needed schooling because of the children they would raise. Pierce herself believed girls and boys were equally able. She did not argue that women should enter the men's colleges or professions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Williamsburg Bray School, 1760 to 1774" -->
From 1760 to 1774 Black children in Williamsburg, Virginia, most of them enslaved, learned to read at a charity school. The men who paid for it also meant to teach them to accept slavery as God's will. The Associates of Dr. Bray, a church charity in London, set up the school on Benjamin Franklin's advice. Thomas Dawson, the president of the College of William & Mary, backed it. One teacher, Ann Wager, taught there for all fourteen years.

The pupils were three to ten years old, and thirty or more might crowd into the room on an ordinary day. They learned to spell and to read aloud clearly. They worked up through *The Child's First Book*, the Psalms, the New Testament, the Book of Common Prayer and the Bible. Lessons began with the pupils reciting the Apostles' Creed, the Lord's Prayer and the catechism from memory. Girls were also taught to sew.

The count of pupils is not settled. Colonial Williamsburg states that "hundreds of students attended." William & Mary gives 300 to 400, and Encyclopedia Virginia gives about 200. The names of more than 80 pupils survive on three school lists, from 1762, 1765 and 1769. One of them is a child named Locust, sent by the Armistead family, who enslaved people near Williamsburg.

William & Mary's own account states that the school's "faith-based curriculum justified slavery." Ann Wager taught enslaved children that their slavery was God's plan. In the same lessons, the children learned to read.

The school closed in 1774, when Ann Wager died. A second Bray school, at Fredericksburg, Virginia, ran from 1765 to about 1769 under the merchant Fielding Lewis, with 40 to 50 pupils. It closed because fewer pupils came and local enslavers opposed it.

The Williamsburg schoolhouse still stands. In 2020 researchers confirmed that a building on the William & Mary campus, later a private home and then student housing, was the school. On February 10, 2023, it was moved to Colonial Williamsburg. It is the oldest surviving schoolhouse for Black children in the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ann-wager" name="Ann Wager" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ann Wager

> **Who:** The only teacher the Williamsburg Bray School ever had. She was hired to teach Black children to read and to teach them that slavery was right.
> **When and where:** Williamsburg, Virginia, 1760 to 1774.

Ann Wager taught every class the Williamsburg Bray School ever held. She was born in about 1716. Her husband, William Wager, died in 1748, and she began teaching to support herself.

From 1760 she was the Bray School's only teacher. She was paid 20 pounds a year, and her rent was paid too. Colonial Williamsburg puts that at "roughly equivalent to a journeyman worker's yearly wage." A journeyman was a skilled worker who had finished an apprenticeship and worked for wages. She taught at the school until she died on August 20, 1774, and the school closed with her death.

Her pupils were enslaved and free Black children of Williamsburg. She taught them to spell, to read aloud, and to recite prayers and the catechism. The men who ran the charity in London wanted the children taught that being enslaved was God's plan for them and that they should obey. The children came out of her lessons able to read.

One of her pupils, on the 1765 list, was a boy named Isaac Bee. In the summer of 1774, when he was eighteen or nineteen, he ran away from Lewis Burwell, the man who claimed to own him. In September 1774 Burwell placed an advertisement in the *Virginia Gazette*. He wrote that Isaac Bee believed he had a right to be free, because his father was a free man. He offered a reward of 40 shillings, a shilling being one twentieth of a pound, for Isaac Bee's return. He warned that Isaac Bee could read, and that "he may easily get some One to forge a Pass for him." Bray School researchers at William & Mary matched the runaway to the pupil on the 1765 list. The advertisement does not say whether he was caught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ann-wager" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Georgia copies South Carolina's ban" -->
In 1755 the members of Georgia's colonial House of Assembly passed the colony's first slave code, a set of laws for controlling enslaved people. Its authors copied South Carolina's act of 1740 and made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to write, or to use one as a scribe. The assembly passed another slave law in 1770. The records disagree on whether the 1770 law also banned teaching enslaved people to read, or whether that ban came only in 1829.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Moor's school, Samson Occom and Dartmouth" -->
In 1754 Eleazar Wheelock, a Connecticut minister, opened Moor's Indian Charity School at Lebanon, Connecticut, for Native students. From 1761 he took girls as well. Students past and present complained that Wheelock made the Native students do more manual labor, hard work with the hands, than was fair. He quarreled with many of his best students, including Samson Occom, a Mohegan minister who had studied with him. In 1769 Oneida families took all their children out of the school. The Dartmouth scholars who edit Wheelock's papers write that the Oneida acted "perhaps due to concerns about corporal punishment." Corporal punishment means hitting a child as a punishment.

From 1766 to 1768 Occom preached across England and Scotland to raise money for the school. He raised 12,026 pounds. Wheelock used the money to start a college in New Hampshire instead. The royal governor of New Hampshire, John Wentworth, signed Dartmouth College's charter in 1769. The charter named its purpose as "the education and instruction of youth of the Indian tribes in this land," for "civilizing and christianizing children of pagans." In the charter, pagans meant people who were not Christian. Only two Native students went with Wheelock to New Hampshire. Occom never set foot on the campus of the college his tour paid for. About 75 Native students enrolled at Dartmouth before 1972.

Wheelock enslaved people. Dartmouth's own researchers have found papers naming at least seventeen people he enslaved, among them Exeter, Chloe, Hercules, Peggy and Caesar. Between 1743 and 1769 his papers record that he bought six men and two women.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="noah-webster" name="Noah Webster" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Noah Webster

> **Who:** A schoolteacher who wrote the spelling book that most American children learned from, and later a dictionary.
> **When and where:** Born in West Hartford, Connecticut, on October 16, 1758. Died in New Haven on May 28, 1843.

Noah Webster wrote the spelling book that American children learned from for more than a hundred years. He was born on October 16, 1758, in the part of Hartford, Connecticut, that is now West Hartford.

He finished Yale in 1778. He taught school in Glastonbury, Hartford and West Hartford, and in 1782 in Goshen, New York. He believed the new country needed its own schoolbooks and one settled way of spelling to hold it together.

In 1783 he published the first part of *A Grammatical Institute of the English Language*. People called it the Blue-Backed Speller, after its blue cover. It did not change many spellings. It took spelling rules that earlier dictionaries had used only some of the time, and it used them every time.

Families and schools used the speller from 1783 into the early 1900s. By one estimate it had sold 15 million copies by 1837 and about 60 million by 1890. Other estimates run as high as 100 million copies in all. Nobody kept an exact count.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="noah-webster" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Teaching as a job" -->
In these years teaching was not yet a career with its own training or pay scale. Webster's few years in four schools, on his way to law and writing, are one recorded pattern. Other men made teaching their life's work. Ezekiel Cheever taught for 70 years and Christopher Dock for more than 40. Nobody counted teachers in the 1700s, so nobody knows which pattern was more common.
