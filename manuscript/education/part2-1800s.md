## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Horace Mann argued in Massachusetts for schools that were free to the family and paid for by taxes. Members of school boards hired more and more women to teach, because they could pay a woman far less than a man. In Massachusetts in 1838 a woman teacher earned $6.49 a month and a man $23.10. The first school paid for by a state to train teachers opened in 1839. Deaf children and blind children got schools of their own. In the South, lawmakers made teaching an enslaved person to read a crime and wrote whipping into the punishment. In Boston, school officials kept a five-year-old Black girl out of the school nearest her home. Judges on the state's highest court ruled that the city could keep her out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horace Mann and the common school" -->
In 1837 Massachusetts lawmakers set up a state Board of Education to look after the common schools. A common school was a school meant for all the children of a town, paid for by the town's taxes instead of fees from parents. Horace Mann, a lawyer and state senator, became the board's first secretary. Under the law, the board's members could not make any town do anything. Mann's job was to gather facts, write reports and argue. He did it for eleven years, until 1848.

Mann wrote twelve yearly reports, and in 1838 he started a magazine, the *Common School Journal*. He made the same three arguments again and again. Every child had a right to an education. Taxes on everyone should pay for schools, instead of fees charged to parents. Teachers should be trained before they taught.

The free school Mann argued for was in practice a school for white children. While he was secretary, Boston's school officials ran separate schools for Black children. Two years after he left the board, Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw of the state's highest court ruled that the city could keep them separate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Horace Mann

> **Who:** The first secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education. For eleven years he argued that schools should be free and paid for by taxes.
> **When and where:** Born in Franklin, Massachusetts, on May 4, 1796. Died in 1859.

Horace Mann spent eleven years arguing that every child should have a free school. As a boy he had spent only a few weeks a year in school himself. He was born on May 4, 1796, into a poor farming family in Franklin, Massachusetts. His father died when he was thirteen. He braided straw for the hat-makers of Franklin to earn money. Until he was about fifteen he spent no more than about ten weeks a year in school. A different retelling of his own memories puts it at no more than six weeks a year between the ages of ten and twenty.

A traveling teacher named Barrett taught him Latin and Greek, and he got into Brown University. He graduated first in his class in 1819. He became a lawyer, and then a member of the Massachusetts House of Representatives from 1827 and the state Senate from 1835. He became president of the Senate.

In 1837 he became secretary of the new state Board of Education. In 1843 he visited schools in Prussia and Saxony, two German states, and in Holland. He wrote about that trip in his seventh report, and thirty-one schoolmasters of Boston attacked it in print.

In 1848 he was elected to the US House of Representatives, in the seat left empty when John Quincy Adams died. There he spoke against slavery and against letting it spread. In 1853 he became the first president of Antioch College, in Yellow Springs, Ohio. He died in 1859.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="horace-mann" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The one-room school and the school day" -->
A country school of these years had one room and one teacher. Pupils of every age, from small children to nearly grown young men and women, sat together. A wood stove stood in the middle of the room. Children wrote on slates, thin boards of dark stone, with slate pencils, and wiped them clean to use again. At the front stood a recitation bench. A pupil came up to the bench and said a lesson out loud to the teacher while the others worked at their desks. Many children walked to school.

Accounts of country schools from before about 1900 describe a school year of two terms. A summer term, roughly May to August, was for the younger children, and women taught it. A winter term, roughly November to April, was for the older boys, who could be spared from farm work then. Men were often hired for the winter term to keep order. Children stayed home to plant in the spring and to harvest in the autumn.

Part of a teacher's pay was room and meals. The teacher lived in the house of each family in the district in turn, one family after another, through the term. This was called boarding round.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first school for teachers, 1839" -->
The first school paid for by a state to train teachers opened at Lexington, Massachusetts, on July 3, 1839. Three students came on the first day. Schools like it were called normal schools. The name comes from the French *école normale*, a school that taught the norms, or rules, of teaching. The principal was Cyrus Peirce, a minister whom Horace Mann had persuaded to come from Nantucket. By 1842 the school had 42 students. It moved twice and is now Framingham State University.

Members of the Massachusetts Board of Education set the entry rules for the state's normal schools. A man had to be at least 17 years old and a woman at least 16. Every student had to pass a test in spelling, geography and arithmetic. The course lasted one year and was free to a student who promised to teach in the state's schools.

In 1843 Mary Elizabeth Miles graduated from the Lexington normal school. She was the first African American to graduate from any public teachers college in the United States. She married Henry Bibb, a man who had escaped slavery, and worked with him in Ontario, in Canada, in the movement to end slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-swift-education" name="Mary Swift" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Swift

> **Who:** A seventeen-year-old in the first class of the first state school for teachers in America. She kept a journal of it.
> **When and where:** Born in Nantucket, Massachusetts, on June 22, 1822. Died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on March 2, 1909.

Mary Swift was one of the first people in America trained to teach at a state school for teachers, and her journal of it survives. She entered the new normal school at Lexington in 1839, three weeks after her seventeenth birthday. Her class graduated on April 4, 1840.

She wrote in her journal through the school's first term. The principal, Cyrus Peirce, kept a journal of the same days. Both survive, and in 1926 they were printed together as one book, *The First State Normal School in America: The Journals of Cyrus Peirce and Mary Swift*.

In 1841 she became the teacher of Laura Bridgman, a deaf and blind girl at the Perkins Institution in Boston. Swift spoke to her by spelling words letter by letter into her hand. She kept the job until she married Edwin Lamson in 1846. In 1878 she published a book about Bridgman, built mostly from her teachers' journals. It was called *Life and Education of Laura Dewey Bridgman, the Deaf, Dumb, and Blind Girl*. In that title, "dumb" meant unable to speak.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-swift-education" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why school boards hired women" -->
Members of school boards hired women because they could pay them far less. School journals of the time printed the monthly wages, and a teacher usually got her room and meals on top of the wage. In Connecticut in 1838, with about as many men as women teaching, men were paid $14.50 a month and women $5.75. In Massachusetts that year, 2,411 men and 3,826 women taught. The men were paid $23.10 a month and the women $6.49. The gap was smaller in Pennsylvania, where many more men than women taught: men got $18.50 a month and women $11.30.

The officials who made the savings said so in writing. Samuel Lewis, the Superintendent of Common Schools, or official in charge of Ohio's common schools, wrote in his report of 1839 that counties that hired women "are able to do twice as much with the same money." He went on: "As the business of teaching is made more respectable, more females engage in it, and the wages are reduced." In his fourth report, in 1841, Horace Mann welcomed the "change . . . rapidly taking place" in the hiring of women teachers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="catharine-beecher-education" name="Catharine Beecher" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Catharine Beecher

> **Who:** She ran a school for girls, then spent decades arguing that teaching was women's work.
> **When and where:** Born in East Hampton, New York, on September 6, 1800. Died in Elmira, New York, in 1878.

Catharine Beecher spent decades arguing that teaching was women's work, and she helped send hundreds of women out to teach. She was the eldest child of the preacher Lyman Beecher and a sister of Harriet Beecher Stowe. In 1822 the man she was engaged to marry, a Yale mathematics professor named Alexander Metcalf Fisher, drowned when the ship *Albion* wrecked off the coast of Ireland. She never married. She went into teaching.

Accounts differ on when she opened the Hartford Female Seminary in Hartford, Connecticut. Today in Connecticut History and the National Women's History Museum give May 20, 1823, and say she opened it with her sister Mary. Connecticut History, a state history website, gives 1824 and her sister Isabella. The girls there studied geography, world history, Latin, rhetoric, philosophy and the sciences. Most girls' schools of the time taught manners and fine arts instead. Within three years the school had 100 full-time pupils. In 1831 she moved west and opened the Western Female Institute in Cincinnati. It ran out of money after a money crisis called the Panic of 1837.

In 1835 she published *An Essay on the Education of Female Teachers*. In it she argued that teaching was part of a mother's work and that women were the right people to raise the next generation. If the country's leading women became "refined, well educated, pious and active," she wrote, "the salt is scattered through the land to purify and save." Pious means devoted to religion. By the salt she meant women like these, spread across the country as teachers, making it better. In 1847 she founded the Board of National Popular Education. Its members trained women in Hartford and sent them to teach in the West and the South. By 1857 the board's own reports counted 452 teachers sent. In 1852 she founded the American Woman's Educational Association.

She opposed giving women the vote. She believed a woman's proper places were the home and the schoolroom.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="catharine-beecher-education" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hitting children, and an argument about it" -->
In his seventh report, in 1843, Horace Mann argued against beating children as the ordinary way to keep order in a classroom. He also argued for teaching reading by whole words, instead of letter by letter. In 1844 thirty-one Boston schoolmasters answered him in print, in a pamphlet called *Remarks on the Seventh Annual Report of the Hon. Horace Mann*. They attacked his plan to train teachers, and they defended beating. Mann answered them in a second pamphlet that same year, and in 1845 they answered him again. All three pamphlets survive, and the Library of Congress holds copies.

A novel of the time describes the tools of punishment in a village schoolroom. Charles Dickens, an English writer, published *The Old Curiosity Shop* in 1840 and 1841. In it, a cane and a ruler hang on hooks on the schoolroom wall. Near them, on a shelf, sits "the dunce's cap, made of old newspapers." A dunce's cap was a pointed paper hat that a teacher made a pupil wear to shame him. This English novel is one of the earliest written descriptions of a dunce's cap kept in a schoolroom. Nobody counted how often American teachers made a pupil wear one.

The word dunce comes from the name of John Duns Scotus, a thinker who died in 1308 and was admired in his own lifetime. Later scholars who disliked his way of arguing used his followers' name as an insult, and dunce came to mean a stupid person. A popular story says his followers wore pointed hats to funnel knowledge into their heads. No record supports that story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The books children learned from" -->
William Holmes McGuffey taught at Miami University in Ohio from 1826 to 1836. A Cincinnati publishing firm, Truman and Smith, paid him to make a set of reading books. The First and Second *Eclectic Readers* came out in 1836, the Third and Fourth in 1837, the Fifth in 1844 and the Sixth in 1857. Eclectic means chosen from many sources. The readers mixed proverbs, grammar and passages from Shakespeare with lessons about patriotism, religion, good behavior, games and sports. Librarians at Miami University keep more than 300 editions of the readers. They estimate that the readers sold more than 150 million copies over about a hundred years, most of them in the Midwest.

Children still learned spelling from Noah Webster's speller. In 1828 Webster published *An American Dictionary of the English Language*, with 70,000 words, their meanings and where they came from. A set cost $20.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools for deaf children and blind children" -->
In 1815 Dr. Mason Fitch Cogswell and other Hartford men asked Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet to go to Europe and learn how teachers there taught deaf children. In Paris he studied at a school for the deaf, and he came back with Laurent Clerc, a deaf French teacher. With Cogswell and ten other Hartford men behind them, they opened the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, on April 15, 1817. The first classes met in a hotel building, with seven full-time pupils. One of them was Cogswell's deaf daughter, Alice. Lessons were in sign language, a language made with the hands.

In 1819 Connecticut's lawmakers voted the school a yearly grant of money. The school's own history describes this as the first state aid to a school in America. In 1820 members of Congress gave the school land in the Alabama Territory.

The first school for blind children in the United States was set up in Boston on March 2, 1829. Dr. John Dix Fisher started it after he saw a school for blind children in Paris. Samuel Gridley Howe became its first director in 1831. The school opened in 1832 in his father's house, with six pupils. In 1839 Thomas Handasyd Perkins gave money for a larger building, and the school was named for him. It is Perkins School for the Blind today.

Laura Bridgman was born in Hanover, New Hampshire, on December 21, 1829. When she was two, scarlet fever, a disease that causes a high fever and a rash, left her deaf and blind. She came to the Boston school on October 12, 1837, two months before her eighth birthday. Howe taught her by touch. He gave her raised letters and objects together, again and again. After about two months she understood that the letters stood for the things. She was the first deaf and blind person in the United States to receive a formal education.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A Catholic school system begins" -->
In the public schools of Philadelphia, the school day began with a reading aloud from the King James Bible, the Bible used by Protestants. The Catholic Church used a different English Bible, the Douay-Rheims. In New York, Catholic families said the public schools were Protestant schools that mocked their religion.

The first free Catholic school in the country was older than this fight. In 1810 Elizabeth Ann Seton and her Sisters of Charity, a group of Catholic nuns, opened Saint Joseph's Academy and Free School at Emmitsburg, Maryland. They taught the poor girls of the parish, the area served by their church, for no charge.

In New York City a private group called the Public School Society ran the public schools with state money. In June 1840 John Hughes, a Catholic bishop in New York, challenged the Society. A bishop is a church leader in charge of the priests and churches of a whole area. Governor William Seward suggested that Catholics ask for a share of the school money, and New York Catholics asked for it, to run schools of their own. In April 1842 state lawmakers shut down the Society. They put elected school boards in its place and banned religious teaching in public schools, which was not what Hughes had asked for. Protestants still controlled the elected boards. Hughes set out to build a separate system of Catholic schools. Someone threw stones at his house.

In Philadelphia the fight ended in killings. In 1842 Bishop Francis Patrick Kenrick asked the city's Board of Controllers of Public Schools to let Catholic children read their church's Bible. He also objected that Catholic children had to sing Protestant hymns in school. In February 1844 a school director in Kensington, Hugh Clark, suggested stopping the Bible readings until the board could agree on a rule. A rumor spread that he had ordered the Bible out of the schools.

On May 6, 1844, nativists held a meeting in Kensington, and a fight began. A nativist was someone who opposed immigrants. George Shiffler, a young nativist, was shot dead. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia's account of the riots does not say who shot him. By the end of the day a second man had been killed, and that account says he was apparently only watching. Several more people were wounded, and two of them later died.

On May 8 mobs burned houses, a Catholic seminary, which is a school for priests, and two Catholic churches, St. Michael's and St. Augustine's. Hugh Clark's house was among the houses burned. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia does not name the people in the mobs.

In July, in Southwark, a Catholic priest's brother stored guns in the basement of the Church of St. Philip Neri. Crowds gathered and demanded the guns, and the sheriff called in the militia, the state's citizen soldiers. From July 5 to 7 the crowd and the militia fought each other with guns. At least two people in the crowd were killed at once. By the next morning four militiamen and probably about a dozen people in the crowd were dead. Counts that add up the dead from May and July put the total at about twenty or more.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Teaching an enslaved person to read becomes a crime" -->
Between 1819 and 1834, lawmakers in six Southern states made it a crime to teach enslaved people to read. The states were Virginia, North Carolina, Georgia, Louisiana, Alabama and South Carolina. The lawmakers wrote the punishments into the laws. The South Carolina law of 1740 had banned only the teaching of writing. The new laws banned reading too, and some reached free Black people as well.

Virginia's lawmakers widened their laws in three steps. On January 24, 1804, they barred enslaved people from meeting at night at churches, meetinghouses or anywhere else. On March 2, 1819, they banned enslaved people from "any school or schools for teaching them reading or writing, either in the day or night." On April 7, 1831, they reached free Black people too: "All meetings of free negroes or mulattoes, at any school house...for teaching them reading or writing...shall be considered as an unlawful assembly." An unlawful assembly was a meeting the law banned, which officials could break up. Negro was a word used then for a Black person, and mulatto for a person with one Black parent and one white parent.

North Carolina's lawmakers passed their law in the session of 1830 and 1831. Its opening words state their reason. Teaching enslaved people to read and write "has a tendency to excite dissatisfaction in their minds, and to produce insurrection and rebellion." An insurrection is an armed uprising. The law set the punishments by who the teacher was:

- A white person who taught an enslaved person to read or write was fined between $100 and $200, or jailed.
- A free Black person who did it could be whipped, with no fewer than 20 and no more than 39 lashes, or else fined or jailed, as the court chose.
- An enslaved person who taught another enslaved person got 39 lashes on the bare back.

A lash is one blow with a whip. A whipping under these laws meant blows across the bare back with a leather whip or a switch, a thin stick cut from a tree, hard enough to break the skin. Thirty-nine was the usual highest number of lashes in Southern law. Under the same North Carolina law, judges had to instruct grand juries to enforce it. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether a person should be charged with a crime.

The other states set their own punishments. Georgia's lawmakers passed their law in 1829. A Black person who taught another to read or write was fined and whipped. A white person was fined up to $500 and could be jailed. Louisiana's lawmakers in 1830 set one to twelve months in prison for anyone who taught an enslaved person to read or write. Alabama's lawmakers in 1832 set a fine of $250 to $500 for teaching any Black person, enslaved or free. Under Virginia's law of 1831, any judge or justice of the peace, a local judge, could break up a school for free Black people. He could also order up to twenty lashes for each person caught there. A white person found guilty of teaching Black people to read or write was fined $50 and could be jailed for two months. Teaching an enslaved person carried a fine of $10 to $100. South Carolina's lawmakers widened their old law in 1834.

Only the North Carolina, Georgia and Virginia laws set the whip, and only for Black people. Louisiana's and Alabama's laws set the same prison term or fine for every teacher, whatever the teacher's race.

Two events fell in the middle of these years. In 1829 David Walker published his *Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World*. Walker was a free Black man from North Carolina who ran a used-clothing store in Boston. His *Appeal* urged Black people to resist slavery, and sailors carried copies into the South. The Georgia, Louisiana and North Carolina laws, and Virginia's law of 1831, all came after it. In August 1831 Nat Turner, an enslaved preacher, led a revolt of enslaved people in Southampton County, Virginia. They killed at least 55 white people. Afterward, militias and other armed white men killed more than 100 Black people, and some estimates run to 200. Many of them had no part in the revolt. A county court led by Judge Jeremiah Cobb sentenced Turner to death, and the sheriff hanged him in Jerusalem, Virginia, on November 11, 1831. Alabama's law of 1832 and South Carolina's of 1834 came after the revolt. Virginia's laws of 1804 and 1819 came before both events.

Enslaved people learned to read anyway. About 5 percent of enslaved people in Virginia could read by 1775. In the decades before the Civil War, while these laws were in force, the share doubled to about 10 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal money for schooling Native children, from 1819" -->
Federal officials began paying for the schooling of Native children in 1819. On March 3, 1819, the members of Congress passed the Civilization Fund Act. Under it, the lawmakers set aside $10,000 a year to hire people to teach Native nations farming and to teach their children "reading, writing, and arithmetic." The law states its purpose as "providing against the further decline and final extinction of the Indian tribes" and "introducing among them the habits and arts of civilization." Extinction here meant the Native nations dying out completely. Government officials gave much of the money to churches and other religious groups. Lawmakers in Congress kept paying into the fund until 1873. In a report published in 2022, investigators for the US Department of the Interior date the start of the federal Indian boarding school system to 1819.

The Choctaw Academy in Kentucky was one of these schools. It opened in November 1825 on the farm of Richard Mentor Johnson, a US senator who later became Vice President. Twenty-one Choctaw boys came first. Its first head was a Baptist minister, Thomas Henderson. It was paid for with the Choctaw Nation's own money. Under a treaty of 1825, a written agreement between the United States and the Choctaw Nation, the US government owed the Choctaw $6,000 a year for schooling, and that money went to Johnson's school. Choctaw leaders tried to break with the academy and take back control of the money in 1829 and again in 1833. They failed.

More than 600 students from 17 Native nations went to the Choctaw Academy. Johnson enslaved at least sixty people. Among them were Julia Chinn and the two daughters she had with him, Adaline and Imogene. By law he owned Chinn, and she could not refuse him. Enslaved people did most of the farming, building and cleaning at the school. Inspectors who visited in 1840 "confirmed the many complaints of poor living conditions, inadequate education, food, and clothing, as well as forced manual labor." Inadequate means not good enough. In 1842 the Choctaw leader Peter Pitchlynn took every Choctaw student out of the school. The last thirteen Chickasaw students left in 1848.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A school open to Black and white students dragged away: Canaan, 1835" -->
In 1835 men in Canaan, New Hampshire, drove Black students out of their school. Noyes Academy in Canaan had opened in March 1835 with 28 white students and 14 Black students. Its trustees, the people in charge of the school, had voted in 1834 to admit students of every race. The Black students included Henry Highland Garnet, Alexander Crummell, Thomas S. Sidney and Julia Williams.

In July 1835 the voters at a Canaan town meeting declared the school a public nuisance, meaning a harm to the town. On August 10, 1835, men from Canaan and the towns around it hitched teams of oxen to the school building with chains. They pulled it off its foundation and dragged it down the road. The town's own history says it took two days, and the building ended as a heap near the meeting house. One account counts about seventy-five men and nearly a hundred oxen. Another gives a hundred yoke of oxen. A yoke is a pair of oxen joined by a wooden frame, so a hundred yoke would be two hundred animals. Garnet fired a shotgun from a window, and the crowd drew back. The townspeople drove the Black students out of Canaan. No record shows that anyone was ever punished for it. Garnet, Crummell and Sidney went on to study at the Oneida Institute, near Utica, New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-roberts-education" name="Sarah Roberts" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Roberts

> **Who:** A five-year-old Black girl put out of the Boston school nearest her home. Her father sued the city.
> **When and where:** Boston, Massachusetts, 1848 to 1850.

Sarah Roberts was five years old when members of Boston's school committee kept her out of the school nearest her home, because she was Black. She lived on Andover Street in Boston. The school city officials let her attend was the Abiel Smith School, the school for Black children. To get there she had to walk past five schools for white children.

Her father, Benjamin F. Roberts, asked the Boston Primary School Committee four times to let her into the nearest school. Each time the committee members refused. In 1848 he sent her to the nearest school anyway, and she was put out of it. The National Park Service's account of the case does not name the person who put her out. He sued the city under a Massachusetts law of 1845. That law let a child who had been wrongly shut out of a public school claim damages, a payment of money for the harm.

Her lawyers were Charles Sumner and Robert Morris. Morris was twenty-five and one of the first Black lawyers in the United States. The judges of the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, the state's highest court, heard the case in 1849. In 1850 Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw ruled for the city. He wrote that the school committee "have come to the conclusion, that the good of both classes of school will be best promoted, by maintaining the separate primary schools for colored and for white children." Colored was a word used then for Black people.

Black Bostonians kept up the fight. In 1855 Massachusetts lawmakers outlawed separate public schools by race.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these fifty years, state lawmakers began to order children to go to school, starting with Massachusetts in 1852. Lawmakers in Congress gave states public land, which the United States had taken from Native nations, to found colleges. Nearly four million people had been enslaved in 1860. Once freed, they built schools, and by 1869 they paid for all or part of 1,094 of them, by the count of a federal inspector. White men burned some of those schools, whipped their teachers and killed at least one teacher, and the same inspector wrote that down too. Southern lawmakers made separate schools for Black and white children the law. In 1896 the justices of the Supreme Court called separate schools lawful in a case about train cars. In 1899 they refused to stop a Georgia school board that had closed a Black high school and kept paying for white ones. From 1879 federal officials sent Native children to boarding schools far from home, where staff cut their hair, took their clothes and forbade their languages. More than 180 children died at Carlisle, the first federal boarding school built away from the reservations, the lands set aside for Native nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Laws that made children go to school" -->
A compulsory attendance law is a law that requires children to go to school. Massachusetts lawmakers passed the first one in 1852. It covered children from 8 to 14 years old. Each child had to attend at least twelve weeks a year, six of them in a row. By 1900, 32 states had such a law. By 1918 every state had one. Mississippi was the last. How strictly officials enforced the early laws is not known.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colleges built with Native land: the Morrill Acts" -->
On July 2, 1862, the Morrill Act became law. Under it, each state received 30,000 acres of public land for each of its senators and representatives in Congress. State officials sold the land and used the money to found colleges that taught agriculture and the mechanic arts, which means engineering and machine work. These were called land-grant colleges. At them, the children of farmers and working people could go to college.

The United States had taken the land from Native nations. In 2020 two journalists, Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone, traced it through land records. By their count it came to about 10.7 million acres, taken from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities. US officials took it through more than 160 land cessions. A land cession was an agreement in which a nation gave up land, and Lee and Ahtone write that these were backed by violence. US officials paid less than $400,000 for that land. Its sale raised $17.7 million for 52 colleges.

In 1890 members of Congress passed a second Morrill Act. Under it, states got money instead of land. The law required each state's lawmakers either to admit Black students to the land-grant college the state already had or to set up a separate one for them. Lawmakers in most Southern states set up separate colleges. Some of today's historically Black colleges and universities, the colleges first founded for Black students, began that way. Alcorn State University in Mississippi, founded in 1871, is the oldest public historically Black land-grant college.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The schools freed people built" -->
In March 1865 members of Congress set up the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, called the Freedmen's Bureau, inside the War Department. Its officials helped people who had just been freed from slavery. John W. Alvord was its inspector of schools. He wrote a report on the schools every six months, from 1866 to 1870.

In the half year that ended on December 31, 1869, Alvord counted 4,027 schools of all kinds, 8,907 teachers and 210,018 pupils. He explained what his own count meant. A person who went to more than one kind of school was counted more than once. So the total showed "the amount of tuition furnished, rather than the exact number of persons taught." He meant that the total measured how much teaching was given, not how many different people were taught.

Freed people paid for all or part of 1,094 of the day and night schools. In Alvord's words, they "sustained wholly or in part 1,094" of them, and they owned 554 of the school buildings. Bureau officials supplied 635 buildings.

Many children had to pick cotton in the autumn, so many schools did not open until December. Many others ran right through the summer. In July 1869, 1,020 schools were open. In August the number was 1,048, and in September 1,482. In that half year, 34,320 pupils were advanced readers, 27,471 studied geography, 30,749 studied arithmetic and 39,437 studied writing. Another 3,834 were in high schools and normal schools, training for professions.

Alvord estimated that in its five years the Bureau's schools had given "an elementary education to about one million of pupils." That did not count the parents and other adults who had learned to read at home, many of them from their own children. Bureau officials stopped paying for schools in 1870.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attacks on the freed people's schools" -->
White men burned freed people's schools, whipped their teachers and killed at least one of them, and Alvord recorded the attacks in his reports. In his report of January 1868 he wrote: "Teachers were proscribed and ill-treated, school-houses burned, and threats so strong that many schools could not be opened." Proscribed means openly condemned and shut out. In the Oxford district of Mississippi, "A Mr. Borum started a school about twelve miles from this place and it was immediately burned." The report does not say who burned it.

His report of January 1870 names places and people. In Georgia late in 1869, bands of "armed and disguised men," which the report calls "K. K. K.," attacked people in county after county. K. K. K. stands for the Ku Klux Klan. The Confederacy was the group of Southern states that broke away and fought the United States in the Civil War. Men who had fought for it started the Klan in Pulaski, Tennessee, in December 1865. Its members whipped, burned and murdered freed people and the people who helped them. At Greensboro, Georgia, men drove the teacher R. H. Gladding out of town. The white man he boarded with was "taken out of his own house in the night and unmercifully whipped." Abram Colby had backed the school, and he had just been elected as one of the state's lawmakers. He was a Black man, and he "was also taken out of his house and beaten nearly to death." The report does not name the men who did any of this. The mayor told Gladding that the town's officials could not protect him.

In Dresden, Tennessee, two students from Fisk University ran a school of about fifty children. On the night of September 2, 1869, "men masked and armed" took them from their boarding house into the woods. The men "severely whipped" them and told them to stop teaching and leave or be killed. The report states that local officials were told about it and "nothing was done in the matter." At Slaughter Neck, Delaware, drunken white men burned the school. The report states that the evidence was given to the state's attorney and the governor was told, "yet nothing was done to bring the parties to justice." It does not say who gave the evidence. Black families there rebuilt the school with help from a Delaware charity and some Bureau money.

In May 1866 white mobs and police in Memphis, Tennessee, killed 46 Black people. They burned every Black church and school in the city, 8 schools in all, by the count of a committee of Congress. On July 11, 1870, members of the Ku Klux Klan lynched William Luke at Cross Plains, Alabama. To lynch someone is to kill the person without any trial. Luke was a preacher from Flesherton, Ontario, in Canada, who taught freed people for Talladega College. The Klansmen killed Black men with him. One count gives six dead in all, and another gives eight.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charlotte-forten-education" name="Charlotte Forten" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charlotte Forten

> **Who:** The first Black graduate of the Salem Normal School and the first Black teacher in Beaufort County, South Carolina.
> **When and where:** Born in Philadelphia in 1837. Died in Washington, D.C., on July 23, 1914.

Charlotte Forten went south in 1862, while the Civil War was still being fought, to teach people who had just been freed from slavery. She was the first Black teacher in Beaufort County, South Carolina.

She was born in Philadelphia in 1837, into a well-off Black family that worked to end slavery. Philadelphia's school officials would not take her, so her family had her taught at home by tutors. In 1853 she moved to Salem, Massachusetts, to live with the Remond family and go to school there. In 1856 she graduated from the Salem Normal School, its first African American graduate. She had studied subjects from algebra and Latin to optics, the science of light. Her principal, Richard Edwards, found her a job at the Epes Grammar School. She became the first African American teacher in Salem's public schools, teaching white children.

In her journal she wrote that her time in Salem was "the happiest of my life." She also wrote: "I wonder that every colored person is not a misanthrope. Surely we have everything to make us hate mankind." A misanthrope is a person who hates other people.

In October 1862, in the middle of the war, Forten sailed to St. Helena Island, off South Carolina, to teach at the Penn School with Laura Towne and Ellen Murray. The school had opened that year with 41 pupils. By 1865 it had 436 pupils in four buildings. She taught there until the war ended and kept a journal the whole time. In May and June 1864 she published an account of it, "Life on the Sea Islands," in the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine.

After the war she taught in Boston and in Charleston, South Carolina. By 1872 she was teaching in Washington, D.C., at the school that became Dunbar High School.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charlotte-forten-education" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A teacher jailed in Norfolk, 1854" -->
Officials in Virginia used the 1831 law against a white teacher. Margaret Douglass, a white woman in Norfolk, and her daughter Rosa opened a school for free Black children in June 1852. A Mr. Robinson, whose two daughters Margaret Douglass had begun to teach, asked her to do it. The Douglasses charged three dollars for each quarter of a year. Twenty-five boys and girls came, and the school ran openly for about eleven months.

On the morning of May 9, 1853, the city constable, Cherry, came to the house with a second officer, Cox. A constable was a town officer who kept order and made arrests. Mayor Simon S. Stubbs had sent them. The officers wrote down the name of every child and every parent. They marched the two teachers and the children to the mayor's office. The mayor read out the Virginia law and told them its highest penalty was a fine of one hundred dollars and six months in jail. A grand jury charged the two women with meeting with Black children "for the purpose of instructing them to read and to write." Rosa left Norfolk for New York before the trial.

In November 1853 a jury found Margaret Douglass guilty and set her fine at one dollar. On January 10, 1854, Judge Baker of the Norfolk court added a month in the city jail. He said the jail term was "an example to all others in like cases disposed to offend." She served the month. Afterward she published her own account of the case, with the court papers printed in it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Separate schools by law, upheld by the courts" -->
After the Civil War, Southern lawmakers wrote the separation of Black and white pupils into law. Tennessee is one example. Its lawmakers passed a school law that separated the races just after the war. The state's new constitution, of 1870, forbade mixed schools. In 1873 Tennessee's lawmakers wrote the rule out in full: "white and colored persons shall not be taught in the same school, but in separate schools." In much of the North, members of city school boards kept Black children apart by their own rules. In San Francisco, school officials opened a separate school for Black children in 1854 and one for Chinese children in 1859. In 1870 they closed the Chinese school, and Chinese American children were left with no public school at all.

In 1896 the justices of the United States Supreme Court decided *Plessy v. Ferguson*, a case about train cars. In 1890 Louisiana's lawmakers had required railroads to seat Black and white passengers in separate cars. In 1892 Homer Plessy, a shoemaker in New Orleans, sat in a car for white passengers. He had arranged his arrest ahead of time, to test the law in court. The justices ruled 7 to 1 that the law was allowed. In their ruling they called separate schools "the most common instance" of lawful separation by race. The first case they cited was Sarah Roberts's case from Boston. They also cited state court rulings from Ohio, Missouri, California, Louisiana, New York, Indiana and Kentucky. They pointed out that laws passed by Congress required separate schools in Washington, D.C.

In 1899 the justices refused to stop a Georgia school board that had closed a Black high school. Since 1880 the members of the school board of Richmond County, in Augusta, Georgia, had run the Ware High School for Black students. On July 10, 1897, the board members closed it and said they needed the money for Black primary schools. They kept paying for high schools for white students. Three Black parents, J. W. Cumming, James S. Harper and John C. Ladeveze, sued. On December 18, 1899, Justice John Marshall Harlan wrote the Court's ruling. Cutting off the white schools' money, he wrote, "would only be to take from white children educational privileges enjoyed by them, without giving to colored children additional opportunities." He wrote that school funding was "a matter belonging to the respective states." The board won. The justices refused to stop the money for the white high schools. Harlan did not rule on whether separate schools themselves were lawful, because the parents had not asked the Court to.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women go to college" -->
College leaders began to admit women in these years, and new colleges opened for women only. The University of Iowa's own archive states that it admitted women "on an equal basis with men" beginning in 1860. Mary Hickey Wilkinson graduated from its law school in 1873. Madelon Stockwell entered the University of Michigan in February 1870. The first woman at Cornell University, Jennie Spencer, came in 1870. She left because there was nowhere near the campus for a woman to live. Cornell's leaders admitted women regularly from 1872.

Matthew Vassar founded Vassar College in Poughkeepsie, New York, in 1861. It opened on September 26, 1865, with 353 students. Wellesley College opened on September 8, 1875, with 314 students, and Smith College opened that fall with 14. Bryn Mawr College opened in September 1885. On April 11, 1881, Sophia B. Packard and Harriet E. Giles, with the pastor Frank Quarles, started a school in the basement of Friendship Baptist Church in Atlanta. It had eleven pupils, ten women, some of whom had been enslaved, and one girl. It became Spelman College.

Some men argued that study would harm women's bodies. In 1873 Edward H. Clarke, a professor at Harvard Medical School, published *Sex in Education*. Smith College's own history states that he warned women against studying "in a boy's way." He warned that it could make a woman's womb and ovaries waste away, make her more like a man, leave her unable to have children, drive her insane, or even kill her. Clarke wrote that the results of such study were "monstrous brains and puny bodies." Mary Putnam Jacobi answered him with evidence. She sent a thousand lists of questions to women and wrote up the answers. Her essay won Harvard's Boylston Prize in 1876. She was the first woman to win it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A one-room school, counted: the Freeman School" -->
The Freeman School near Beatrice, Nebraska, was built of brick in 1872 and used as a school until 1967, for 95 years. It had one main room and a coat hall at the front. Eighteen desks stood in three rows that stepped up toward the back. A wood stove stood in the middle of the room, and a recitation bench stood at the front.

Pupils from 4 to 20 years old learned there together, seated by age. The most pupils in any one year was 33, in 1880. From 1882 to 1900 the teacher was paid $32 to $40 a month. Running the school cost $75 in 1872. In 1873, $27 went for firewood. The building is now part of Homestead National Historical Park.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beating children, by the rules" -->
Teachers in this era still hit children, and school rules required them to write each beating down. Boston's school rules stated that each teacher had to send in a written report at the end of every month. It was a "report of all cases in which corporal punishment has been inflicted." Corporal punishment means an adult hitting a child on purpose, to hurt the child, as a punishment. The report had to "state the name of the pupil, the amount of punishment, and the reason for its infliction." The chairmen of the district committees then reported the totals every three months, along with the average for each teacher.

Under the same rules, a teacher had to explain the offense to the child first. The blows were to be only "blows on the hand with a rattan," a thin, bendable cane cut from a kind of palm. That limit did not hold when a pupil refused to take the punishment, and the rules did not say what was allowed then. No teacher was to shut a child "in a closet or wardrobe." A girl in a grammar school, the school that came after primary school, could not be hit without the approval of the master, the head teacher.

In 1867 New Jersey's lawmakers banned hitting children in the state's public schools. They were the first state lawmakers in the country to do it. No other state followed for 104 years, until Massachusetts in 1971.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Josephine Foster

> **Who:** A schoolgirl beaten for whispering in class. Her parents charged her teachers with cruelty, and the school committee took no action.
> **When and where:** The Allston Grammar School, Cambridgeport, Massachusetts, June 12, 1866.

A teacher hit Josephine Foster on the hand fifteen to twenty times at her school for whispering and then refusing to be hit. The members of the Cambridge School Committee ordered four thousand copies of their own account of what happened to her printed.

On the morning of June 12, 1866, she refused to do what the master, B. W. Roberts, told her in her writing lesson. He made her stand in front of the class. That afternoon, while he was out of the room, an assistant teacher, Sarah M. Gray, caught her whispering and asked what she thought of whispering. Josephine answered "I don't know" in a way the teacher took as defiance. Miss Gray took her and the other girl to the recitation room. She hit the other girl's hand a few times with a rattan and sent her back. Then she told Josephine to hold out her hand. Josephine said, "I won't be whipped."

Roberts came back. He would not hit her himself, and said it would be "less humiliating" for her to be hit by a woman teacher. He told Miss Gray to use his ferule instead of the cane. The committee's report describes a ferule as "a flexible instrument, made of two flat pieces of leather, sewed together, and filled with some soft substance." The report adds that the ferule was "a common implement of punishment in schools," in use in several other grammar schools in the city.

Josephine again said she would not be whipped, and she began to scream before anyone hit her. Another teacher, Miss Hodges, held her left hand down while Miss Gray struck her right hand. The screams carried into the schoolroom and set some of the boys laughing. Roberts ordered the boys to march to piano music, a regular two-minute exercise, and used those two minutes to come into the recitation room. He held her hand himself and told her she would be hit until she stopped crying out. Miss Gray struck five blows while he was there. After he left, Josephine held out her hand herself and took three to five more light blows. The report states: "In all, from fifteen to twenty blows were inflicted." For four days afterward, her left arm and thumb showed marks from being held. Red marks on her right hand were gone by eight o'clock that evening.

The report gives her age twice, and differently. On one page it says she was sixteen. On another it says her teachers thought she was "not over thirteen."

On the next day her parents, Charles and Elizabeth Foster, wrote to the school committee. They charged all three teachers "for cruelty in punishment inflicted on our daughter," and asked the committee to "have justice done us." The committee members held a hearing on June 21, 1866. Its members voted, all of them in favor, that "the case does not require any action on the part of this Board." James Walker and others sent the committee a separate petition, a written request signed by a group of people, asking its members to end the beating of girls. The committee members did not end it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josephine-foster-education" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Teaching becomes mostly women's work" -->
By the time federal officials first counted teachers across the whole country, most of them were already women. In the school year 1869 to 1870, there were about 201,000 public school teachers: 78,000 men and 123,000 women. By 1899 to 1900 there were about 423,000: 127,000 men and 296,000 women. That was seven women in every ten teachers.

The change came at different times in different regions. In 1850, 60 percent of teachers in New England were women. In the South the share was 24 percent, and in the Midwest it was 81 percent. The historians Mary Lou Breitborde and Kelly Kolodny write about school superintendents, the officials in charge of a district's schools. In their account, the superintendents were sure they were hiring young women skilled at managing children "at half the price of men."

After Massachusetts, normal schools for training teachers opened in New York and Philadelphia in the 1840s. More opened in Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Jersey, Michigan and Illinois in the 1850s, in Kansas and Wisconsin in the 1860s, and in Iowa in the 1870s. By 1870 there were 39 normal schools in 18 states. By 1900 there were 180.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the summer break came from" -->
Many people say that schools close in summer so that children could help bring in the harvest. The historian Kenneth Gold, who wrote a history of summer schooling, says the record shows the opposite. A farming district's school year had a short winter term and a short summer term, because farm families needed their children most in the spring planting and the autumn harvest. Those are the months the school year covers now.

City schools stayed open far longer. New York City's schools were open 248 days in 1842. Nobody had to attend, so children came when they could. Across the whole country, the average school term was 132 days in 1869 to 1870, and the average pupil attended 78 of those days. By 1899 to 1900 the average term was 144 days, and the average pupil attended 99. Late in the 1800s school reformers worked to make the city and country calendars match. They settled on a long summer break as the middle ground between the two. One reason for it was that teachers could be trained during the break.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Catholic schools become a system" -->
From November 9 to December 7, 1884, the Catholic bishops of the United States met in Baltimore. Fourteen archbishops came. An archbishop is a bishop who leads the main church area of a region. With them came sixty-one bishops or their representatives and six abbots, the heads of monasteries. They declared that Catholic schools were an "absolute necessity." They required every pastor, the priest in charge of a local church, to set up a school. They wanted the schools to be free where possible. They ruled: "Parents must send their children to such schools unless the bishop should judge the reason for sending them elsewhere to be sufficient." With that order the bishops set out to build a system of Catholic parish schools beside the public schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boarding schools for Native children: Carlisle" -->
In 1879 federal officials opened a boarding school for Native children at Carlisle, Pennsylvania, far from the children's homes. The man who ran it, Richard Henry Pratt, set out to wipe out the children's languages and ways of life. Speaking in Denver in 1892, he described the aim in his own words: "that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him and save the man."

Pratt was an Army officer. In 1875 Army officers arrested 72 people from several Native nations, called them ringleaders, and sent them by train to Fort Marion, a fort in St. Augustine, Florida. War Department officials put Pratt in charge of them. At Fort Marion, Pratt cut the prisoners' hair, put them in military uniforms, drilled them like soldiers, taught them English, and hired them out as laborers. He also sold the things they made and drew. Ten of the prisoners died while he held them, between 1875 and 1878. At Carlisle, Pratt and his staff later cut the children's hair, put them in uniforms, drilled them and taught them English, as he had done to the prisoners at Fort Marion.

Officials in Washington set up the school in 1879. On February 14, 1879, Spencer Baird of the Smithsonian wrote to Secretary of the Interior Carl Schurz to support Pratt's request for a school. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Ezra A. Hayt, gave his answer on August 22, 1879. He allowed Pratt to choose 70 students from the Sioux nations and 30 from the Kiowa, Comanche, Cheyenne and Arapaho nations. On August 23, 1879, Secretary of War George W. McCrary ordered the empty Carlisle Barracks, an old Army post, handed over for the school. On October 6, 1879, the first children arrived: 83 of them, 24 girls and 59 boys. They came from the Rosebud and Pine Ridge agencies in Dakota Territory, the federal offices that ran those reservations. Some accounts give 82, and the National Park Service says about 140. The school formally opened a few weeks later.

Staff cut the children's hair and took away their clothes, their moccasins and their family belongings. They dressed the boys in military uniforms and the girls in Victorian dresses, the long dresses white women wore then. They gave the children English names. The published records name Pratt, who ran the school, but not the staff members who did the cutting and the punishing. Luther Standing Bear, a Lakota boy brought to Carlisle in its first year, wrote later: "Now, after having my hair cut . . . I felt that I was no more Indian but would be an imitation of a white man."

Pratt ran the school like an Army post. The day began at six in the morning and ended with Taps, a bugle call, and room inspection at nine at night. The children marched in groups like soldiers in drills, and they changed movements when officials rang a bell. A fence six feet high ran around the grounds. Sunday school, chapel and Bible study were required. The staff forbade the children to speak their own languages, and they often enforced that rule by hitting the children. Staff sent a child who broke a rule to the guardhouse, the post's old jail, or school officials sentenced the child to hard labor, which means heavy work given as a punishment. The published records do not name the staff members who did it.

Half of each day was lessons and half was forced work without pay, and the children's work kept the school running. The President's proclamation about Carlisle in 2024, an official statement, describes it as "compulsory labor." Boys worked at blacksmithing, tinsmithing, making wagons, carpentry, tailoring, making shoes and harnesses, baking, painting, printing and farming. Girls cooked, washed laundry and cleaned. The children built a printing office, a gymnasium, a hospital, a laundry and a warehouse with their own hands. Under what Pratt called the outing system, staff sent children to live with white families and work for them for part of each year. The children's wages went into accounts held by school officials, not into their hands. School officials paid for the gymnasium with money they took from the children's own trust accounts, money the federal government kept for them, and called it donations.

More than 180 children died at Carlisle, by the federal government's count. The National Park Service counts at least 168 who died of three diseases. Tuberculosis is a disease that destroys the lungs. Pneumonia is an infection of the lungs. Influenza is the flu. Both counts are minimums, which means the real number may be higher. Many of the children are buried at the Carlisle Barracks cemetery. On May 23, 1881, Chief Spotted Tail and other Rosebud Sioux parents asked the government to send their dead children home. Army officials returned the remains of nine of the children to the Rosebud Sioux Tribe on July 14, 2021, 140 years later. Their names were Dennis Strikes First (Blue Tomahawk), Rose Long Face (Little Hawk), Lucy Take The Tail (Pretty Eagle), Warren Painter (Bear Paints Dirt), Ernest Knocks Off (White Thunder), Maud Little Girl (Swift Bear), Alvan, also called Roaster and Kills Seven Horses, Friend Hollow Horn Bear, and Dora Her Pipe (Brave Bull). The records printed with their names do not give their ages or what they died of.

By the time Carlisle closed in 1918, about 7,800 children from more than 140 Native nations had gone through the school, by the federal count. The National Park Service gives more than ten thousand from over 142 nations. Some children were five years old when they arrived. Carlisle was the first federal boarding school built away from the reservations, and officials opened many more. On March 3, 1893, members of Congress passed a law about the food rations that the government gave Native families. Rations were set amounts of food handed out at the reservations. Under the law, the Secretary of the Interior could stop the rations of any Native family whose children aged eight to twenty-one had not attended school the year before. That included rations a treaty had promised.

In 2024 officials of the US Department of the Interior counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools between 1819 and 1969. They counted at least 973 children who died in them and 74 burial sites at 65 of the schools. They identified by name 18,624 children who went to them. Their report states that the real number of deaths is higher and that its list of names is incomplete. The Department's 2022 report lists the punishments staff used across the system. They locked children alone in a room, which is called solitary confinement. They flogged children. Flogging means beating a person with a whip or a rod. They held back food, whipped children, and slapped and cuffed them, which means hitting with an open hand. The report lists flogging and whipping separately and does not say what tools staff used. Staff sometimes made older children punish younger ones. In a proclamation of December 9, 2024, President Joe Biden made the Carlisle school grounds a national monument, a place the federal government keeps for its history. It states that "many Native children were subjected to sexual abuse at the schools." Sexual abuse means forcing sexual acts on a child. The proclamation does not say who abused the children or how many.

Children kept their languages alive by speaking them in secret. At the schools they made friends with children from other nations, and those friendships were one root of the Native organizing of the next century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zitkala-sa-education" name="Zitkala-Ša" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Zitkala-Ša

> **Who:** Quaker missionaries took her from her home at eight to a boarding school in Indiana. She wrote down what was done to her there and published it.
> **When and where:** Born on the Yankton Indian Reservation, South Dakota, on February 22, 1876. Died in Washington, D.C., on January 26, 1938.

Zitkala-Ša was eight years old when staff at a boarding school tied her to a chair and cut off her braids. She later published her own account of it. Her Dakota name means Red Bird. Her English name was Gertrude Simmons. In 1884 Quaker missionaries, people sent out by the Quaker church to spread its faith, took her from the Yankton Reservation to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute, a boarding school at Wabash, Indiana. She stayed three years and went home. She went back at fifteen and graduated in 1895.

On her first day there, she learned that the staff meant to cut the girls' hair. She knew what that meant at home: "Our mothers had taught us that only unskilled warriors who were captured had their hair shingled by the enemy. Among our people, short hair was worn by mourners, and shingled hair by cowards!" Shingled means cut short. Her friend Judewin said, "We have to submit, because they are strong." Zitkala-Ša answered, "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!"

She hid under a bed in a dormitory. "I remember being dragged out, though I resisted by kicking and scratching wildly. In spite of myself, I was carried downstairs and tied fast in a chair." Then, she wrote, "I cried aloud, shaking my head all the while until I felt the cold blades of the scissors against my neck, and heard them gnaw off one of my thick braids. Then I lost my spirit." She wrote that she "moaned for my mother, but no one came to comfort me," and that she was now "only one of many little animals driven by a herder."

She went on to Earlham College in Indiana and studied violin at the New England Conservatory in Boston. In 1899, at twenty-three, she went to Carlisle to teach music. In 1900 the *Atlantic Monthly* printed three of her essays about her school years. Her braids were cut at White's Institute in Indiana, years before she worked at Carlisle. The federal proclamation of 2024 places her haircut at Carlisle, and that is wrong.

She spent the rest of her life working for Native people's rights. From 1916 she was secretary of the Society of American Indians, and she edited its *American Indian Magazine*. In 1926 she and her husband founded the National Council of American Indians, and she led it until she died. She is buried at Arlington National Cemetery. The year of death cut into her headstone, 1936, is two years wrong.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zitkala-sa-education" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="booker-t-washington" name="Booker T. Washington" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Booker T. Washington

> **Who:** Born enslaved, he learned to read first from a number on a salt barrel, and he founded the school at Tuskegee.
> **When and where:** Born at Hale's Ford, Franklin County, Virginia, in 1856. Died on November 14, 1915.

Booker T. Washington was born enslaved, and he founded a school for Black students at Tuskegee, Alabama, that he ran for 34 years. He was born in 1856 on the James Burroughs farm at Hale's Ford, in Franklin County, Virginia. He lived with his mother, Jane, who was the farm's cook, and two half-siblings in a one-room kitchen cabin. As a small boy he carried water to the field workers and carried other children's schoolbooks for them.

Slavery ended for him and his family in 1865. They moved to Malden, West Virginia, where he packed salt, mined coal and worked as a butler. The first thing he learned to read was the number 18, on the salt barrels. It was the number of his stepfather's crew of salt packers.

At sixteen he traveled more than 500 miles to the Hampton Institute in Virginia. He arrived, in his own account, dirty, hungry and with fifty cents. He paid his way by working as the school's janitor. He graduated at nineteen and went home to Malden to teach. In 1879 he went back to Hampton as a teacher.

In June 1881 he reached Tuskegee. On July 4, 1881, he opened his school there with thirty students, in a shack lent by a Black church, Butler Chapel A.M.E. Zion. He was its first teacher and its principal, and he stayed principal until he died. The students learned trades and farming along with book subjects. They made the bricks and put up the school's buildings themselves.

Washington argued that Black Americans should build skills and property first and press for their political rights later. He made that argument in a speech in Atlanta on September 18, 1895. W. E. B. Du Bois, another Black educator, named the speech the Atlanta Compromise and attacked it. In 1901 Washington became the first Black American invited to dine at the White House. He died in 1915, at 59.
