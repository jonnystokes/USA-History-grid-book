## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, dams across the rivers of New England stopped the salmon from swimming upstream to lay their eggs. By 1850 most of New England's forest had been cut down. Estimates of how much run from 60 to 80 percent of the land.

In 1832 the painter George Catlin wrote about keeping part of the Plains as "a nation's park." He meant a park that would hold both the bison and the Native nations who lived there. In these same years John James Audubon painted the birds of North America. Henry David Thoreau lived for two years in a cabin he built at Walden Pond, in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dams stop the fish" -->
Atlantic salmon live in the ocean but swim up rivers to spawn, which means to lay their eggs. A dam across a river blocks them on the way. The first dam across the Connecticut River went up at Turners Falls, Massachusetts, in 1798. It cut the salmon off from the water where they spawned. By the War of 1812, no salmon came into the Connecticut River at all.

The Great Stone Dam was built across the Merrimack River at Lawrence, Massachusetts, in 1847 and 1848. It stood 30 miles from the sea, and salmon could not get past it. By 1865 the salmon runs were gone from the rivers of southern New England. A run is the yearly trip of fish up a river. The runs of shad, another fish, collapsed as well.

In 1857 the writers of a report in New Hampshire named the causes. They blamed the dams, fishing with no limits, and mill machinery that killed young fish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bare East" -->
By 1850 most of the forest of New England had been cleared. Estimates of how much run from about 60 percent to 80 percent of the land, and some places were cleared even more. One lower figure that writers often quote says as little as a quarter of New England was still forest. The estimates do not agree.

One cause of the clearing comes with a number. By 1840 New England farmers kept about 4 million Merino sheep, a kind of sheep raised for its wool. Farmers cleared forest to make pasture for them.

Across the eastern United States, the forest reached its smallest size around 1872. By then it covered roughly half the land it had covered in 1620. After that, many eastern farmers left their farms and moved west. Trees grew back on the fields they left behind.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A park for people and bison" -->
In 1832 the painter George Catlin traveled up the Missouri River onto the northern Plains. He feared that the bison and the Plains nations would be destroyed together. He wrote of "a magnificent park," which he called "a nation's park, containing man and beast, in all the wild and freshness of their nature's beauty." In Catlin's plan, the people who lived on the land would stay on it with the animals.

The first national park came 40 years later, at Yellowstone in 1872. The officials who ran Yellowstone and later parks removed the Native people who lived on that land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-james-audubon" name="John James Audubon" movie="John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature (2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John James Audubon

> **Who:** A painter and naturalist, a person who studies animals and plants in the wild. He painted *The Birds of America*, 435 life-size pictures of birds.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1785 to 1851. His book came out from 1827 to 1838. In 1813, on the way to Louisville, Kentucky, he saw a flock of passenger pigeons that he reckoned at more than 1 billion birds.
> **Movie:** *John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature* (PBS American Masters, 2007)

John James Audubon painted the birds of North America at their real size. His book *The Birds of America* holds 435 of these paintings. It came out in parts from 1827 to 1838, and about 18 years of work lay behind it.

In 1813 Audubon was riding to Louisville, Kentucky, when a flock of passenger pigeons flew over him. Passenger pigeons were wild pigeons that flew in huge flocks. In Audubon's own account, one flock darkened the sky for three days. He did the arithmetic himself and put that single flock at more than 1 billion birds.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-james-audubon" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-david-thoreau" name="Henry David Thoreau" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry David Thoreau

> **Who:** A writer who also worked as a land surveyor, measuring woodlots for a living.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1817 to 1862. He lived in a cabin at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts, from July 4, 1845, to September 6, 1847.

Henry David Thoreau wrote books, and he earned money as a land surveyor. A surveyor measures land and maps it. Thoreau measured woodlots, pieces of land kept for growing trees.

On July 4, 1845, Thoreau moved into a cabin he had built himself at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts. He lived there until September 6, 1847, which comes to two years, two months and two days. His book about those years, *Walden*, came out in 1854.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1776 to 1887, US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres of land from Native nations, by one historian's count. In 1862 members of Congress passed laws that handed large parts of that land to railroad companies, to settlers called homesteaders and to the states for colleges.

On the Plains, hide hunters killed the bison. About 30 million bison lived there in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. Army generals and a Secretary of the Interior wrote that they welcomed the killing, because the Plains nations lived on the bison. Market hunters also killed passenger pigeons by the millions at their nesting grounds.

In the same years, President Abraham Lincoln set aside Yosemite in 1864, and President Ulysses S. Grant made Yellowstone the first national park in 1872. Volunteer soldiers had driven the Ahwahneechee people out of Yosemite Valley in 1851. At Yellowstone, the park's superintendent had the Tukudika people removed in 1879.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great taking, in acres" -->
From 1776 to 1887, US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres from Native nations. They took it by treaty and by executive order, which is a command from the president. Historian Claudio Saunt, of the University of Georgia, made this count. He built it on maps that Charles C. Royce drew in 1899. Royce mapped every piece of land the nations gave up from 1784 to 1894.

Once officials had taken the land, they called it public land, meaning land the national government claimed. Members of Congress then passed it on to railroads, settlers and colleges.

On July 1, 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. Under it, railroad companies received "every alternate section of public land, designated by odd numbers," five sections for each mile of track on each side of the line. Alternate means every other one. The act also states: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." To extinguish a title means to end someone's legal ownership of land. The lawmakers were telling officials to end the nations' ownership of the land along the railroads as fast as they could.

From 1850 to 1871, members of Congress granted railroad companies about 129 million to 131 million acres. Sources differ, and some counts reach 175 million acres by including grants the companies later lost. State lawmakers granted the railroads about 51 million acres more. The Northern Pacific railroad alone got about 44 million acres.

Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862. Under it, a person could claim up to 160 acres of public land. A claimant who lived on the land or farmed it for five years got to keep it. Over the life of the law, 270 million acres passed to private owners this way, which is 10 percent of the area of the United States. The National Park Service states that much of this land "was seized from the Native Americans who previously inhabited it." Seized means taken by force.

On July 2, 1862, Lincoln signed the Morrill Act. Under it, each state received 30,000 acres of public land for each of its senators and representatives in Congress. State officials sold the land to pay for colleges of "agriculture and the mechanic arts," which means farming and the use of machines. The National Archives, the office that keeps the government's records, states that the act "resulted in the expropriation of over 10 million acres from Native American tribal lands." Expropriation means taking property away from its owners.

In 2020 the reporters Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone, writing for the magazine *High Country News*, traced 10.7 million acres of this land. It came from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities, through more than 160 land cessions that the reporters call "violence-backed." US officials paid less than $400,000 in all to end the nations' ownership of it. By the early 1900s, sales of the land had raised $17.7 million for 52 universities. In 2020 the universities still held more than 500,000 acres of it.

One example comes from Minnesota. In the summer of 1851, four bands of Dakota people signed treaties at Mendota and Traverse des Sioux. The reporters describe the conditions as "withholding of rations, the threat of violence, enforced starvation." Rations are supplies of food. The Dakota received "less than 2.4¢ per acre." The University of Minnesota's share of the money from the land came to "nearly $580,000."

Members of Congress passed the Dawes Act in 1887. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation to live on. Under the Dawes Act, US officials cut reservations into separate plots, 160 acres for a family farm or 320 acres for grazing animals. This is called allotment. Officials then sold the land left over, which they called "surplus." By the National Park Service's count, reservation land fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres in 1934, a loss of 65 percent. The Meriam Report, another source, gives 137 million and 47 million. Either way, about 90 million acres passed out of the nations' hands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire made a crime, 1850" -->
On April 22, 1850, members of California's legislature passed a law called the *Act for the Government and Protection of Indians*. A legislature is the group of people elected to make a state's laws. Section 10 of the law made it a crime to set the prairie on fire. It also punished anyone who refused to help put out a prairie fire. Under the text, such people "shall be subject to fine or punishment, as a Court may adjudge proper." That means a judge would choose the punishment.

Section 10 did not name Native people. The whole law was about Native people, though, and burning the land on purpose was a Native practice. The news outlet ICT reports that under California law, cultural fire was "explicitly banned" for more than 150 years. Cultural fire means burning done by Native people to care for the land.

Under other sections of the same law, white people could take custody of Native children until the children were grown. Custody means control over a child and where the child lives. Officials could also arrest Native people they called "vagrants," meaning people with no home or work. Within 24 hours, officials could hire those people out "to the best bidder" for up to four months.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yosemite and the Ahwahneechee" -->
The Ahwahneechee people lived in Yosemite Valley, in California's Sierra Nevada mountains. Their headman, or leader, was Tenaya. In 1851 volunteer soldiers burned their villages and their food and drove them out of the valley.

Early in 1851, California's governor, John McDougal, called for volunteers after attacks on miners and traders. About 200 men on horseback formed the Mariposa Battalion on January 24, 1851. A battalion is a unit of soldiers. Their commander was Major James D. Savage, a trader. State officials supplied their camp gear and supplies. The battalion's pay came from federal money, handled by US Indian commissioners.

The battalion's men first entered Yosemite Valley on March 27, 1851, by the date the National Park Service gives. The Park Service states that they "systematically burned villages and food supplies and forced men, women, and children away from their homes."

Lafayette H. Bunnell rode with the battalion and wrote an account of it in 1880. He writes that in Captain John Boling's company, men on foot had orders "to burn any caches they might find." A cache is a hidden store of food. Acorns were the people's main food for the winter. On the upper San Joaquin River, next to Yosemite, Bunnell says the huts and "adjacent supplies of acorns, were at once given to the flames." He estimated the acorns burned there at 800 to 1,000 bushels, and pine nuts too. A bushel is a measure of dry goods that fills a large basket. In Yosemite Valley, Bunnell found that "nearly all the acorns had been burnt." The battalion's men marched Tenaya and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River.

Battalion men held Tenaya's youngest son and another young man as hostages, tied to a tree. A hostage is a person held prisoner to force others to do something. Bunnell writes that the guards let the two untie themselves "in order to fire upon them, expecting to kill them both." One guard shot Tenaya's son in the back and killed him. The other young man escaped. Bunnell describes the shooter only as "a young man" urged on by "an old Texan," and he names neither of them. Captain Boling told the guards "that they had committed murder." Bunnell writes that "no punishment was ever inflicted upon the perpetrators," meaning the men who did it. Tenaya died in 1853 in a fight with Mono Lake Paiutes. Accounts of the cause differ.

On June 30, 1864, President Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant. It set aside Yosemite as the first land that US lawmakers protected and kept for public use. Lincoln's government gave it to California to manage. Yosemite became a national park on October 1, 1890. The National Parks Conservation Association, a group that speaks for the parks, says the 1890 park came "with authorization to remove anyone making homes without permission." Authorization means official permission. Ahwahneechee families came back and lived in the valley anyway, and they worked for the hotels and the park.

The Park Service states that by 1910, more than 90 percent of the Ahwahneechee who had lived in the valley were dead or missing. To Tu Ya, also called Maria Lebrado Ydrte, saw the 1851 raid as a child. She saw her home in Yosemite Valley destroyed and her uncle murdered. The Park Service calls her the last survivor of the raid. In the late 1920s she came back to Yosemite and told her story.
