## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, dams across the rivers of New England stopped the salmon from swimming upstream to lay their eggs. By 1850 farmers had cut down most of New England's forest, and a boom in sheep raising was one cause.

In 1832 the painter George Catlin wrote about keeping a huge park for the bison and the Plains nations, which he called "a nation's park." He meant a park that would hold both the bison and the Native nations who lived there. In these same years John James Audubon painted the birds of North America. Henry David Thoreau lived for two years in a cabin he built at Walden Pond, in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dams stop the fish" -->
Atlantic salmon live in the ocean but swim up rivers to spawn, which means to lay their eggs. A dam across a river blocks them on the way. The first dam across the Connecticut River went up at Turners Falls, Massachusetts, in 1798. It cut the salmon off from the water where they spawned. By the War of 1812, no salmon came into the Connecticut River at all.

Workers for the Essex Company, a company formed to use the river's water to power mills, built the Great Stone Dam across the Merrimack River at Lawrence, Massachusetts. They finished it in 1848. It stood 30 miles from the sea, and salmon could not get past it. By 1865 the salmon runs were gone from the rivers of southern New England. A run is the yearly trip of fish up a river. The runs of shad, another fish, collapsed as well.

In 1857 the writers of a report in New Hampshire named the causes. They blamed the dams, fishing with no limits, and mill machinery that killed young fish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bare East" -->
By 1850 New Englanders had cut down most of their forest. Estimates of how much was cleared run from about 60 percent to 80 percent of the land, and some places were cleared even more. One estimate puts the forest left in 1850 as low as a quarter of New England.

A boom in Merino sheep, a kind of sheep raised for its wool, was one cause of the clearing. By 1840 New England farmers kept about 4 million sheep, more sheep than there were people. Farmers cleared still more forest to feed them.

Across the eastern United States, the forest reached its smallest size around 1872. By then it covered roughly half the land it had covered in 1620. After that, many eastern farmers left their farms and moved west. Trees grew back on the fields they left behind.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A park for people and bison" -->
In 1832 the painter George Catlin traveled far up the Missouri River, into the West. He feared that the bison and the Plains nations would be destroyed together. He wrote of "a magnificent park," which he called "a nation's park, containing man and beast, in all the wild and freshness of their nature's beauty." In Catlin's plan, the people who lived on the land would stay on it with the animals.

Forty years later, in 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law, and under it Yellowstone became the first national park. In 1879 Philetus W. Norris, Yellowstone's superintendent, the official in charge of the park, had the Tukudika people who lived there forced out and taken to a reservation. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation to live on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-james-audubon" name="John James Audubon" movie="John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature (2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John James Audubon

> **Who:** A painter and naturalist, a person who studies animals and plants in the wild. He painted *The Birds of America*, 435 life-size pictures of birds.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1785 to 1851. His book came out from 1827 to 1838. In 1813, on the way to Louisville, Kentucky, he saw a flock of passenger pigeons that he figured at more than 1 billion birds.
> **Movie:** *John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature* (PBS American Masters, 2007)

John James Audubon painted the birds of North America at their real size. His book *The Birds of America* contains 435 of these paintings. It came out from 1827 to 1838. Audubon worked on it for about 18 years.

In 1813 Audubon was riding to Louisville, Kentucky, when a flock of passenger pigeons flew over him. Passenger pigeons were wild pigeons that flew in huge flocks. In Audubon's own account, one flock darkened the sky for three days. He did the arithmetic himself and put that single flock at more than 1 billion birds.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-james-audubon" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-david-thoreau" name="Henry David Thoreau" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry David Thoreau

> **Who:** A writer who also worked as a land surveyor, measuring woodlots for a living.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1817 to 1862. He lived in a cabin at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts, from July 4, 1845, to September 6, 1847.

Henry David Thoreau wrote about wild nature, and he earned his money measuring land. He worked as a land surveyor, a person who measures land and maps it. Thoreau measured woodlots, pieces of land kept for growing trees.

On July 4, 1845, Thoreau moved into a cabin he had built himself at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts. He lived there until September 6, 1847, which comes to two years, two months and two days. His book about those years, *Walden*, came out in 1854.

On April 23, 1851, Thoreau gave a talk called "Walking" in Concord. It came out as an essay in June 1862, just after he died. In it he wrote: "in Wildness is the preservation of the world." Preservation means keeping something safe.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-david-thoreau" -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1862 members of Congress passed laws for giving away land taken from Native nations. Under those laws, officials gave the land to railroad companies, to settlers called homesteaders and to the states for colleges. By one historian's count, US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres from the nations between 1776 and 1887.

On the Plains, hide hunters killed most of the bison for their skins, which are called hides. About 30 million bison lived there in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. The Plains nations lived on the bison. Army generals wrote in favor of the killing, to take away the nations' food and force them onto reservations. Columbus Delano, the Secretary of the Interior and one of the president's top officials, wrote that he would not seriously regret the bison's disappearance. Without the bison, he wrote, Native people would have to live by farming. Professional hunters also killed passenger pigeons at their nesting grounds, a reported 50,000 birds a day for months at one Michigan nesting in 1878.

In 1851 volunteer soldiers drove the Ahwahneechee people out of Yosemite Valley. President Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant in 1864. In 1872 President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law, and under it Yellowstone became the first national park. In 1879 Philetus W. Norris, the park's superintendent, had the Tukudika people forced out and taken to a reservation. In 1892 John Muir helped found the Sierra Club, a club formed in part to protect the forests and mountains of California's Sierra Nevada.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Land taken from Native nations, in acres" -->
From 1776 to 1887, US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres from Native nations. They took it by treaty and by executive order. A treaty is a written agreement between nations, and an executive order is a command from the president. Historian Claudio Saunt, of the University of Georgia, made this count. He built it on maps that Charles C. Royce published in 1899. Royce mapped every piece of land that US officials took from the nations by treaty or order from 1784 to 1894.

Once officials had taken the land, it counted as public land, meaning land that US officials claimed for the nation. Members of Congress then passed it on to railroads, settlers and colleges.

On July 1, 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. Under it, railroad companies received "every alternate section of public land, designated by odd numbers." They got five of these sections for each mile of track on each side of the line. Alternate means every other one. A section is one square mile, or 640 acres. The act also states: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." To extinguish a title means to end someone's legal ownership of land. The lawmakers were telling officials to end the nations' ownership of the land along the railroads as fast as they could.

From 1850 to 1871, members of Congress granted railroad companies about 129 million to 131 million acres. The counts differ, and some reach 175 million acres by including grants the companies later lost. State lawmakers granted the railroads about 51 million acres more. The Northern Pacific railroad alone got about 44 million acres.

Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862. Under it, a person could claim up to 160 acres of public land. A person who lived on the land or farmed it for five years got to keep it. Over the life of the law, US officials handed 270 million acres to private owners this way, which is 10 percent of the area of the United States. A National Park Service page states that much of this land "was seized from the Native Americans who previously inhabited it." Seized means taken by force.

On July 2, 1862, Lincoln signed the Morrill Act. Under it, each state received 30,000 acres of public land for each of its senators and representatives in Congress. State officials sold the land to pay for colleges of "agriculture and the mechanic arts," which means farming and the use of machines. A page about the act from the National Archives, the office that keeps the government's records, states that the act "resulted in the expropriation of over 10 million acres from Native American tribal lands." Expropriation means taking property away from its owners.

In 2020 the reporters Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone, writing for the magazine *High Country News*, traced 10.7 million acres of this land. It came from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities. US officials got it through more than 160 takings of land that the reporters call "violence-backed," meaning that violence or the threat of it forced the nations to give it up. US officials paid less than $400,000 in all to end the nations' ownership of it. By the early 1900s, sales of the land had raised $17.7 million for 52 universities. In 2020 the universities still held more than 500,000 acres of it.

One example comes from Minnesota. In the summer of 1851, four bands of Dakota people signed treaties at Mendota and Traverse des Sioux. The reporters describe the conditions as "withholding of rations, the threat of violence, enforced starvation." Rations are supplies of food. The Dakota received "less than 2.4¢ per acre." The University of Minnesota's share of the money from the land came to "nearly $580,000."

Members of Congress passed the Dawes Act in 1887. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation to live on. Under the Dawes Act, US officials cut reservations into separate plots, 160 acres for a family farm or 320 acres for grazing animals. This is called allotment. Officials then sold the land left over, which they called "surplus." By the National Park Service's count, reservation land fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres in 1934, a loss of 65 percent. The Meriam Report, a 1928 study of Native life made for Secretary of the Interior Hubert Work, records 137 million and 47 million. Either way, the nations lost about 90 million acres.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire made a crime, 1850" -->
On April 22, 1850, California's state lawmakers passed a law called the *Act for the Government and Protection of Indians*. Under Section 10 of the law, it was a crime to set the prairie on fire, or to refuse to help put out a prairie fire. Section 10 states that such people "shall be subject to fine or punishment, as a Court may adjudge proper." That means a judge would choose the punishment.

Section 10 speaks of "any person or persons." The whole law was about Native people, though, and burning the land on purpose was a Native practice. Reporters at the news outlet ICT write that under California law, cultural fire was "explicitly banned" for more than 150 years. Cultural fire means burning done by Native people to care for the land.

Under other sections of the same law, white people could take custody of Native children until the children were grown. Custody means control over a child and where the child lives. Officials could also arrest Native people they called "vagrants," meaning people with no home or work. Within 24 hours, officials could hire those people out "to the best bidder" for up to four months. This was forced labor: the arrested person had to work for whoever won the bidding.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yosemite and the Ahwahneechee" -->
The Ahwahneechee people lived in Yosemite Valley, in California's Sierra Nevada mountains. Their headman, or leader, was Tenaya. In 1851 volunteer soldiers burned their villages and their food and drove them out of the valley.

James D. Savage was a trader who had moved onto Ahwahneechee land to mine gold and to trade. In December 1850, Ahwahneechee fighters attacked his trading post on the Fresno River. One account says Chowchilla fighters joined them. They took his supplies and killed two of his men.

Early in 1851, California's governor, John McDougal, called for volunteers after attacks on miners and traders. About 200 men on horseback formed the Mariposa Battalion on January 24, 1851. A battalion is a unit of soldiers. Their commander was Major James D. Savage. State officials supplied their camp gear and supplies. Federal money paid for the battalion, through federal officials called US Indian commissioners.

The battalion's men first entered Yosemite Valley on March 27, 1851, by the Park Service's date. A National Park Service page states that they "systematically burned villages and food supplies and forced men, women, and children away from their homes."

Lafayette H. Bunnell rode with the battalion and wrote an account of it in 1880. He writes that in Captain John Boling's company, men on foot had orders "to burn any caches they might find." A cache is a hidden store of food. Acorns were the people's main food for the winter.

On the upper San Joaquin River, next to Yosemite, Bunnell says the huts and "adjacent supplies of acorns, were at once given to the flames." He estimated the acorns burned there at 800 to 1,000 bushels, and pine nuts too. A bushel is a measure of dry goods that fills a large basket. In Yosemite Valley, Bunnell found that "nearly all the acorns had been burnt." The battalion's men marched Tenaya and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River.

Battalion men held Tenaya's youngest son and another young man as hostages, tied to a tree. A hostage is a person held prisoner to force others to do something. Bunnell writes that the guards let the two untie themselves "in order to fire upon them, expecting to kill them both." One guard shot Tenaya's son in the back and killed him. The other young man escaped.

Bunnell describes the shooter only as "a young man" urged on by "an old Texan," and he names neither of them. Captain Boling told the guards "that they had committed murder." Bunnell writes that "no punishment was ever inflicted upon the perpetrators," meaning the men who did it. Tenaya was killed in 1853 in a fight with Mono Lake Paiutes. Accounts of why the fight happened differ.

On June 30, 1864, President Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant. With it, US lawmakers set aside Yosemite. It was the first land that the nation's lawmakers set aside to protect and to keep for public use. California's state officials took charge of it under the grant. Yosemite became a national park on October 1, 1890. The National Parks Conservation Association is a group that campaigns for the parks. An article by the group states that the 1890 park came "with authorization to remove anyone making homes without permission." Authorization means official permission. Ahwahneechee families came back and lived in the valley anyway, and they worked for the hotels and the park.

A Park Service page states that by 1910, more than 90 percent of the Ahwahneechee who had lived in the valley were dead or missing. The page does not say how each of them died or went missing. To Tu Ya, also called Maria Lebrado Ydrte, saw the 1851 raid as a child. She saw the battalion's men destroy her home in Yosemite Valley, and during the raid she saw her uncle murdered. To Tu Ya was Tenaya's granddaughter, so her uncles were Tenaya's sons. The Park Service page does not state which uncle was killed or who killed him. It describes her as the last survivor of the raid. In the late 1920s she came back to Yosemite and told her story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hide hunters and the railroads" -->
Hide hunters, who sold the skins for money, killed most of the Plains bison. About 30 million bison lived on the Plains in the early 1800s. By 1900 fewer than 1,000 were left, and one count in 1884 found only 325. The hunters called themselves "buffalo runners," because bison are also called buffalo.

The first railroad across the country, finished in 1869, carried hunters out to the herds. In 1871 leather makers at a tannery in Pennsylvania found a way to turn bison hides into leather for harnesses and machine belts. A tannery is a place where animal skins are made into leather. Each hide sold for $1 to $3. The Kansas Pacific and the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe railroads carried the hides east.

Lieutenant Colonel Richard Irving Dodge, an army officer, made an estimate of the hides shipped. By his figures, three railroads carried 1,378,389 hides from the southern Plains in 1872, 1873 and 1874. He counted 459,463 hides on the Santa Fe line and guessed that each of the other two lines carried as many. Dodge also figured that before 1874, hunters wasted so many hides that for every hide shipped, about three bison had been killed. His totals were 3,158,730 bison killed by white hunters and about 1,215,000 killed by Native hunters. William T. Hornaday of the Smithsonian Institution reported these totals in 1889. They are Dodge's estimates, not counts.

Dodge described Kansas in the fall of 1873: "Where there were myriads of buffalo the year before, there were now myriads of carcasses. The air was foul with a sickening stench." Myriads means huge numbers. Carcasses are the dead bodies of animals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Officers who wanted the bison gone" -->
Army officers encouraged the hide hunters. Several officers also wrote that killing the bison would force the Plains nations onto reservations.

Historians disagree about whether the army had an official plan to wipe out the bison. Historian David D. Smits argues that the army followed a plan that was mostly never written down. Historians Robert M. Utley and Robert Wooster write that the army had no such official policy. Smits reports that no one has found a written order to kill off the bison. Utley writes that officials "applauded the slaughter," because they saw that it would "force the Indian onto the reservation." Historian William A. Dobak questions how much of the killing the army caused.

In 1867 an Englishman named W. F. Butler told Dodge he was sorry for killing more than 30 bulls, which are grown male bison. Butler wrote in 1911, decades later, that Dodge answered: "Kill every buffalo you can! Every buffalo dead is an Indian gone."

On May 10, 1868, General William T. Sherman wrote to General Philip H. Sheridan: "as long as Buffalo are up on the Republican the Indians will go there." The Republican is a river on the Plains. Sherman went on: "I think it would be wise to invite all the sportsmen of England and America there this fall for a Grand Buffalo hunt, and make one grand sweep of them all." In 1869 Sherman became the commanding general of the whole army.

Sheridan answered in a letter of October 15, 1868: "The best way for the government is to now make them poor by the destruction of their stock, and then settle them on the lands allotted to them." By "stock," Sheridan meant the nations' bison and horses. A month earlier, Lieutenant Colonel Luther P. Bradley had written in his journal that his orders were "to kill all the buffalo we find, and drive the Arapahoes and Cheyennes south, and the Sioux north."

At least one officer disagreed. On January 20, 1872, Colonel William B. Hazen wrote: "The theory that the buffalo should be killed to deprive the Indians of food is a fallacy." A fallacy is a false idea. He called the killing "wicked and wanton waste." Wanton means done for no good reason.

Officers at army posts, among them Forts Griffin, Dodge, Concho and Richardson, gave the hunters protection and supplies and helped them ship hides. In 1873 hide hunters asked Dodge, then in command at Fort Dodge, what he would do if they hunted south into Texas. The 1867 Medicine Lodge Treaty had reserved that hunting ground for the Kiowa, Comanche, Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho. The hunter J. Wright Mooar recalled Dodge's answer in 1930: "Boys, if I were a buffalo hunter I would hunt buffalo where buffalo are."

In December 1874, during the Red River War of 1874 and 1875, Colonel Nelson A. Miles wrote out his plan: "I would leave as few Indians or Buffalo behind, and as little for them to subsist on as possible." To subsist means to stay alive. In a book about his own life, published in 1896, Miles wrote that "the buffalo, like the Indian, stood in the way of civilization and in the path of progress, and the decree had gone forth that they must both give way." A decree is an order.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Officials in Washington" -->
Leaders of the Plains nations asked officials to stop the hunters, and the officials did not stop them. In 1871 a Sioux delegation asked Columbus Delano to keep white hunters out of Sioux country. A delegation is a group sent to speak for its people. Delano was the Secretary of the Interior, one of the president's top officials. He refused.

In 1872 Colonel Edward Wynkoop reported words that Little Robe, a Cheyenne chief, had said to him. Wynkoop had been the government's agent to the Cheyenne. An Indian agent was the US official in charge of a reservation. Little Robe said: "What do you think my people ought to do when they see their cattle, the buffaloes, killed by your race when they are not hungry?" Only Wynkoop's report records these words.

Delano's yearly report for 1873 records that he "would not seriously regret the total disappearance of the buffalo from our western prairies, in its effect on the Indians." He saw the loss of the bison as "a means of hastening their sense of dependence upon the products of the soil and their own labors." He meant that without the bison, Native people would have to live by farming.

Members of both houses of Congress passed a bill to protect the bison in 1874. Under it, no one but a Native person could kill a female bison, or more males than needed for food. The fine would be $100 for each animal. President Grant did not sign the bill. Members of Congress ended their meeting before he signed it, so the bill never became law. This is called a pocket veto.

Grant had promised a Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho delegation that hunters would be kept out of their country. On September 30, 1874, their agent, John D. Miles, wrote that "they looked in vain." In vain means without success: they watched for the hunters to be kept out, and the hunters kept coming.

Texas lawmakers brought in their own bill to protect the bison in 1875. The *Handbook of Texas*, published by the Texas State Historical Association, states that Sheridan protested it, "contending that peace with the Indians could be maintained only if their food supply was eliminated." The lawmakers did not pass the bill.

A speech that Sheridan is said to have given against the bill comes from one source only. The former hide hunter John R. Cook wrote about it in 1907, about 32 years later. Cook wrote that Sheridan told the lawmakers to give each hunter "a medal of bronze, with a dead buffalo on one side and a discouraged Indian on the other." In Cook's account, Sheridan also said: "let them kill, skin, and sell until the buffaloes are exterminated." Exterminated means killed off completely. Smits reports that no official record of Sheridan speaking to the lawmakers has turned up, but he judges Cook believable. Writing in *Harper's* magazine in 2008, Christopher Ketcham called the speech "believed to be apocryphal," which means probably made up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-mayer" name="Frank Mayer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank Mayer

> **Who:** A buffalo runner, one of the hunters who killed bison for their hides, from 1872 to 1878.
> **When and where:** The Plains in the 1870s. He died in Fairplay, Colorado, in February 1954, at a reported age of 104.

Frank H. Mayer killed bison for their hides from 1872 to 1878. At about age 100 he told his story to a writer, Charles B. Roth. It came out in 1958 as a book, *The Buffalo Harvest*, four years after Mayer died. The book describes the business: the rifle, the wagons, the skinners who cut the hides off the dead animals, and hides that sold for a dollar or two each.

Mayer said that "army officers in charge of plains operations encouraged the slaughter of buffalo in every possible way." They gave hunters "free ammunition, all you could use." "All you had to do to get it was apply at any frontier army post," he said. "I received thousands of rounds in this way." Mayer also quoted an officer he did not name: "if we kill the buffalo we conquer the Indian." The last part of the book records Mayer's regret for what the runners did.

Mayer told this story when he was very old. The numbers in it are his own, and some people have questioned even his age.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-mayer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pigeon nestings" -->
Professional hunters killed passenger pigeons where the birds gathered to nest, and by the mid-1890s the wild flocks were almost gone. At their peak, an estimated 3 to 5 billion passenger pigeons lived in North America. They may have made up four of every ten birds on the continent.

In 1871 an estimated 136 million pigeons nested across about 850 square miles of Wisconsin. In 1878 professional netters, who caught the birds in nets, and shooters worked one of the last great nesting grounds, near Petoskey, Michigan. They killed a reported 50,000 birds a day for months. Railroads carried the dead birds to city markets. Hunters learned where each big nesting ground was from telegraph messages, which were sent over wires.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yellowstone and the Tukudika" -->
On March 1, 1872, President Grant signed a law, and under it Yellowstone became the first national park. In the law, members of Congress set the park apart "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." The Tukudika, a band of Mountain Shoshone also called the Sheep Eaters, already lived there. A Park Service page states that they had lived in that area "for thousands of years." The Shoshone, Bannock, Crow, Blackfeet and other nations also have ties to the park's land.

Philetus W. Norris was Yellowstone's superintendent, the official in charge, from 1877 to 1882. In the fall of 1879 he found a Tukudika camp near Bunsen Peak that its people had just left. Norris asked the Indian agent at Fort Washakie for help. An Indian agent was the US official in charge of a reservation. The writer John Clayton, in a 2024 article in *Mountain Journal*, names that agent as James Patten. The agent sent a party of Shoshone, who found the Tukudika and brought them to the Wind River Reservation.

In the words of a Park Service page, the Tukudika "were forcibly removed to reservations." Forcibly means by force. The letters between Norris and Patten have not survived. The records do not say how many Tukudika were taken or what force the Shoshone party used.

In the spring of 1880, Norris met in Washington with a Crow delegation. Officials were asking the Crow to give up 1.6 million acres. That spring Norris also got a "solemn promise" from people at the Lemhi agency, the office of another Indian agent, and at the Wind River Reservation to stay out of the park.

A Park Service page states that early superintendents "actively discouraged tribes from visiting the newly established park to hunt or collect resources." In his reports, Norris called the Tukudika "feeble and harmless." Feeble means weak. He wrote that Native people stayed away from the geysers out of "superstitious awe," meaning fear that came from beliefs about spirits. The writer John Clayton, in *Mountain Journal* in 2024, reports that historians and Shoshone scholars treat that claim as false. With that claim, Norris helped support a false story that no one had lived on the park's land.

In 1896 justices of the US Supreme Court decided a case about a Bannock man named Race Horse. He had hunted elk in Wyoming under the 1868 Fort Bridger Treaty, in which US officials promised a right to hunt on "unoccupied lands." Wyoming officials arrested him. Justice Edward Douglass White wrote the Court's decision, in *Ward v. Race Horse*. He called the treaty right "temporary and precarious," which means easily lost. He ruled that the right had ended when Wyoming became a state. After the ruling, hunting under the treaty in and around Yellowstone was a crime under Wyoming law. On May 20, 2019, in *Herrera v. Wyoming*, the justices threw out the 1896 decision. They ruled that the Crow people's treaty right to hunt had not ended when Wyoming became a state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The forest reserves" -->
In 1864 George Perkins Marsh, a scholar and diplomat from Vermont, published a book called *Man and Nature*. A diplomat is a person who represents a country in other countries. In the book, Marsh argued that whole societies collapse when their people destroy the forests and wear out the soil. Writers at the Forest History Society and the National Park Service link his book to the Forest Reserve Act of 1891. Under that law, a president could set aside public forest land. Those forest reserves were the start of the national forests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-muir" name="John Muir" movie="John Muir in the New World (2011)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Muir

> **Who:** A writer born in Scotland who wrote magazine articles about Yosemite and helped found the Sierra Club.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1838 to 1914. He came to California in 1868 and was president of the Sierra Club from 1892 to 1914.
> **Movie:** *John Muir in the New World* (PBS American Masters, 2011)

John Muir was born in Scotland in 1838 and came to California in 1868. He wrote magazine articles about Yosemite. Readers across the country learned about Yosemite from them, and protecting it became a national cause.

On May 28, 1892, Muir and others founded the Sierra Club in San Francisco. It started with 182 members. The founders wrote down the club's aims. One was "to explore, enjoy, and render accessible the mountain regions of the Pacific Coast," which means to make them easy for people to reach. Another was to win the help of "the people and government in preserving the forests and other natural features of the Sierra Nevada." Muir was the club's first president, and he led it until he died in 1914.
