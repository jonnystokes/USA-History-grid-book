## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 5 minutes**

New Englanders put their rivers to work and cut down most of their forests in the first half of the 1800s, and a few Americans began to write about saving wild places. Mill dams across the rivers stopped the salmon from swimming upstream to lay their eggs. By 1850 farmers had cleared most of New England's forest, partly to feed a boom in sheep.

The painter George Catlin wrote in 1832 about keeping a huge park for the bison and the Plains nations, which he called "a nation's park." He meant a park that would hold both the bison and the Native nations who lived there. In these same years John James Audubon painted the birds of North America, and Henry David Thoreau lived for two years in a cabin he built at Walden Pond, in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dams stop the fish" -->
Atlantic salmon live in the ocean but swim up rivers to spawn, or lay their eggs. A dam across a river blocks them on the way. The first dam across the Connecticut River went up at Turners Falls, Massachusetts, in 1798, and it cut the salmon off from the water where they spawned. By the War of 1812, no salmon came into the Connecticut River at all.

**The Great Stone Dam.** Workers for the Essex Company, a company formed to use the river's water to power mills, built the Great Stone Dam across the Merrimack River at Lawrence, Massachusetts. They finished it in 1848. It stood 30 miles from the sea, and salmon could not get past it. By 1865 the salmon runs, the yearly trips of fish up a river, were gone from the rivers of southern New England. The runs of shad, another fish, collapsed as well.

The writers of an 1857 report in New Hampshire named three causes: the dams, fishing with no limits, and mill machinery that killed young fish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bare East" -->
New Englanders had cut down most of their forest by 1850. Estimates of how much was cleared run from about 60 percent to 80 percent of the land, and some places were cleared even more. One estimate puts the forest left in 1850 as low as a quarter of New England.

A boom in Merino sheep, a kind of sheep raised for its wool, was one cause of the clearing. By 1840 New England farmers kept about 4 million sheep, more sheep than there were people, and they cleared still more forest to feed them.

Across the eastern United States, the forest reached its smallest size around 1872, roughly half the land it had covered in 1620. After that, many eastern farmers left their farms and moved west, and trees grew back on the fields they left behind.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A park for people and bison" -->
The painter George Catlin traveled far up the Missouri River, into the West, in 1832. He feared that the bison and the Plains nations would be destroyed together. He wrote of "a magnificent park," which he called "a nation's park, containing man and beast, in all the wild and freshness of their nature's beauty." In Catlin's plan, the people who lived on the land would stay on it with the animals.

Forty years later, in 1872, Yellowstone became the first national park. By 1879 its superintendent, the official in charge, had the Tukudika people who lived there forced out and taken to a reservation, land set aside for a Native nation to live on. The next era tells how.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-james-audubon" name="John James Audubon" movie="John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature (2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John James Audubon

> **Who:** A painter and naturalist, a person who studies animals and plants in the wild. He painted *The Birds of America*, 435 life-size pictures of birds.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1785 to 1851. His book came out from 1827 to 1838. In 1813, on the way to Louisville, Kentucky, he saw a flock of passenger pigeons that he figured at more than 1 billion birds.
> **Movie:** *John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature* (PBS American Masters, 2007)

**One flock of passenger pigeons darkened the sky for three days.**

John James Audubon painted the birds of North America at their real size. His book *The Birds of America* contains 435 of these paintings. It came out from 1827 to 1838, and Audubon worked on it for about 18 years.

Audubon was riding to Louisville, Kentucky, in 1813 when a flock of passenger pigeons, wild pigeons that flew in huge flocks, passed over him. In his own account, that one flock darkened the sky for three days. He did the arithmetic himself and put it at more than 1 billion birds.

**Why this story is here:** About a hundred years after Audubon's count, the passenger pigeon was gone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-james-audubon" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-david-thoreau" name="Henry David Thoreau" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry David Thoreau

> **Who:** A writer who also worked as a land surveyor, measuring woodlots for a living.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1817 to 1862. He lived in a cabin at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts, from July 4, 1845, to September 6, 1847.

**He lived at Walden Pond for two years, two months and two days.**

Henry David Thoreau wrote about wild nature, and he earned his money measuring land. He worked as a land surveyor, measuring and mapping woodlots, pieces of land kept for growing trees.

Thoreau moved into a cabin he had built himself at Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts, on July 4, 1845. He lived there until September 6, 1847. His book about those years, *Walden*, came out in 1854.

Thoreau gave a talk called "Walking" in Concord on April 23, 1851. It came out as an essay in June 1862, just after he died. In it he wrote that "in Wildness is the preservation of the world," using preservation to mean keeping something safe.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 22 minutes**

US officials gave away the land they had taken from Native nations in these fifty years, and hunters killed nearly all the bison, the animals the Plains nations lived on. Members of Congress passed laws in 1862 for giving the land to railroad companies, to settlers called homesteaders and to the states for colleges. By one historian's count, US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres from the nations between 1776 and 1887.

On the Plains, hide hunters killed the bison for their skins, which are called hides. About 30 million bison lived there in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. Army generals wrote in favor of the killing, to take away the nations' food and force them onto reservations. Professional hunters killed passenger pigeons at their nesting grounds, a reported 50,000 birds a day for months at one Michigan nesting in 1878.

The first parks were made on Native land, and soldiers and officials forced out the people who lived there. Volunteer soldiers drove the Ahwahneechee people out of Yosemite Valley in 1851, and President Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant in 1864. Yellowstone became the first national park in 1872, and its superintendent had the Tukudika people forced out in 1879. John Muir helped found the Sierra Club in 1892, in part to protect the forests and mountains of California's Sierra Nevada.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Land taken from Native nations, in acres" -->
US officials took more than 1.5 billion acres from Native nations from 1776 to 1887, more than half the area of the United States today. They took it by treaty and by executive order, a command from the president. Historian Claudio Saunt, of the University of Georgia, made this count from maps that Charles C. Royce published in 1899, which show every piece of land US officials took from the nations by treaty or order.

Once officials had taken the land, it counted as public land, land that US officials claimed for the nation. Members of Congress then passed it on to railroads, settlers and colleges.

**Railroads.** President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act on July 1, 1862. Under it, railroad companies received "every alternate section of public land, designated by odd numbers," meaning every other square mile. For each mile of track, a company got five of these sections on each side of the line.

The act also states: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." With those words, which use extinguish to mean end, the lawmakers told officials to end the nations' legal ownership of the land along the railroads as fast as they could. From 1850 to 1871, members of Congress granted railroad companies about 130 million acres, an area bigger than California.

**Homesteads.** Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862. Under it, a person could claim up to 160 acres of public land and keep it after living on it or farming it for five years. Over the life of the law, US officials handed 270 million acres to private owners this way, 10 percent of the area of the United States. A National Park Service page states that much of this land "was seized from the Native Americans who previously inhabited it." Seized means taken by force.

**Colleges.** Lincoln signed the Morrill Act on July 2, 1862. Under it, each state received 30,000 acres of public land for each of its senators and representatives in Congress. State officials sold the land to pay for colleges of "agriculture and the mechanic arts," which means farming and the use of machines.

A page about the act from the National Archives, the office that keeps the government's records, states that the act "resulted in the expropriation," or taking away, "of over 10 million acres from Native American tribal lands."

The reporters Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone traced that land for the magazine *High Country News* in 2020. US officials got it through takings that the reporters call "violence-backed," meaning that violence or the threat of it forced the nations to give the land up. Officials paid the nations less than $400,000 in all. By the early 1900s, sales of the land had raised $17.7 million for 52 universities, more than 40 times what the nations were paid. In 2020 the universities still held more than 500,000 acres of it.

One example comes from Minnesota. Four bands of Dakota people signed treaties at Mendota and Traverse des Sioux in the summer of 1851. The reporters describe the conditions as "withholding of rations," or food supplies, "the threat of violence, enforced starvation." The Dakota received "less than 2.4¢ per acre."

**Allotment.** Chapter 2 tells how federal officials used the Dawes Act of 1887 against the nations. Here is what became of the land. Under the act, federal officials cut reservations into plots, 160 acres for the head of a family and less for others, and opened the land left over, which they called "surplus," to white settlers.

By the National Park Service's count, reservation land fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres in 1934. About 90 million acres, 65 percent of what the nations held, passed out of their hands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire made a crime, 1850" -->
California's state lawmakers passed a law called the *Act for the Government and Protection of Indians* on April 22, 1850. Under Section 10 of the law, it was a crime to set the prairie on fire, or to refuse to help put out a prairie fire. Section 10 states that such people "shall be subject to fine or punishment, as a Court may adjudge proper." That means a judge would choose the punishment.

Section 10 speaks of "any person or persons." The whole law was about Native people, though, and burning the land on purpose was a Native practice. Reporters at the news outlet ICT write that under California law, cultural fire, burning done by Native people to care for the land, was "explicitly banned" for more than 150 years.

**The rest of the law.** Under other sections of the same law, white people could take custody of Native children, meaning control over the children and where they lived, until the children were grown. Officials could also arrest Native people they called "vagrants," meaning people with no home or work. Within 24 hours, officials could hire those people out "to the best bidder" for up to four months. This was forced labor: the arrested person had to work for whoever won the bidding.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yosemite and the Ahwahneechee" -->
The Ahwahneechee people lived in Yosemite Valley, in California's Sierra Nevada mountains, and their headman, or leader, was Tenaya. In 1851 volunteer soldiers burned their villages and their food and drove them out of the valley.

**How it started.** James D. Savage was a trader who had moved onto Ahwahneechee land to mine gold and to trade. Ahwahneechee fighters attacked his trading post on the Fresno River in December 1850, took his supplies and killed two of his men. One account says Chowchilla fighters joined them.

California's governor, John McDougal, called for volunteers early in 1851 after attacks on miners and traders. About 200 men on horseback formed the Mariposa Battalion, a unit of soldiers, on January 24, 1851. Their commander was Major James D. Savage. State officials supplied their camp gear and supplies. Federal money paid for the battalion, through federal officials called US Indian commissioners.

**What the battalion did.** The battalion's men first entered Yosemite Valley on March 27, 1851, by the Park Service's date. A National Park Service page states that they "systematically burned villages and food supplies and forced men, women, and children away from their homes."

Lafayette H. Bunnell rode with the battalion and wrote an account of it in 1880. He writes that in Captain John Boling's company, men on foot had orders "to burn any caches they might find," the hidden stores of food. Acorns were the people's main food for the winter.

On the upper San Joaquin River, next to Yosemite, Bunnell says the huts and "adjacent supplies of acorns, were at once given to the flames." He estimated the acorns burned there at 800 to 1,000 bushels, and pine nuts too. A bushel fills a large basket, so that was 800 to 1,000 large baskets of winter food.

In Yosemite Valley, Bunnell found that "nearly all the acorns had been burnt." The battalion's men marched Tenaya and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River.

**Tenaya's son.** Battalion men held Tenaya's youngest son and another young man as hostages, prisoners held to force others to do something, tied to a tree. Bunnell writes that the guards let the two untie themselves "in order to fire upon them, expecting to kill them both." One guard shot Tenaya's son in the back and killed him. The other young man escaped.

Bunnell describes the shooter only as "a young man" urged on by "an old Texan," and he names neither of them. Captain Boling told the guards "that they had committed murder." Bunnell writes that "no punishment was ever inflicted upon the perpetrators," meaning the men who did it. Tenaya was killed in 1853 in a fight with Mono Lake Paiutes. Accounts of why the fight happened differ.

**The park.** President Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant on June 30, 1864. With it, US lawmakers set aside Yosemite, the first land the nation's lawmakers set aside to protect and to keep for public use. California's state officials took charge of it under the grant. Yosemite became a national park on October 1, 1890.

The National Parks Conservation Association, a group that campaigns for the parks, states in an article that the 1890 park came "with authorization," or official permission, "to remove anyone making homes without permission." Ahwahneechee families came back and lived in the valley anyway, and they worked for the hotels and the park.

**The count of the dead.** A Park Service page states that by 1910, more than 90 percent of the Ahwahneechee who had lived in the valley were dead or missing. The page does not say how each of them died or went missing.

To Tu Ya, also called Maria Lebrado Ydrte, saw the 1851 raid as a child. She was Tenaya's granddaughter. She saw the battalion's men destroy her home in Yosemite Valley, and during the raid she saw her uncle murdered.

Because she was Tenaya's granddaughter, Tenaya's sons were her uncles, so the son the guard shot was one of them. The Park Service page does not say whether he was the uncle she saw killed, and it does not name who killed that uncle. The page describes To Tu Ya as the last survivor of the raid. She came back to Yosemite in the late 1920s and told her story.

**By 1887:** Railroads, homesteaders and colleges held hundreds of millions of acres that had belonged to Native nations, and US officials had begun cutting up the reservations that were left.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hide hunters and the railroads" -->
Hide hunters, who sold the skins for money, killed most of the Plains bison. About 30 million bison lived on the Plains in the early 1800s. By 1900 fewer than 1,000 were left, about one for every 30,000, and one count in 1884 found only 325. The hunters called themselves "buffalo runners," because bison are also called buffalo.

**The railroads.** The first railroad across the country, finished in 1869, carried hunters out to the herds. Leather makers at a tannery in Pennsylvania, a place where animal skins are made into leather, found a way in 1871 to turn bison hides into leather for harnesses and machine belts. Each hide sold for $1 to $3. The Kansas Pacific and the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe railroads carried the hides east.

Lieutenant Colonel Richard Irving Dodge, an army officer, estimated that three railroads carried about 1.4 million hides from the southern Plains in 1872, 1873 and 1874. He had a count from one line, the Santa Fe, and guessed that the other two lines carried as many.

Dodge also figured that hunters wasted so many hides before 1874 that about three bison were killed for every hide shipped. By his estimate, that adds up to more than 3 million bison killed by white hunters in those three years, and about 1.2 million more killed by Native hunters, about 4.4 million in all. William T. Hornaday of the Smithsonian Institution reported these totals in 1889. They are Dodge's estimates, not counts.

Dodge described Kansas in the fall of 1873: "Where there were myriads of buffalo the year before, there were now myriads of carcasses. The air was foul with a sickening stench." (Myriads means huge numbers, and carcasses are the dead bodies of animals.)
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Officers who wanted the bison gone" -->
Army officers encouraged the hide hunters. Several officers also wrote that killing the bison would force the Plains nations onto reservations.

**Known:** No one has found a written order to kill off the bison.
**Disputed:** Historians disagree about whether the army had a plan anyway. Robert Utley writes that the army had no official policy, but that officials cheered the killing because it would force the nations onto reservations. David Smits argues that the army followed a plan that was mostly never written down. William Dobak doubts that the army caused much of the killing at all.

**What the officers wrote.** General William T. Sherman wrote to General Philip H. Sheridan in May 1868 that the nations would go wherever the bison were. He suggested inviting hunters from England and America to a great buffalo hunt to "make one grand sweep of them all." In 1869 Sherman became the commanding general of the whole army.

Sheridan answered in a letter of October 15, 1868, that the best way was "to now make them poor by the destruction of their stock, and then settle them on the lands allotted to them." By "stock," Sheridan meant the nations' bison and horses. A month earlier, another officer had written in his journal that his orders were to kill all the buffalo his men found.

Not every officer agreed. Colonel William B. Hazen wrote in 1872 that killing the buffalo to take away the nations' food was a false idea, and he called the killing a waste.

**Help from the forts.** Officers at army posts, among them Forts Griffin, Dodge, Concho and Richardson, gave the hunters protection and supplies and helped them ship hides. In 1873 hide hunters asked Dodge, then in command at Fort Dodge, what he would do if they hunted south into Texas, on hunting grounds reserved for the Kiowa, Comanche, Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho under the 1867 Medicine Lodge Treaty. One of the hunters later recalled that Dodge told them to hunt buffalo where the buffalo were.

Colonel Nelson A. Miles wrote out his plan in December 1874, during the Red River War of 1874 and 1875: "I would leave as few Indians or Buffalo behind, and as little for them to subsist on as possible." (To subsist is to stay alive.) In a book about his own life, published in 1896, he wrote that the buffalo and the Indian had both stood in the way of what he called civilization.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Officials in Washington" -->
Leaders of the Plains nations asked officials to stop the hunters, and the officials did not stop them. A Sioux delegation, a group sent to speak for its people, asked Columbus Delano in 1871 to keep white hunters out of Sioux country. Delano was the Secretary of the Interior, one of the president's top officials. He refused.

Colonel Edward Wynkoop had been the Cheyenne's Indian agent, the US official in charge of their reservation. In 1872 he reported words that Little Robe, a Cheyenne chief, had said to him: "What do you think my people ought to do when they see their cattle, the buffaloes, killed by your race when they are not hungry?" Only Wynkoop's report records these words.

Delano's yearly report for 1873 records that he "would not seriously regret the total disappearance of the buffalo from our western prairies, in its effect on the Indians." He saw the loss of the bison as "a means of hastening their sense of dependence upon the products of the soil and their own labors." He meant that without the bison, Native people would have to live by farming.

**A law that never was.** Members of both houses of Congress passed a bill to protect the bison in 1874. Under it, no one but a Native person could kill a female bison, or more males than needed for food, and the fine would be $100 for each animal. President Grant did not sign the bill. Members of Congress ended their meeting before he signed it, so the bill never became law. This is called a pocket veto.

Grant had promised a Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho delegation that hunters would be kept out of their country. Their agent, John D. Miles, wrote on September 30, 1874, that "they looked in vain." They watched for the hunters to be kept out, and the hunters kept coming.

Texas lawmakers brought in their own bill to protect the bison in 1875. The *Handbook of Texas*, published by the Texas State Historical Association, states that Sheridan protested it, "contending that peace with the Indians could be maintained only if their food supply was eliminated." The lawmakers did not pass the bill.

**The speech in Texas.** A speech that Sheridan is said to have given against the bill comes from one source only. The former hide hunter John R. Cook wrote about it in 1907, about 32 years later. In Cook's account, Sheridan told the lawmakers to give each hunter "a medal of bronze, with a dead buffalo on one side and a discouraged Indian on the other." Cook also has him say: "let them kill, skin, and sell until the buffaloes are exterminated," or killed off completely.

**Disputed:** No official record of Sheridan speaking to the lawmakers has turned up. Smits reports that, but he judges Cook believable. Writing in *Harper's* magazine in 2008, Christopher Ketcham called the speech "believed to be apocryphal," which means probably made up.

**By 1890:** One count in 1884 had found only 325 bison left on the Plains, and no federal law protected the ones that remained.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-mayer" name="Frank Mayer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank Mayer

> **Who:** A buffalo runner, one of the hunters who killed bison for their hides, from 1872 to 1878, who later said the army helped the runners.
> **When and where:** The Plains in the 1870s. He died in Fairplay, Colorado, in February 1954, at a reported age of 104.

**He said the army gave hunters "free ammunition, all you could use."**

Frank H. Mayer killed bison for their hides from 1872 to 1878. At about age 100 he told his story to a writer, Charles B. Roth. It came out in 1958 as a book, *The Buffalo Harvest*, four years after Mayer died.

The book describes the business: the rifle, the wagons, the skinners who cut the hides off the dead animals, and hides that sold for a dollar or two each.

Mayer said that "army officers in charge of plains operations encouraged the slaughter of buffalo in every possible way." They gave hunters free ammunition. "All you had to do to get it was apply at any frontier army post," he said. "I received thousands of rounds in this way." Mayer also quoted an officer he did not name: "if we kill the buffalo we conquer the Indian."

The last part of the book records Mayer's regret for what the runners did.

**Not known:** Mayer told the story when he was very old, the numbers in it are his own, and some people have questioned even his age.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-mayer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pigeon nestings" -->
Professional hunters killed passenger pigeons where the birds gathered to nest, and by the mid-1890s the wild flocks were almost gone. At their peak, an estimated 3 to 5 billion passenger pigeons lived in North America. By one estimate they made up four of every ten birds on the continent.

About 136 million pigeons nested across some 850 square miles of Wisconsin in 1871, by one estimate. Professional netters, who caught the birds in nets, and shooters worked one of the last great nesting grounds, near Petoskey, Michigan, in 1878. They killed a reported 50,000 birds a day for months. Railroads carried the dead birds to city markets. Hunters learned where each big nesting was from telegraph messages, which were sent over wires.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yellowstone and the Tukudika" -->
President Grant signed a law on March 1, 1872, and under it Yellowstone became the first national park. In the law, members of Congress set the park apart "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." The Tukudika, a band of Mountain Shoshone also called the Sheep Eaters, already lived there. A Park Service page states that they had lived in that area "for thousands of years." The Shoshone, Bannock, Crow, Blackfeet and other nations also have ties to the park's land.

Chapter 1 tells this removal briefly, and here is how it happened. Philetus W. Norris, Yellowstone's superintendent from 1877 to 1882, found a Tukudika camp near Bunsen Peak in the fall of 1879 and asked the Indian agent at Fort Washakie for help. The agent, whom the writer John Clayton names as James Patten, sent a party of Shoshone, who brought the Tukudika to the Wind River Reservation. In the words of a Park Service page, the Tukudika "were forcibly removed to reservations."

**Not known:** The records do not say how many Tukudika were taken or what force the Shoshone party used, and the letters between Norris and Patten have not survived.

**Keeping people out.** Norris met in Washington with a Crow delegation in the spring of 1880, while officials were asking the Crow to give up 1.6 million acres. That spring Norris also got a "solemn promise" from people at the Lemhi agency, the office of another Indian agent, and at the Wind River Reservation to stay out of the park.

A Park Service page states that early superintendents "actively discouraged tribes from visiting the newly established park to hunt or collect resources." In his reports, Norris called the Tukudika "feeble," or weak, "and harmless." He wrote that Native people stayed away from the geysers out of "superstitious awe," meaning fear that came from beliefs about spirits. Clayton reports that historians and Shoshone scholars treat that claim as false. With it, Norris helped support a false story that no one had lived on the park's land.

**The court case.** Justices of the US Supreme Court ruled on Native hunting in and around the park in 1896. Wyoming officials had arrested a Bannock man named Race Horse for hunting elk in Wyoming. He hunted under the 1868 Fort Bridger Treaty, in which US officials promised a right to hunt on "unoccupied lands."

Justice Edward Douglass White wrote the Court's decision, in *Ward v. Race Horse*. He called the treaty right "temporary and precarious," which means easily lost, and he ruled that the right had ended when Wyoming became a state. After the ruling, hunting under the treaty in and around Yellowstone was a crime under Wyoming law.

More than 120 years later, on May 20, 2019, the justices threw out the 1896 decision in a different case, *Herrera v. Wyoming*, about the Crow people's own treaty. They ruled that the Crow treaty right to hunt had not ended when Wyoming became a state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The forest reserves" -->
George Perkins Marsh, a scholar from Vermont who also represented the United States in other countries, published a book called *Man and Nature* in 1864. In it, Marsh argued that whole societies collapse when their people destroy the forests and wear out the soil. Writers at the Forest History Society and the National Park Service link his book to the Forest Reserve Act of 1891. Under that law, a president could set aside public forest land. Those forest reserves were the start of the national forests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-muir" name="John Muir" movie="John Muir in the New World (2011)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Muir

> **Who:** A writer born in Scotland whose magazine articles made Yosemite known across the country, and who helped found the Sierra Club.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1838 to 1914. He came to California in 1868 and was president of the Sierra Club from 1892 to 1914.
> **Movie:** *John Muir in the New World* (PBS American Masters, 2011)

**The Sierra Club started with 182 members in San Francisco.**

John Muir was born in Scotland in 1838 and came to California in 1868. He wrote magazine articles about Yosemite. Readers across the country learned about Yosemite from them, and protecting it became a national cause.

Muir and others founded the Sierra Club in San Francisco on May 28, 1892. The founders wrote down the club's aims. One was "to explore, enjoy, and render accessible the mountain regions of the Pacific Coast," which means to make them easy for people to reach. Another was to win the help of "the people and government in preserving the forests and other natural features of the Sierra Nevada."

Muir was the club's first president, and he led it until he died in 1914. In May 1903 he took President Theodore Roosevelt camping in Yosemite.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-muir" -->

**If you remember one thing:** US officials gave away the land they had taken from Native nations, hunters killed nearly all the bison the Plains nations lived on, and soldiers and officials forced Native people out of the first parks.
- 1851: Volunteer soldiers drive the Ahwahneechee out of Yosemite Valley.
- 1862: Lincoln signs the Pacific Railway Act, the Homestead Act and the Morrill Act, the laws under which officials give away public land.
- 1872: Yellowstone becomes the first national park. In 1879 its superintendent has the Tukudika forced out.
- 1872 to 1874: Three railroads carry about 1.4 million bison hides east.
**Next:** After 1900, presidents put hundreds of millions of acres under federal protection, and some of that land had belonged to Native nations.
