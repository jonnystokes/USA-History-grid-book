## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Federal officials took charge of hundreds of millions of acres of land in these years. President Theodore Roosevelt alone put about 230 million acres under federal protection from 1901 to 1909. The officials ran national forests, parks, bird refuges and monuments. Some of the protected land had belonged to Native nations. Glacier National Park, created in 1910, lies on land the Blackfeet sold in 1895, after about 600 of their people starved to death in one winter.

In 1914 the last passenger pigeon died in the Cincinnati Zoo. Park managers in Yellowstone poisoned the park's wolves until they killed the last pack in 1926. In the 1930s the soil of the southern Plains blew away, because farmers had plowed up the grass that held it. In 1935 Forest Service leaders ordered every wildfire put out by the next morning. Young men in the Civilian Conservation Corps planted billions of trees.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Roosevelt's acres" -->
From 1901 to 1909, President Theodore Roosevelt put about 230 million acres under federal protection. The land included 150 national forests, 5 national parks, the first 18 national monuments, 51 federal bird reservations and 4 national game preserves. Counts vary a little from source to source. A national forest is public land where federal foresters manage the trees. A bird reservation is land kept safe for wild birds.

On March 14, 1903, Roosevelt made Pelican Island, in Florida, the first federal bird reservation. It became the first piece of what is now the National Wildlife Refuge System. In 1905 the U.S. Forest Service was set up to run the national forests. Gifford Pinchot was its first chief.

On June 8, 1906, Roosevelt signed the Antiquities Act. Under it, a president could protect land by proclamation, which is an official announcement. Land protected this way is called a national monument. On September 24, 1906, Roosevelt made Devils Tower the first national monument.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-land-environment" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="The Roosevelts: An Intimate History (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt

> **Who:** The president from 1901 to 1909. In those years he put about 230 million acres of land under federal protection.
> **When and where:** The White House, 1901 to 1909. Yosemite, California, in May 1903.
> **Movie:** *The Roosevelts: An Intimate History* (PBS, Ken Burns, 2014), a documentary about the whole Roosevelt family

In May 1903 President Theodore Roosevelt went camping in Yosemite with John Muir. The two men spent three nights sleeping in the open. They camped under the Grizzly Giant, a giant sequoia tree, and in a snowstorm near Sentinel Dome. They also camped near Bridalveil Fall.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-land-environment" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Glacier and the Blackfeet" -->
Glacier National Park lies on land the Blackfeet sold to the United States in 1895. They sold it after a winter in which about 600 of their people starved to death.

In 1870 US Army troops attacked Heavy Runner's camp of Piikani people on the Marias River and killed more than 200 men, women and children. The Piikani are one of the Blackfeet peoples. By 1880 the bison were nearly gone. Members of Congress then cut the money for annuities. Annuities were the food and goods the United States owed a tribe every year by treaty, as payment for land.

Early storms held up supplies in the winter of 1883 and 1884. In December a wagonload of bacon reached the Blackfeet agency near Browning. The Montana Historical Society's textbook describes it as "so old it was contaminated with maggots." Maggots are the young of flies, and they feed on rotting meat. The textbook gives 600 Blackfeet dead of starvation, about one in four of the tribe. Other accounts give counts from 500 to 800. The Assiniboine, another nation, lost about 300 people. The textbook calls it the Starvation Winter.

The same textbook states: "After Starvation Winter, the tribes sold the only resource they had left: land." In 1895 the Blackfeet sold a strip of about 800,000 acres for $1.5 million. Prospectors, people who search for gold and other metals, hoped the strip held gold. Many Blackfeet say the tribe offered the land as a 99-year lease, which is a rental, and never sold it. They also say that because of a surveying mistake, the United States got another 45,000 acres. Under the agreement, the Blackfeet kept the right to hunt, fish, cut wood and gather plants on the land.

At the talks, Little Dog told the US commissioners, the officials sent to buy the land, that the Blackfeet "did not ask the government to come and buy their land." White Calf, a Piikani chief, said: "Chief Mountain is my head. Now my head is cut off."

Prospectors found no gold. In 1897 the strip became a forest reserve. On May 11, 1910, members of Congress made most of it Glacier National Park. After that, the Blackfeet's rights on the land "essentially vanished," in the words of the National Parks Conservation Association, a group that campaigns for the parks. In 1932 a judge of a US District Court ruled that the Blackfeet had no right to hunt in the park. In 1973 a judge ruled that Blackfeet people could enter the park without paying the entrance fee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hetch Hetchy" -->
People who wanted to protect land split into two sides over Hetch Hetchy Valley. Conservation means protecting land, water and wildlife. Gifford Pinchot's side said conservation meant using the land carefully, forever. John Muir's side said the best of it should be left wild.

Hetch Hetchy Valley lies inside Yosemite National Park. After the earthquake and fire of 1906, San Francisco's city leaders wanted the valley for a reservoir. A reservoir is a lake made by a dam to store water. Pinchot backed the city. Muir and the Sierra Club fought the dam for years.

Members of Congress sided with the city and passed the Raker Act. President Wilson signed it on December 19, 1913. Muir died a year later, on December 24, 1914. The O'Shaughnessy Dam was finished in 1923, and the valley is under water today.

On August 25, 1916, members of Congress passed the National Park Service Organic Act. Under it, officials of a new federal bureau, the National Park Service, took charge of protecting the parks. Lawmakers passed it partly in reaction to the Hetch Hetchy fight.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gifford-pinchot" name="Gifford Pinchot" movie="Seeking the Greatest Good (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gifford Pinchot

> **Who:** The first American trained as a professional forester, and the first chief of the U.S. Forest Service. Later he was twice governor of Pennsylvania.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1865 to 1946. He ran the Forest Service from 1905 to 1910.
> **Movie:** *Seeking the Greatest Good* (WVIA and PBS, 2013)

Gifford Pinchot was the first American trained as a professional forester. A forester is a person who studies forests and manages them. He defined conservation as "the greatest good to the greatest number for the longest time." To Pinchot, that meant using the public lands carefully and keeping them useful forever.

Pinchot became the first chief of the Forest Service in 1905. President Taft fired him in 1910, during a quarrel known as the Ballinger dispute. Pinchot and John Muir had been friends until they took opposite sides over Hetch Hetchy. Pinchot later served two terms as governor of Pennsylvania.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gifford-pinchot" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last passenger pigeons" -->
The passenger pigeon died out on September 1, 1914, when the last bird of the species died at the Cincinnati Zoo. The last wild one that anyone confirmed was shot in Ohio in 1900. A few people reported seeing wild passenger pigeons until about 1902. No one confirmed those sightings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="press-clay-southworth" name="Press Clay Southworth" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Press Clay Southworth

> **Who:** A 14-year-old boy who shot the last confirmed wild passenger pigeon.
> **When and where:** Pike County, Ohio, on March 24, 1900.

On March 24, 1900, Press Clay Southworth, age 14, shot a passenger pigeon in Pike County, Ohio. He asked his mother first, then used his family's 12-gauge shotgun. His bird is the last wild passenger pigeon that anyone has confirmed. His family had it mounted, which means stuffed and set up for display. It got buttons for eyes, so people called it "Buttons." The Ohio History Connection, in Columbus, keeps it today. Southworth told the story himself decades later.

The last passenger pigeon of all was a bird named Martha. She died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914, at about 29 years old. Her body was frozen in a 300-pound block of ice and shipped to the Smithsonian Institution, which still has it. Sol Stephan, the zoo's general manager, had cared for Martha, and he saved her feathers in a cigar box. His accounts of where Martha came from changed over the years. The zoo's own records do not agree with each other about her either.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="press-clay-southworth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yellowstone's wolves" -->
Between 1914 and 1926, park managers in Yellowstone killed at least 136 wolves. In 1926 they killed the park's last wolf pack. The National Park Service's history of the park lists these numbers. Several later news accounts say park rangers shot the last two wolf pups near Soda Butte that year.

The managers mostly used poison, especially one called strychnine. They put it inside the bodies of dead animals for wolves to eat. Dan Stahler, a biologist, told *Smithsonian* magazine in 2026: "We're talking crates and crates of this poisoning." In 1915 the park's superintendent called wolves "a decided menace" to the animals they hunted. A menace is a danger.

The killing in Yellowstone was part of a program to wipe out predators on public lands across the whole country. A predator is an animal that hunts other animals for food. The program's targets were wolves, bears, cougars and coyotes. Rewards for dead wolves went back almost 300 years. The first recorded wolf bounty in the colonies was set in Massachusetts in 1630. A bounty is a reward paid for each dead animal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dust Bowl" -->
In the 1930s, wind carried away the soil of the southern Plains, because farmers had plowed up the grass that held it. Wheat sold at high prices during World War I. Farmers with tractors plowed up tens of millions of acres of shortgrass, the short native grass of the southern Plains. The roots of that grass formed sod, a thick mat of roots and earth that held the soil in place. A drought, a long time with too little rain, began in 1931. With the sod gone, the dry soil blew away.

By December 1934, about 35 million acres of farmland had been ruined for growing crops, by the count of the US Department of Agriculture. Another 100 million acres had lost all or most of their topsoil, the top layer of soil where crops grow. Another 125 million acres were losing it fast. In 1935 alone, an estimated 850 million tons of topsoil blew off the southern Plains.

The worst single dust storm struck on April 14, 1935, a day called Black Sunday. It crossed the Plains as a black wall of dust moving at 60 miles an hour.

That spring, a soil scientist named Hugh Hammond Bennett was testifying, or giving evidence, at a hearing in Washington. Dust from the Plains darkened the sky outside. Bennett said: "This, gentlemen, is what I've been talking about." Sources disagree about the day. Some say late March 1935, and others say early April.

Members of Congress declared soil erosion "a national menace." Erosion is the wearing away of soil by wind or water. The Soil Conservation Service was set up on April 27, 1935, with Bennett as its director. The methods that followed included contour plowing, which means plowing along the curves of a slope instead of up and down it. They also included shelterbelts, which are rows of trees planted to slow the wind, and planting grass on the land again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="caroline-henderson" name="Caroline Henderson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Caroline Henderson

> **Who:** A farmer and writer who homesteaded in the Oklahoma Panhandle and stayed on her land through the dust years.
> **When and where:** Near Eva, Oklahoma, from 1907. She lived from 1877 to 1966.

Caroline Henderson graduated from Mount Holyoke College. In 1907 she began homesteading near Eva, in the Oklahoma Panhandle. When the dust storms came in the 1930s, she kept farming there and did not leave.

Henderson wrote letters about the dust years, and the *Atlantic Monthly* magazine printed them as "Letters from the Dust Bowl." The best known appeared in May 1936. That same month, Henry A. Wallace, the Secretary of Agriculture, wrote to praise her. Her letters run from 1908 to 1966. The University of Oklahoma Press later collected them in a book, *Letters from the Dust Bowl*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="caroline-henderson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Every fire put out" -->
Forest Service rules from 1935 to 1978 required crews to control every wildfire by the morning after someone spotted it. In 1910 a fire called the Big Burn covered 3 million acres of the Northern Rockies and killed at least 78 people. After it, Forest Service leaders set out to put out every fire. In 1935 they made the "10 a.m. policy." Under it, crews had to bring each fire under control by 10 o'clock the morning after it was spotted.

From 1933 to 1942, about 3 million young men worked in the Civilian Conservation Corps, a federal work program. Because the corps had so many men, Forest Service leaders could carry out the 10 a.m. policy. They planted between 2 billion and 3.5 billion trees. The figure most often given is about 3 billion. They also fought fires, worked to stop erosion and built trails and buildings in more than 800 parks.

In 1944 Forest Service officials worked with the Ad Council, an advertising group, to start the Smokey Bear campaign. Its slogan, "Only YOU can prevent forest fires," dates from 1947. In 1950 a bear cub was rescued from the Capitan Gap fire in New Mexico, and the cub became the living Smokey Bear.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="aldo-leopold" name="Aldo Leopold" movie="Green Fire: Aldo Leopold and a Land Ethic for Our Time (2011)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aldo Leopold

> **Who:** A forester for the U.S. Forest Service who later became a professor at the University of Wisconsin. He wrote *A Sand County Almanac*.
> **When and where:** He lived from 1887 to 1948. He worked in New Mexico in the 1920s and later in Wisconsin.
> **Movie:** *Green Fire: Aldo Leopold and a Land Ethic for Our Time* (2011)

Aldo Leopold began as a forester for the U.S. Forest Service. In 1924 he got the Gila Wilderness, in New Mexico, set aside. Sources call it the first area in the world officially given the label "wilderness area."

Leopold later taught at the University of Wisconsin. He bought a worn-out farm in Wisconsin and rebuilt its soil and its woods. On April 21, 1948, he had a heart attack and died while helping to fight a grass fire on a neighbor's land.

His book *A Sand County Almanac* came out in 1949, the year after he died. In it he called for a "land ethic." An ethic is a set of ideas about right and wrong. In Leopold's land ethic, people count themselves as members of the land community and stop acting as its conquerors. The book includes his essay about watching the "green fire" fade from the eyes of a wolf that had just been shot.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aldo-leopold" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1970 and 1973, members of Congress passed the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act, and the Environmental Protection Agency opened. Before those laws passed, people had seen the damage. The pesticide DDT left bald eagles with eggs so thin they broke. In 1969 oil and trash on the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland caught fire. At Love Canal, in Niagara Falls, New York, families lived on and beside about 21,000 tons of buried chemical waste.

In 1982 residents of Warren County, North Carolina, lay in the road in front of trucks carrying poisoned soil to a landfill in a mostly Black community. Studies in 1983 and 1987 found hazardous waste sites mostly near Black communities and other communities of color. In 1987 Rev. Benjamin F. Chavis Jr. named the pattern "environmental racism." In Yosemite Valley, Park Service officials evicted the last Native families in 1969.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="DDT and the eagles" -->
DDT is a pesticide, a chemical made to kill insects. It was in wide use from the mid-1940s. DDT thinned the eggshells of raptors, which are birds that hunt other animals. The shells grew so thin that the eggs broke in the nest. By 1963 only 417 nesting pairs of bald eagles were left in the lower 48 states, meaning every state except Alaska and Hawaii.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rachel-carson" name="Rachel Carson" movie="Rachel Carson (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rachel Carson

> **Who:** A marine biologist, a scientist who studies life in the sea, and a writer. She had worked as a writer and editor for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. She wrote *Silent Spring*.
> **When and where:** She lived from 1907 to 1964. *Silent Spring* came out in September 1962. She died in Silver Spring, Maryland.
> **Movie:** *Rachel Carson* (PBS American Experience, 2017)

Rachel Carson first became famous for a book called *The Sea Around Us*. In September 1962 she published *Silent Spring*. In it she laid out what persistent pesticides were doing to birds, soil, water and people. A persistent pesticide is one that stays in the soil, water and living things for a long time.

People in the chemical industry attacked her. Carson answered them with evidence. On April 3, 1963, she spoke on the television program *CBS Reports*. By then she was already sick with breast cancer. She died of it on April 14, 1964. In 1972 officials of the Environmental Protection Agency banned DDT.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rachel-carson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Yosemite's last village" -->
In 1969 Park Service officials evicted the last Native families who lived in Yosemite Valley. To evict means to force people out of their homes. Their cabins were destroyed.

In the 1920s, Ahwahneechee and Paiute families lived in a village in the valley and worked in the park. The Ahwahneechee are also called the Southern Sierra Miwok. Native workers helped build the Ahwahnee Hotel, which was finished in 1927. In 1933 the Park Service built cabins at a new site, called Wahhoga, for families who had been moved out of the old village. The families paid rent, and they paid for their power and water.

In 1953 Park Service officials made a housing rule. Under it, only permanent government workers could live in the village. As each home fell empty, the officials destroyed it. In 1969 they evicted the families who were still there.

Deborah Tucker's parents lived in the village. She told the National Parks Conservation Association: "The Park Service came and burned the cabins out." Some later magazine accounts say the empty cabins were burned in a firefighting training exercise. A Park Service web page states that a Native home near Camp 4 "was razed in the 1960s when the last Indian village was removed from Yosemite Valley." Razed means torn down completely. Jay Johnson, the last Native park worker who lived in the valley, retired and left in 1996.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The river that burned" -->
On June 22, 1969, oil and trash floating on the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, caught fire. The fire burned for about 20 to 30 minutes before it was brought under control. It was at least the 13th fire on the river since 1868. A fire in 1912 killed five men. A fire in 1952 did $1.3 million in damage.

On August 1, 1969, *Time* magazine showed the burning river to readers across the country. Carl Stokes was Cleveland's mayor and the first Black mayor of a major US city. The day after the fire, he held a news conference at the river and called on the state and federal governments to act.

Five months before the fire, in January 1969, an oil spill blackened the beaches at Santa Barbara, California.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Earth Day and the new laws" -->
On April 22, 1970, an estimated 20 million Americans took part in Earth Day events. The 1970 census counted 203,392,031 people in the United States, so about one in ten Americans took part. Senator Gaylord Nelson organized Earth Day with Denis Hayes.

The National Environmental Policy Act became law on January 1, 1970. The Environmental Protection Agency, called the EPA, opened on December 2, 1970. Its officials enforce the federal laws against pollution. Members of Congress passed the Clean Air Act in 1970. In 1972 EPA officials banned DDT, and members of Congress passed the Clean Water Act. The Endangered Species Act became law on December 28, 1973. An endangered species is a kind of plant or animal in danger of dying out.

After the DDT ban, bald eagles came back. They grew from 417 nesting pairs in 1963 to about 10,000 pairs by 2007. That year, federal officials took the bald eagle off the endangered list.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Love Canal" -->
From the 1940s into the 1950s, workers for Hooker Chemical buried more than 20,000 tons of chemical waste in an old canal in Niagara Falls, New York. Families later lived on and beside it, and the chemicals seeped into their homes.

The canal, called Love Canal, covered 15 acres. Hooker Chemical and Plastics Corporation began using it in 1942. Sources give the amount and the years a little differently. One figure is about 21,000 tons from 1942 to 1953. A chronology kept by the University at Buffalo states "over 20,000 tons of toxic chemicals" from 1942 to 1952. Investigators later found more than 200 chemical compounds there. The chronology lists 200 tons of dioxin. Dioxin is a group of chemicals that cause cancer and harm babies before they are born.

In 1953 Hooker sold the covered canal to the Niagara Falls Board of Education for $1. Hooker's officials wrote into the deed "a disclaimer of responsibility for future damages due to the presence of buried chemicals." A deed is the paper that passes ownership of land to a new owner. A disclaimer is a statement that refuses blame. A school, the 99th Street School, and about 100 homes were built on and beside the canal.

By the 1970s, chemicals were coming up into yards and basements. On October 3, 1976, chemicals from the buried waste were reported "seeping into basements of homes." In November 1976, testers found 15 chemicals, including three toxic chlorinated hydrocarbons, a kind of chemical that contains chlorine. Families in the neighborhood counted miscarriages, birth defects and illness. A miscarriage is the death of an unborn baby before it can live outside its mother's body. A birth defect is a problem in a baby's body that is there when the baby is born. Dr. Beverly Paigen, a scientist, found a "high rate of birth defects and miscarriages among Love Canal families."

On August 2, 1978, Dr. Robert Whalen, New York State's health commissioner, declared a state of emergency. He closed the 99th Street School and ordered pregnant women and children under 2 moved out. On August 7, President Jimmy Carter approved emergency federal money so that state officials could buy homes. State officials moved the first 236 families out, at a cost of $10 million. Some sources say 239 families.

On May 17, 1980, scientists in a study paid for by the EPA found chromosome damage in 11 of the 36 residents tested. Chromosomes are the strands in each cell that carry a person's genes. Damage to them can raise the risk of cancer, and of birth defects in the person's children. That month, residents held two EPA officials for about six hours to demand that the rest of the families be moved. Carter then declared a second emergency, which opened the way to move 710 more families. In all, about 950 families left.

In April 1980, New York State's lawyers sued Occidental Petroleum, which owned Hooker by then, and two Hooker companies for $635 million. Under settlements made in 1994 and 1995, Occidental paid New York State and the federal government more than $225 million for the cleanup and the moves. In December 1980, members of Congress passed a law called Superfund. Under it, companies that polluted a site could be made to pay for cleaning it up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lois-gibbs" name="Lois Gibbs" movie="Lois Gibbs and the Love Canal (1982)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lois Gibbs

> **Who:** A 27-year-old housewife at Love Canal whose son went to the 99th Street School. She started the Love Canal Homeowners Association.
> **When and where:** Love Canal, in Niagara Falls, New York, from 1978 to 1980.
> **Movie:** *Lois Gibbs and the Love Canal* (CBS television movie, 1982), with Marsha Mason playing Gibbs

Lois Gibbs was 27 years old and a housewife when she learned about the buried chemicals at Love Canal. Her son went to the 99th Street School, which stood on and beside the old canal. Officials brushed off her worries. In August 1978 she organized her neighbors into the Love Canal Homeowners Association.

Gibbs later founded the Center for Health, Environment & Justice. Through it, she spent the rest of her working life helping other neighborhoods fight the pollution near their homes. In 1990 she won the Goldman Environmental Prize, an award given to people who protect the environment. A CBS television movie about her aired on February 17, 1982.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lois-gibbs" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Warren County" -->
In September 1982, residents of Warren County, North Carolina, lay down in the road in front of trucks. The trucks carried soil poisoned with PCBs to a new landfill near Afton, a mostly Black community. An exhibit by the University of North Carolina Library and other sources describe the protest as the start of the environmental justice movement. People in that movement fight a pattern in which polluting sites are placed next to poor, Black, brown and Native communities.

PCBs are a group of chemicals made by people. They were used in electrical, heating and hydraulic equipment, which means machines worked by liquid under pressure. An EPA web page states that PCBs cause "a variety of adverse health effects in animal and humans." Adverse means harmful.

The poisoned soil came from roadsides. Robert Ward's company, the Ward Transformer Company of Raleigh, hired Robert Burns and his sons to get rid of oil that contained PCBs. The Burns family ran a business called Transformer Sales, in Allegany, New York. In the summer of 1978, their trucks sprayed about 31,000 gallons of the oil at night. They sprayed it on the shoulders, or edges, of about 240 miles of roads in 14 North Carolina counties.

In December 1978, Governor James B. Hunt Jr. announced a plan to bury about 60,000 tons of the soil scraped from those roadsides. He chose a landfill at Afton, in Warren County. State officials bought farmland there from a farmer who was in money trouble. Warren County had the highest share of African American residents in North Carolina, and it was one of the poorest counties in the state. The Afton community was 84 percent African American.

Most people near Afton drank water from wells. The water table there, the level underground where the soil is full of water, was only about 5 to 10 feet down. The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights later wrote that the site "was not scientifically the most suitable site, because the water table was a mere 5-10 feet below the surface."

Residents formed a group called Warren County Citizens Concerned About PCBs and sued. They lost in 1982. In September 1982 the trucks began to deliver the soil, and protesters lay in the road. Officers arrested about 500 people. One later count says more than 500. State officials went ahead and built the landfill. Governor Hunt promised that the soil would be cleaned of poison once a way to do it existed. The cleanup was finished by 2003 and cost $17.1 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counting who lives near the waste" -->
In the 1980s, two studies found that hazardous waste sites sat mostly near Black communities and other communities of color. Hazardous waste is waste that can harm people's health. Communities of color are places where most people are not white.

Members of Congress asked the US General Accounting Office, a federal office that investigates for Congress, to study landfills for hazardous waste in eight southeastern states. Its report came out on June 1, 1983. The report identifies four commercial landfills in those states. One was the Warren County landfill. The others were in Sumter County, Alabama, Chester County, South Carolina, and Sumter County, South Carolina. All four were in areas where Black residents made up 38 to 90 percent of the population.

In 1987 the Commission for Racial Justice of the United Church of Christ published a national study, *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States*. Charles Lee wrote it. He found that race was "the most potent variable in predicting where these facilities were located, more powerful than household income, the value of homes and the estimated amount of hazardous waste generated by industry." In plain words, the race of the people nearby predicted where a waste site would be better than how much money they had.

When the report came out, the commission's leader, Rev. Benjamin F. Chavis Jr., gave a name to "intentionally selecting communities of color for wastes disposal sites and polluting industrial facilities." He called it "environmental racism." The authors of a follow-up study in 1994 found that people of color were "47 percent more likely to live near a hazardous waste facility than white Americans."

On February 11, 1994, President Bill Clinton signed Executive Order 12898. In it he directed federal agencies to find and deal with pollution that fell hardest on communities of color and poor communities. A 2007 report by the United Church of Christ states that the EPA "has failed to implement" the order, meaning its officials had not carried it out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wolves back in Yellowstone" -->
Starting in January 1995, gray wolves brought from Canada were released in Yellowstone National Park. By the end of 1996, 31 wolves had been moved there. Park managers had killed the park's last wolf pack in 1926. With wolves back in the park, elk changed how they behaved, and the willows and stream banks changed too. Yellowstone became the standard example of a top predator changing the land around it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The year 2024 was the warmest ever measured, both for the whole world and for the lower 48 states. Heat and drought dried forests left full of fuel by a century of putting out every fire. In 2020, fires burned about 4.2 million acres of California. Lake Mead and Lake Powell, two reservoirs on the Colorado River, fell to record lows in 2021 and 2022. Fire managers now set fires on purpose again, and Yurok people teach cultural fire to government fire crews.

In Louisiana, Shell's managers agreed in 2002 to pay to move the families of Old Diamond, a neighborhood beside a Shell chemical plant. In 2023 federal lawyers sued the owner of a plant near an elementary school in LaPlace, and in 2025 they dropped the suit. Counts of bald eagles, bison and wolves have grown again. On July 13, 2026, President Donald Trump cut Bears Ears National Monument, in Utah, by about 91 percent. As of September 26, 2026, the land he removed was open to mining claims, and lawsuits against the cut were waiting in federal court.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The warmest year" -->
In January 2025, scientists at NOAA, the federal agency that tracks weather and climate, reported that 2024 was the warmest year on record for the planet. The world averaged 1.29 degrees Celsius above the average for the 20th century, the years 1901 to 2000. Scientists at NASA and other groups agreed. The lower 48 states averaged 55.5 degrees Fahrenheit in 2024, which was 3.5 degrees above their 20th-century average. Seventeen states set records.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire comes back bigger" -->
After a century of putting out every fire, the woods held heavy loads of fuel, the dead wood and brush that burns. Heat and drought dried that fuel out.

On November 8, 2018, the Camp Fire killed 85 people in California and destroyed nearly all of the town of Paradise. About 19,000 buildings burned. The encyclopedia *Britannica* and a timeline by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a federal science agency, list these figures. The Camp Fire was the deadliest wildfire in the United States in a century.

In 2020, fires burned about 4.2 million to 4.3 million acres of California, about 4 percent of the state. By the count of CAL FIRE, California's fire agency, that was the most in modern records. One fire, called the August Complex, passed 1 million acres. It became known as the first "gigafire" in the state's modern history, meaning a fire that burned more than a million acres.

Fire managers at government agencies now set fires on purpose to burn off fuel. These planned fires are called prescribed burns, and prescribed burning is official policy again.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="margo-robbins" name="Margo Robbins" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Margo Robbins

> **Who:** A member of the Yurok Tribe who helped found the Cultural Fire Management Council, to bring traditional burning back to Yurok land.
> **When and where:** The Klamath River country of northern California. The first community burn was in 2012, and the council formed in 2013.

Margo Robbins, a member of the Yurok Tribe, wanted hazel for weaving baskets. When hazel is burned, it sends up new shoots that grow straight, and weavers need straight shoots. Her first goal was to weave baskets to carry her grandchildren.

Robbins and others held their first community burn on Yurok land along the Klamath River in 2012. In 2013 they formed the Cultural Fire Management Council. Cultural fire means burning done by Native people to care for the land. In 1850 California lawmakers had made setting the prairie on fire a crime. After the council's burns, deer came back to the burned ground at Weitchpec. Council members now run training exchanges, called TREX, that teach cultural burning to fire crews.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="margo-robbins" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The shrinking river" -->
From 2000 to 2021, the Southwest had its driest 22 years in at least 1,200 years. Scientists from UCLA and Columbia University found this by studying tree rings, which show how much a tree grew each year. In February 2022 they published their study in the journal *Nature Climate Change*. They blamed about 42 percent of the drought's severity on climate change caused by people.

About 40 million people depend on the Colorado River for water, and so does much of the country's winter vegetable crop. Lake Mead and Lake Powell, two reservoirs on the river, fell to record lows in 2021 and 2022, news reports stated. By the middle of 2022, Lake Mead was about a quarter full.

In May 2023, officials of Arizona, California and Nevada agreed to cut their use of the river by at least 3 million acre-feet through the end of 2026. An acre-foot is enough water to cover one acre of land one foot deep. The cut is about a tenth of the water those three states are allowed to take. Federal officials agreed to pay the states for the water they gave up. News reports that cited the federal Bureau of Reclamation stated these figures.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cancer Alley" -->
The stretch of Louisiana between Baton Rouge and New Orleans holds a long line of chemical plants. A 2007 report by the United Church of Christ describes this strip as "so-called 'cancer alley,' the chemical manufacturing corridor between Baton Rouge and New Orleans." A corridor is a long, narrow strip of land. The towns of Norco and LaPlace are both in it.

The same report records who lived near commercial hazardous waste sites across the country in 2000, using census figures. In neighborhoods with such sites, 56 percent of the people were people of color. Elsewhere, 30 percent were. The poverty rate was 18 percent near the sites and 12 percent elsewhere. Forty of the 44 states with such sites showed the same gap.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="margie-richard" name="Margie Richard" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Margie Richard

> **Who:** A schoolteacher from the Old Diamond neighborhood of Norco, Louisiana, who led her neighbors' campaign to make Shell pay to move them away from its plant.
> **When and where:** Norco, Louisiana. The campaign ended in 2002. She won the Goldman Environmental Prize in April 2004.

Four generations of Margie Richard's family lived in the Old Diamond neighborhood of Norco, Louisiana. Their home stood 25 feet from the fence of a Shell Chemical plant. Shell had bought land in Diamond and built the plant there in the early 1950s. The neighborhood sat between the chemical plant and a Shell refinery, a plant that turns crude oil into fuels. Residents of Old Diamond kept a record of the cancers and birth defects among their neighbors.

In 1973 gas leaked from a Shell pipeline. A spark from a lawnmower set it on fire. The fire burned Leroy Jones, age 16, to death while he was cutting grass. It also burned to death Helen Washington, who was resting on her porch. In 1988 an explosion at the Shell refinery killed seven workers and damaged homes in Diamond.

In a 2004 interview with *Grist* magazine, Richard remembered: "The elder people were always coughing ... black soot falling on the grass, on our houses." She also said: "My sister died at a very early age from a rare bacterial disease."

Richard led the neighborhood's campaign for about 13 years. Residents tested their own air with buckets built to catch air samples. They took their case to Shell's shareholder meetings, where the company's owners gather, and to Shell in the Netherlands. In 2002 Shell's managers agreed to pay to move every Old Diamond family that wanted to leave. They also agreed to cut the plant's emissions, the pollution it sends into the air, by 30 percent, and to put $5 million into a community fund. Shell paid at least $80,000 for each house and $50,000 for each trailer, about $30 million in all.

In April 2004 Richard became the first African American to win the Goldman Environmental Prize.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="margie-richard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The plant by the school" -->
Federal lawyers sued the owner of a plant in LaPlace, Louisiana, in 2023, and they dropped the suit in 2025. The plant released a chemical likely to cause cancer into the air near an elementary school.

The plant is in St. John the Baptist Parish. A parish in Louisiana is like a county in other states. The company DuPont built the plant to make neoprene, a kind of man-made rubber. The Japanese company Denka bought it about 2015, according to AP news reporting in 2025. To make neoprene, the plant used a chemical called chloroprene, and it released chloroprene into the air.

In 2010 EPA scientists found that chloroprene is "likely to be carcinogenic to humans." Carcinogenic means causing cancer. They also found that children breathing it build up a lifetime risk of cancer faster than adults do.

In 2023, more than 300 children went to the 5th Ward Elementary School, about 450 feet from the plant. The EPA's recommended long-term level for chloroprene is 0.2 micrograms in each cubic meter of air. A microgram is one millionth of a gram. In their 2023 lawsuit, federal lawyers reported the averages at the five air monitors closest to the plant. The lowest was "more than four times" the recommended level. The highest was "more than 14 times" that level. In March 2025, AP reporters wrote that the community around the plant is about 59 percent Black. They also reported that the plant's air pollution posed the highest cancer risk from any single source to a mostly Black population in the country.

On February 28, 2023, lawyers for the US Justice Department, acting for the EPA under President Biden, sued Denka under the Clean Air Act. They asked the court to force deep cuts in the plant's chloroprene pollution. A trial was set for April 2025. On March 7, 2025, under President Trump, Justice Department lawyers dropped the suit. Their press release stated that dropping it carried out the president's order called "Ending Radical and Wasteful Government DEI Programs and Preferencing." DEI stands for diversity, equity and inclusion.

On May 13, 2025, Denka's managers announced that the LaPlace plant would stop making chloroprene "for an indefinite period," meaning with no end date. They named losses of more than $100 million, rising costs, falling production and trouble finding workers. Articles in trade magazines also listed the pollution rules as a reason. The plant was not making chloroprene as of May 2025.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plastic in the rain" -->
More than 1,000 tons of tiny plastic pieces fall each year on protected land in the western United States. Janice Brahney, a scientist at Utah State University, and her team found this in a study published in the journal *Science* on June 12, 2020. The pieces are called microplastic, which means bits of plastic too small to see easily. They fall with rain and with dust. The team sampled 11 parks and other protected areas. Each year's total equals the weight of more than 120 million plastic bottles. Most of the pieces were tiny fibers from clothing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Comebacks" -->
Bald eagles, bison and wolves have come back in large numbers since they nearly disappeared. In the 2020s, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service counted about 71,400 nesting pairs of bald eagles. In 1963 there had been 417.

About 30 million bison lived on the Plains in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. Recent counts by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the National Bison Association put about 420,000 bison in commercial herds, which are raised for sale. About 20,500 Plains bison live in herds kept for conservation.

Recent counts put about 500 wolves in the Greater Yellowstone area and more than 1,600 in the northern Rockies. State and tribal counts of wolves differ. People are still fighting in court over whether wolves should stay on the endangered list.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Land given back" -->
In December 2020, members of Congress passed a law returning the National Bison Range to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes. The range is an 18,800-acre bison refuge on the Flathead Reservation. It was taken from the reservation in 1908. The tribes took over full management of the range in 2022.

In 2018, Park Service officials in Yosemite agreed to share the care of Wahhoga, the site of the valley's last Native village, for 30 years. They share it with the American Indian Council of Mariposa County. In the winter of 2025, a roundhouse, a round building for gatherings, was finished there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bears Ears" -->
Since 2016, three presidents have created, cut, restored and cut again Bears Ears National Monument in Utah. As of September 26, 2026, most of the land was out of the monument and open to mining claims, and no court had ruled on the latest cut.

On December 28, 2016, President Obama created the monument, with 1.35 million acres. Five tribes had proposed it: the Hopi Tribe, the Navajo Nation, the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, the Ute Indian Tribe and the Pueblo of Zuni. In December 2017, President Donald Trump cut it by about 85 percent, to about 201,876 acres. On October 8, 2021, President Biden restored it. In June 2022, the five tribes signed an agreement with the Bureau of Land Management and the Forest Service to manage the monument together.

On July 13, 2026, Trump signed a proclamation that cut Bears Ears to 121,096 acres, a cut of about 91 percent. About 1.24 million acres left the monument. What is left is two pieces, Shash Jáa, about 106,816 acres, and Indian Creek, about 14,279 acres. The proclamation lists minerals in the removed land: silver, copper, uranium, vanadium and zinc. The same day, Trump cut Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument from about 1.87 million acres to about 181,500 acres. Reporters for the *Moab Sun News* published these figures on July 18, 2026.

In the July proclamation, Trump also ended the Bears Ears Commission. Elected representatives of the five tribes sat on it, and they had managed the monument with federal officials under the 2022 agreement. Reporters for the *Moab Sun News* wrote that an advisory committee took its place, with tribes holding 5 of about 15 seats. The tribes say Interior Department officials promised in writing in June 2025 to consult the commission before any change to the boundaries, and that no one consulted it before July 13. Their statement said: "Our Tribes were not informed of or asked about this decision, and that's unacceptable."

Under the proclamation, the removed land opened on September 11, 2026, to new mining claims, mineral leasing, grazing and timber cutting. A mining claim is a legal claim to the right to mine minerals on public land. Mineral leasing means renting out the right to take minerals from the ground. By noon on September 11, 16 claims had been recorded in San Juan County. By September 23, staff of a conservation group called SUWA counted 23 claims in the removed Bears Ears land. Kimmerle Mining had filed 20 of them, and George W. Schultz had filed 3. Kimmerle Mining had tried to file 7 claims on July 14, before the land opened. Kyle Kimmerle, of Kimmerle Mining, had been a plaintiff, a person who brings a lawsuit, in one of the suits against Biden's 2021 restoration.

On September 2, 2026, three sets of plaintiffs went to the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, a federal court in Washington. Eight groups, among them Utah Diné Bikéyah, Archaeology Southwest and the National Trust for Historic Preservation, asked the court to reopen their 2017 case and add a complaint against the July 13 proclamation. Two more coalitions, one led by the groups NRDC and SUWA and one led by The Wilderness Society, asked to renew their own 2017 cases. They argue that the Antiquities Act of 1906 lets a president create a monument and does not let a president undo one. Utah's members of Congress called the cuts "a return to an appropriate application of the Antiquities Act." The tribes' own 2017 case, *Hopi Tribe v. Trump*, is still pending, which means undecided, in the same court. As of September 26, 2026, no court had ruled on the 2026 cuts, and no judge had ordered them stopped.
