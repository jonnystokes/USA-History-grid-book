## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 3 of 37 · Land and Environment · 10 eras · about 95 minutes**

**Americans cleared this land, used it up and then saved pieces of it, and at almost every step the people who lost most had the least say: first Native nations, later poor and Black neighborhoods.**

Miantonomi, a leader of the Narragansett nation, told the Montaukett people on Long Island in 1642 what the English were doing to the land. The plains had once been full of game, he said, but the English were cutting down the grass and the trees. Their cows and horses ate the grass, their hogs spoiled the clam banks, and he warned that his listeners would all starve. An English soldier wrote his words down. The next year, English officials sentenced Miantonomi to death.

This chapter follows the land itself through ten eras, from Native nations burning forests on purpose to government fire crews learning that same kind of burning from Yurok people today. Keep an eye on two things. The first is the numbers. Beavers, salmon, forests, 30 million bison and billions of passenger pigeons fall era by era. Some, like the eastern forests and the bison, later climb back, and the passenger pigeon never does. The second is who had to move. The families driven out of the first national parks were Native. The neighborhoods beside the waste dumps of the 1900s were far more likely than others to be Black or poor.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did Native nations shape the land before Europeans came?
- What happened to the 30 million bison and the billions of passenger pigeons?
- Who was pushed out when parks were made, and who lived next to the waste?

**Five minutes?** Read "Yosemite and the Ahwahneechee" in 1850 to 1900.
**Can skip:** "Whaling from Nantucket", "The forest reserves", "Plastic in the rain". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 2 minutes**

Native nations shaped the land of North America for thousands of years before any European arrived. In the eastern forests, people set fires on purpose to keep the woods open and to grow food for the animals they hunted. Farmers planted three crops together in a way that kept the soil good. In southwest Florida, the Calusa built pens in the water to hold live fish.

Animals shaped the land too. Beavers dammed streams across the continent, and bison grazed the Plains. Nobody knows how many bison lived there before 1500. The figure usually given, about 30 million, is for the early 1800s, before hunters killed off the herds.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Native people burned the woods" -->
For thousands of years, Native people in what is now the eastern United States burned their forests on purpose to keep them open. They set low fires in the understory, the layer of brush and small plants under the tall trees. They burned the same ground again and again. The fires helped oaks and other trees that can live through fire. Food plants grew back after each burn, and so did food for game, the wild animals people hunted.

**What the trees show.** Researchers at Penn State, a university in Pennsylvania, published a study in the journal *Annals of Forest Science* in 2019. They found that which trees grew in the eastern forests depended more on Native burning than on the climate. Even small groups of people burned large areas, over and over. European colonists treated this burning as unimportant.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three crops in one field" -->
Native farmers grew corn, beans and squash together in the same field, three crops known as the Three Sisters. The beans put nitrogen, a natural substance that plants need to grow, into the soil. The wide squash leaves shaded the ground and held the soil in place. The tall corn stalks gave the bean vines something to climb.

Growing the three crops together kept the soil good for years. A field planted with only one crop, year after year, wears out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beavers, bison and fish pens" -->
Beavers changed whole watersheds, all the land that drains into one stream or river. Their dams held back water in ponds. The ponds slowed floods, and the wet ground around them became meadows. Nobody counted the beavers, so the numbers are estimates. They run from 60 million to 400 million beavers in North America before the fur trade, the buying and selling of animal skins between European traders and Native trappers.

Bison lived on the Plains, the wide grasslands in the middle of the continent. About 30 million lived there in the early 1800s. Estimates for the years before Europeans came are less certain.

In what is now southwest Florida, the Calusa people built watercourts between 1300 and 1400. These were pens in the water that held live fish until people needed them for food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

Two things changed the land of the Americas in the 1500s: new animals arrived from Europe, and most of the Native people who had farmed the land died. Ships brought pigs, horses, cattle and earthworms. Pigs that escaped from the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto and his men went wild in the Southeast, and European earthworms moved into northern forests that had never had worms.

Most of the Native people died of diseases that came across the ocean with Europeans: with European settlers, and with the Africans whom Europeans had enslaved and carried over. European soldiers and settlers killed others in wars to take Native lands, and many starved. Forest grew back over the farmland of the people who died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pigs, horses and cattle" -->
Pigs that escaped from Hernando de Soto's herd became the ancestors of the feral hogs of the Southeast, pigs from farm stock that live in the wild.

**Thirteen pigs.** De Soto landed near Tampa Bay, in what is now Florida, in May 1539, with about 600 men and a herd of pigs. The written histories of his expedition list the first herd as 13 pigs. By the time de Soto died in 1542, the herd had grown to about 700. That count leaves out the pigs the men ate or gave away and the pigs that escaped.

Horses and cattle came with the Spanish. Columbus brought horses on his second voyage, in 1493, and Spanish colonists brought horses and cattle to their colony in New Mexico in 1598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Earthworms" -->
Before Europeans came, the forests of northern North America had no earthworms. Glaciers, thick sheets of ice that once covered the north, had scraped the ground bare of them. For roughly the last 12,000 years, the forests that grew back after the ice stood on a thick, soft layer of fallen leaves called duff. Native wildflowers and young trees grew in it.

European earthworms crossed the ocean hidden in ballast soil, heavy dirt carried low in a ship to keep it steady. Worms also came in the soil around the roots of plants brought from Europe. Later they spread west with wagons, horses and farms. Where European worms move into a northern forest, they eat the duff that the wildflowers and young trees need.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forest over lost farms" -->
Most of the Native people of the Americas died after 1492. Epidemics, diseases that spread quickly to many people, killed most of them. European settlers brought the diseases, and so did enslaved Africans whom Europeans had carried across the ocean. The diseases included smallpox, measles and influenza, and Native people had never been exposed to any of them before.

European soldiers and settlers made war on Native nations to take their land, and they enslaved Native people. Famine, a lack of food so great that people starve, followed when too few people were well enough to farm.

**What the deaths did to the land.** A team of researchers at University College London, in England, published an estimate in 2019. They estimated that the Native population of the Americas fell from about 60 million to about 6 million within roughly a hundred years. Other estimates of the starting number differ. For North America north of Mexico alone, they run from 1 million to 18 million.

The team estimated that about 55.8 million hectares of farmland grew back into forest after its farmers died. A hectare is a square 100 meters on each side, so that is an area bigger than California.

The new trees took carbon dioxide, a gas that traps heat, out of the air. By the team's estimate, the regrowth lowered the carbon dioxide in the air by 7 to 10 parts in every million. It cooled the whole planet by about 0.15 degrees Celsius, about a quarter of a degree Fahrenheit. All of these figures are this one team's estimates.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 4 minutes**

English colonists and the Native nations of New England had different ideas in the 1600s about what land was for. The colonists treated land as property that one owner held forever, marked off with fences and recorded in deeds, the papers that say who owns a piece of land. The Native nations used their land by season, with planting grounds, fishing places and hunting grounds, and no one person owned it forever.

The historian William Cronon describes these two ways of using land in his 1983 book *Changes in the Land*. The colonists let their cattle and hogs roam free. The animals got into Native planting fields, and colonists and Native people quarreled over it again and again, as the Narragansett leader Miantonomi told his listeners in 1642.

Trappers changed the land too. They caught so many beavers for the fur trade in these years that the beaver's numbers began a long fall.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The beaver trade" -->
Hat makers in Europe wanted beaver fur to make felt, a thick cloth made by pressing fur together. Native trappers caught the beavers, and European traders paid for the furs with goods made in Europe. The number of beavers in the Northeast began to fall in the 1600s.

The fall went on until about 1900, when about 100,000 beavers were left in North America, most of them in Canada. Today there are roughly 15 million, against the 60 million to 400 million of the years before the fur trade. All of these numbers are estimates.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first limits on cutting trees" -->
A Plymouth Colony rule of 1626 forbade anyone to cut timber, wood for building, on the colony's land, or to sell it, without official permission. It was among the earliest conservation rules in the colonies, rules for protecting land, water, plants and animals so that people do not use them up.

William Penn wrote rules for settlers in Pennsylvania in 1681. For every five acres a settler cleared of trees, Penn's rules required the settler to leave one acre, a piece of land a little smaller than a football field, still covered in trees. The rules named oaks and mulberry trees, which were useful for ships and for silk. Settlers widely ignored this rule.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="miantonomi" name="Miantonomi" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Miantonomi

> **Who:** A sachem, or leader, of the Narragansett nation, who said out loud in 1642 what English settlers were doing to Native land.
> **When and where:** A speech to the Montaukett people on Long Island in 1642, written down by the English soldier Lion Gardener. He was killed near present-day Norwich, Connecticut, in 1643.

**English officials sentenced him to death and handed him to his enemy.**

Miantonomi spoke to the Montaukett people on Long Island in 1642. English settlement in New England was then about one generation old, and he told them what the English were doing to the land.

Lion Gardener, an English soldier, wrote the speech down in his *Relation*, an account he set down in 1660, years later. By Gardener's record, Miantonomi said that "our fathers had plenty of deer and skins and our plains were full of game and turkeys."

Then he said that the English "with scythes cut down the grass, and with axes fell the trees" (a scythe is a long curved blade for cutting grass, and to fell a tree is to cut it down). He went on: "their cows and horses eat the grass, and their hogs spoil our clam banks, and we shall all be starved."

**Known:** The complaints match what colonists were doing at the time, cutting the forests and letting their cattle and hogs roam into Native fields.
**Not known:** Nobody can check Miantonomi's exact words, because an Englishman wrote them down years after the speech.

**The sentence.** Uncas, the sachem of the Mohegan nation, captured Miantonomi in 1643 and took him to Hartford. That September the commissioners of the United Colonies of New England met in Boston. They were English officials from the Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut and New Haven colonies. They passed the case to a group of ministers, or church leaders. Then the commissioners sentenced Miantonomi to death and handed him back to Uncas to carry out the sentence.

Governor John Winthrop of Massachusetts Bay wrote in his journal that "they all agreed that he ought to be put to death." He gave their reason, spelling the name a different way: "Uncas could not be safe while Miantonomo lived."

Mohegan men took Miantonomi back to the place near present-day Norwich, Connecticut, where he had been captured. There one of Uncas's men, walking behind him, killed him with a hatchet blow to the head. Mohegan tradition and later histories name that man as Wawequa, the brother of Uncas. By the historian Neal Salisbury's account, several Englishmen went along to make sure the killing was carried out.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 3 minutes**

Colonists of the early 1700s used land and sea as if there would always be more. Tobacco planters in Virginia, the owners of large farms called plantations, wore out the soil of a field in three or four years. Then they cleared new ground farther up the rivers and moved on.

In New England the king's officials saved one thing, the largest white pines, for the masts of the Royal Navy, Britain's navy. Whalers from the island of Nantucket were sailing far out into the ocean to hunt sperm whales by the middle of the century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The king's pines" -->
The king's officials kept the largest white pines in New England for the masts of the Royal Navy's ships, the tall poles that hold up the sails. The rule appeared in the Massachusetts Bay charter of 1691, the written grant from the king that set up the colony's government. It covered every white pine 24 inches across or more.

Under the White Pine Act of 1722, cutting any white pine more than 12 inches across without a license, or written permission, was against the law in New Hampshire and nearby colonies. The king's trees carried a mark called the Broad Arrow, three slashes cut with a hatchet. Royal officials enforced these laws loosely until the 1760s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Worn-out tobacco fields" -->
A tobacco field in colonial Virginia gave good crops for only three or four years. After that, planters cleared new ground and let the old field grow over with scrub, brush and small trees. The land around Jamestown wore out early. Planters moved up the rivers and reached the Rappahannock and the Potomac by about 1650, and they kept moving up the rivers all through the 1700s. Because new land was cheap, they kept wearing out one field after another.

**Whose land it was.** The new land along the Rappahannock River was the homeland of the Rappahannock Tribe. The tribe's own history states that English settlement there began illegally in the 1640s. By that history, Rappahannock leaders sold their first land to the English in 1651. They spent more than ten years in county courts trying to get paid for that sale and others, and they never received full payment.

The tribe's history goes on to say that by the late 1660s, settlers and bands of settlers who acted as their own law had forced the Rappahannock to move. The tribe moved first inland on the north side of the river, and later to its old hunting grounds on the south side. In 1683, the history states, officials of the Virginia colony forced the tribe out of their homes and moved them to a place called Portobago Indian Town.

**Who did the work.** Indentured servants and enslaved Africans worked the tobacco fields. An indentured servant worked for a master for a set number of years, often four to seven, to pay for the trip to America. Planters held enslaved Africans as property and forced them to work without pay for life. Under a Virginia law of 1662, a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life too. Growing tobacco took a great deal of hand work, and planters wanted more and more workers to do it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whaling from Nantucket" -->
Whalers from Nantucket, an island off Massachusetts, were hunting sperm whales far out in the ocean by the middle of the 1700s. Before that, New Englanders hunted right whales, another kind of whale, from the beaches. On Nantucket, people tell a story that deep-sea whaling began there in 1712, when wind blew Christopher Hussey's boat out to sea and the men aboard killed a sperm whale.

**Known:** Obed Macy first wrote the story down in his history of Nantucket in 1835, more than 120 years later, and historians at the Nantucket Historical Association call 1712 the traditional date.
**Not known:** No record from 1712 shows that Hussey's voyage happened the way the story tells it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 5 minutes**

After independence, American leaders turned the land beyond the mountains into squares that could be sold, and they took it from the nations who lived there with treaties signed under threat and then with armies. In 1785 members of Congress ordered the land north and west of the Ohio River measured into a grid. The Shawnee, the Miami, the Lenape (Delaware) and other nations lived on that land. That January, US officials had claimed most of Ohio in a treaty, a written agreement between nations, signed under threat.

Surveyors measured the land under army guard. US generals led armies against the nations, who had joined together to stop settlement, from 1790 to 1794. After General Anthony Wayne's army defeated them in 1794, leaders of eleven nations signed a treaty in 1795 in which they handed most of Ohio to the United States.

The king's claim to New England's biggest white pines ended with independence. Before that, in 1772, men in Weare, New Hampshire, beat a sheriff who came to arrest a mill owner fined for cutting them. Once the king's claim was gone, settlers crossed the Appalachian Mountains and cleared the forest for farms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ebenezer-mudgett" name="Ebenezer Mudgett" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ebenezer Mudgett

> **Who:** A sawmill owner in Weare, New Hampshire, fined for cutting the king's white pines, who led the Pine Tree Riot.
> **When and where:** South Weare, New Hampshire, April 13 and 14, 1772.

**At dawn, 20 to 30 men with soot-blackened faces beat the sheriff.**

Ebenezer Mudgett owned a sawmill in Weare, New Hampshire. He had been fined for cutting the king's white pines, the trees kept by law for the masts of the Royal Navy.

Sheriff Benjamin Whiting rode into South Weare on April 13, 1772, with a warrant to arrest Mudgett, a paper from a court that allowed the arrest. Mudgett told him he would pay in the morning.

At dawn on April 14, Mudgett came back with 20 to 30 men who had blackened their faces with soot, the black powder that smoke leaves behind. They beat the sheriff and his deputy with rods and drove them out of town. The fight became known as the Pine Tree Riot.

**The trial.** Eight men stood trial in the Superior Court at Amherst, New Hampshire, in September 1772, for rioting and for beating Whiting. They pleaded guilty. The four judges fined each man only 20 shillings, in British coins, and made them pay the cost of the hearing. After the riot, officials mostly stopped enforcing the law that kept the pines for the navy.

**Known:** Some accounts count the riot as a forerunner, an earlier event of the same kind, of the Boston Tea Party, a protest in December 1773.
**Not known:** No one has proved that the riot led to the Tea Party.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ebenezer-mudgett" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The grid, 1785" -->
Members of the Confederation Congress, the national government of the United States before the Constitution, passed a law called the Land Ordinance on May 20, 1785. It specified how the public land would be sold. Public land was land that US officials claimed for the nation, and Native nations lived on much of it. Under the ordinance, surveyors had to survey that land, which means measure it and map it, before anyone could buy it.

The surveyors divided the land into townships, squares six miles long on each side. They cut each township into 36 sections. A section was one square mile, which is 640 acres. The sections were numbered, and members of Congress set aside section 16 in every township to support public schools.

After changes to the system in 1796, surveyors carried the grid across the continent to the Pacific Ocean. People in airplanes over the Midwest can still see its checkerboard pattern on the ground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the grid covered" -->
The land north and west of the Ohio River was the home of the Shawnee, Lenape, Wyandot, Miami, Ottawa, Chippewa, Potawatomi and other nations in 1785. As members of Congress saw it, the historian George W. Knepper writes, "Indian claims to that land had to be cleared." Here, cleared means ended, so that no Native nation still had a right to the land.

**The treaty at Fort McIntosh.** US commissioners, officials sent to do a certain job, met leaders of several nations at Fort McIntosh in January 1785, four months before the Land Ordinance passed. In the treaty they made there, the commissioners said that most of the Native people of Ohio had to stay inside a reserve, an area set aside for them, between the Cuyahoga and Maumee rivers. Under the treaty, Knepper writes, most of Ohio would be open for settlement.

The Shawnee refused to obey the treaty. Knepper writes that it was made under threat by minor chiefs who had no right to speak for their people. A year later, US commissioners forced the Shawnee into another treaty, at Fort Finney.

**The first survey.** Surveyors for the national government began measuring this land in eastern Ohio in September 1785, and they stopped after a few miles when they heard of an attack by Native people. They went back to work in 1786 under army guard, and they measured the land for sale while the nations who lived on it still rejected the treaty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The war for Ohio and the Treaty of Greenville" -->
Chapter 2 tells this war from the nations' side. The nations of the region had joined in a confederacy, a union of nations, to stop American settlement. Led by Little Turtle of the Miami and others, its warriors beat American armies in 1790 and 1791, and General Anthony Wayne's army defeated the confederacy at Fallen Timbers in 1794. Here is what the war meant for the land.

**The land the nations lost.** A year after Fallen Timbers, on August 3, 1795, leaders of eleven northwestern nations signed the Treaty of Greenville. In it they handed nearly 17 million acres to the United States, an area a little bigger than West Virginia. Under the treaty, the nations kept only the northwest quarter of Ohio and the lands beyond it, behind a boundary called the Greenville Treaty Line.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Clearing after independence" -->
After independence, the British king no longer had any right to the white pines in the United States. Settlers crossed the Appalachian Mountains, some of them into the Northwest Territory, the US name for the land north and west of the Ohio River, and cut down the forest to make farms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
