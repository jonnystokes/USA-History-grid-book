## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1937, people made an element for the first time, using material from a machine at Berkeley, California. By the end of 1949, teams working in the United States had made or found seven more elements. They were astatine, neptunium, plutonium, curium, americium, promethium and berkelium.

In the same fifty years, the elements Americans dug up and used poisoned many of the people who worked with them. Women who painted watch dials swallowed radium and died of it. Workers who made a lead additive for gasoline died of lead poisoning. Arsenic from a copper smelter in Montana killed farm animals. From 1941, Navajo and other Native miners dug uranium without being warned of its dangers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The well that would not burn" -->
Helium is an element, a gas that does not burn. In May 1903, a drilling company struck a roaring gas well on William Greenwell's farm near Dexter, Kansas. The people of Dexter planned a celebration. They pushed a burning bale of hay toward the gas to light a giant flame, and the gas put the fire out. Local people made fun of it as "wind gas."

Erasmus Haworth, a Kansas geologist, carried samples of the gas to the University of Kansas. There the chemists Hamilton P. Cady and David F. McFarland tested it. On December 7, 1905, they showed that the gas was 1.84 percent helium. Scientists had thought helium was very rare on Earth. Cady and McFarland found large amounts of it in the natural gas under the Great Plains.

Airships flew in World War I, and helium became valuable to armies because it does not burn. In 1925 the members of Congress passed the Helium Act. Under it, federal workers began producing helium and storing it underground in the Bush Dome of the Cliffside gas field, near Amarillo, Texas. That store became the Federal Helium Reserve. For decades it held nearly all of the world's supply of helium.

Under a 1927 law, no one could sell American helium to other countries. For that reason, the German airship *Hindenburg* flew on hydrogen, a gas that burns. On May 6, 1937, the *Hindenburg* caught fire and burned at Lakehurst, New Jersey. After the fire, President Franklin D. Roosevelt considered selling helium to Germany. In 1938 Hitler annexed Austria, which means he made it part of Germany. Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, then refused to sell.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium on the watch dials" -->
Radium is an element that Marie and Pierre Curie found in France in 1898. Radium is radioactive, which means its atoms break apart on their own and give off radiation. Radiation is energy that shoots out of an atom as it breaks apart. Some of it travels as tiny, fast particles that can damage the living cells of a body. The kind that radium gives off is called alpha radiation.

In the early 1900s, American companies mixed radium into paint so that watch numbers glowed in the dark. Other sellers put radium into tonics, drinks they claimed would make people healthy.

Two doctors, Sabin Albin von Sochocky and George S. Willis, founded the Radium Luminous Materials Corporation in New Jersey in 1914. After World War I, its owners reorganized it as the U.S. Radium Corporation, with a plant in Orange, New Jersey. At its biggest, the company employed as many as 250 young women, some only 15 years old. Its treasurer, Arthur Roeder, replaced von Sochocky as president in January 1921. Howard Barker was the vice president and chief physicist.

In Ottawa, Illinois, the Radium Dial Company ran another dial-painting plant. It belonged to the Standard Chemical Company of Pittsburgh, and Joseph Kelly was its president.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology is a federal science laboratory. A history it published counts more than 3,000 girls and young women who painted radium dials across the country in the 1910s and 1920s. Northern Public Radio gives about 4,000. Neither source names the records behind its count.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="radium-girls-elements" name="The Radium Girls" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The Radium Girls

> **Who:** Young women, some of them teenagers, who painted glowing radium numbers on watch and instrument dials and were poisoned by the paint.
> **When and where:** The U.S. Radium Corporation in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917, and the Radium Dial Company in Ottawa, Illinois, into the 1930s.

From 1917, young women at the U.S. Radium Corporation plant in Orange, New Jersey, painted watch numbers with radium paint. To shape each brush to a fine tip, they drew it between their lips. This was called lip-pointing, and the method went by the words "lip, dip, paint." Each time a painter lip-pointed, she swallowed a little radium. The women went home glowing, and some people called them ghost girls. Lip-pointing at the company went on while Arthur Roeder was president, and it ended after 1926.

Radium belongs to the same chemical family as calcium, the mineral in bones and teeth. So the painters' bodies took the swallowed radium into their bones and teeth, the way they take in calcium. There the radium kept giving off radiation from inside the body. Radiation damaged the bone marrow, the soft center of the bones that makes blood cells, until the marrow stopped making them. Doctors call this aplastic anemia. The jawbone died and broke down, which doctors call necrosis of the jaw. Some women got bone cancer.

A history published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology states that the radiation "had eaten away their bones from the inside out." Some women's spines collapsed, and they had to wear braces. Their jawbones shattered under light pressure. When doctors examined dial painters' bodies after death, they found 99 percent of the radioactivity in the skeleton.

Amelia Maggia, called Mollie, painted dials at the company until she died in 1922. Her body was dug up on October 25, 1927. It held 48.4 micrograms of radium. A microgram is one millionth of a gram. Her body held 500 times the amount later set as the limit for a human body.

Edwin Leman, a chemist at the company, died of aplastic anemia on June 5, 1925. Sarah Maillefer, a dial painter, died on June 18, 1925. Harrison Martland, the medical examiner of Essex County, New Jersey, examined Leman's body. Martland gave the jaw disease the name "radium jaw." Von Sochocky, one of the two doctors who founded the company, died of radium poisoning on November 14, 1928.

Grace Fryer began painting dials at the Orange plant on April 10, 1917, and worked there until 1920. In 1924 a dentist, Theodor Blum, found damage to her jawbone of a kind he had never seen. In May 1927 the lawyer Raymond Berry sued the company for five women. They were Grace Fryer, Katherine Schaub, Quinta Maggia Macdonald, Albina Maggia Larice and Edna Hussman.

Company officials first offered each woman $10,000, with her medical and legal costs taken out of it. The women refused. On June 4, 1928, they accepted $10,000 each, plus their doctor's bills and a pension of $600 a year. A pension is money paid every year for the rest of a person's life. A federal judge, William Clark, arranged the deal. The company's officials admitted no blame, and Berry took 10 percent. Grace Fryer died in 1933.

In Illinois, supervisors at Radium Dial told the women the paint was safe, and even healthy. They kept the women lip-pointing. Catherine Wolfe Donohue painted dials there and began to feel sick in 1925. Radium Dial's managers fired her in 1931. In her own words, "my limping was causing much talk." She lost half her body weight, and parts of her jaw fell out.

The lawyer Leonard J. Grossman took Catherine Donohue's case for no pay. The case went before the Illinois Industrial Commission, a state board that decided claims by workers hurt on the job. During the hearing she learned that her illness would kill her, and she collapsed. The hearing moved to her home. On April 5, 1938, the commissioners ruled that her work at Radium Dial had made her sick. They awarded her $277 a year for life. In July 1938 they threw out the company's appeal and added $730.

Catherine Donohue died on July 27, 1938, at the age of 35. Grossman said hers was the eleventh death among women who had done that work at the plant. Northern Public Radio later counted fifteen Ottawa workers dead of radium. Radium Dial's lawyers appealed all the way to the United States Supreme Court and lost, and the case ended in October 1938. Because Radium Dial had left Illinois, the Ottawa women together collected only $10,000.

Joseph Kelly lost his post as Radium Dial's president in 1934. He opened a new dial company, Luminous Processes, a few blocks away and hired many of the same workers. That plant ran until 1976. That year, officials of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, a federal agency, found radiation there 1,666 times the allowed level.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium sold as medicine" -->
William J. A. Bailey owned Bailey Radium Laboratories in East Orange, New Jersey. He sold Radithor, which was radium dissolved in water. Bailey had falsely claimed degrees from Harvard and from Vienna. In 1927 he had pleaded guilty to practicing medicine without the right to do it. His company sold more than 400,000 bottles of Radithor at $1 each.

Eben Byers was a rich Pittsburgh businessman. From December 1927 through 1930, he drank about 1,400 bottles of Radithor, an average of three a day. The radium destroyed his bones. Byers gave testimony to the Federal Trade Commission, a federal agency. On December 19, 1931, the commission's officials ordered Bailey to stop claiming that Radithor was harmless or that it healed people. Byers died months later, in 1932, at the age of 51.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead in gasoline and paint" -->
Lead is a soft, heavy metal and an element. Lead poisoning is the harm lead does to the body once a person swallows it or breathes it in. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) is the federal office that studies dangers at work. Its list of what large doses of lead do includes paralysis, which means a person cannot move part of the body. The list also includes madness, gout (painful, swollen joints), colic (sharp pains in the belly) and death.

Thomas Midgley Jr., a researcher at General Motors (GM), found that adding tetraethyl lead, a liquid compound of lead, to gasoline stopped engine "knock." Knock is a pinging sound in an engine that runs on gasoline. Charles Kettering ran GM's research. In August 1924, GM and Standard Oil of New Jersey formed the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation to sell tetraethyl lead. The DuPont company made it.

The workers who made tetraethyl lead died of lead poisoning, and the counts of the dead differ. Bill Kovarik counted them in a 2005 article in a journal about workers' health. At Standard Oil's Bayway refinery in Elizabeth, New Jersey, seven men died in October 1924, and 33 went to the hospital. Between September 1923 and the fall of 1924, ten more workers died at a DuPont plant in Deepwater, New Jersey. At GM's laboratory in Dayton, Ohio, at least two died, and 40 went to the hospital. Kovarik's numbers add up to at least 19 dead. Other accounts give five dead at Bayway and 35 in the hospital. In 2021, NIOSH counted 15 worker deaths from making tetraethyl lead in the 1920s.

On October 30, 1924, Midgley held a press conference. He poured tetraethyl lead on his hands and breathed its vapor for about 60 seconds, to show it was safe. A few months earlier, lead had made him seriously ill. At the same event he acknowledged the two deaths in Dayton. He also said more than 50 workers were "under observation" for lead poisoning.

The Surgeon General is the head of the Public Health Service, the federal office in charge of the nation's health. On May 20, 1925, Surgeon General Hugh Cumming brought more than 100 people from industry and from public health to a meeting in Washington. Frank Howard of Standard Oil said the industry could not give up "what has come to the industry like a gift from heaven." Yandell Henderson studied how the body works at Yale. He called tetraethyl lead "one of the most dangerous things in the country today." Alice Hamilton of Harvard told Kettering there were "thousands of things better than lead."

In 1926 a committee appointed by the Surgeon General reported "no good grounds" for a ban and asked for more study. The Ethyl company paid Robert Kehoe of the University of Cincinnati to study lead. Kehoe set out the industry's "safe level" theory, the claim that lead below a certain amount did no harm. That claim stayed the industry's position for decades.

Painters also used paint that contained lead. NIOSH states that between 1925 and 1927, more painters died of lead poisoning than workers in all other jobs combined. The International Labour Organization is a world body that sets rules for workplaces. In 1922 its member countries adopted a ban on lead paint for the insides of buildings. The United States did not join the ban.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercury in Danbury's hat shops" -->
The hatters of Danbury, Connecticut, kept breathing mercury at work after 1900. In 1913 Connecticut lawmakers added mercury poisoning to the state's workmen's compensation law. Under a law of that kind, employers pay workers who are hurt or made sick by their jobs.

In 1922, Alice Hamilton examined 100 Danbury hatters and found 43 with mercury poisoning. Some shook so hard from tremors that they could not feed themselves. The union hatters kept demanding investigations. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employers. Government researchers made a full study only in 1937.

Officials of the Public Health Service then worked out an agreement with the hatters' unions and the hat manufacturers in the 26 states that made hats. Under it, hat shops stopped using mercury. On December 1, 1941, Connecticut Governor Robert A. Hurley announced his state's ban. He said "it was possible to manufacture good fur felt hats without making physical wrecks out of hundreds of workers." The hat shops used hydrogen peroxide in place of mercury. Mercury is still in Danbury's soil and in the mud of the Still River.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Arsenic smoke in Montana" -->
Arsenic is an element and a strong poison. A smelter is a plant where workers melt ore to get the metal out of it. In 1902 the Anaconda Copper Mining Company started its Washoe smelter at Anaconda, Montana, about 25 miles from the copper mines of Butte. When the smelter melted copper ore, arsenic went up its smokestack with the smoke. Farmers nearby reported livestock dying in the smelter's first year.

In 1904 farmers in the Deer Lodge Valley formed an association to sue the company. The case was *Bliss v. Anaconda Copper Mining Co.* In 1909 the judges of a federal appeals court found that arsenic had poisoned the farmers' livestock and crops. The judges still refused to close the smelter. The company then bought out many of the farmers. From the rest it bought "smoke easements." An easement is a legal right to use land that someone else owns. A smoke easement gave the company the right to send its smoke over a farm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uranium from Navajo land" -->
Uranium is a heavy metal element. Its atoms break apart slowly and give off radiation. From 1941, Native American miners, among them Navajo, Pueblo and Hopi people, worked in uranium and vanadium mines in the Southwest. In 1943 the Vanadium Corporation of America began mining uranium in secret on Navajo land.

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is the federal agency that protects the country's air, water and land. EPA reports that from 1944 to 1986, mining companies dug nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore out of Navajo land. They mined under leases with the Navajo Nation. A lease is an agreement to use land for a set time in return for payment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A false first, and the machine at Berkeley" -->
In 1931 the physicist Fred Allison of Alabama Polytechnic, now Auburn University, announced two new elements. He called element 85 "alabamine" and element 87 "virginium." He said he had found them with his "magneto-optical" machine. Scientists at other labs tried his method and could not get the same results. They showed that the signals Allison saw came from his equipment, not from any element. His claims were dropped. A Berkeley team made the real element 85 in 1940.

Ernest Lawrence built his first cyclotron at Berkeley, California, in 1930 and 1931. A cyclotron is a machine that whirls tiny charged particles in a spiral until they move very fast. When the fast particles hit a target, they can turn atoms of one element into atoms of another. Lawrence and his teams built bigger cyclotrons through the 1930s. With these machines, Berkeley's Radiation Laboratory became the source of most of the new elements made in the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Ernest Lawrence

> **Who:** The physicist who invented the cyclotron, the machine behind most of the elements discovered in the United States.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from the 1930s to the 1950s. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1939 and died in 1958.

Ernest O. Lawrence built his first cyclotron at Berkeley in 1930 and 1931. His teams then built larger ones. The 60-inch cyclotron of 1939 had a magnet that weighed 220 tons. The Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley became an independent laboratory in 1936.

Lawrence won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1939. The Nobel Prize is an international award given each year for work in science and other fields.

Lawrence's machines made the material for several new elements. A metal sheet from his 37-inch cyclotron held the first technetium. His 60-inch cyclotron made astatine in 1940, and it made the ingredients for plutonium. Lawrence died in 1958. Element 103, lawrencium, carries his name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ernest-lawrence-elements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elements made by people" -->
Mendeleev's periodic table had a gap at element 43, below manganese. Early in 1937, Lawrence mailed a thin sheet of a metal called molybdenum to Emilio Segrè in Palermo, Sicily. The sheet had been part of the 37-inch cyclotron. Segrè and the mineralogist Carlo Perrier, a scientist who studies minerals, took a few billionths of a gram of element 43 out of it. They named it technetium, from a Greek word meaning "artificial." Technetium was the first element made by people. Italian scientists did the chemistry, and the material came from an American machine.

Dale R. Corson, Kenneth R. MacKenzie and Segrè made element 85 at Berkeley in 1940. They struck the element bismuth with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. Element 85 was the element Allison had called "alabamine." In 1947 its makers named it astatine, from a Greek word for "unstable."

Also in 1940, Edwin McMillan and Philip Abelson found element 93 in uranium that had been struck in the cyclotron. It was the first element heavier than uranium. Uranium was named for the planet Uranus, so they named element 93 neptunium, for Neptune, the next planet out.

On December 14, 1940, Glenn Seaborg, McMillan, Joseph W. Kennedy and Arthur C. Wahl made element 94 in the 60-inch cyclotron. On the night of February 23 and 24, 1941, they proved by chemistry that it was a new element. They did it in Room 307 of Gilman Hall at Berkeley, which is now a National Historic Landmark. They named the element plutonium, for the planet Pluto. Seaborg chose the symbol Pu instead of "Pl" as a joke, because "pee-yoo" is what people say about a bad smell. The naming committee accepted it. Government officials kept the work secret from the start. During World War II, American scientists used plutonium and uranium to build atomic bombs.

Seaborg's circle made element 96 in the summer of 1944, with Ralph A. James and Albert Ghiorso on the team. They named it curium, for Marie and Pierre Curie. Element 95 came next, in 1944 and 1945, with Leon O. Morgan also on the team. It was named americium, for the Americas. Much of this work took place at the Metallurgical Laboratory, a secret wartime lab in Chicago.

On November 11, 1945, Seaborg was a guest on a children's radio show called *Quiz Kids*. A boy asked him whether any new elements had turned up during the war. Seaborg said yes, elements 95 and 96. He told the boy five days before he planned to announce them to the American Chemical Society.

Jacob A. Marinsky, Lawrence E. Glendenin and Charles D. Coryell found element 61 in 1945 at Clinton Laboratories in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. The lab is now Oak Ridge National Laboratory. They found the element in the leftovers of uranium atoms that had been split. Grace Mary Coryell, Charles Coryell's wife, suggested the name promethium. It honors Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods in Greek stories. The team announced the element in 1947. Promethium is still the only element ever discovered in Tennessee.

In December 1949, Stanley G. Thompson, Ghiorso and Seaborg made element 97 at Berkeley. They struck americium with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. They named it berkelium, for Berkeley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-segre-elements" name="Emilio Segrè" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Segrè

> **Who:** An Italian physicist who stayed in the United States after Italy's anti-Jewish laws took away his university job, and who helped find two elements.
> **When and where:** Palermo, Sicily, and Berkeley, California, from 1937 into the 1940s. He lived in the United States from 1938 and died in 1989.

Emilio Segrè worked with Carlo Perrier in Palermo in 1937. From the metal sheet that Ernest Lawrence mailed them, they identified technetium, the first element made by people.

In the summer of 1938, Segrè was visiting Berkeley. While he was there, he learned that Mussolini's government in Italy had passed new racial laws. The laws took university jobs away from Jews, and Segrè was Jewish. He stayed in the United States and became an American citizen.

At Berkeley, Segrè helped make astatine in 1940. With Glenn Seaborg, he showed in 1941 that atoms of plutonium-239 could be split. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1959 for his work on the antiproton. The antiproton is a tiny particle with the opposite electric charge of the proton, one of the particles in the center of every atom.

### Glenn Seaborg

> **Who:** The chemist whose teams found plutonium and nine more elements, and the first person to see an element named for him while he was alive.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, and wartime Chicago, from 1940 to 1974. He died in 1999.

Glenn T. Seaborg led the team that made plutonium in December 1940 and proved it was a new element in February 1941. In all, he helped discover ten elements, numbers 94 through 102 and number 106. One of the ten, nobelium, was later credited to scientists at Dubna in the Soviet Union.

In 1951, Seaborg shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Edwin McMillan for their work on the elements heavier than uranium. From 1961 to 1971, he was chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, the federal agency in charge of atomic energy.

In 1997 the element 106 was named seaborgium, after him. Seaborg could receive a letter with an address written only in element names: Seaborgium, Lawrencium, Berkelium, Californium, Americium. That stood for Seaborg, at the Lawrence laboratory, in the city of Berkeley, in California, in America.
