## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 25 minutes**

Radium, lead, mercury, arsenic and uranium poisoned many of the Americans who worked with them in the first half of the 1900s. Young women who painted watch dials swallowed radium because their supervisors told them to shape their brushes with their lips, and many of them died of radium poisoning. At least 15 workers died of lead poisoning in the 1920s at the plants that made a lead additive for gasoline.

Arsenic from a copper smelter in Montana killed farm animals. From 1941 Navajo and other Native miners dug uranium, and Navajo miners later reported that no one had told them of its dangers.

At the same time, scientists began making new elements, substances of only one kind of atom, with a machine at Berkeley, California. The first, in 1937, was identified in Italy, in material that had come out of that machine. By the end of 1949, teams working in the United States had made or found seven more: astatine, neptunium, plutonium, curium, americium, promethium and berkelium.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The gas well that put out the fire" -->
Helium is an element, a gas that does not burn. A drilling company struck a roaring gas well on William Greenwell's farm near Dexter, Kansas, in May 1903. The people of Dexter planned a celebration. They pushed a burning bale of hay toward the gas to light a giant flame, and the gas put the fire out. Local people made fun of it as "wind gas."

Erasmus Haworth was a Kansas geologist, a scientist who studies rocks. He carried samples of the gas to the University of Kansas. There the chemists Hamilton P. Cady and David F. McFarland tested it. On December 7, 1905, they showed that the gas was 1.84 percent helium. Scientists had thought helium was rare on Earth, and Cady and McFarland found large amounts of it in the natural gas under the Great Plains.

**Helium for airships.** Airships flew in World War I, and helium became valuable to armies because it does not burn. Members of Congress passed the Helium Act in 1925. Under it, federal workers, people who worked for the national government, began producing helium and storing it underground in the Bush Dome of the Cliffside gas field, near Amarillo, Texas. That store became the Federal Helium Reserve, and for decades it held nearly all of the world's supply of helium.

Under a 1927 law, no one could sell American helium to other countries. For that reason, the German airship *Hindenburg* flew on hydrogen, a gas that burns. On May 6, 1937, the *Hindenburg* caught fire as it came in to land at Lakehurst, New Jersey. Of the 97 people on board, 35 died, and so did a man working on the ground.

After the fire, President Franklin D. Roosevelt considered selling helium to Germany. Then in 1938 Hitler took over Austria and made it part of Germany, and Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, refused to sell. Ickes headed the federal department in charge of public lands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium on the watch dials" -->
Marie and Pierre Curie found the element radium in France in 1898. Radium is radioactive: its atoms break apart on their own and give off radiation, energy that shoots out of an atom as it breaks apart. Some radiation travels as tiny, fast particles that can damage the living cells of a body. The kind radium gives off is called alpha radiation.

Workers at American companies mixed radium into paint in the early 1900s so that watch numbers glowed in the dark. Other sellers put radium into tonics, drinks they claimed would make people healthy.

**The companies.** Two doctors, Sabin Albin von Sochocky and George S. Willis, founded the Radium Luminous Materials Corporation in New Jersey in 1914. After World War I the company was reorganized and renamed the U.S. Radium Corporation, with a plant in Orange, New Jersey. At its biggest, the company employed as many as 250 young women, some only 15 years old.

Arthur Roeder, who had been the company's treasurer, in charge of its money, replaced von Sochocky as president in January 1921. Howard Barker was the vice president and chief physicist. In Ottawa, Illinois, the Radium Dial Company ran another dial-painting plant. It belonged to the Standard Chemical Company of Pittsburgh, and Joseph Kelly was its president.

**Known:** More than 3,000 girls and young women painted radium dials across the country in the 1910s and 1920s, by the count of the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a federal science laboratory.
**Not known:** The full count, since Northern Public Radio gives about 4,000 and neither count comes with a list of the painters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="radium-girls-elements" name="The Radium Girls" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The Radium Girls

> **Who:** Young women, some of them teenagers, who painted glowing radium numbers on watch and instrument dials and were poisoned by the paint.
> **When and where:** The U.S. Radium Corporation in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917, and the Radium Dial Company in Ottawa, Illinois, into the 1930s.

**The method had a name: "lip, dip, paint."**

Young women at the U.S. Radium Corporation plant in Orange, New Jersey, painted watch numbers with radium paint from 1917. To shape each brush to a fine tip, a painter drew it between her lips, then dipped it in the paint and painted: lip, dip, paint. Drawing the brush through the lips was called lip-pointing. Each time a painter lip-pointed, she swallowed a little radium. The women went home glowing, and some people called them ghost girls.

**Who told them to.** Their supervisors told them to lip-point and said the paint was safe. Von Sochocky, one of the company's founders, grew worried about lip-pointing and told the painters to stop, but the supervisors contradicted him, according to Northern Public Radio. Arthur Roeder was the company's president from January 1921, and he still signed papers for the company in 1926. Lip-pointing there went on until after 1926. No surviving record names the New Jersey supervisors.

**What the radium did.** Radium belongs to the same chemical family as calcium, the mineral in bones and teeth. So the painters' bodies took the swallowed radium into their bones and teeth, the way they take in calcium, and there it kept giving off radiation from inside the body. The radiation damaged the bone marrow, the soft center of the bones that makes blood cells, until the marrow stopped making them. Doctors call this aplastic anemia.

The jawbone died and broke down, which doctors call necrosis of the jaw, and some women got bone cancer. A history published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology states that the radiation "had eaten away their bones from the inside out." Some women's spines collapsed, and they had to wear braces. Their jawbones shattered under light pressure. When doctors examined dial painters' bodies after death, they found 99 percent of the radioactivity in the skeleton.

**The first deaths.** Grace Fryer began painting dials at the Orange plant on April 10, 1917, and worked there until 1920. Another painter there, Amelia Maggia, called Mollie, painted dials at the company until she died in 1922. In 1924 a dentist found damage to Grace Fryer's jawbone of a kind he had never seen.

In June 1925 a chemist at the company died of aplastic anemia, and a dial painter died 13 days later. The medical examiner of Essex County, New Jersey, the doctor who looks into sudden and suspicious deaths there, examined the chemist's body. He gave the jaw disease the name "radium jaw."

**The lawsuit.** Grace Fryer and four other dial painters sued the company in May 1927. Their lawyer was Raymond Berry. Mollie Maggia's body was dug up on October 25, 1927. It held 48.4 micrograms of radium, millionths of a gram, which is 500 times the amount later set as the limit for a human body.

Company officials first offered each woman $10,000, with her medical and legal costs taken out of it. The women refused. In June 1928 they accepted $10,000 each, their medical bills and a pension of $600 a year, money paid every year for the rest of their lives. A committee of three doctors had to approve the bills. Some accounts give the amount each woman got as $15,000.

A federal judge arranged the deal on June 4, and the agreement was signed on June 8. The company's officials admitted no blame, and Berry took 10 percent. Von Sochocky, one of the two doctors who founded the company, died of radium poisoning on November 14, 1928. Grace Fryer died in 1933.

**Ottawa, Illinois.** The Radium Dial Company advertised for painters in Ottawa in September 1922. The advertisement named a Miss Murray as the person young women should apply to. Catherine Wolfe, who later married and took the name Donohue, described how Miss Murray taught them to point the brushes between their teeth. Supervisors at Radium Dial told the women the paint was safe, and even healthy, and kept them lip-pointing.

Radium Dial's managers hired doctors to examine the painters from 1925. The doctors told one painter that she was very healthy. Nobody told her that she had tested positive for radiation, her niece said later.

Catherine Wolfe Donohue painted dials there and began to feel sick in 1925. Radium Dial's managers fired her in 1931. In her own words, "my limping was causing much talk." She lost half her body weight, and parts of her jaw fell out.

Joseph Kelly lost his post as Radium Dial's president in 1934. He opened a new dial company, Luminous Processes, a few blocks away and hired many of the same workers. That plant ran until 1976, when officials of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, a federal agency, found radiation there 1,666 times the allowed level.

**The hearing.** A lawyer took Catherine Donohue's case for no pay, before the Illinois Industrial Commission, a state board that decided claims by workers hurt on the job. At a hearing on February 10, 1938, she brought a box of pieces of bone that she said had come from her jaw. When the judge asked her doctor about her chances of living, she collapsed, and several men carried her from the room. She weighed 71 pounds.

The hearing moved to her home. On April 5, 1938, the hearing officer ruled that her work at Radium Dial had made her sick and awarded her $5,661, which included a pension of $277 a year for life. On July 6 the members of the commission threw out the company's appeal and added $730.

Catherine Donohue died on July 27, 1938, at the age of 35. According to NPR Illinois, she weighed less than 60 pounds. Her lawyer said hers was the eleventh death among women who had done that work at the plant, and Northern Public Radio later counted fifteen Ottawa workers dead of radium. Radium Dial's lawyers appealed all the way to the United States Supreme Court and lost, and the case ended in October 1938. Because Radium Dial had left Illinois, the Ottawa women together collected only $10,000.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radium sold as medicine" -->
William J. A. Bailey owned Bailey Radium Laboratories in East Orange, New Jersey. He sold Radithor, which was radium dissolved in water. Bailey had falsely claimed degrees from Harvard and from Vienna, and in 1927 he had pleaded guilty to practicing medicine without the right to do it. His company sold more than 400,000 bottles of Radithor at $1 each.

Eben Byers was a rich Pittsburgh businessman. He hurt his arm in 1927 at a party on a train after the Harvard-Yale football game, and he began drinking Radithor to heal it. He stopped in 1930, when his teeth began falling out. Accounts of how much he drank differ. The Museum of Radiation and Radioactivity at Oak Ridge Associated Universities says he averaged three bottles a day for about two years from December 1927. Other accounts give about 1,400 bottles in all.

The radium destroyed his bones. A government investigator reported that his whole upper jaw except two front teeth, and most of his lower jaw, had been removed. The rest of his bones were slowly breaking down, and holes were forming in his skull.

Byers gave testimony to the Federal Trade Commission, the federal office whose officials act against companies that cheat or mislead buyers. On December 19, 1931, the commission's officials ordered Bailey to stop claiming that Radithor was harmless or that it healed people. Byers died months later, in 1932, at the age of 51.

**By 1938:** Doctors had named radium jaw, a federal judge had arranged the New Jersey painters' settlement, and Illinois officials had ruled that Catherine Donohue's work at Radium Dial made her sick.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead in gasoline and paint" -->
Lead is a soft, heavy metal and an element, and it poisons people who swallow it or breathe it in. Scientists at the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the federal office that studies dangers at work, list what large doses of lead do. Their list includes paralysis (being unable to move part of the body), madness, gout (painful, swollen joints), colic (sharp pains in the belly) and death.

**The additive.** Thomas Midgley Jr., a researcher at General Motors (GM), found that tetraethyl lead, a liquid made of lead joined with other chemicals, stopped engine "knock," a pinging sound in gasoline engines. In August 1924 GM and Standard Oil of New Jersey set up the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation to sell it, and the DuPont company made it.

**The dead.** Workers who made tetraethyl lead died of lead poisoning at three plants. Standard Oil ran one, the Bayway refinery in Elizabeth, New Jersey, a plant that turned oil into gasoline and other products. Seven men died there in October 1924, and 33 went to the hospital, according to Bill Kovarik, who counted the dead in a 2005 article in a journal about workers' health.

Ten more workers died at a DuPont plant in Deepwater, New Jersey, between September 1923 and the fall of 1924. At GM's laboratory in Dayton, Ohio, at least two died, and 40 went to the hospital.

**Known:** At least 15 workers died from making tetraethyl lead in the 1920s, the count NIOSH scientists gave in 2021.
**Disputed:** Kovarik's plant-by-plant numbers add up to at least 19, while other accounts give five dead at Bayway, not seven, and 35 in the hospital.

**How the work poisoned them.** The tanks that held the chemicals were not closed off from the workers, who had to open them between steps, Kovarik writes. Workers squeezed half-melted lead through metal grates with shovels and with their boots.

At Bayway, poisoned men had to be held down and tied into straitjackets, coats that pin a person's arms to the body. Kovarik's account does not say who restrained them. Their bodies were black and blue from muscle spasms they could not stop. They saw things that were not there, such as flying insects, and some could suddenly turn violent or want to kill themselves. Workers called tetraethyl lead "loony gas."

**What the bosses said.** Frank Mann, the Bayway plant supervisor in 1924, said, "These men probably went insane because they worked too hard." A month before the deaths, in September 1924, engineers from DuPont had visited Bayway and called its safety measures "grossly inadequate."

Joseph G. Leslie, a Bayway worker poisoned there, was kept in a mental hospital for 40 years. Only his wife, Gertrude, and later his son knew he was alive, and everyone else believed he had died. Kovarik does not say who sent Leslie to the hospital or who spread the story that he was dead. He names no manager of the Deepwater plant or the Dayton laboratory apart from Midgley and Charles Kettering, who ran GM's research.

Midgley held a press conference on October 30, 1924. He poured tetraethyl lead on his hands and breathed its vapor for about 60 seconds, to show it was safe. A few months earlier, lead had made him seriously ill. At the same event he acknowledged the two deaths in Dayton and said more than 50 workers were "under observation" for lead poisoning.

**The meeting in Washington.** Hugh Cumming, the Surgeon General, ran the Public Health Service, the federal office in charge of the nation's health. On May 20, 1925, he brought more than 100 people from industry and from public health to a meeting in Washington.

Frank Howard of Standard Oil said the industry could not give up "what has come to the industry like a gift from heaven." Yandell Henderson, who studied how the body works at Yale, called tetraethyl lead "one of the most dangerous things in the country today." Alice Hamilton of Harvard told Kettering there were "thousands of things better than lead."

Members of a Surgeon General's committee reported in 1926 that they found "no good grounds" for a ban, and asked for more study. The Ethyl company paid Robert Kehoe of the University of Cincinnati to study lead. Kehoe set out the industry's "safe level" theory, the claim that lead below a certain amount did no harm, and the companies held to that claim for decades.

**Lead paint.** Painters also used paint that contained lead. Between 1925 and 1927, more painters died of lead poisoning than workers in all other jobs combined, NIOSH states.

Delegates of the International Labour Organization, a world body that sets rules for workplaces, met in Geneva in November 1921. They agreed on a treaty to ban lead paint for the insides of buildings. The ban took effect in 1923 in the countries that accepted it. The United States did not belong to the organization until 1934, and it was still outside the ban in 2017.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercury in Danbury's hat shops" -->
Danbury's hatters kept breathing mercury at work after 1900, because they still used it to turn fur into felt. Connecticut lawmakers added mercury poisoning to the state's workmen's compensation law in 1913. Under a law of that kind, employers pay workers who are hurt or made sick by their jobs.

Alice Hamilton of Harvard examined 100 Danbury hatters in 1922 and found 43 with mercury poisoning. Some shook so hard from tremors that they could not feed themselves.

**Who pushed for a fix.** The hatters in the union, a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employers, kept demanding investigations. Grace Mailhouse Burnham, who had set up the Workers' Health Bureau, found a way to make felt without mercury in the Soviet Union. She could not get the hat manufacturers to agree to use it.

Government researchers made a full study only in 1937. They found many hatters poisoned by mercury. They also found that drinking and smoking had not caused the hatters' symptoms, as some manufacturers and insurance companies had claimed in court. The historian Shirley Wajda's account of the Danbury shakes names no factory owner.

**The end of mercury felt.** Officials of the Public Health Service then worked out an agreement with the hatters' unions and the hat manufacturers in the 26 states that made hats. Under it, hat makers stopped using mercury and used hydrogen peroxide in its place.

Connecticut Governor Robert A. Hurley announced his state's ban on December 1, 1941. He said "it was possible to manufacture good fur felt hats without making physical wrecks out of hundreds of workers." Mercury is still in Danbury's soil and in the mud of the Still River.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Arsenic smoke in Montana" -->
Arsenic is an element and a strong poison. The Anaconda Copper Mining Company started its Washoe smelter, a plant where workers melt ore, rock that holds metal, to get the metal out, at Anaconda, Montana, in 1902. It stood about 25 miles from the copper mines of Butte. When workers melted copper ore there, arsenic went up the smokestack with the smoke, and farmers nearby reported livestock dying in the smelter's first year.

Farmers in the Deer Lodge Valley formed an association in 1904 to sue the company. Fred J. Bliss, whose 320-acre farm lay about five miles from the smelter, filed the case, *Bliss v. Anaconda Copper Mining Co.*, in 1905. In January 1909 a federal judge, William Hunt, agreed that arsenic from the smelter had poisoned livestock and the hay and grass they ate, but he refused to close the smelter.

**The judge's reason.** Closing the smelter, Hunt wrote, would throw thousands of the company's workers out of their jobs and do harm that could never be undone to the cities of Anaconda and Butte. Bliss could ask a court for money for his losses instead. The smelter's superintendent, a Mr. Mathewson, told the court that no known way of catching the arsenic would work at a smelter that size, and in April 1909 Hunt dismissed the case.

**Buying the smoke rights.** The company's officers then bought out many of the farmers. From the rest they bought "smoke easements," legal rights to send the company's smoke over a farm. Staff of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the federal office whose staff work to protect the country's air, water and land, wrote a history of the site. Neither that history nor the 1909 rulings name the officers who chose the buyouts, and neither counts the people harmed by the arsenic.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uranium from Navajo land" -->
Uranium is a heavy metal element whose atoms break apart slowly and give off radiation. From 1941, Native American miners, among them Navajo, Pueblo and Hopi people, worked in mines in the Southwest that dug uranium and vanadium, another metal element. The Vanadium Corporation of America began mining uranium in secret on Navajo land in 1943.

Miners working for mining companies dug nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore out of Navajo land from 1944 to 1986, EPA staff report. The companies mined under leases with the Navajo Nation, agreements to use land for a set time in return for payment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A false first, and the machine at Berkeley" -->
**By 1943:** Hat makers had stopped using mercury, Anaconda's owners had bought the right to send smoke over the farms that were left, and a company was secretly mining uranium on Navajo land.

While those poisonings went on, physicists, scientists who study matter and energy, were hunting for new elements. Fred Allison, a physicist at Alabama Polytechnic, now Auburn University, announced two new elements in 1931. He called element 85 "alabamine" and element 87 "virginium," and said he had found them with a machine in his lab.

Scientists at other labs tried his method and could not get the same results. They showed that the signals Allison saw came from his equipment, not from any element, and scientists stopped accepting his claims. A Berkeley team made the real element 85 in 1940.

**The machine at Berkeley.** Ernest Lawrence built his first cyclotron, a machine that whirls tiny charged particles in a spiral up to high speed, at Berkeley, California, in 1930 and 1931. When the fast particles hit a target, they can turn atoms of one element into atoms of another. With his machines, Berkeley's Radiation Laboratory became the place most of the new elements made in the United States came from.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ernest-lawrence-elements" name="Ernest Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ernest Lawrence

> **Who:** The physicist who invented the cyclotron, the machine behind most of the elements discovered in the United States.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from the 1930s to the 1950s. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1939 and died in 1958.

**Lawrence's first cyclotron was small enough to hold in one hand.**

Lawrence made his first cyclotron out of glass, sealing wax and bronze, and it was small enough to hold in one hand. He and his teams then built larger and larger ones, and the 60-inch cyclotron of 1939 had a magnet that weighed 220 tons. The Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley became an independent laboratory in 1936. Lawrence won the Nobel Prize in Physics, a world prize given each year, in 1939.

His machines made the material for several new elements. His 37-inch cyclotron supplied the metal sheet that held the first element made by people, and teams used his 60-inch cyclotron to make astatine and plutonium in 1940. Lawrence died in 1958. Element 103, lawrencium, carries his name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ernest-lawrence-elements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elements made by people" -->
Mendeleev's periodic table of 1869, the chart of the elements in order, had a gap at element 43, below manganese. Early in 1937, Lawrence mailed a thin sheet of a metal called molybdenum to Emilio Segrè in Palermo, Sicily. The sheet had been part of the 37-inch cyclotron.

**The first element made by people.** Segrè and Carlo Perrier, a scientist who studies minerals, took a few billionths of a gram of element 43 out of the sheet. They named it technetium, from a Greek word meaning "artificial." Technetium was the first element made by people. Italian scientists did the chemistry, and the material came from an American machine.

**The first elements found in America.** Teams in American labs made or found seven new elements in the 1940s. Most of their names came from planets, places and people.

Segrè and two other Berkeley scientists made element 85 in 1940 by striking the element bismuth with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. It was the element Allison had called "alabamine." In 1947 its makers named it astatine, from a Greek word for "unstable."

Edwin McMillan and a colleague found element 93 the same year, in uranium that had been struck in the cyclotron. It was the first element heavier than uranium. Uranium was named for the planet Uranus, so they named element 93 neptunium, for Neptune, the next planet out.

**Plutonium.** Glenn Seaborg's team made element 94 in the 60-inch cyclotron on December 14, 1940. On the night of February 23 and 24, 1941, they proved by chemistry that it was a new element, in Room 307 of Gilman Hall at Berkeley. The room is now a National Historic Landmark.

They named the element plutonium, for the planet Pluto. Seaborg chose the symbol Pu instead of "Pl" as a joke, because "pee-yoo" is what people say about a bad smell, and the members of the naming committee accepted it. Government officials kept the work secret from the start. During World War II, American scientists used plutonium and uranium to build atomic bombs.

**96 before 95.** Seaborg's team made element 96 in the summer of 1944 and named it curium, for Marie and Pierre Curie. They identified element 95 late that year and named it americium, for the Americas.

An element's number counts the protons in its atoms, not the order in which it was found. These two were so hard to separate from each other that the team nicknamed them "pandemonium" and "delirium," words for wild uproar and madness. Much of this work took place at the Metallurgical Laboratory, a secret wartime lab in Chicago.

**Told on a quiz show.** Seaborg was a guest on a children's radio show called *Quiz Kids* on November 11, 1945. A boy asked him whether any new elements had turned up during the war. Seaborg said yes, elements 95 and 96. He told the boy five days before he planned to announce them to the American Chemical Society, a national group of chemists.

Charles D. Coryell and two other chemists found element 61 in 1945 at Clinton Laboratories in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, now Oak Ridge National Laboratory, in the leftovers of uranium atoms that had been split. Grace Mary Coryell, Charles Coryell's wife, suggested the name promethium, for Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods in Greek stories. The team announced the element in 1947. Promethium is still the only element ever discovered in Tennessee.

Seaborg's team made element 97 at Berkeley in December 1949 by striking americium with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. They named it berkelium, for Berkeley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emilio-segre-elements" name="Emilio Segrè" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emilio Segrè

> **Who:** An Italian physicist who stayed in the United States after Benito Mussolini and his officials barred Jews like him from university jobs in Italy, and who helped find two elements.
> **When and where:** Palermo, Sicily, and Berkeley, California, from 1937 into the 1940s. He lived in the United States from 1938 and died in 1989.

**Mussolini's officials took Jews' university jobs while Segrè was visiting Berkeley.**

Emilio Segrè was Jewish, and he was in Berkeley for the summer when he learned the news. Benito Mussolini and his officials in Italy had issued new racial laws against Jews. Under them, Italian officials took university jobs away from Jews. Segrè stayed in the United States and became an American citizen.

He had already helped identify technetium in Palermo. At Berkeley, Segrè helped make astatine in 1940, and with Glenn Seaborg he showed in 1941 that atoms of plutonium-239 could be split.

He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1959 for his work on the antiproton, a tiny particle with the opposite electric charge of the proton, a particle in the center of every atom.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emilio-segre-elements" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="glenn-seaborg" name="Glenn Seaborg" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Glenn Seaborg

> **Who:** The chemist whose teams found plutonium and nine more elements, and the first person to see an element named for him while he was alive.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, and wartime Chicago, from 1940 to 1974. He died in 1999.

**A letter addressed only in element names would reach him, Seaborg joked.**

The address was Seaborgium, Lawrencium, Berkelium, Californium, Americium. That stood for Seaborg, at the Lawrence laboratory, in the city of Berkeley, in California, in America. Element 106 was named seaborgium after him in 1997.

Glenn T. Seaborg led the team that made plutonium in December 1940 and proved it was a new element in February 1941. In all, he helped discover ten elements, numbers 94 through 102 and number 106. One of the ten, nobelium, was later credited to scientists at Dubna in the Soviet Union.

Seaborg shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Edwin McMillan for their work on the elements heavier than uranium. From 1961 to 1971 he was chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, the federal agency in charge of atomic energy.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="glenn-seaborg" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 17 minutes**

Scientists at Berkeley and at Dubna, a lab in the Soviet Union, argued for decades over who had made the newest elements first. Teams at Berkeley and other American labs made or claimed elements 98 through 106 between 1950 and 1974, and Dubna's scientists claimed several of the same ones. In 1992 and 1993 the members of an international panel gave some credit to Berkeley, some to Dubna, and split the rest.

Uranium mining on Navajo land reached its peak in the same years. Miners breathed radioactive gas underground, and most small mines had no fresh air blown in until the 1960s or later. Government researchers and mine owners did not tell the miners the danger, and hundreds of miners died of lung disease and cancer. Other scientists proved that lead was harming children's brains, and between 1971 and 1996 federal officials limited and then banned lead in house paint and in gasoline for cars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elements from Berkeley and from a bomb test" -->
Kenneth Street Jr., Stanley G. Thompson, Albert Ghiorso and Glenn Seaborg made element 98 at Berkeley early in 1950 by striking curium with helium particles. They announced it on March 17, 1950, and named it californium, for the state and its university.

**Two elements from a bomb.** Elements 99 and 100 turned up in the dust of Ivy Mike, the test of a hydrogen bomb, a bomb far more powerful than the first atomic bombs. The test took place at Enewetak Atoll, a ring of small coral islands in the Pacific Ocean, on November 1, 1952. The blast wiped out one of the atoll's islands, Elugelab, turning it to vapor.

Drone planes with no one aboard flew through the mushroom cloud, carrying filter papers to catch the dust. Workers then shipped tons of coral from the blast site to Berkeley. In the dust and coral, Ghiorso and teams from Berkeley, Argonne and Los Alamos found two new elements. In the blast, uranium atoms had soaked up huge numbers of neutrons, tiny particles in the center of an atom.

Government officials classified the discoveries, keeping them secret. The scientists published them on August 1, 1955, with the names einsteinium and fermium. By then both men they honored had died, Enrico Fermi in November 1954 and Einstein in April 1955. Neither man was told that an element would carry his name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Seventeen atoms, one at a time" -->
A Berkeley team made element 101 in 1955 from about a billion atoms of einsteinium, all the einsteinium that existed. The team was Ghiorso, Bernard G. Harvey, Gregory R. Choppin, Thompson and Seaborg. They struck the einsteinium with particles, and the new atoms flew off onto a gold foil, a way of catching atoms that Ghiorso had worked out. The team dissolved the foil and rushed it through the chemistry.

They made 17 atoms of element 101 in all. It was the first element identified one atom at a time. In the scientists' own telling, Ghiorso wired the detector to the building's fire alarm, so the bell rang for each new atom. He forgot to disconnect it, and the alarm later emptied the building late at night.

The team named element 101 mendelevium, for Dmitri Mendeleev, the Russian who made the periodic table. That was during the Cold War, the long contest between the United States and the Soviet Union, and Seaborg asked the United States government for approval before he used a Russian's name. A French chemist later told Ghiorso that the name did more for American and Soviet relations than most diplomacy, the work of officials who handle dealings between countries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fight over elements 102 to 106" -->
Berkeley and Dubna each claimed to have made elements 102 through 106 first. Each side gave the elements its own names and argued for them in science journals for decades. In 1992 and 1993 a panel of chemists and physicists called the Transfermium Working Group, named for the elements after fermium, element 100, split the credit between the two labs, and the names were settled in 1997.

**Element 102.** Scientists in three countries worked on element 102. A team in Stockholm, Sweden, claimed it in 1957 and named it nobelium, for Alfred Nobel, then took back its claim. Berkeley scientists claimed the element in 1958, and scientists at Dubna did experiments on it in 1966. The panel's members credited Dubna. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), the world organization of chemists whose officials approve element names, kept the Swedish name nobelium anyway, because scientists had used it for more than 30 years.

The panel's members split the credit for elements 103, 104 and 105 between Berkeley and Dubna. They credited Berkeley alone with element 106. Element 103 kept the name lawrencium, for Ernest Lawrence.

**Seaborgium.** IUPAC's officials proposed a rule in 1994 against naming elements for living people. Under that rule, they could not have approved the Berkeley name for element 106, seaborgium. In 1997 they set the proposal aside and approved the final names: rutherfordium for element 104, after the scientist Ernest Rutherford, dubnium for element 105, after the town of Dubna, and seaborgium for element 106.

Glenn Seaborg was the first person to see an element named for him while he was alive. He kept a periodic table with seaborgium on it until he died in 1999.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-ghiorso" name="Albert Ghiorso" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Ghiorso

> **Who:** The builder of instruments who helped discover more elements than anyone else in history, without ever earning a doctorate.
> **When and where:** Berkeley, California, from 1944 to 1974, with work into the 1990s. He died in 2010.

**He helped discover 12 elements and never earned a doctorate.**

Albert Ghiorso helped discover americium through seaborgium, numbers 95 to 106, without a doctorate, the highest university degree. Guinness World Records lists his 12 as the most elements discovered or co-discovered by one person.

Ghiorso built the tools the element hunters needed, among them the detectors. He worked out the method of catching new atoms on a foil, which his team used to find mendelevium in 1955. And he ran the HILAC, a machine at Berkeley that sped up particles to make new elements.

One story his colleagues told about him has him driving fresh, radioactive samples across the lab in a Volkswagen car.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-ghiorso" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="darleane-hoffman-elements" name="Darleane Hoffman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Darleane Hoffman

> **Who:** A nuclear chemist who proved that plutonium, an element people had made in labs, also occurs in nature.
> **When and where:** Los Alamos, New Mexico, from 1953, and Berkeley, California, from 1984. She died in 2025, at the age of 98.

**She found plutonium, an element made in labs, in ancient California rock.**

Darleane C. Hoffman was a nuclear chemist, a chemist who studies atoms that break apart and give off radiation. She began work at Los Alamos in 1953. In 1971 she found traces of plutonium-244 in ancient rock from the Mountain Pass mine in California. Until then plutonium had been known only as an element made in labs, and her find showed that tiny amounts of it also occur naturally on Earth.

Hoffman showed that atoms of fermium-257 could split in two on their own. She was the first woman to lead a science division at Los Alamos. From 1984 she led work at Berkeley on the chemistry of the heaviest elements, studying them one atom at a time.

She won the National Medal of Science in 1997. In 2000 she won the American Chemical Society's Priestley Medal, named for Joseph Priestley, as only the second woman ever to receive it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="darleane-hoffman-elements" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Elements at home" -->
Many home smoke detectors hold a speck of americium-241, about a third of a millionth of a gram. The americium gives off alpha particles inside a small chamber. The particles let a tiny electric current run through the air in the chamber. Smoke that drifts in disturbs the current, and the alarm goes off.

Silicon is an element that was discovered in Sweden in 1824. Computer chips are made of it, and Silicon Valley takes its name from the element in the chips made there.

The federal helium program was $1.4 billion in debt by 1996. That year members of Congress passed the Helium Privatization Act and ordered the Federal Helium Reserve sold.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uranium miners on Navajo land" -->
While the element hunters argued over credit, uranium miners on Navajo land were breathing radioactive gas. Their ore went to one buyer. EPA staff state that the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC), the federal agency in charge of atomic energy, was the only buyer until 1966 and kept buying ore until 1970. Doug Brugge and Rob Goble, writing in the *American Journal of Public Health* in 2002, say the government was the only buyer from World War II until 1971.

About 10,000 people worked in uranium mining in all, and about a quarter of them were Navajo. The mining peaked in the middle of the 1950s, with about 750 mines working. The companies named in the records include the Vanadium Corporation of America, Kerr-McGee, the United Nuclear Corporation and Hydro Resources Inc.

**What radon does.** When uranium atoms break apart, they turn into radon, a radioactive gas. Radon atoms then break apart into radioactive specks called radon daughters, and miners breathed them in. Brugge and Goble explain that the daughters stay in the lungs next to delicate cells and hit those cells with strong doses of radiation. The harm named in the records is lung cancer and lung fibrosis, scarring that makes the lungs stiff, so that breathing gets harder.

Navajo miners report that no one told them about the dangers of uranium mining. They say they got no protective equipment and no ventilation, the fans and shafts that blow fresh air into a mine and push radon out. Scientists already knew about the danger from uranium mines in Europe. Brugge and Goble found that few protections were in place for American miners before 1962, and they came slowly after that.

**A warning turned down.** Members of a federal panel, the Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments, traced who made these choices in their 1995 report. In 1948 Merril Eisenbud, a safety expert in the AEC's New York office, urged AEC officials to write safety rules into the agency's contracts to buy ore. Officials of two AEC divisions, Biology and Medicine and Raw Materials, rejected his advice. They said that under the law the AEC had no power over safety in the mines.

**The study that did not warn them.** Public Health Service workers led by Duncan Holaday and Victor Archer followed the miners from 1950 to measure their cancer risk. Accounts give the study group two sizes. Linda M. Richards, writing in 2013 for *Distillations*, the magazine of the Science History Institute, counts 3,415 miners, 779 of them Native American. The 1995 federal committee counted about 4,100. Brugge and Goble found that the researchers did not tell the miners about the risks they were studying.

At first the researchers left the minority miners out of the results, saying they wanted "a homogeneous population," a group of people who are all alike. Duncan Holaday later testified in court that the researchers made a spoken agreement with the mine owners not to tell the miners what they found. They made that deal so the owners would let them into the mines.

Staff of the AEC and of the Public Health Service met on January 25, 1951. The staff at that meeting believed the radon in the mines was at levels that would cause cancer. They agreed that ventilation could lower it, but they called it "unrealistic" to set a limit the mine owners had to meet.

**Hueper's speech.** Wilhelm Hueper, a cancer researcher at the National Cancer Institute, warned about radon. His figures showed lung cancer in 40 to 75 percent of the radioactive ore miners in Europe. In 1952 Shields Warren of the AEC asked the institute's director to stop Hueper from giving those figures in a speech to the Colorado Medical Society. The director ordered Hueper to cut them, and Hueper did not give the speech.

Warren then asked that Hueper be fired. Hueper kept his job, but Victor Archer later said Hueper was forbidden to travel west of the Mississippi River for his research. The 1995 committee report that gives Archer's account does not say who gave that order.

Public Health Service officials urged the mining companies to ventilate. Some large mines got ventilation in the 1950s and 1960s, but most small mines got none until the 1960s or later. Secretary of Labor Willard Wirtz set the first federal radon limit for these mines in 1967. The committee's members found that the mine owners, the state governments and the federal government all had a duty to ventilate the mines after 1951.

**In court.** A federal judge found in 1984, in a case called *Begay v. United States*, that Public Health Service officials, working under the Surgeon General, decided not to warn the miners because they feared many would quit. The judge still ruled that the miners could not sue the United States government. The law that lets people sue the government for harm does not cover choices that officials make as a matter of policy, and the judge found that the choice not to warn the miners was one of those.

In the ruling, the judge wrote that the miners' situation "cries for redress," meaning it called out to be put right, and called it a "tragedy of the nuclear age." Appeals judges upheld the ruling in 1985.

A study from 2000, cited by Brugge and Goble, found 94 deaths from lung cancer among Navajo people from 1969 to 1993, and 63 of them had been uranium miners. Richards reported that by the early 1980s "hundreds of Indian miners had died from lung diseases and cancers."

**Known:** The 1995 federal committee found that 410 of the 4,100 miners in the Public Health Service study had died of lung cancer by 1990, more than five times the 75 deaths expected.
**Not known:** How many Navajo miners died in all, since none of these accounts gives a full count.

**The law of 1990.** Members of Congress passed the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act on October 5, 1990, and President George H. W. Bush signed it on October 15. Under it, a uranium worker made sick by radiation on the job could claim one payment of $100,000, if the worker met the law's rules.

Harris Charley was a Navajo uranium miner. His records show he breathed 1,192 "working level months" of radon, a unit that measures how much radon a miner breathed over time. He died of lung fibrosis in 1986.

His son, Perry H. Charley, became an environmental scientist and worked with Stewart Udall, a former Secretary of the Interior, to investigate the miners' illnesses. In 1996 he helped start the Uranium Education Program at Diné College, which carries the Navajo people's own name for themselves. Navajo organizers, among them Harry Tome, Perry Charley and Phil Harrison, pushed for help for the miners from the 1960s on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The spill at Church Rock" -->
An earthen dam owned by the United Nuclear Corporation broke near Church Rock, New Mexico, on July 16, 1979. The dam held waste from the company's uranium mill, a plant that takes uranium out of crushed rock. The waste was tailings, the ground-up rock left behind after the uranium is taken out.

The waste ran at least 80 miles down the Rio Puerco, a river that flows past the homes of about 1,700 Navajo people. Accounts give slightly different amounts. Richards gives 1,000 tons of radioactive tailings and 93 million gallons of acid, radioactive water. New Mexico's Environmental Improvement Division, the state office that checked for pollution, gives 1,100 tons and 94 million gallons.

Measured in curies, a unit of radioactivity, the spill released 46. The accident at the Three Mile Island nuclear power plant, four months earlier, released 13. The U.S. Geological Survey called Church Rock "the largest accidental radioactive release in the United States."

The authors of the Environmental Improvement Division's own report saw it differently. They concluded that the spill's effects "on people and the environment were quite limited." They wrote that the water pumped out of the mines into the Puerco River might be a greater long-term danger.

**What officials did.** Within a few hours, officials of the Environmental Improvement Division began testing the river. They ordered United Nuclear to stop sending waste to the broken pond, and the company stopped milling for about three months. No official declared an emergency or ordered anyone to leave.

New Mexico's governor, Bruce King, turned down the Navajo Nation's request to have the area declared a federal disaster area, according to news accounts. The state's report on the spill names no United Nuclear official as responsible for the dam.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead: the proof and the bans" -->
Thousands of American children went to the hospital each year in the 1960s with lead encephalopathy, swelling and damage of the brain, and about one in four of them died. A 2007 review in the journal *Environmental Health Perspectives* states those numbers.

**Where the lead came from.** Before the 1960s, the poisoned children usually lived in old, poorly kept slum housing, David Rosner and Gerald Markowitz wrote in 2012. The children had swallowed flakes of lead paint and went into convulsions, fits of violent shaking they could not control. Rosner and Markowitz call lead paint in older buildings the major source of lead around children.

Lead harms a child's growing brain. It lowers intelligence and causes trouble with reading, paying attention and behavior, and the 2007 review states that no safe level of lead is known. The review names the main ones to blame: the paint and pigment industry, the oil industry and a few scientists paid by industry.

**The proof.** Clair Patterson, a Caltech scientist who studied the chemistry of rocks and the Earth, measured lead in the air at about 1,000 times its natural level. In a 1965 paper he called the "safe level" idea of Robert Kehoe, the scientist the Ethyl company had paid to study lead, "an ill-defined opinion unsupported by any evidence."

Herbert Needleman, a children's doctor in Pittsburgh, measured the lead in the baby teeth of about 2,500 schoolchildren in the 1970s. Children with more lead in their teeth did worse in school. Scientists paid by the lead industry challenged his work, and later reviews backed his findings, according to Bill Kovarik, who wrote the 2005 article about the tetraethyl lead deaths.

**The bans.** Members of Congress passed the Lead-Based Paint Poisoning Prevention Act in 1971. Under it, homes built or repaired with federal money could have only limited amounts of lead paint, and in 1972 the limit was set at 0.5 percent of the paint. Officials of the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission banned lead paint in all homes in 1978, and from then on house paint could hold no more than 0.06 percent lead. Homes built before 1978 are still likely to have some lead paint.

EPA officials began work on a rule to cut the lead in gasoline in 1972 and 1973. Gas stations began selling unleaded gasoline, and car makers built cars for it, in 1975 and 1976. The Clean Air Act's ban on leaded gasoline for cars and trucks took effect on January 1, 1996. On January 29, EPA Administrator Carol M. Browner announced the last step in 25 years of cutting lead out of gasoline. She said the amount of lead in children's blood had fallen 70 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poisoned ground put on the cleanup list" -->
EPA officials put both the Anaconda smelter site in Montana and the Church Rock mill on the National Priorities List in 1983. The list includes the country's most dangerous waste sites, chosen for cleanup under the federal Superfund program.

Smelting at Anaconda had stopped in 1980. EPA staff count nearly 100 years of smelting at the whole site, which left arsenic, cadmium, copper, lead and zinc over more than 200 square miles. EPA staff list Atlantic Richfield, the company that bought the smelter in 1977, as responsible for the cleanup.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 2000" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Between 1950 and 2000, researchers proved what radon did to uranium miners and what lead did to children, and federal officials banned leaded gasoline and began paying sick uranium workers.
**One number:** 70 percent. That is how far the amount of lead in children's blood had fallen by January 1996, after 25 years of cutting lead out of gasoline, by the count Carol Browner announced.
**Next:** After 2000, Russian and American scientists finished the seventh row of the periodic table together, and lead from old pipes got into the tap water of Flint, Michigan.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 11 minutes**

The heaviest elements yet came from Russian and American scientists working together. Joint teams made elements 114 through 118 between 1998 and 2010. The atoms formed at Dubna, in Russia, and scientists from American labs brought detectors and, for element 117, the only target material in the world that would work. In 2015 and 2016 IUPAC's officials recognized the last four elements of the periodic table's seventh row and gave them names.

The poisons of earlier years were still in the ground and the pipes. As of June 2026, EPA staff count 523 abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation. Managers appointed by Michigan's governor switched the water supply of Flint, Michigan, in 2014, and into 2015 the water carried lead from old pipes to the city's people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Finishing the seventh row" -->
Scientists at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, Russia, worked with scientists from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California. For elements 115 and 117, scientists from Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Vanderbilt University joined them. The first atom of element 114 formed at Dubna in December 1998, and element 116 followed in 2000.

IUPAC's officials named element 114 flerovium in May 2012, for Russia's Flerov Laboratory. They named element 116 livermorium, for the Livermore laboratory and the city of Livermore, California.

**The row filled.** IUPAC's officials recognized elements 113, 115, 117 and 118 on December 30, 2015, which filled the seventh row of the periodic table, and they announced the names on November 28, 2016. Scientists at RIKEN, a research institute in Japan, made element 113, the first element discovered in Asia, and it became nihonium. Element 115 was named moscovium, for the region around Moscow, and element 117 tennessine, for Tennessee.

Element 118 became oganesson, for Yuri Oganessian, the Dubna physicist who led the hunt for these heavy elements. It was only the second element named for a living person, after seaborgium. His colleagues asked him to leave the conference call before they voted on the name. IUPAC's officials credited these elements to the joint teams, made up of scientists from several countries who worked together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tennessine and the berkelium target" -->
Joe Hamilton of Vanderbilt University started the partnership on element 117 when he introduced Jim Roberto of Oak Ridge to Yuri Oganessian. To make element 117, the Dubna team needed a target made of berkelium-249, and the only place that could make it was a research reactor at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The berkelium spent 250 days in the reactor. Oak Ridge chemists then spent 90 days working on it and ended up with 22 milligrams, thousandths of a gram.

**Five trips across the Atlantic.** The berkelium was shipped in June 2009. Russian customs officials, who check what comes into a country at its border, twice rejected the paperwork, so the package crossed the Atlantic Ocean five times. At Dubna, scientists fired a beam of calcium-48 atoms at the target for six months and made six atoms of element 117. The team published the discovery in April 2010.

Tennessee became the second American state named on the periodic table, after California. The atoms of tennessine formed in Russia. Promethium, found at Oak Ridge in 1945, is still the only element discovered in Tennessee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clarice-phelps" name="Clarice Phelps" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clarice Phelps

> **Who:** A nuclear chemist at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and a U.S. Navy veteran. Her laboratory's staff profile describes her as the first Black woman involved in the discovery of an element.
> **When and where:** Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Tennessee, from 2009. Her berkelium work began in 2010, and the laboratory recognized her in 2019.

**She helped purify berkelium for the experiments that confirmed element 117.**

Clarice Phelps served in the U.S. Navy and graduated from Tennessee State University. She joined Oak Ridge National Laboratory in 2009, and her first job there was as a nuclear operations technician.

She worked in the laboratory's Nuclear Security and Isotope Technology Division from 2010. There she was part of a team that helped purify berkelium-249, removing everything else from it. Scientists used that berkelium to confirm the discovery of element 117. The berkelium for the first run at Dubna had shipped in June 2009, before she began this work.

A 2019 news release from Oak Ridge National Laboratory described her as "the first African American woman to be involved with the discovery of an element, tennessine." That first is her laboratory's claim. IUPAC's officials credited Oak Ridge as a laboratory, not its scientists one by one. The same year, she was named to the Periodic Table of Younger Chemists, a project of IUPAC and the International Younger Chemists Network.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarice-phelps" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Toward element 120, and the end of federal helium" -->
Scientists in the Heavy Element Group at Berkeley Lab made two atoms of livermorium in a new way in 2024. They fired a beam of titanium-50 at plutonium-244 in the lab's 88-Inch Cyclotron, and Berkeley Lab's news center reported the result on July 23, 2024. The test showed that the method could work for element 120.

Element 120 would start the eighth row of the periodic table, and it would be the heaviest atom ever made. The Berkeley team began runs to make it in 2025, firing titanium-50 at californium-249. As of August 2026, no one had announced a discovery.

**The end of federal helium.** Federal officials sold the Federal Helium System, which included the reserve, the Cliffside gas field and a pipeline 423 miles long, to Messer, a company that sells industrial gases. The sale was completed in June 2024. Messer's accepted bids, opened in January 2024, totaled $423.35 million. Officials of the Bureau of Land Management, a federal agency, stated in 2024 that the sale in the end sent about $460 million to the U.S. Treasury, the government's money office.

Helium is still scarce. Hospitals need it for MRI machines, which take pictures of the inside of the body, and rocket makers and computer chip makers use it too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uranium on Navajo land today" -->
Elsie Mae Begay, a Navajo woman, lived in Monument Valley below the abandoned Skyline uranium mine. Two of her sons who had lived with her died, one of lung cancer and the other of a tumor. Linda Richards, who reported her story in 2013, calls them rare cancers. Richards does not say whether doctors linked the sons' cancers to the mine.

Begay's family hogan, a traditional Navajo house, gave off radiation up to 100 times the acceptable level, the Associated Press reported. When her children were young, they had played in piles of uranium tailings. Richards writes that people testing Navajo homes found dangerous radioactivity where families had built with mine debris or mixed it into concrete floors. EPA officials treated the Begay hogan as nuclear waste, and in 2001 men in radiation suits took it apart and hauled it away.

**The Navajo ban.** Members of the Navajo Nation Council passed the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act in 2005. Under it, uranium mining is banned on Navajo land.

**How many mines.** EPA staff count 523 abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation as of June 2026. They have money to study and clean up 230 of them, fewer than half, and they list 46 as priority mines. Richards, counting abandoned mine sites rather than mines, found at least 1,032 on Navajo land in 2013, and 38 water sources with more radioactive elements than health limits allow.

EPA officials had reached agreements and settlements over the mines worth more than $1.7 billion by June 2026. A settlement is an agreement to pay money to end a legal fight.

About 3,975 uranium workers had applied for payments under the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act by March 2001, according to Brugge and Goble. The law ran out for a time. Members of Congress renewed it as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which became law on July 4, 2025. According to the Department of Justice in September 2026, workers must file claims by December 31, 2027, and a uranium worker who qualifies receives $100,000, paid once.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead in the water of Flint" -->
Flint, Michigan, is a majority-Black city. Emergency managers switched its water supply in April 2014. Michigan Governor Rick Snyder had appointed them to run the city in place of its elected leaders. Flint had been getting Detroit's water, drawn from Lake Huron, and the new supply came from the Flint River.

**Who made the switch.** Governor Snyder later appointed the Flint Water Advisory Task Force to find out what went wrong. Its 2016 report states that Flint's emergency manager Ed Kurtz approved using the Flint River, and that Darnell Earley, the emergency manager in April 2014, oversaw the switch. A timeline published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the federal health agency, states that the river water had no corrosion control, a treatment that keeps water from eating into pipes.

Detroit's water carried a chemical called orthophosphate, which protects pipes from corrosion. The Flint River water had none, and it was high in chloride, a salt. The river water ate into the city's old pipes.

Flint's service lines carry water from the main line under the street into each house. Between 10 and 80 percent of them were made of lead, according to an estimate published in 2016. Lead was in the water for about 18 months, reporters for NPR, a national radio network, said in 2023.

**The children's blood.** Mona Hanna-Attisha, a children's doctor at Hurley Medical Center in Flint, and her colleagues compared blood tests of 1,473 children in Flint with 2,202 outside the city, in a study published in 2016. Among Flint children under 5, the share with high lead in their blood about doubled, from 2.4 percent in 2013 to 4.9 percent in 2015. In the parts of the city with the most lead in the water, it rose from 4.0 to 10.6 percent.

Children absorb 40 to 50 percent of the lead they swallow in water, and adults absorb 3 to 10 percent. Babies who drank formula mixed with tap water were at the highest risk.

**The warnings.** Miguel Del Toral, an EPA expert on lead in water, warned officials of Michigan's Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ) on February 27, 2015. He wrote that MDEQ's instructions for collecting water samples made the lead readings come out low, and that a federal rule, the Lead and Copper Rule, required Flint to have corrosion control. The same day, Stephen Busch of MDEQ wrote back that Flint had working corrosion control. The task force found that this was wrong.

Del Toral asked another MDEQ official on April 25, 2015, how Flint could meet the rules with no corrosion control. That official traded e-mails about his question with Busch and Mike Prysby of MDEQ. In July 2015 MDEQ's spokesman, Brad Wurfel, wrote that Flint's residents "do not need to worry about lead in the water supply."

Hanna-Attisha and her colleagues at Hurley Medical Center told pregnant women and children to stop drinking the water in September 2015. Flint was connected again to a regional water system in 2015. Snyder asked for federal help on January 14, 2016, President Barack Obama declared a federal emergency in Flint two days later, and EPA officials issued their own emergency order on January 21.

**Legionnaires' disease.** Legionnaires' disease, a lung infection and a kind of pneumonia, is caused by bacteria that grow in poorly treated water. Genesee County, where Flint is, had 90 cases from June 2014 to October 2015. NPR reporters linked the outbreak in 2018 to low chlorine, the chemical that kills germs in drinking water, in Flint's water.

**Known:** State health officials counted 10 dead in one report and 12 in another.
**Disputed:** The official count, because reporters for the PBS program *Frontline* found more deaths from pneumonia that it missed.

**The charges.** None of the officials charged over Flint's water was convicted of a felony, one of the most serious kinds of crime. Michigan's attorney general, the state's top lawyer, charged 15 officials in 2016 and 2017, Busch, Prysby and Earley among them. Seven of them took plea deals for lesser crimes called misdemeanors. In June 2019 the prosecutors who had taken over the cases dropped the charges against the other eight, saying the cases were only partly built.

The same prosecutors charged Governor Snyder and eight other officials in January 2021, Earley among them. Snyder faced two counts of willful neglect of duty, knowingly failing to do his job. The state's health director and its chief medical executive each faced nine counts of involuntary manslaughter, causing a person's death through carelessness without meaning to kill.

**How the cases ended.** A one-judge grand jury, a single judge who decides alone whether to charge someone with a crime, had brought the 2021 charges. The judges of the Michigan Supreme Court ruled in 2022 that this broke the state constitution. The 2021 cases ended on October 31, 2023, with no one found guilty.

Flint's people sued. U.S. District Judge Judith Levy approved a settlement of $626.25 million on November 10, 2021, for more than 90,000 Flint residents and businesses.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- Who lost their land when Americans found gold and copper on it? The Cherokee lost theirs after gold turned up in Georgia in 1829, when Georgia lawmakers gave it away by lottery and soldiers forced them west in 1838. The Ojibwe bands of Lake Superior gave up the copper country in the Treaty of La Pointe in 1842, and members of Congress took the Black Hills from the Sioux in 1877, after Custer's men found gold there.
- What did the people in charge say about the radium, lead and uranium that poisoned workers? Supervisors told the dial painters the paint was safe, the Bayway supervisor said his poisoned men had worked too hard, and Thomas Midgley breathed the vapor of tetraethyl lead in front of reporters to show it was safe. Public Health Service researchers agreed with mine owners not to tell the uranium miners what they found.
- When did someone in the United States first discover a new element? In 1940, at Berkeley, California, when teams using Ernest Lawrence's cyclotron made astatine and neptunium, and then plutonium. Every element before that had been found abroad, and Fred Allison's 1931 claims were false.

**What changed:** Nobody anywhere knew what an element was in 1500, and by 2010 scientists from American labs had helped make element 118, the heaviest yet.

**Five dates to keep**
- 1799: Conrad Reed finds a 17-pound gold nugget in North Carolina.
- October 4, 1842: Ojibwe bands give up the Keweenaw copper country in the Treaty of La Pointe.
- June 8, 1928: The New Jersey dial painters sign their settlement with the U.S. Radium Corporation.
- December 14, 1940: Glenn Seaborg's team makes plutonium at Berkeley.
- April 2014: Emergency managers switch Flint's water to the Flint River.

**Not known:** How many Navajo uranium miners died of their work in all, because each count that exists covers only some of them.

**Where to go:** Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site, on the Saugus River near Boston, is the place where Hammersmith's furnace, forge and mill worked from the 1640s until about 1670.
