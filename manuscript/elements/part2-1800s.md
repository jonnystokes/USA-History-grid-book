## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, miners rushed to dig gold in the South and copper in the North. The Georgia gold lay under Cherokee land, and the Michigan copper lay under Ojibwe land. Georgia lawmakers gave the Cherokee land away by lottery, a drawing of names by chance. In 1842 a United States official signed a treaty, a written agreement between nations, under which Ojibwe bands gave up the copper country. The bands were groups within the Ojibwe nation.

A mineral rush happens when thousands of people hurry to a place where someone has found a valuable metal. Everything is made of tiny pieces called atoms, and an element is a substance made of only one kind of atom, such as gold or copper. Nobody in the United States discovered a new element in these years. In Europe, chemists such as Humphry Davy and Jöns Jacob Berzelius found one element after another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold on Cherokee land" -->
In 1799 Conrad Reed, a 12-year-old boy, found a 17-pound lump of gold in a creek on his family's farm in North Carolina. After that, more people dug for gold on the Reed farm and along the creeks near it. That mining grew from 1803. Miners in North Carolina dug more gold than miners in any other state until the California gold rush of 1849.

The first printed report of gold in Georgia appeared in the *Georgia Journal*, a newspaper, on August 1, 1829. The gold was in the Cherokee Nation, the homeland of the Cherokee people. By the spring of 1830, about 4,000 miners had crowded onto Cherokee land to dig. The gold town of Dahlonega takes its name from the Cherokee word for "golden."

Georgia's lawmakers met as the Georgia General Assembly. On December 24, 1831, they passed a law. Under it, they divided "the original Cherokee Indian territory" into lots and gave it away by lottery. In a lottery, names are drawn by chance. In the Gold Lottery, winners received lots of 40 acres in the gold country for a fee of $10 a lot. The drawings ran from October 22, 1832, to May 1, 1833. Single men aged 18 or older who were United States citizens, widows and married men could enter if they had lived in Georgia for three years. Families of orphans could enter too. The lawmakers barred anyone who had mined gold in the Cherokee territory since June 1, 1830. They also barred anyone who lived in the Cherokee territory, so the Cherokee who lived there could not enter. The lots went to white Georgians.

In 1838 President Martin Van Buren sent General Winfield Scott to force the remaining Cherokee off their land and west of the Mississippi River. Scott commanded about 7,000 soldiers and members of state militias, which were part-time armies run by the states. These troops forced the Cherokee into stockades, which are pens fenced with tall wooden posts. In June 1838 a missionary wrote that the Cherokee "have been dragged from their houses." In Georgia, he wrote, "multitudes were allowed no time to take any thing with them except the clothes they had on."

Scott's soldiers forced more than 16,000 Cherokee to leave for the west in 1838 and 1839. This forced journey is called the Trail of Tears. Over 4,000 Cherokee died of cold, hunger and disease along the way, by the usual count. National Park Service staff count more than a thousand deaths on the trip itself and perhaps several thousand more from the results of the removal. The demographer Russell Thornton, a scientist who studies the size of populations, puts the Cherokee losses at about 8,000 or more.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Copper on Ojibwe land" -->
Douglass Houghton was the state geologist of Michigan, a scientist the state paid to study its rocks. In 1840 he published a report on the copper of Michigan's Upper Peninsula. The Keweenaw Peninsula there has veins of pure native copper. Veins are long, thin bands of metal running through rock, and native copper is copper that lies in the ground as pure metal. Native miners had worked those same veins for thousands of years.

The land belonged to Ojibwe bands. On October 4, 1842, a United States commissioner named Robert Stuart signed the Treaty of La Pointe with Ojibwe bands. A commissioner is an official sent to make an agreement. Under the treaty, the Ojibwe bands gave up land along the south shore of Lake Superior. That land included the Keweenaw copper country.

The treaty states that the Ojibwe would receive $12,500 a year in coins and $10,500 a year in goods for 25 years. It also states that $75,000 would go toward debts the Ojibwe owed to traders. The Ojibwe kept the right to hunt on the land "until required to remove by the President of the United States." That meant they could hunt there only until the President ordered them to leave.

The treaty never uses the word copper, but people came to call it the Copper Treaty. According to the Bad River Band of Ojibwe and the Lac Courte Oreilles Ojibwa College, United States officials wanted the copper.

In 1843, United States government officials opened an office at Copper Harbor to handle land with metal in it. Thousands of prospectors poured into the Upper Peninsula from that year on. A prospector is a person who searches the ground for metal. That rush was the nation's first great copper rush. The Keweenaw stayed the country's biggest source of copper into the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ontonagon Boulder" -->
The Ontonagon Boulder is a single lump of pure copper that weighs 3,708 pounds. It lay in the Ontonagon River country near Lake Superior. The Ojibwe had known the boulder for a long time, and they held it sacred. The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community, made up of the L'Anse and Ontonagon bands of Lake Superior Ojibwe, states that people made offerings at it.

Julius Eldred was a merchant from Detroit. In the summer of 1841 he made a deal for the boulder with Okandikan, a leader of the Ontonagon band of Ojibwe. Eldred agreed to pay $45 right away and $105 more if he managed to move it. In 1843 Eldred hauled the sacred boulder away. Walter Cunningham was an agent of the United States government in charge of the lands with metal in them. Before Eldred could leave Copper Harbor, Cunningham made him hand the boulder over to the government in Washington. Later, the members of Congress paid Eldred money to make up for his loss.

The boulder sat in a yard of the War Department until 1860. The War Department was the part of the United States government that ran the army. In 1860 the boulder was moved to the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, D.C., and put on display.

In 1991, the Keweenaw Bay Indian Community formally asked the Smithsonian to return the boulder. In 2000 Smithsonian officials refused. They said the boulder did not fit the meaning of a sacred object under the law on returning Native objects. The boulder stayed at the Smithsonian.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead at Galena" -->
Miners also dug lead in these years, in Missouri and in the Galena district of Illinois and Wisconsin. Galena is a lead ore, a rock that contains lead. The town of Galena, Illinois, is named for that ore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1886 Charles Martin Hall, a 22-year-old in Oberlin, Ohio, found a cheap way to make aluminum. By the middle of the 1890s, the price of aluminum had fallen from about $12 a pound to less than $1. Scientists abroad already knew aluminum as an element. What Hall found was a process, a series of steps for making the metal.

Gold miners in California used mercury and released about 13 million pounds of it at their mines and into rivers. Hatmakers in Danbury, Connecticut, breathed mercury at work and were poisoned by it. Miners took gold from the Black Hills, land that belonged to the Sioux. In 1877 the members of Congress voted to take the Black Hills from the Sioux.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercury in the rivers and the hat shops" -->
Mercury is an element. It is a metal that stays liquid at room temperature, and people also call it quicksilver. Gold miners used mercury to catch the fine bits of gold in the gravel they dug. They lost mercury into the rivers.

The United States Geological Survey is the government office that studies the country's rocks and water. Its scientists found that California mines produced more than 220 million pounds of mercury between 1850 and 1981. The mercury came from mines in the Coast Range, a line of mountains along the California coast. About 26 million pounds of it went into California's gold mining. Placer miners wash gold out of sand and gravel in streams. They released about 10 million pounds of mercury. About 80 to 90 percent of that 10 million pounds was released in the Sierra Nevada mountains. Miners at hardrock mines, which are dug into solid rock, released about 3 million pounds more.

By 2005, California officials had warned people about eating fish from about 20 lakes, rivers and other waters, because of mercury. The Geological Survey's scientists list the ways this mercury reaches people: in fish they eat, in mud they handle and in vapor they breathe. A vapor is a gas that rises off a liquid or a solid. The form of mercury that builds up most easily in living bodies, fish included, is called methylmercury. It is also the form most poisonous to people. Fish in the Bear and Yuba river areas carry enough mercury to put people's health at risk, the scientists found. They give no count of people poisoned by the gold miners' mercury.

People in Danbury, Connecticut, had made hats since 1780. To make felt, a thick cloth of pressed fur, they soaked fur in a liquid that contained mercury nitrate, a compound of mercury. A compound is a substance made of two or more elements joined together. The fur then matted together into felt. This step was called carroting. As they worked, the hatters breathed in the mercury as vapor and as dust.

The hatters' mercury poisoning first appeared in medical writing in 1860. People called it the Danbury shakes. The poisoned hatters had tremors, which are shaking movements a person cannot stop. Their skin itched, burned or peeled. Some lost their hair and teeth. They sweated, swelled up, felt dizzy and got confused. The mercury slurred their speech and made them stumble, and people mistook the shaking hatters for drunks. In bad cases, hatters had hallucinations, which means they saw or heard things that were not there.

The members of Connecticut's State Board of Health watched the mercury's effects in Danbury's hat factories in the 1880s and 1890s. They did not act, because the carroting was not harming people outside the factories. The historian Shirley Wajda, who wrote the history of the Danbury shakes for Connecticut Humanities, names no factory owner and gives no count of hatters poisoned before 1900.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Silver, copper and a table from Russia" -->
In 1859, miners found the Comstock Lode at Virginia City, Nevada. A lode is a long, rich band of ore inside rock. The Comstock was the richest find of silver ore ever made in North America. Its ore held gold as well as silver, and miners rushed there too.

In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table of the elements in Russia. The periodic table is a chart that arranges the elements in order, with elements that act alike grouped together. Mendeleev used his table to predict elements that nobody had found yet. Every element that Americans discovered later went into Mendeleev's table.

Electric lights and the telephone spread through American cities in these years. Both ran on copper wire. The copper came from Michigan, and later from Butte, Montana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold from the Black Hills" -->
The Black Hills, in what is now South Dakota, belonged to the Sioux. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 was an agreement between the United States and the Sioux. It states that the Black Hills were set apart for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux.

In 1874, the Army officer George A. Custer, a lieutenant colonel, led an expedition into the Black Hills. An expedition is a long journey made by a group of people for a purpose. Miners went along with Custer's soldiers. The men of the expedition confirmed that there was gold. In August 1874 they sent the news out, and miners poured in. In late 1875, President Ulysses S. Grant ordered the Army to stop keeping the miners out of the Black Hills, the land the 1868 treaty had set apart for the Sioux. By 1876, about 11,000 prospectors were at Custer City.

In August 1876 the members of Congress voted to stop paying for food for the Sioux unless the Sioux gave up the Black Hills and their hunting grounds outside the reservation. The members of Congress asked the President to choose a commission, a group of officials given a special job. George Manypenny led the commission to the Sioux that September. He and the other commissioners brought an agreement written in advance. They told the Sioux that the United States no longer had to feed them, and they showed the agreement only to chiefs and leading men. Only about 10 percent of adult Sioux men signed it. The 1868 treaty required three-fourths of them to sign. The members of Congress made it law anyway in 1877.

On June 30, 1980, in a case called *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the 1877 law was a taking. In law, a taking happens when a government takes property and must pay its owner. The justices awarded the Sioux $17.1 million plus interest from 1877. Interest here meant extra money for every year the Sioux had waited to be paid. The total came to about $106 million in all. The Sioux refused the money. Taking it would have meant giving up their claim to the land. By 2022, the money they refused had grown to about $2 billion, according to South Dakota Public Broadcasting.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An aluminum tip for the Washington Monument" -->
In 1884, workers finished the Washington Monument with a pyramid of aluminum on its top. Aluminum was rare then. In 1884 it cost about $1 an ounce, the same price as silver. That comes to about $16 a pound. Other price records for the early 1880s give a lower figure, about $12 a pound.

An engineer is a person who plans and builds large works such as bridges and buildings. Colonel Thomas Lincoln Casey of the Army Corps of Engineers, the Army's group of engineers, was the engineer in charge of finishing the monument. He talked over the metal for the tip with William Frishmuth, who owned a foundry in Philadelphia. A foundry is a factory where workers melt metal and pour it into molds. Frishmuth chose aluminum, and Casey agreed. Aluminum matched the color of the monument's granite and would not stain. It also polished well and could be engraved.

Frishmuth cast a pyramid that weighed 100 ounces. It was the largest piece of aluminum anyone had yet cast. He first asked $75 for it and then sent a bill for $256.10. An assistant of Casey's named Davis bargained him down to $225.

Before the tip went up, Frishmuth showed it at the Tiffany's store in New York without permission. The tip sat on the floor, so visitors could step over the top of the Washington Monument. Workers set it in place on December 6, 1884.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" name="Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall

> **Who:** A 22-year-old Ohio college graduate who invented the cheap way to make aluminum, and his older sister, a chemist whose notes helped win the fight over his patent.
> **When and where:** February 23, 1886, in the family woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio.

On February 23, 1886, Charles Martin Hall made beads of aluminum metal in his family's woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio. Hall was 22 and had just finished at Oberlin College. With batteries he had made himself, he melted a substance called cryolite and dissolved aluminum oxide in it. Aluminum oxide is a compound of aluminum and oxygen. When Hall ran electricity through the melted mix, aluminum metal came out.

His older sister, Julia Brainerd Hall, had studied chemistry at Oberlin College. She helped with the experiments, watched them and wrote them down. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the sole right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. When Hall later had to fight for his patent, Julia's notes helped him win.

In France, Paul Héroult found the same process in the same year, working on his own. Both men were born in 1863, and both died in 1914. The method is called the Hall-Héroult process. Aluminum makers around the world still use it.

Aluminum cost about $12 a pound in the early 1880s. In 1888 the investors who backed Hall formed the Pittsburgh Reduction Company to make aluminum his way. After aluminum makers began to use Hall's process, the price fell to about $2 a pound. By 1893 it was about 78 cents a pound. The company's owners renamed it the Aluminum Company of America, or Alcoa, in 1907. Aluminum became cheap enough for kitchen foil and pots and pans.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Helium, almost found in America" -->
Around 1890, William Hillebrand, a chemist at the United States Geological Survey, got a gas out of uranium ore. The gas did not join with other substances. Hillebrand recorded it as nitrogen and missed the chance to find helium.

In 1895, William Ramsay in London followed up on Hillebrand's paper. Ramsay got a gas out of cleveite, a uranium mineral, and examined it with a spectroscope, an instrument that splits light into its colors. He saw a yellow line that belongs to helium. Scientists had seen helium's line in the light of the sun since 1868. Nobody had found helium on Earth before. The credit for finding helium on Earth goes to Ramsay.
