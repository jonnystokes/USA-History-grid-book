## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans rushed to dig gold in the South and copper in the North. The Georgia gold lay under Cherokee land, and the Michigan copper lay under Ojibwe land. Georgia lawmakers gave the Cherokee land away by lottery. In 1842 a United States official signed a treaty under which Ojibwe bands gave up the copper country.

A mineral rush happens when thousands of people hurry to a place where someone has found a valuable metal. Nobody in the United States discovered a new element in these years. In Europe, chemists such as Humphry Davy and Jöns Jacob Berzelius found one element after another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold on Cherokee land" -->
After Conrad Reed's find, more people dug for gold on the Reed farm and along the creeks near it in North Carolina. That mining grew from 1803. North Carolina produced more gold than any other state until the California gold rush of 1849.

The *Georgia Journal*, a newspaper, printed the first report of gold in Georgia on August 1, 1829. The gold was in the Cherokee Nation, the homeland of the Cherokee people. By the spring of 1830, about 4,000 miners had crowded onto Cherokee land to dig. The gold town of Dahlonega takes its name from the Cherokee word for "golden."

Georgia's lawmakers met as the Georgia General Assembly. On December 24, 1831, they passed an act that divided "the original Cherokee Indian territory" into lots and gave it away by lottery. In a lottery, names are drawn by chance. The Gold Lottery handed out lots of 40 acres in the gold country between October 22, 1832, and May 1, 1833.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Copper on Ojibwe land" -->
Douglass Houghton was the state geologist of Michigan. In 1840 he published a report on the copper of Michigan's Upper Peninsula. The Keweenaw Peninsula there has veins of pure native copper. Native miners had worked those same veins for thousands of years.

The land belonged to Ojibwe bands. A treaty is a written agreement between two nations. On October 4, 1842, a United States commissioner named Robert Stuart signed the Treaty of La Pointe with Ojibwe bands. A commissioner is an official sent to make an agreement. Under the treaty, the Ojibwe bands ceded, or gave up, land along the south shore of Lake Superior. That land included the Keweenaw copper country.

The treaty states that the Ojibwe would receive $12,500 a year in coins and $10,500 a year in goods for 25 years. It also states that $75,000 would go toward debts the Ojibwe owed to traders. The Ojibwe kept the right to hunt on the land "until required to remove by the President of the United States."

The treaty never uses the word copper, but it became known as the Copper Treaty. Pages written by the Bad River Band of Ojibwe and by the Lac Courte Oreilles Ojibwa College state the reason: United States officials wanted the copper.

In 1843, federal officials opened a mineral land office at Copper Harbor. Thousands of prospectors poured into the Upper Peninsula from that year on. A prospector is a person who searches the ground for metal. It was the nation's first great copper rush. The Keweenaw stayed the country's biggest source of copper into the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ontonagon Boulder" -->
The Ontonagon Boulder is a single lump of pure copper that weighs 3,708 pounds. It lay in the Ontonagon River country near Lake Superior. The Ojibwe had known the boulder for a long time, and they held it sacred. The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community, an Ojibwe nation, states that people made offerings at it.

In 1843, Julius Eldred, a merchant from Detroit, hauled the boulder away. Officials of the War Department then seized it from him. The War Department was the part of the United States government that ran the army. In 1860 the boulder went to the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, D.C.

In 1991, the Keweenaw Bay Indian Community formally asked the Smithsonian to return the boulder. Smithsonian officials refused. The request is still open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead at Galena" -->
Miners also dug lead in these years, in Missouri and in the Galena district of Illinois and Wisconsin. Galena is a lead ore. The town of Galena, Illinois, is named for that ore.
