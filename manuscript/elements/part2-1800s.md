## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 5 minutes**

Thousands of miners rushed onto Native land to dig gold in Georgia from 1829 and copper in Michigan from 1843. The Georgia gold lay under Cherokee land, and the Michigan copper lay under Ojibwe land. Georgia lawmakers gave the Cherokee gold country away by lottery, a drawing of names by chance. In 1842 a United States official signed a treaty, a written agreement between nations, under which Ojibwe bands, groups within the Ojibwe nation, gave up the copper country.

Nobody in the United States discovered a new element in these years. In Europe, chemists such as Humphry Davy and Jöns Jacob Berzelius found one element after another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold on Cherokee land" -->
North Carolina came first. After Conrad Reed's find in 1799, more people dug for gold on the Reed farm and along the creeks near it, and that mining grew from 1803. Miners in North Carolina dug more gold than miners in any other state until the California gold rush of 1849.

The first printed report of gold in Georgia appeared in the *Georgia Journal*, a newspaper, on August 1, 1829. The gold was in the Cherokee Nation, the homeland of the Cherokee people. By the spring of 1830, about 4,000 miners had crowded onto Cherokee land to dig. The gold town of Dahlonega takes its name from the Cherokee word for "golden."

**The Gold Lottery.** Members of the Georgia General Assembly, the state's lawmakers, passed a law on December 24, 1831. Under it they divided "the original Cherokee Indian territory" into lots and gave it away by lottery. In the Gold Lottery, winners received lots of 40 acres in the gold country for a fee of $10 a lot. The drawings ran from October 22, 1832, to May 1, 1833.

The Cherokee who lived on the land could not enter, because the lawmakers barred anyone who lived in the Cherokee territory. The lots went to white Georgians. Men who were United States citizens, widows and the families of orphans could enter if they had lived in Georgia for three years and had not mined gold in the Cherokee territory since June 1, 1830.

**Forced out.** President Martin Van Buren sent General Winfield Scott in 1838 to force the remaining Cherokee off their land and west of the Mississippi River. Scott commanded about 7,000 soldiers and members of state militias, part-time armies run by the states. His troops forced the Cherokee into stockades, pens fenced with tall wooden posts.

A missionary wrote in June 1838 that the Cherokee "have been dragged from their houses." In Georgia, he wrote, "multitudes were allowed no time to take any thing with them except the clothes they had on."

Scott's soldiers forced more than 16,000 Cherokee to leave for the west in 1838 and 1839, on the journey called the Trail of Tears. Over 4,000 died of cold, hunger and disease along the way, by the usual count. Russell Thornton, a scientist who studies the size of populations, puts the Cherokee losses at about 8,000 or more. Chapter 2, Native Nations, tells the Trail of Tears in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Copper on Ojibwe land" -->
Douglass Houghton was Michigan's state geologist, a scientist the state paid to study its rocks. In 1840 he published a report on the copper of the state's Upper Peninsula. The Keweenaw Peninsula there has veins, long thin bands running through the rock, of pure native copper. Native miners had worked those same veins for thousands of years.

The land belonged to Ojibwe bands. Robert Stuart, an official the United States sent to make an agreement, signed the Treaty of La Pointe with Ojibwe bands on October 4, 1842. Under the treaty, the bands gave up land along the south shore of Lake Superior, including the Keweenaw copper country.

The treaty states that the Ojibwe would receive $12,500 a year in coins and $10,500 a year in goods for 25 years. It also states that $75,000 would go toward debts the Ojibwe owed to traders. The Ojibwe kept the right to hunt on the land "until required to remove by the President of the United States." That meant they could hunt there only until the President ordered them to leave.

The treaty never uses the word copper, but people came to call it the Copper Treaty. The Bad River Band of Ojibwe and the Lac Courte Oreilles Ojibwa College both say that United States officials wanted the copper.

**The copper rush.** United States officials opened an office at Copper Harbor in 1843 to handle land with metal in it. From that year on, thousands of prospectors, people who search the ground for metal, poured into the Upper Peninsula in the nation's first great copper rush. The Keweenaw stayed the country's biggest source of copper into the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ontonagon Boulder" -->
Julius Eldred, a merchant from Detroit, made a deal in the summer of 1841 for the sacred Ontonagon Boulder, the 3,708-pound lump of pure copper. He made it with Okandikan, a leader of the Ontonagon band of Ojibwe. Eldred agreed to pay $45 right away and $105 more if he managed to move the boulder. He hauled it away in 1843.

Walter Cunningham was the United States government's agent in charge of the lands with metal in them. Before Eldred could leave Copper Harbor, Cunningham made him hand the boulder over to the government in Washington. Later, members of Congress paid Eldred money to make up for his loss.

The boulder sat in a yard of the War Department, the part of the government that ran the army, until 1860. Then it was moved to the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, D.C., and put on display.

The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community, made up of the L'Anse and Ontonagon bands of Lake Superior Ojibwe, formally asked the Smithsonian to return the boulder in 1991. Smithsonian officials refused in 2000. They said the boulder did not fit the meaning of a sacred object under the law on returning Native objects, and it stayed at the Smithsonian.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead at Galena" -->
Miners also dug lead in these years, in Missouri and in the Galena district of Illinois and Wisconsin. The town of Galena, Illinois, is named for galena, a lead ore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 7 minutes**

Mercury from the gold mines got into California's rivers and fish, and mercury in the hat shops of Danbury, Connecticut, poisoned the hatters who breathed it. California's gold miners released about 13 million pounds of it at their mines and into rivers. Miners also took gold from the Black Hills, land that belonged to the Sioux, and in 1877 members of Congress voted to take the hills from them.

Aluminum, rare at the start of these years, was cheap by the end of them. In 1886 Charles Martin Hall, a 22-year-old in Oberlin, Ohio, found a cheap way to make it. By the middle of the 1890s its price had fallen from about $12 a pound to less than $1. Scientists abroad already knew aluminum as an element. What Hall found was a process, a series of steps for making the metal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercury in the rivers and the hat shops" -->
Mercury is an element, a metal that stays liquid at room temperature, also called quicksilver. Gold miners used it to catch the fine bits of gold in the gravel they dug, and they lost mercury into the rivers.

**How much mercury.** California's gold miners used about 26 million pounds of mercury, and about half of it, some 13 million pounds, escaped into the ground and the rivers. Scientists at the United States Geological Survey, the government office for studying the country's rocks and water, made the count.

Placer miners, who wash gold out of sand and gravel in streams, lost about 10 million pounds of mercury, 80 to 90 percent of it in the Sierra Nevada mountains. Miners at hardrock mines, which are dug into solid rock, lost about 3 million pounds more.

**Still in the fish.** The Geological Survey's scientists list three ways this mercury reaches people: in fish they eat, in mud they handle and in the air they breathe. In the air it is a vapor, a gas rising off a liquid or a solid. The form that builds up most easily in living bodies, fish included, is called methylmercury. It is also the form most poisonous to people.

California officials had warned people about eating fish from about 20 lakes, rivers and other waters by 2005, because of mercury. Fish in the Bear and Yuba river areas carry enough mercury to put people's health at risk, the scientists found. They give no count of people poisoned by the gold miners' mercury.

**The Danbury shakes.** People in Danbury, Connecticut, had made hats since 1780. To make felt, a thick cloth of pressed fur, they soaked fur in a liquid that contained mercury nitrate, a compound of mercury, and the fur matted together into felt. This step was called carroting. As they worked, the hatters breathed in the mercury as vapor and as dust.

Doctors first described the hatters' mercury poisoning in medical writing in 1860, and people called it the Danbury shakes. The poisoned hatters had tremors, shaking they could not stop. Their skin itched, burned or peeled. Some lost their hair and teeth. They sweated, swelled up, felt dizzy and got confused. The mercury slurred their speech and made them stumble, and people mistook the shaking hatters for drunks. In bad cases, hatters had hallucinations: they saw or heard things that were not there.

The members of Connecticut's State Board of Health watched the mercury's effects in Danbury's hat factories in the 1880s and 1890s. They did not act, because the carroting was not harming people outside the factories. The historian Shirley Wajda, who wrote the history of the Danbury shakes for Connecticut Humanities, names no factory owner and gives no count of hatters poisoned before 1900.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Silver, copper and a table from Russia" -->
Miners found the Comstock Lode, a long, rich band of ore inside the rock, at Virginia City, Nevada, in 1859. It was the richest find of silver ore ever made in North America. Its ore held gold as well as silver, and miners rushed there too.

Dmitri Mendeleev, in Russia, published his periodic table of the elements in 1869: a chart that arranges the elements in order, with elements that act alike grouped together. Mendeleev used his table to predict elements that nobody had found yet. Every element that Americans discovered later went into it.

Americans strung copper wire through their cities in these years for electric lights and the telephone. The copper came from Michigan, and later from Butte, Montana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold from the Black Hills" -->
The Black Hills, in what is now South Dakota, belonged to the Sioux under the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868. Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer led an Army expedition into the hills in 1874, with miners along, and the men confirmed that there was gold. Miners poured in, and in late 1875 President Ulysses S. Grant ordered the Army to stop keeping them out.

Members of Congress then voted to cut off the Sioux's food until they gave up the hills. In 1877 they took the hills by law, though only about one in ten adult Sioux men had signed the paper giving them up. Chapter 12, Landmarks, tells that story in full. It also tells about the money the justices of the Supreme Court awarded the Sioux in 1980, about $106 million, which the Sioux have refused.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An aluminum tip for the Washington Monument" -->
Chapter 12, Landmarks, tells how workers finished the Washington Monument in 1884. Its tip was a pyramid of aluminum, a metal that was rare then. Aluminum cost about $1 an ounce, the same as silver, though some price records from those years give a little less.

Colonel Thomas Lincoln Casey of the Army Corps of Engineers was the engineer in charge of finishing the monument. He talked over the metal for the tip with William Frishmuth, the owner of a Philadelphia foundry, a factory where workers melt metal and pour it into molds. Frishmuth chose aluminum, and Casey agreed. Aluminum matched the color of the monument's granite and would not stain. It also polished well and could be engraved.

Frishmuth cast a pyramid that weighed 100 ounces, the largest piece of aluminum anyone had yet cast. After some bargaining over his bill, he was paid $225.

Before the tip went up, Frishmuth showed it at the Tiffany's store in New York without permission. The tip sat on the floor, so visitors could step over the top of the Washington Monument. Workers set it in place on December 6, 1884.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall

> **Who:** A 22-year-old Ohio college graduate who invented the cheap way to make aluminum, and his older sister, a chemist whose notes helped win the fight over his patent.
> **When and where:** February 23, 1886, in the family woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio.

**Charles Hall made aluminum in a woodshed, with batteries he built himself.**

Hall was 22 and had just finished at Oberlin College. In the family woodshed he melted a substance called cryolite and dissolved aluminum oxide, a compound of aluminum and oxygen, in it. On February 23, 1886, he ran electricity from batteries he had built himself through the melted mix, and beads of aluminum metal came out.

His older sister, Julia Brainerd Hall, had studied chemistry at Oberlin College. She helped with the experiments, watched them and wrote them down. Later Hall had to fight for his patent, the government paper that gave him alone the right to make and sell his invention for a set number of years. Julia's notes helped him win.

In France, Paul Héroult found the same process in the same year, working on his own. Both men were born in 1863, and both died in 1914. The method is called the Hall-Héroult process, and aluminum makers around the world still use it.

**Cheap aluminum.** Aluminum cost about $12 a pound in the early 1880s. In 1888 the investors who backed Hall formed the Pittsburgh Reduction Company to make aluminum his way. After aluminum makers began to use his process, the price fell to about $2 a pound, and by 1893 to about 78 cents. The company's owners renamed it the Aluminum Company of America, or Alcoa, in 1907. Aluminum became cheap enough for kitchen foil and pots and pans.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Helium, almost found in America" -->
William Hillebrand, a chemist at the United States Geological Survey, got a gas out of uranium ore around 1890. The gas did not join with other substances. Hillebrand recorded it as nitrogen and missed the chance to find helium.

William Ramsay in London followed up on Hillebrand's paper in 1895. He got a gas out of cleveite, a uranium mineral, and examined it with a spectroscope, an instrument that splits light into its colors. He saw a yellow line that belongs to helium. Scientists had seen helium's line in the light of the sun since 1868, but nobody had found helium on Earth before. The credit for finding it on Earth goes to Ramsay.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
