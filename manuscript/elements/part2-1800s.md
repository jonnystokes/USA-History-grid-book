## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans rushed to dig gold in the South and copper in the North. The Georgia gold lay under Cherokee land, and the Michigan copper lay under Ojibwe land. Georgia lawmakers gave the Cherokee land away by lottery. In 1842 a United States official signed a treaty under which Ojibwe bands gave up the copper country.

A mineral rush happens when thousands of people hurry to a place where someone has found a valuable metal. Nobody in the United States discovered a new element in these years. In Europe, chemists such as Humphry Davy and Jöns Jacob Berzelius found one element after another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold on Cherokee land" -->
After Conrad Reed's find, more people dug for gold on the Reed farm and along the creeks near it in North Carolina. That mining grew from 1803. North Carolina produced more gold than any other state until the California gold rush of 1849.

The first printed report of gold in Georgia appeared in the *Georgia Journal*, a newspaper, on August 1, 1829. The gold was in the Cherokee Nation, the homeland of the Cherokee people. By the spring of 1830, about 4,000 miners had crowded onto Cherokee land to dig. The gold town of Dahlonega takes its name from the Cherokee word for "golden."

Georgia's lawmakers met as the Georgia General Assembly. On December 24, 1831, they passed an act that divided "the original Cherokee Indian territory" into lots and gave it away by lottery. In a lottery, names are drawn by chance. In the Gold Lottery, winners received lots of 40 acres in the gold country. The drawings ran from October 22, 1832, to May 1, 1833.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Copper on Ojibwe land" -->
Douglass Houghton was the state geologist of Michigan. In 1840 he published a report on the copper of Michigan's Upper Peninsula. The Keweenaw Peninsula there has veins of pure native copper. Native miners had worked those same veins for thousands of years.

The land belonged to Ojibwe bands. A treaty is a written agreement between two nations. On October 4, 1842, a United States commissioner named Robert Stuart signed the Treaty of La Pointe with Ojibwe bands. A commissioner is an official sent to make an agreement. Under the treaty, the Ojibwe bands ceded, or gave up, land along the south shore of Lake Superior. That land included the Keweenaw copper country.

The treaty states that the Ojibwe would receive $12,500 a year in coins and $10,500 a year in goods for 25 years. It also states that $75,000 would go toward debts the Ojibwe owed to traders. The Ojibwe kept the right to hunt on the land "until required to remove by the President of the United States."

The treaty never uses the word copper, but it became known as the Copper Treaty. Pages written by the Bad River Band of Ojibwe and by the Lac Courte Oreilles Ojibwa College state the reason: United States officials wanted the copper.

In 1843, federal officials opened a mineral land office at Copper Harbor. Thousands of prospectors poured into the Upper Peninsula from that year on. A prospector is a person who searches the ground for metal. That rush was the nation's first great copper rush. The Keweenaw stayed the country's biggest source of copper into the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ontonagon Boulder" -->
The Ontonagon Boulder is a single lump of pure copper that weighs 3,708 pounds. It lay in the Ontonagon River country near Lake Superior. The Ojibwe had known the boulder for a long time, and they held it sacred. The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community, an Ojibwe nation, states that people made offerings at it.

In 1843, Julius Eldred, a merchant from Detroit, hauled the boulder away. Officials of the War Department then seized it from him. The War Department was the part of the United States government that ran the army. In 1860 the boulder went to the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, D.C.

In 1991, the Keweenaw Bay Indian Community formally asked the Smithsonian to return the boulder. Smithsonian officials refused. The request is still open.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lead at Galena" -->
Miners also dug lead in these years, in Missouri and in the Galena district of Illinois and Wisconsin. Galena is a lead ore. The town of Galena, Illinois, is named for that ore.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1886 Charles Martin Hall, a 22-year-old in Oberlin, Ohio, found a cheap way to make aluminum. By the middle of the 1890s, the price of aluminum had fallen from about $12 a pound to less than $1. Scientists abroad already knew aluminum as an element. What Hall found was a process, a series of steps for making the metal.

Gold miners in California used mercury and released about 13 million pounds of it. Hatmakers in Danbury, Connecticut, breathed mercury at work and were poisoned by it. Miners took gold from the Black Hills, land that belonged to the Sioux. In 1877 the members of Congress took the land itself.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercury in the rivers and the hat shops" -->
Mercury is an element. It is a metal that stays liquid at room temperature, and people also call it quicksilver. Gold miners used mercury to catch the fine bits of gold in the gravel they dug. They lost mercury into the rivers.

The United States Geological Survey is the government office that studies the country's rocks and water. Its scientists found that California mines produced more than 220 million pounds of mercury between 1850 and 1981. The mercury came from mines in the Coast Range. About 26 million pounds of it went into California's gold mining. Placer miners wash gold out of sand and gravel in streams. They released about 10 million pounds of mercury, 80 to 90 percent of it in the Sierra Nevada mountains. Miners at hardrock mines, which are dug into solid rock, released about 3 million pounds more.

In 2005, about 20 lakes, rivers and other waters in California had official warnings about eating their fish, because of mercury.

People in Danbury, Connecticut, had made hats since 1780. To make felt, a thick cloth of pressed fur, they soaked fur in a liquid that contained mercury nitrate, a compound of mercury. The fur then matted together into felt. The hatters called this step carroting. As they worked, they breathed in the mercury as vapor and as dust. A vapor is a gas that rises off a liquid or a solid.

Doctors first recorded the hatters' mercury poisoning in medical writing in 1860. People called it the Danbury shakes. The poisoned hatters had tremors, which are shaking movements a person cannot stop. The mercury slurred their speech and made them stumble. In bad cases, hatters had hallucinations, which means they saw or heard things that were not there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Silver, copper and a table from Russia" -->
In 1859, miners found the Comstock Lode at Virginia City, Nevada. A lode is a rich vein of ore. The Comstock was the richest deposit of silver ever found in North America. Its ore held gold as well as silver, and it set off its own rush.

In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic system of the elements in Russia. The periodic table is a chart that arranges the elements in order, with elements that act alike grouped together. Mendeleev used his table to predict elements that nobody had found yet. Every element that Americans discovered later went into Mendeleev's table.

Electric lights and the telephone spread through American cities in these years. Both ran on copper wire. The copper came from Michigan, and later from Butte, Montana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gold from the Black Hills" -->
The Black Hills, in what is now South Dakota, belonged to the Sioux. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 was an agreement between the United States and the Sioux. It states that the Black Hills were set apart for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux.

In 1874, Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer led an Army expedition into the Black Hills, and miners went with it. The men of the expedition confirmed that there was gold. In August 1874 they sent the news out, and miners poured in. In late 1875, President Ulysses S. Grant ordered the Army to stop keeping the miners out. By 1876, about 11,000 prospectors were at Custer City.

In 1876, only about 10 percent of adult Sioux men signed an agreement that gave up the Black Hills. The 1868 treaty required three-fourths of them to sign. The members of Congress made it law anyway in 1877.

On June 30, 1980, in a case called *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the 1877 law was a taking. In law, a taking happens when a government takes property and must pay its owner. The justices awarded the Sioux $17.1 million plus interest from 1877, about $106 million in all. The Sioux refused the money. Taking it would have meant giving up their claim to the land. By 2022, the money they refused had grown to about $2 billion, according to South Dakota Public Broadcasting.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An aluminum tip for the Washington Monument" -->
In 1884, workers finished the Washington Monument with a small pyramid of aluminum on its top. Aluminum was rare then. It cost about $1 an ounce, the same price as silver.

Colonel Thomas Lincoln Casey of the Army Corps of Engineers was the engineer in charge of finishing the monument. He talked over the metal for the tip with William Frishmuth, who owned a foundry in Philadelphia. A foundry is a factory where workers melt metal and pour it into molds. Frishmuth chose aluminum, and Casey agreed. Aluminum matched the color of the monument's granite and would not stain. It also polished well and could be engraved.

Frishmuth cast a pyramid that weighed 100 ounces. It was the largest piece of aluminum anyone had yet cast. He first asked $75 for it and then sent a bill for $256.10. An assistant of Casey's named Davis bargained him down to $225.

Before the tip went up, Frishmuth showed it at the Tiffany's store in New York without permission. The tip sat on the floor, so visitors could step over the top of the Washington Monument. Workers set it in place on December 6, 1884.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" name="Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Martin Hall and Julia Brainerd Hall

> **Who:** A 22-year-old Ohio college graduate who invented the cheap way to make aluminum, and his older sister, a chemist whose notes helped win the fight over his patent.
> **When and where:** February 23, 1886, in the family woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio.

On February 23, 1886, Charles Martin Hall made beads of aluminum metal in his family's woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio. He was 22 and had just finished at Oberlin College. He used batteries he had made himself. He melted a substance called cryolite and dissolved aluminum oxide in it. Aluminum oxide is a compound of aluminum and oxygen. When Hall ran electricity through the melted mix, aluminum metal came out.

His older sister, Julia Brainerd Hall, had studied chemistry at Oberlin College. She helped with the experiments, watched them and wrote them down. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the sole right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. When Hall later had to fight for his patent, Julia's notes helped him win.

In France, Paul Héroult found the same process in the same year, working on his own. Both men were born in 1863, and both died in 1914. The method is called the Hall-Héroult process. Factories around the world still make aluminum this way.

Aluminum cost about $12 a pound in the early 1880s. After factories began to use Hall's process, the price fell to about $2. By 1893 it was about 78 cents a pound. In 1888 the men who put money into Hall's work formed the Pittsburgh Reduction Company. They renamed it the Aluminum Company of America, or Alcoa, in 1907. Aluminum became cheap enough for kitchen foil and pots and pans.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-martin-hall-julia-brainerd-hall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Helium, almost found in America" -->
Around 1890, William Hillebrand, a chemist at the United States Geological Survey, got a gas out of uranium ore. The gas did not join with other substances. Hillebrand recorded it as nitrogen.

In 1895, William Ramsay in London followed up on Hillebrand's paper. He examined the gas with a spectroscope, an instrument that splits light into its colors. He saw a yellow line that belongs to helium. Scientists had seen helium's line in the light of the sun since 1868. Nobody had found helium on Earth before. The credit for finding helium on Earth went to Ramsay.
