## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, factory owners began to run their workers' days by bells and clocks. In the mills of Lowell, Massachusetts, young women worked about 12 hours a day. Children tended machines, and Irish immigrants dug canals and railroads. Workers who joined together to strike were first tried in court as criminals. By 1842 a Massachusetts judge had ruled that a union could be lawful. Through these years workers fought for a working day of ten hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Unions on trial, 1806" -->
In November 1805 the union shoemakers of Philadelphia, the cordwainers, held a turn-out. In 1806 eight union shoemakers were tried in the Mayor's Court of Philadelphia. The court found them guilty "of a conspiracy to raise their wages." A conspiracy is a secret plan by two or more people to do something against the law. Each man was fined $8 and the costs of the trial. Under this ruling, workers who formed a union to raise their pay could be punished as criminals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children, diggers and charcoal burners" -->
Much of the work in these years was done by children. No law stopped a child from working in a mill. Children tended the machines and did the doffing. To doff was to take the full bobbins, the spools that held the spun thread, off a spinning machine and put empty ones on.

Gangs of laborers dug the new canals and graded the new railroad lines, which means they leveled the ground for the tracks. Many of these men were immigrants from Ireland.

At Hopewell Furnace, an ironworks in Pennsylvania, the furnace burned charcoal to make iron. Charcoal is wood that has been burned slowly, with little air, until only a black fuel is left. Of the 213 workers on Hopewell's payroll from 1835 to 1837, 112 were woodcutters. They were paid by the cord, a stack of cut wood of a set size. About 20 colliers, the men who made the charcoal, worked there in the 1830s. Each tended 8 or 9 smoldering mounds of wood, day and night, and was paid by the bushel of charcoal. The colliers lived in huts beside the mounds.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Duffy's Cut, 1832" -->
In late June 1832, 57 Irish immigrants arrived on the ship *John Stamp*. They came from Donegal, Tyrone and Derry in Ireland, and their average age was 22. A contractor named Philip Duffy hired them to dig a cut for the Philadelphia and Columbia Railroad. A cut is a channel dug through high ground so the tracks can run level. They also built up a bank of earth for the tracks. The site was at mile 59 of the line, in Chester County, Pennsylvania.

By early August the disease cholera had reached their camp. All 57 men died. Local people stayed away from the camp.

From 2009 to 2012, a team of researchers dug up some of the men's remains. Some of the skulls had been struck by blows at about the time of death. One man had a bullet in his skull, fired at close range. None of the men had wounds from trying to defend themselves. Someone beat several of the men and shot one. The records do not say who, and no record shows that anyone was charged. The historian who led the dig, William E. Watson, points to fear of cholera and perhaps to nativism, which is hatred of immigrants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lowell and the mill girls" -->
The mill city of Lowell, Massachusetts, was built at Pawtucket Falls on the Merrimack River. The falls were a fishing and gathering place of the Pennacook people. In the 1600s two Pennacook towns, Pawtucket and Wamesit, stood where Lowell stands now. Their leaders were the sachems Passaconaway and his son Wannalancet. A sachem is a chief. During King Philip's War, in 1675 and 1676, Wannalancet and his people moved north to St. Francis in Canada. By the end of the war they no longer lived in the lower Merrimack valley.

The Lowell mills belonged to a group of Boston merchants called the Boston Associates. Among them were Francis Cabot Lowell, Nathan Appleton and Patrick Tracy Jackson. Their mills spun cotton that enslaved people had grown in the South. The mills also made a cheap cloth that the owners sold to plantations to clothe enslaved people.

Most of the mill workers were young New England women, aged 15 to 25. They are known as the mill girls. Under the companies' rules they had to work at least a year, to live in a company boardinghouse and to go to church. On average a mill girl stayed about three years.

A report of the Massachusetts lawmakers in 1845 recorded the working day. From May through August, work began at 5 in the morning and ended at 7 at night. The workers had half an hour for breakfast and 45 minutes for the midday meal. From September through April, work began "as soon as they can see" and ran until 7:30 at night. Across the year, the mills ran an average of twelve hours and ten minutes a day. Bells rang the start and the end of work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Lowell turn-outs of 1834 and 1836" -->
In February 1834 the mill managers announced wage cuts of 12 to 25 percent. About 800 women turned out, which meant they walked off the job together. The strike failed.

In October 1836 the mill owners raised the price the women paid to live in the boardinghouses. That was the same as a cut in pay. About 1,500 women struck and formed the Factory Girls' Association. They were better organized than in 1834, and this time they won part of what they asked for.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" name="Harriet Hanson Robinson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harriet Hanson Robinson

> **Who:** A child worker in a Lowell mill who led the girls of her room out on strike at age 11, and later wrote a book about the mill girls.
> **When and where:** Lowell, Massachusetts, 1835 to 1848. Born February 8, 1825. Died 1911.

Harriet Hanson was 11 years old when she led the girls of her room out on strike in October 1836. She had started work in a Lowell mill at 10, as a doffer.

On the day of the turn-out, the girls in her room could not decide whether to leave. She later remembered saying, "I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether anyone else does or not." She walked out, and the others followed her.

She kept working in the mills until 1848. In 1898 she published a book about her years there, called *Loom and Spindle, or Life Among the Early Mill Girls*. In later life she worked for women's right to vote. She died in 1911.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ten-hour day" -->
Workers across the country wanted a working day of ten hours. In 1835 coal heavers on the docks of the Schuylkill River in Philadelphia stopped work. A coal heaver moved coal on and off boats. Other workers joined them. By June 10, workers in more than forty trades had struck, nearly 20,000 people in all. It was the first general strike in the United States. In a general strike, workers in many different trades stop work at the same time. Philadelphia's city officials agreed to the ten-hour day and to raises.

On March 31, 1840, President Martin Van Buren ordered a ten-hour day for laborers on federal public works. His order covered only workers paid by the federal government. It did not cover anyone who worked for a private employer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Commonwealth v. Hunt, 1842" -->
In 1842 the highest court in Massachusetts, the Supreme Judicial Court, decided a case called Commonwealth v. Hunt. Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw ruled that a trade union was not a criminal conspiracy just for being a union. Workers could lawfully form a union, and they could lawfully strike. The 1806 ruling in Philadelphia had treated a union as a crime. Shaw's ruling allowed unions in Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sarah Bagley and the ten-hour petition" -->
In the winter of 1844 and 1845, Lowell mill workers formed the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association. Its president was Sarah Bagley. The women sent petitions to the Massachusetts legislature, the state's lawmakers, asking for a ten-hour day. Their petitions led the lawmakers to investigate conditions in the mills.

In 1845 Bagley spoke to a committee of the Massachusetts House. She told them that she had "worked in the Lowell Mills eight years and a half." The committee's chairman was William Schouler. All the committee members agreed "that legislation is not necessary at the present time." They turned down the ten-hour petition. Bagley said afterward that the committee had "misrepresented" what she told them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1850 to 1900, working people in mines, mills, rail yards and sweatshops worked twelve-hour days, and children worked beside adults. Workers formed the first national unions to fight for higher pay and an eight-hour day. Police, private guards, state militia and federal soldiers broke many of their strikes, and they shot and killed strikers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chinese railroad strike, 1867" -->
In the 1860s thousands of Chinese workers built the Central Pacific Railroad through the Sierra Nevada mountains of California. White workers on the line earned $40 a month. The Chinese workers earned $35. On June 25, 1867, several thousand of them went on strike. Estimates of their number run from 2,000 to 5,000.

They asked for the same pay as white workers, shorter hours and an end to whipping. The company's managers cut off the food supply to their camps. After eight days the strike ended with no promises from the company, though pay later rose a little. It was the largest strike by workers in the country up to that time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Avondale, 1869" -->
On September 6, 1869, a fire broke out at the Avondale coal mine in Plymouth Township, near Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania. Above the mine stood the breaker, a tall wooden building where coal was broken up and sorted. The breaker stood over the mine's only shaft, the one way in and out. Sparks from the furnace that pushed air through the mine set the breaker on fire.

The men and boys below had no other way out. The fire killed 110 people. Among the dead were five boys and two men who tried to rescue the workers. In 1870 Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed a mine safety law that required more than one exit from every mine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Knights of Labor" -->
On December 9, 1869, a tailor named Uriah Smith Stephens founded the Knights of Labor in Philadelphia. At first it was a secret society. Terence V. Powderly later led it and opened it up to the public. In 1879 the Knights had fewer than 10,000 members. By 1886 they had about 700,000.

The Knights let women join, and in some places Black workers too. Their leaders wanted more than higher wages. They wanted wider changes in how working people lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Servants, children and sweatshops" -->
The census of 1870 was the first to count women's work carefully. It found 867,354 domestic servants, people paid to cook, clean and care for other families' homes. They made up about half of all women who worked for pay. Domestic service stayed the largest job for women in every census from 1870 to 1940.

The same census found that about 1 in 8 children had jobs. By 1900 it was more than 1 in 5. From 1890 to 1910, at least 18 percent of all children aged 10 to 15 worked. In Philadelphia, the pay children brought home made up 28 to 46 percent of their family's income, depending on the family's ethnic group.

In the South, cotton mill owners hired children in growing numbers. In 1900, 25,000 of the nearly 100,000 textile workers in the South were children under 16. By 1904 there were 50,000 children in the mills, and 20,000 of them were under 12. They worked 12-hour days, six days a week. Mill owners said the fingers of adult farm workers were too "knotted and gnarled" for mill work.

In the hard-coal fields of Pennsylvania, breaker boys sat on benches in the breakers and picked slate, a kind of rock, out of the coal. In the cities, people sewed clothing in sweatshops, crowded workshops set up in lofts and in tenements. A tenement was a cheap apartment building packed with families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Railroad Strike, 1877" -->
In July 1877 John W. Garrett, the president of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, cut his workers' wages by 10 percent. It was not the first cut. Workers at Martinsburg, West Virginia, walked off the job. The strike spread through West Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, Missouri and Illinois.

Governors called up their state militias, the part-time soldiers of each state. President Rutherford B. Hayes issued orders for West Virginia on July 18, Maryland on July 21 and Pennsylvania on July 23, and he sent federal troops. In Pittsburgh on July 21, militiamen brought in from Philadelphia opened fire on a crowd. They killed more than twenty people, among them a woman and three small children. The official death count for Pittsburgh was 26, and the real number was likely higher. In Reading, Pennsylvania, at least ten people were killed. By September 1877, state and federal troops had broken the strike. The workers won nothing at the time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telephone operators" -->
The telephone brought a new job. Calls went through a switchboard, where an operator connected each caller to the person being called. The first operators were teenage boys. On September 1, 1878, a telephone company in Boston hired Emma Nutt as the first woman operator. Her sister Stella was hired the same day. Emma Nutt earned $10 a month for a 54-hour week. Telephone companies replaced the boys with women, and by the late 1880s nearly all operators were women.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The eight-hour day and Haymarket, 1886" -->
On May 1, 1886, workers across the country went on strike for an eight-hour day. In Chicago about 35,000 workers struck. On May 3, Chicago police fired on strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine Company plant and killed at least two of them.

On May 4, workers held a meeting in Chicago's Haymarket Square to protest the killings. Inspector John Bonfield marched on the meeting with 176 police officers and ordered the crowd to break up. Then someone threw a dynamite bomb at the police. No one has ever proved who threw it. The police opened fire. Sources differ on how many died. The Library of Congress counts seven police officers and at least four people in the crowd. The Chicago History Museum counts eight police officers dead and sixty wounded, and says an unknown number in the crowd were killed or wounded.

A Chicago jury convicted eight anarchists of conspiracy. Anarchists are people who believe there should be no government. The eight were Albert Parsons, August Spies, Louis Lingg, Samuel Fielden, Adolph Fischer, Michael Schwab, George Engel and Oscar Neebe. None was shown to have thrown the bomb. On November 11, 1887, Parsons, Spies, Engel and Fischer were hanged by order of the court. Lingg killed himself in his cell with a small bomb that someone smuggled in to him.

In June 1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld of Illinois pardoned the three men still in prison. He wrote that "the trial and the conduct of the judge had been shamefully unjust." He found that the judge was biased, the jury had been packed against the defendants, and evidence had been faked. After Haymarket, most members left the Knights of Labor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The American Federation of Labor" -->
On December 8, 1886, leaders of craft unions met in Columbus, Ohio, and founded the American Federation of Labor, the AFL. A craft union takes in the skilled workers of one trade, such as cigar makers or carpenters. The AFL's unions bargained with employers for higher wages and shorter hours. Its leaders called this "pure and simple" unionism.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-gompers" name="Samuel Gompers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Gompers

> **Who:** A cigar maker who became the first president of the American Federation of Labor.
> **When and where:** Born in London, January 27, 1850. Worked in New York. President of the AFL from 1886 to 1924, except for 1895.

Samuel Gompers led the American Federation of Labor for almost forty years. He was its first president, and he served from 1886 to 1924, except for one year, 1895.

He was born on January 27, 1850, in Spitalfields, in London. He made cigars from boyhood. In New York he rolled cigars in tenement shops.

Gompers built the AFL around wages, hours and working conditions. In 1886 its unions had about 140,000 members. By 1924 they had nearly 3 million.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-gompers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Brakemen and the coupler" -->
A brakeman on a railroad joined, or coupled, the cars of a train by hand. He stood between two cars as they moved together and guided an iron link into a socket. Brakemen's hands were crushed, and missing fingers were called "the badge of the experienced worker." Thousands of brakemen died.

A safer way already existed. Eli Janney patented an automatic coupler in 1873, and the Westinghouse air brake had existed since 1869. Railroad owners did not put them on most of their cars. In 1893 members of Congress voted through the Safety Appliance Act, which required both on trains by 1900. Coupling accidents fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902, while the number of railroad workers grew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892" -->
In 1892 steelworkers at Carnegie Steel's works in Homestead, Pennsylvania, were on strike, and they held the plant. On July 6, 300 agents of the Pinkerton detective agency, a private company, tried to land from barges and take the plant back. Henry Clay Frick had hired them for Carnegie Steel. Strikers and Pinkertons fought a gun battle. Seven workers and three Pinkerton agents were killed. Some accounts count more.

On July 12, 8,000 state militiamen, sent by the governor of Pennsylvania, arrived at Homestead. By November the strike was over and the union at Homestead was broken.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pullman, 1894" -->
The Pullman Palace Car Company made and repaired railroad cars near Chicago. Its workers lived in Pullman, Illinois, a company town. In a company town, one company owns the houses and collects the rent. In 1893 and 1894 the company's managers cut wages by about 25 to 35 percent. They did not cut the rents.

The workers belonged to the American Railway Union, founded in June 1893. It was the first industrial union, which means it took in every kind of railroad worker in one union, not one craft at a time. In June 1894 delegates at the union's convention voted for a national boycott. Railroad workers across the country refused to handle trains that carried Pullman cars.

Attorney General Richard Olney, the federal government's top lawyer, took the union to court. On July 2, 1894, federal judges William Woods and Peter Grosscup issued an injunction. An injunction is a court order that tells people to stop doing something. The judges ordered Debs and the union's other officers to stop anything that got in the way of trade between the states or of the mail. On July 3, over the protest of Governor Altgeld, President Grover Cleveland ordered federal troops into Chicago. More than 16,000 troops were called up across the country. The soldiers arrested boycotters and rioters and sometimes shot them.

About 30 people were killed across the country. Some counts reach 34. The judges' order and the soldiers ended the boycott. Days before, on June 28, 1894, President Cleveland had signed a law making Labor Day a federal holiday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jennie-curtis" name="Jennie Curtis" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jennie Curtis

> **Who:** A seamstress in the Pullman shops whose speech helped start the Pullman boycott.
> **When and where:** Pullman, Illinois, 1893 and 1894. Born 1871. Died 1917.

Jennie Curtis spoke to the American Railway Union's convention in June 1894 and asked the union for help. Her speech helped persuade the delegates to vote for the Pullman boycott.

She sewed carpets and drapery for railroad cars in Pullman's repair shops. Before the hard times of 1893 she earned $1.50 to $2.25 a day. Then the company cut her pay twice in one week in November 1893. Her pay fell to about 80 cents a day. Many of the girls she worked with averaged 40 to 50 cents.

Curtis was president of Local 269, the union's branch for young women workers, which had 125 members. That year she told the United States Strike Commission, a panel that looked into the strike, about wages, rents and abusive supervisors. She said her father had worked for Pullman for 13 years. When he died, he owed back rent, and the company collected it from her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jennie-curtis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" name="Eugene V. Debs" movie="American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eugene V. Debs

> **Who:** A locomotive fireman who founded the American Railway Union and went to jail for leading the Pullman boycott.
> **When and where:** Terre Haute, Indiana, and Chicago. Founded the union in June 1893.
> **Movie:** *American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs* (2017), a documentary directed by Yale Strom. It is about Debs's life.

Eugene V. Debs went to jail for six months for leading the Pullman boycott. As a young man in Terre Haute, Indiana, he was a locomotive fireman, the worker who shoveled coal into the engine's fire. He became an officer of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, a union for that one craft.

In 1893 he founded the American Railway Union, which took in all railroad workers. During the Pullman boycott he refused to obey the court's injunction. For that, in 1895, he served six months in the jail at Woodstock, Illinois.

Debs later ran for president five times as a Socialist. In 1918 he gave a speech against the war, and he was sentenced to ten years in prison for it. He ran for president for the last time in 1920 from his prison cell, as Convict 2253. He got between 900,000 and 1 million votes. President Warren Harding shortened his sentence, and Debs went free at Christmas 1921.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lattimer, 1897" -->
On September 10, 1897, nearly 400 immigrant coal miners marched from Harwood to Lattimer, Pennsylvania, to support a strike. They carried no weapons. Luzerne County Sheriff James Martin had deputized nearly 90 men, which means he had given them the power to act as officers of the law. The deputies fired on the marchers. They killed 19 men, who were Polish, Slovak and Lithuanian, and wounded 38. Many were shot in the back.

Martin and his deputies were tried for murder from February 1 to March 2, 1898. The jury found every one of them not guilty. By 1900 the United Mine Workers was the fastest-growing union in the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convict leasing" -->
In the South after the Civil War, state officials leased prisoners to mines, railroads and planters, who worked them without pay. Most of the prisoners were Black men, arrested under new sets of laws called codes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
