## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 9 minutes**

Factory owners in the first half of the 1800s ran their workers' days by bells. In the mills of Lowell, Massachusetts, young women worked about 12 hours a day. Children tended machines in the mills, and Irish immigrants dug canals and railroads. Through these years workers fought for a working day of ten hours.

Forming a union, a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employers, could be a crime. A Philadelphia jury found shoemakers guilty in 1806 for forming one to raise their pay. By 1842 a Massachusetts judge had ruled that a union could be lawful.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Unions on trial, 1806" -->
The union shoemakers of Philadelphia, the cordwainers, held a turn-out, their word for a strike, in November 1805. Four months later, in March 1806, lawyers led by Jared Ingersoll and Joseph Hopkinson put eight of them on trial. Judge Moses Levy heard the case in the Mayor's Court of Philadelphia, a city court.

**The verdict.** The jury found the eight men guilty of conspiracy, a secret plan by two or more people to break the law. In the jury's words, their crime was "a conspiracy to raise their wages." Each man had to pay a fine of $8 and the costs of the trial. After this verdict, prosecutors could take workers to court as criminals for forming a union to raise their pay.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children, diggers and charcoal burners" -->
Children worked in the mills, and that was legal. They tended the machines and did the doffing: they took the full bobbins, the spools that held the spun thread, off a spinning machine and put empty ones on.

Gangs of laborers dug the new canals and graded the new railroad lines, which means they leveled the ground for the tracks. Many of these men were immigrants from Ireland.

Making iron took many workers too, and much of the work was cutting wood and turning it into fuel. At Hopewell Furnace, a place in Pennsylvania where workers made iron, the furnace burned charcoal, wood burned slowly with little air until only a black fuel is left. Hopewell paid more than 200 workers from 1835 to 1837, and more than 100 of them were woodcutters.

Most woodcutters worked part of the year, cutting and splitting hardwood through the winter with only axes. A woodcutter cut about two cords a day and was paid by the cord, a stack of cut wood of a set size.

About 20 colliers, the men who made the charcoal, worked there in the 1830s. A collier tended slowly burning mounds of wood day and night, for up to two weeks at a time, and lived in a hut beside them. He was paid by the bushel of charcoal, about eight gallons.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who killed the men at Duffy's Cut?" -->
The records do not say. All 57 Irish workers at Duffy's Cut, in Pennsylvania, died in 1832, some of them of cholera, and their remains show that someone killed others.

The 57 men arrived on the ship *John Stamp* in late June 1832. They came from Donegal, Tyrone and Derry in Ireland, and their average age was 22. A contractor named Philip Duffy hired them to dig a cut for the Philadelphia and Columbia Railroad, a channel through high ground so the tracks could run level. They also built up a bank of earth for the tracks. The site was at mile 59 of the line, in Chester County.

**The cholera.** By early August cholera had reached their camp. Cholera is an infection of the gut, spread by water or food that carries traces of a sick person's waste. In bad cases it causes watery diarrhea and vomiting, and the body loses water so fast that a person can go into shock and die. Local people stayed away from the camp.

**The skulls.** A team of researchers dug up some of the men's remains from 2009 to 2012. Some of the skulls had been struck by blows at about the time of death, and one man had a bullet in his skull, fired at close range. None of the remains showed wounds from trying to defend themselves. Someone beat several of the men and shot one.

No record shows that anyone was charged. To explain the killings, the historian who led the dig, William E. Watson, points to fear of cholera and perhaps to nativism, hatred of immigrants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lowell and the mill girls" -->
The owners of the mills built the mill city of Lowell, Massachusetts, at Pawtucket Falls on the Merrimack River. Most of the mill workers were young New England women, aged 15 to 25, known as the mill girls. Under the companies' rules they had to work at least a year, live in a company boardinghouse and go to church. On average a mill girl stayed about three years.

The Lowell mills belonged to Francis Cabot Lowell and other Boston merchants, called the Boston Associates. Their mills spun cotton that enslaved people had grown in the South. The mills also made a cheap cloth that the owners sold to plantations to clothe enslaved people.

**The working day.** A report of the Massachusetts lawmakers in 1845 recorded the hours. From May through August, work began at 5 in the morning and ended at 7 at night, with half an hour for breakfast and 45 minutes for the midday meal. From September through April, work began "as soon as they can see" and ran until 7:30 at night. Across the year the mills ran an average of twelve hours and ten minutes a day, and bells rang the start and the end of work.

**Whose land it was.** The mill owners built on land where the Pennacook people had lived until a war in the 1670s. The falls were a Pennacook fishing and gathering place, and in the 1600s two Pennacook towns stood where Lowell stands now. Their leaders were the sachems, or chiefs, Passaconaway and his son Wannalancet.

**King Philip's War.** Native nations led by Metacom, a Wampanoag leader the English called King Philip, fought the English colonies of New England in 1675 and 1676. About 9,000 people died in the war. Wannalancet kept peace with the English.

**Cocheco, 1676.** Wannalancet led about 400 people from the nations allied with him to Cocheco, in New Hampshire, to make peace with an English captain named Richard Waldron. Waldron broke the peace and took them all prisoner. He later let Wannalancet and about half of the group go.

Waldron sent the others to Boston, where the governor and council of Massachusetts decided what to do with them. Officials in Boston hanged seven or eight of them. About 200 were enslaved for life and shipped to Barbados, an island in the Caribbean Sea, and the records do not name the men who sold them.

Wannalancet and his people moved north to St. Francis in Canada. By the end of the war they no longer lived in the lower Merrimack valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Lowell turn-outs of 1834 and 1836" -->
The mill managers announced wage cuts of 12 to 25 percent in February 1834. About 800 women turned out, and the strike failed.

**The board increase.** The women who ran the boardinghouses told the mill managers in October 1836 that they could not make ends meet. The managers then took more money out of each woman's weekly pay for room and board, which was the same as a cut in pay. About 1,500 women struck and formed the Factory Girls' Association.

They were better organized than in 1834, and this time they won part of what they asked for. Managers at the Boott and Merrimack mills took back the whole increase. At the Hamilton mill, managers took it back only for workers paid by the day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" name="Harriet Hanson Robinson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harriet Hanson Robinson

> **Who:** A child worker in a Lowell mill who led the girls of her room out on strike at age 11, and later wrote a book about the mill girls.
> **When and where:** Lowell, Massachusetts, 1835 to 1848. Born February 8, 1825. Died 1911.

**She was 11 when she led her room out on strike.**

Harriet Hanson had started work in a Lowell mill at 10, as a doffer. On the day of the turn-out in October 1836, the girls in her room could not decide whether to leave. She later remembered saying, "I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether anyone else does or not." She walked out, and the others followed her.

She stayed at the mills until 1848. In 1898 she published a book about her years there, *Loom and Spindle, or Life Among the Early Mill Girls*, and in later life she worked for women's right to vote.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harriet-hanson-robinson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ten-hour day" -->
Workers in Philadelphia, in Lowell and on federal building jobs wanted a working day of ten hours. Coal heavers, the men who moved coal on and off boats on the docks of the Schuylkill River in Philadelphia, stopped work in 1835, and other workers joined them.

**The first general strike.** By June 10, workers in more than forty trades, or kinds of skilled work, had struck, nearly 20,000 people in all. It was the first general strike in the United States, with workers in many different trades stopping work at the same time. Philadelphia's city officials agreed to the ten-hour day and to raises.

President Martin Van Buren ordered a ten-hour day on March 31, 1840, for laborers on building projects of the United States government. His order covered only workers paid by the federal government, not anyone who worked for a private employer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Commonwealth v. Hunt, 1842" -->
The judges of the highest court in Massachusetts, the Supreme Judicial Court, heard a case called Commonwealth v. Hunt in 1842. Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw ruled that in Massachusetts a trade union was not a criminal conspiracy just for being a union. Workers there could lawfully form a union, and they could lawfully strike. Thirty-six years earlier, a Philadelphia jury had found union shoemakers guilty of a crime for doing the same thing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sarah Bagley and the ten-hour petition" -->
Lowell mill workers formed the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association in the winter of 1844 and 1845, with Sarah Bagley as its president. The women sent petitions, written requests signed by many people, to the Massachusetts lawmakers, asking for a ten-hour day. After the petitions came in, the lawmakers looked into conditions in the mills.

**The hearing.** Bagley spoke to a committee of the Massachusetts House in 1845 and told them that she had "worked in the Lowell Mills eight years and a half." The chairman, William Schouler, and all the other members agreed "that legislation is not necessary at the present time," meaning that no new law was needed. They turned down the ten-hour petition. Bagley said afterward that the committee had "misrepresented" what she told them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 15 minutes**

Police, private guards, state militia (the part-time soldiers of each state) and federal soldiers broke many strikes between 1850 and 1900, and they shot and killed strikers. The workers had formed the first national unions, to fight for higher pay and an eight-hour day.

The work itself was dangerous, in mines, mills, rail yards and sweatshops, crowded workshops where people sewed clothing for long hours and low pay. Children worked beside adults. In the cotton mills of the South, they worked 12-hour days, six days a week.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Chinese railroad strike, 1867" -->
Thousands of Chinese workers built the Central Pacific Railroad through the Sierra Nevada mountains of California in the 1860s. White workers on the line earned $40 a month, and the Chinese workers earned $35. Several thousand of them went on strike on June 25, 1867, and estimates of their number run from 2,000 to 5,000.

They asked for the same pay as white workers, shorter hours and an end to whipping. The records do not name the men who whipped them. The company's managers cut off the food supply to their camps. After eight days the strike ended with no promises from the company, though pay later rose a little. It was the largest strike by workers in the country up to that time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why 110 people died at Avondale, 1869" -->
A fire killed 110 people at the Avondale coal mine, in Plymouth Township near Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, on September 6, 1869. Among the dead were five boys and two men who tried to rescue the workers.

**One way out.** Above the mine stood the breaker, a tall wooden building where coal was broken up and sorted. It stood over the mine's only shaft, the one way in and out. Sparks from the furnace that pushed air through the mine set the breaker on fire, and the men and boys below had no other way out. Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed a mine safety law in 1870 that required more than one exit from every mine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Knights of Labor" -->
A tailor named Uriah Smith Stephens founded the Knights of Labor in Philadelphia on December 9, 1869, as a secret society. Terence V. Powderly later led it and opened it up to the public. The Knights had fewer than 10,000 members in 1879 and about 700,000 by 1886.

The Knights let women join, and in some places Black workers too. Besides higher wages, they called for an eight-hour day, equal pay for equal work, and an end to child labor and to the work of prisoners leased to companies. They also wanted the public, not private owners, to own utilities, the companies that supplied things like gas and water.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Servants, children and sweatshops" -->
Census takers count everyone in the country for the United States government every ten years, and in 1870 they counted women's work carefully for the first time. They found 867,354 domestic servants, people paid to cook, clean and care for other families' homes, about half of all women who worked for pay. Domestic service stayed the largest job for women in every census from 1870 to 1940.

**Children at work.** At least 18 percent of all children aged 10 to 15 had jobs from 1890 to 1910, nearly 1 in 5. In Philadelphia, the pay children brought home made up 28 to 46 percent of their family's income, depending on the family's ethnic group.

In the South, cotton mill owners hired children in growing numbers. Of the nearly 100,000 textile workers in the South in 1900, 25,000 were children under 16. By 1904 there were 50,000 children in the mills, and 20,000 of them were under 12. They worked 12-hour days, six days a week. Mill owners said the fingers of adult farm workers were too "knotted and gnarled" for mill work.

In the hard-coal fields of Pennsylvania, breaker boys sat on benches in the breakers and picked slate, a kind of rock, out of the coal. In the cities, people sewed clothing in sweatshops set up in lofts, the open upper floors of buildings, and in tenements, cheap apartment buildings packed with families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Railroad Strike, 1877" -->
John W. Garrett, the president of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, cut his workers' wages by 10 percent in July 1877, and it was not the first cut. Workers at Martinsburg, West Virginia, walked off the job. The strike spread through West Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, Missouri and Illinois.

Governors called up their state militias, and President Rutherford B. Hayes sent federal troops. He issued the orders for West Virginia on July 18, Maryland on July 21 and Pennsylvania on July 23.

**Pittsburgh.** Militiamen brought in from Philadelphia opened fire on a crowd in Pittsburgh on July 21. They killed more than twenty people, among them a woman and three small children. The official death count for Pittsburgh was 26, and the real number was likely higher.

**Reading.** Two days later, in Reading, Pennsylvania, a crowd threw bricks and stones down on militiamen marching through a deep cut for the railroad. Some of the soldiers fired into the crowd and killed at least ten people. By September 1877, state and federal troops had broken the strike, and the workers won nothing at the time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The telephone operators" -->
A new job came with the telephone. Calls went through a switchboard, where an operator connected each caller to the person being called, and the first operators were teenage boys. Emma Nutt became the first woman operator on September 1, 1878, at a telephone company in Boston, and her sister Stella started the same day. Emma Nutt earned $10 a month for a 54-hour week. The companies' managers replaced the boys with women, and by the late 1880s nearly all operators were women.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The eight-hour day and Haymarket, 1886" -->
Workers across the country went on strike for an eight-hour day on May 1, 1886, about 35,000 of them in Chicago. Two days later, Chicago police fired on strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine Company plant and killed at least two of them.

**The bomb.** Workers held a meeting in Chicago's Haymarket Square on May 4 to protest the killings. Inspector John Bonfield marched on the meeting with 176 police officers and ordered the crowd to break up. Then someone threw a dynamite bomb at the police, and the police drew their guns and fired wildly. No one has ever proved who threw the bomb.

**The dead.** Counts differ. A Library of Congress history page lists seven police officers and at least four people in the crowd killed. A Chicago History Museum page lists eight police officers dead and sixty wounded, and an unknown number in the crowd killed or wounded.

**The trial.** A Chicago jury convicted eight anarchists, people who believe there should be no government, of conspiracy. None was shown to have thrown the bomb. Judge Joseph E. Gary sentenced seven of them to death and the eighth, Oscar Neebe, to fifteen years in prison.

Officials at the Cook County Jail hanged four of the men on November 11, 1887: Albert Parsons, August Spies, George Engel and Adolph Fischer. A fifth, Louis Lingg, killed himself in his cell with a small bomb that someone smuggled in to him. The last two, Samuel Fielden and Michael Schwab, had asked for mercy, and the governor of Illinois at the time changed their sentences to life in prison.

**The pardon.** A later governor of Illinois, John Peter Altgeld, pardoned Fielden, Schwab and Neebe, the three men still in prison, in June 1893. The pardon freed them from the rest of their punishment. Altgeld found that the trial had been unfair, and he wrote that Judge Gary ran it with "malicious ferocity," meaning cruel, spiteful anger.

**Altgeld's reasons.** A court officer named Ryce had the job of calling in the men who would sit on the jury. The state's attorney, the lawyer who brought the case, had suggested Ryce for that job. Altgeld wrote that Ryce called in jurors who were already against the men on trial.

Altgeld also wrote that "much of the evidence given at the trial was a pure fabrication," which means it was made up. Some leading police officials, he wrote, had threatened men with torture and had offered money and jobs to anyone who would swear to what the police wanted.

**The Knights.** After Haymarket, most members left the Knights of Labor. By 1890 the Knights had about 100,000 members, down from about 700,000 in 1886.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The American Federation of Labor" -->
Leaders of craft unions, each made up of the skilled workers of one trade such as cigar makers or carpenters, met in Columbus, Ohio, on December 8, 1886. There they founded the American Federation of Labor, the AFL. Its union leaders bargained with employers for higher wages and shorter hours, a way of running unions called "pure and simple" unionism.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-gompers" name="Samuel Gompers" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Gompers

> **Who:** A cigar maker who became the first president of the American Federation of Labor.
> **When and where:** Born in London, January 27, 1850. Worked in New York. President of the AFL from 1886 to 1924, except for 1895.

**A cigar roller from London led the AFL for almost forty years.**

Samuel Gompers made cigars from boyhood, and in New York he rolled them in tenement shops. He became the first president of the American Federation of Labor and served from 1886 to 1924, except for one year, 1895.

Gompers built the AFL around wages and hours. Its unions had about 140,000 members in 1886 and nearly 3 million by 1924, about twenty times as many.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-gompers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why brakemen lost their fingers" -->
A brakeman on a railroad joined, or coupled, the cars of a train by hand. He stood between two cars as they moved together and guided an iron link into a socket. Brakemen's hands were crushed, and missing fingers were called "the badge of the experienced worker." Thousands of brakemen died.

**A safer way.** Eli Janney got a patent for an automatic coupler in 1873, the right to be the only one to make and sell it for a set time. The Westinghouse air brake, a brake worked by air pressure, had existed since 1869. Railroad owners did not put either one on most of their cars.

Members of Congress voted through the Safety Appliance Act in 1893, and it required both on trains by 1900. Coupling accidents fell from about 11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902, while the number of railroad workers grew.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892" -->
**The lockout.** The steelworkers' union contract at Carnegie Steel's works in Homestead, Pennsylvania, was running out in the summer of 1892. Henry Clay Frick, the chairman of Carnegie Steel, cut pay for hundreds of the workers and refused to bargain with the union. On June 29 he shut the plant and locked out all 3,800 workers, keeping them out of their jobs. The workers went on strike, and armed strikers surrounded the plant and closed off the town.

On July 6, 300 agents of the Pinkerton detective agency, a private company that Frick had hired, tried to land from barges and take the plant back. Strikers and Pinkertons fought a gun battle, and seven workers and three Pinkerton agents were killed. Some accounts count more.

Eight thousand state militiamen, sent by the governor of Pennsylvania, arrived at Homestead on July 12. By November the strike had failed, and the steelworkers at Homestead no longer had a union.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pullman, 1894" -->
The Pullman Palace Car Company's workers made and repaired railroad cars near Chicago. They lived in Pullman, Illinois, a company town, where the company owned the houses and its managers collected the rent. The company's managers cut wages by about 25 to 35 percent in 1893 and 1894, and they did not cut the rents.

The workers belonged to the American Railway Union, founded in June 1893. It was the first industrial union, taking in every kind of railroad worker in one union instead of one craft at a time.

**The boycott.** The union's members held a convention, a big meeting of delegates sent by its branches, in June 1894, and the delegates voted for a national boycott of Pullman cars. In the boycott, railroad workers across the country refused to handle trains that carried Pullman cars. While it spread, on June 28, 1894, President Grover Cleveland signed a law making Labor Day a federal holiday.

**The court order.** Attorney General Richard Olney, the federal government's top lawyer, took the union to court. Federal judges William Woods and Peter Grosscup issued an injunction, a court order to stop doing something, on July 2, 1894. They ordered Eugene V. Debs, the union's founder, and its other officers to stop anything that got in the way of the mail or of trade between the states.

**The troops.** The next day, over the protest of Governor Altgeld, President Cleveland ordered federal troops into Chicago. More than 16,000 troops were called up across the country, and the soldiers arrested boycotters and rioters and sometimes shot them.

About 30 people were killed across the country, and some counts reach 34. Accounts of the strike do not say who killed each of them. Federal judges and soldiers ended the boycott.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jennie-curtis" name="Jennie Curtis" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jennie Curtis

> **Who:** A seamstress in the Pullman shops whose speech helped start the Pullman boycott.
> **When and where:** Pullman, Illinois, 1893 and 1894. Born 1871. Died 1917.

**Her pay fell from as much as $2.25 a day to 80 cents.**

Jennie Curtis spoke to the American Railway Union's convention in June 1894, and her speech helped persuade the delegates to vote for the Pullman boycott.

She had worked for five years as a seamstress in the sewing room of Pullman's repair shops. She told how the women there made "all the carpets, and all the silk, satin, plush, and velvet drapings for the dining cars," and they sewed the cloth covers for the seats.

**Two cuts in one week.** Before the depression of 1893, a time when many businesses failed and many people lost their jobs, she earned $1.50 to $2.25 a day. Pullman's managers cut her pay twice in one week in November 1893, and it fell to about 80 cents a day. Many of the girls she worked with averaged 40 to 50 cents.

Curtis was president of Local 269, the union's branch for young women workers, which had 125 members. Later in 1894 she told the United States Strike Commission, a panel that looked into the strike, about wages, rents and abusive supervisors. She said her father had worked for Pullman for 13 years. When he died, he owed back rent, and Pullman's managers collected it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jennie-curtis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" name="Eugene V. Debs" movie="American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eugene V. Debs

> **Who:** A locomotive fireman who founded the American Railway Union and went to jail for leading the Pullman boycott.
> **When and where:** Terre Haute, Indiana, and Chicago. Founded the union in June 1893.
> **Movie:** *American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs* (2017), a documentary directed by Yale Strom. It is about Debs's life.

**He ran for president from a prison cell, as Convict 2253.**

As a young man in Terre Haute, Indiana, Eugene V. Debs was a locomotive fireman, the worker who shoveled coal into the engine's fire. He became an officer of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, a union for that one craft.

He founded the American Railway Union in 1893, for all railroad workers. After the Pullman boycott, judges found him guilty of contempt of court, disobeying a court's order, and he served six months in the jail at Woodstock, Illinois, in 1895.

**Convict 2253.** Debs later ran for president five times as a Socialist, one of those who wanted the public, not private owners, to own the big industries. He gave a speech against the war in 1918, and he was sentenced to ten years in prison for it.

He ran for the last time in 1920 from his prison cell, as Convict 2253, and got between 900,000 and 1 million votes. President Warren Harding shortened his sentence, and Debs went free at Christmas 1921.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eugene-debs-work-workers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lattimer, 1897" -->
Nearly 400 immigrant coal miners marched from Harwood to Lattimer, Pennsylvania, on September 10, 1897, to support a strike, and they carried no weapons. Luzerne County Sheriff James Martin had deputized nearly 90 men, which means he had given them the power to act as officers of the law. The deputies fired on the marchers. They killed 19 men, who were Polish, Slovak and Lithuanian, and wounded 38. Many were shot in the back.

Martin and his deputies were tried for murder from February 1 to March 2, 1898, and the jury found every one of them not guilty. By 1900 the United Mine Workers, the coal miners' union, was the fastest-growing union in the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convict leasing" -->
State officials in the South leased, or rented out, prisoners to mines, railroads and planters after the Civil War, and the people who leased them forced them to work. This was called convict leasing. Most of the prisoners were Black men, and in Alabama more than 90 percent of the state prisoners leased out were Black.

**The Black Codes.** Sheriffs arrested many of them for small offenses under the Black Codes, laws that Southern lawmakers passed from November 1865 to make Black people prove they had work. In Alabama, contractors whipped prisoners who tried to escape or did not work hard enough. Every year, 4 or 5 of every 100 leased state prisoners in Alabama died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Workers built the first national unions in these fifty years, and police, deputies, militiamen and federal soldiers broke many of their strikes by force, firing on strikers and crowds from Pittsburgh to Lattimer.
- 1869: A fire at the Avondale mine kills 110 people, because the mine had only one way out.
- 1886: Workers strike for an eight-hour day, and after the Haymarket bomb officials hang four men who were never shown to have thrown it.
- 1894: Federal judges and soldiers end the Pullman boycott, and about 30 people are killed.
**Next:** After 1900 children still worked in the mines and mills, and a fire in a New York garment factory killed 146 workers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
