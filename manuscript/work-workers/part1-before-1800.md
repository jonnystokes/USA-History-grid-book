## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 14 of 37 · Work and Workers · 10 eras · about 85 minutes**

**For most of American history, employers and masters decided how long people worked, how much they earned and how safe the job was, and workers won shorter days and higher pay mainly by acting together.**

In October 1836 the girls in one room of a Lowell mill, in Massachusetts, could not decide whether to turn out, their word for walking off the job together. The managers had just taken more money out of their pay for room and board. Harriet Hanson was 11 and had worked in the mill since she was 10. She later remembered telling them: "I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether anyone else does or not." She walked out, and they followed her.

This chapter follows the people who did the work, era by era, from the farmers and hunters of the Eastern Woodlands before 1500 to the warehouse and delivery workers of today. Watch what happens when workers act together, the way Harriet's room did. Some won higher pay or a shorter day. Others were beaten, jailed or shot by police, hired guards and soldiers. Watch the deaths at work too. Some of the laws that made jobs safer came only after workers died, in a burning mine or behind a locked factory door.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Which workers were not free to leave their work?
- What happened when workers went on strike?
- What made work less deadly?

**Five minutes?** Read "The shirtwaist makers and the Triangle fire" in 1900 to 1950.
**Can skip:** "The telephone operators", "The Mohawk ironworkers", "The pandemic, 2020". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 2 minutes**

The people of North America worked for their own households and towns before 1500, and they planned the work around the seasons. They farmed, hunted, fished and built canoes. Nobody worked for wages, pay from an employer, and no one hired another person, set their hours or fired them. Work for wages began here only after people from Europe arrived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who did which work?" -->
In the Eastern Woodlands, the forests of eastern North America, women and men did different work. Women farmed corn, beans and squash and gathered wild foods, and men hunted and fished. On the biggest jobs they worked together in larger groups: at planting time and at harvest, for building canoes, and for maple sugaring, boiling down the sap of maple trees to make sugar.

Where farmers grew enough food for whole towns, some people could spend their time on one kind of work. They became traders, stoneworkers or religious leaders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

The first work that newcomers organized in this land was forced work. Spanish settlers in Florida and the Southwest made Native people work for them, with little pay or none. Spanish officials ran that forced work under two systems, the encomienda and the repartimiento.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The encomienda and the repartimiento" -->
Under the encomienda, a system set up by the king of Spain, Spanish settlers could force Native people to work for them and to hand over tribute, payments of goods or work that rulers force people to give. In return the settlers were supposed to give the Native people "protection and religious instruction." Settlers used the encomienda from Florida across the Southwest.

**The repartimiento.** Spanish officials in the colonies added a second system in the middle of the 1500s. Under it, the officials sent Native workers in groups, by turns, to work on Spanish projects, and they were supposed to pay them. In New Mexico, Spanish governors used the repartimiento to farm and to trade. They also forced enslaved people to do the work, though Spanish law forbade it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acoma, 1599: forced work as a sentence" -->
Juan de Oñate, the leader of the Spanish colony in New Mexico, sentenced the survivors of Acoma Pueblo to twenty years of forced labor in February 1599. That meant work they could not refuse and could not leave. Acoma Pueblo is the Acoma people's town, with their fields around it.

**What came before.** Spanish soldiers led by Oñate's nephew, Juan de Zaldívar, came to Acoma in December 1598 to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, the soldiers raped an Acoma woman: they forced her into sex. The Acoma killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men, and accounts differ on the number.

**The attack.** Oñate sent about seventy soldiers back to Acoma in January 1599, led by Vicente de Zaldívar. Over two or three days they killed Acoma men, women and children and burned much of the town. Nobody counted the dead, and estimates run from about 300 to about 1,500.

**The sentences.** Oñate gave the men over 25 two punishments: one foot cut off, and twenty years of forced labor in Spanish households, with no right to leave. Boys and men from 12 to 25 got the twenty years of forced labor only. So did girls and women over 12.

**The feet.** The sentences are in the written record, but whether anyone carried out the cutting of feet is disputed. In the Acoma people's own account, Oñate had the right feet of 24 captive men cut off. Two historians who studied the Spanish records for years, Marc Simmons and John Kessell, came to doubt it, and Kessell points out that no record describes an Acoma man with one foot.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

Three of every four newcomers around Chesapeake Bay between 1630 and 1680 were servants bound to a master for years, and they could not quit. Most got no wages. Over the whole colonial period, between one-half and two-thirds of the Europeans who came to the English colonies came this way.

Masters worked them in the tobacco fields, and a sickness in their first summer killed more than half of them. Virginia judges had runaways whipped and added years to their time. In the same century, English colonists began to hold Africans in slavery for life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bound for years: indentured servants" -->
An indentured servant was bound by a written contract, called an indenture, to work for a master for a set number of years. Most terms ran four to seven years, and the servant could not leave. Most servants got no wages. A skilled servant named Buckland was paid 20 English pounds a year on top of his food, drink, washing and a place to sleep, and his contract was unusual.

**How many.** Counts differ. Most estimates run from one-half to two-thirds of all European immigrants to the colonies, and some go as high as three-quarters. Around Chesapeake Bay, about 50,000 servants arrived between 1630 and 1680, three of every four newcomers. In the 1630s there were about six men for every woman among them.

Servants there worked tobacco "from dawn until dusk, six days a week through the growing season." Many fell sick in their first summer. People called this sickness the seasoning, and it killed more than half of the new arrivals. One writer of the time said immigrants died "like cats and dogs."

**Women servants.** Virginia's lawmakers punished a woman servant who had a baby without being married. She had an extra year added to her term, or she had to pay her master 1,000 pounds of tobacco.

At the end of the term, a master owed the servant "freedom dues," goods to start a free life with. In Virginia a man got ten bushels of corn (a bushel is about eight gallons), thirty shillings in English coins and a musket, a long gun. A woman got fifteen bushels of corn and forty shillings.

Masters also took children and teenagers as apprentices, who worked for them to learn a trade such as printing or shoemaking. At home, families did their own spinning, weaving, candle-making and brewing, the making of beer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Martin's Hundred, on the land of Tsenacomoco" -->
**Why this place.** Richard Frethorne, an indentured servant whose letters home survive, worked at Martin's Hundred. He arrived in December 1622, nine months after a Powhatan attack there.

Martin's Hundred was an English plantation on the James River in Virginia, and its main settlement was Wolstenholme Towne. The plantation stood inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan chiefdom, a union of many Native nations under one high chief.

Tsenacomoco ran north from the James River to the Potomac River, and from Chesapeake Bay west to the places where the rivers drop in falls from higher ground. About 15,000 people lived there.

**The attack of 1622.** The Powhatan leader Opechancanough led an attack on most of the English settlements on March 22, 1622. Colony officials recorded 347 colonists killed. They got no report from some settlements, so the real number was probably higher. The Powhatan fighters killed at least 58 colonists at Martin's Hundred. Its main settlement, Wolstenholme Towne, lost more people than any other settlement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne

> **Who:** An indentured servant in Virginia, probably a young teenager, whose letters home to his parents survive.
> **When and where:** Martin's Hundred, Virginia, from December 1622 until his death, recorded by February 1624.

**"When we are sick there is nothing to comfort us," he wrote home.**

Richard Frethorne reached Martin's Hundred in December 1622 on a ship called the *Abigail*, nine months after the Powhatan attack. He had come as an indentured servant, and he was probably a young teenager.

**His letters.** He wrote to his parents in England in March and April 1623. He begged them to buy out his indenture, or else to send food. Since he left the ship, he wrote, he had eaten nothing but peas and loblollie, a thin porridge boiled in water.

**The muster list.** A muster list made by February 1624, a count of the living and the dead at each settlement, records Frethorne, spelled "Frethram," among the dead at Martin's Hundred.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-frethorne" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Runaways, and a sentence for life" -->
Servants who ran away and were caught faced the whip and more years of work. The governor of Virginia and his council sat together as the colony's highest court, called the General Court.

**Three runaways.** These judges heard the case of three runaway servants of Hugh Gwyn, a planter, or owner of a plantation, on July 9, 1640. They ordered two of them, a Dutchman called Victor and a Scotsman named James Gregory, whipped thirty times each. Both men also had to serve extra years.

The third runaway was John Punch, a Black man. For the same escape, the same judges ordered him to serve his master "for the time of his natural Life," which meant for the rest of his life.

**Branding.** Thirteen days later, on July 22, the same judges ordered other runaways whipped thirty times. They also ordered three of them, Christopher Miller, Peter Wilcocke and a Black man named Emanuel, "to be Burnt in the cheek with the letter R." This punishment is called branding. To brand a person was to press a red-hot iron into the skin, and the burn left the letter on the cheek as a scar for life.

The court records name the letter R and the cheek, but they do not describe the iron used, and they do not name the men who did the whipping and the branding. The judges also ordered Miller and Emanuel to work in shackles, iron rings locked around the legs, for a year or more.

**The 1643 law.** Virginia's lawmakers wrote two punishments for runaways into law in March 1643. A runaway had to serve double the time he had been gone. A servant who ran away a second time was to be "branded in the cheek with the letter R." The law did not state who was to do the branding.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 2 minutes**

A boy who learned a trade between 1700 and 1750 climbed three ranks: apprentice, journeyman and master. A journeyman, the middle rank, was a skilled worker paid wages by the day. Sailors and dock workers were the other large group who worked for wages, and officers of the British navy could seize them and force them to serve at sea.

Around Chesapeake Bay and in the Lowcountry along the southern coast, colonists held more and more people in slavery. Slave traders brought about 8,600 enslaved Africans to Virginia between 1701 and 1710, about 6,200 between 1711 and 1720, and about 13,000 between 1721 and 1730. Chapter 24, Slavery and Freedom, tells the work and the lives of enslaved people in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ladder of a trade" -->
A trade such as printing, shoemaking, carpentry or blacksmithing had those three ranks. A child or teenager started as an apprentice, bound to a master to learn the work. When the apprentice's time was up, he became a journeyman and worked for wages. A journeyman who set up a shop of his own became a master.

In the towns, enslaved men also worked at skilled trades. Owners "hired out" some of these men to other employers, who paid wages for the work, and the owners kept the wages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-work-workers" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A runaway apprentice, bound at 12 to work in his brother James's print shop.
> **When and where:** Boston, New York and Philadelphia, 1718 to 1723.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a two-part documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS in April 2022. It is about Franklin's life.

**At 17, with about four years left to serve, Franklin ran away.**

Benjamin Franklin was 12 in 1718 when he was bound as an apprentice to his brother James, a printer in Boston. Under his indenture he had to work in James's shop until he was 21, and only in the last year would he get a journeyman's wages.

**The beatings.** Franklin later wrote that his brother beat him. He also admitted that he himself may have been "too saucy and provoking," which meant rude enough to make James angry. He ran away in September 1723, first to New York and then to Philadelphia. He reached Philadelphia on October 6, 1723, with almost no money.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-work-workers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sailors, press gangs and the Knowles Riot" -->
Sailors and dock workers earned wages, and officers of Britain's Royal Navy could impress them: seize them and force them to serve on a navy ship. The groups of men sent to grab them were called press gangs.

**The Knowles Riot.** Press gangs working for Commodore Charles Knowles, a navy officer who commanded a group of ships, seized 46 men in Boston in November 1747. Among them were boatmen, sailors and even carpenters. Beginning on November 17, a crowd made up mostly of working people rioted in Boston for three days. They took some of Knowles's officers as hostages and forced Knowles to free the seized men in exchange.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 2 minutes**

Hired workers in Philadelphia began to act together against their employers in the late 1700s. In 1786 journeymen printers there went on strike, stopping work together until their employers met their demands, and they won pay of at least $6 a week. In 1794 journeymen shoemakers in the same city formed a trade union, a group of workers in one trade who join together to bargain, or make a deal, with their employers over pay and hours. Their union lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The printers' strike of 1786" -->
The journeymen printers of Philadelphia struck in 1786 against a cut in their wages. A US Labor Department list of important events in labor history records the start of the strike as June 2, 1786. The printers won, and their employers agreed to pay at least $6 a week.

The printers also set up a fund of money to support one another during the strike, the first strike fund on record. The same Labor Department list calls theirs "the earliest authenticated strike of workers in the United States in a single trade" (authenticated means proven by records).

**The bakers of 1741.** The labor historian Selig Perlman, writing in 1922, noted that the usual list of American strikes began with New York's bakers, who stopped work in 1741. He found that those bakers were master bakers, the shop owners, protesting a city rule that set the price of bread. By his account, the 1786 printers' walkout was the earliest real strike of workers against their employers known in America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The shoemakers' union of 1794" -->
Shoemakers in those days were called cordwainers. Journeymen shoemakers in Philadelphia formed the Federal Society of Journeymen Cordwainers in May 1794, the first trade union in the United States that lasted. Its members called a "turn-out," their word for a strike, to hold a standard wage, the same pay for the same work across the trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
