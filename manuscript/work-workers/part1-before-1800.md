## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the people of North America worked hard, but nobody worked for wages. No one hired another person for pay, set their hours or fired them. People planned their work around the seasons and the needs of their own households. Paid jobs, done for an employer in return for wages, arrived later with newcomers from Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Work by season and by household" -->
In Eastern Woodlands communities, in the forests of eastern North America, men and women did different work. Women farmed corn, beans and squash, and they gathered wild foods. Men hunted and fished.

The biggest jobs brought everyone together. Men and women worked side by side at the harvest, at maple sugaring and at building canoes. At planting time and at harvest time, people gathered into larger groups to get the work done.

Where farming fed whole towns, some people could spend their time on one kind of work. They became traders, stoneworkers or religious leaders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1500s" order="02" chapter="work-workers" label="The 1500s" state="thin" progress="written" -->
## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first work that newcomers organized in this land was forced work. Spanish settlers in Florida and the Southwest made Native people work for them, with little pay or none. No other kind of employment by newcomers is on record here in the 1500s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The encomienda and the repartimiento" -->
The king of Spain and his officials set up a labor system called the encomienda. Under it, Spanish settlers could force Native people to work for them and to hand over tribute. Tribute is a payment of goods or labor that people are forced to give to those who rule them. In return the settlers were supposed to give the Native people "protection and religious instruction." Settlers used the encomienda from Florida across the Southwest.

From the middle of the 1500s, a second system called the repartimiento was added. Under it, Spanish colonial officials sent Native workers in groups, by turns, to work on Spanish projects. On paper the workers were paid. In New Mexico, Spanish governors used the repartimiento, and also slave labor that broke Spain's own laws, to farm and to trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acoma, 1599: forced work as a sentence" -->
Acoma Pueblo is the town of the Acoma people, with their fields around it. In December 1598 and January 1599, Spanish soldiers under Juan de Oñate attacked it. In February 1599 Oñate sentenced the Acoma survivors.

He ordered every man over 25 to have one foot cut off and to serve twenty years. Boys from 12 to 25, and girls and women over 12, were also sentenced to twenty years. To serve meant to work for Spanish households for those twenty years, with no right to leave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1600s" order="03" chapter="work-workers" label="The 1600s" state="full" progress="written" -->
## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most people who worked in the English colonies in the 1600s were not paid wages. They were bound to a master by a contract for a set number of years, and they could not quit. In Virginia, masters worked them in the tobacco fields, and more than half died in their first summer. Colonial judges had runaway servants whipped and added years to their time. In the same century, English colonists began to hold Africans in slavery for life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bound for years: indentured servants" -->
An indentured servant was a person bound by a written contract, called an indenture, to work for a master for a set number of years. Most terms ran four to seven years. The servant could not leave, and the master did not pay wages.

Historians disagree about how many European immigrants came this way. Most estimates run from one-half to two-thirds of all European immigrants to the colonies. Some scholars say it was as many as three-quarters. In the colonies around Chesapeake Bay, about 50,000 servants arrived between 1630 and 1680. They made up three of every four newcomers. In the 1630s there were about six men for every woman among them.

Servants there worked tobacco "from dawn until dusk, six days a week through the growing season." Many newcomers fell sick in their first summer. People called this sickness the seasoning, and it killed more than half of the new arrivals. One writer of the time said immigrants died "like cats and dogs." A woman servant who became pregnant had more time added to her term, or she owed her master 1,000 pounds of tobacco.

At the end of the term, a master owed the servant "freedom dues," goods to start a free life with. In Virginia a man got ten bushels of corn, thirty shillings and a musket. A shilling was an English coin, and a musket was a long gun. A woman got fifteen bushels of corn and forty shillings.

Children and teenagers could be bound in a different way, as apprentices. An apprentice worked for a master in order to learn a trade, such as printing or shoemaking. At home, families did their own spinning, weaving, brewing and candle-making.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Martin's Hundred, on the land of Tsenacomoco" -->
Martin's Hundred was an English plantation on the James River in Virginia. Its main settlement was Wolstenholme Towne. The plantation stood inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan chiefdom. The chiefdom was a union of many Native nations under one high chief. Tsenacomoco ran north from the James River to the Potomac River, and from Chesapeake Bay west to the falls of the rivers. About 15,000 people lived there.

On March 22, 1622, the Powhatan leader Opechancanough led an attack on most of the English settlements. The official count recorded 347 colonists killed. Some settlements sent in no report, so the real number was probably higher. At least 58 colonists died at Martin's Hundred, more than at any other settlement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne

> **Who:** An indentured servant in Virginia, probably a young teenager, whose letters home to his parents survive.
> **When and where:** Martin's Hundred, Virginia, from December 1622 until his death, recorded by February 1624.

Richard Frethorne wrote home from Virginia to beg his parents to buy him out of his indenture. He had arrived at Martin's Hundred in December 1622 on a ship called the *Abigail*, nine months after the attack. He was probably a young teenager.

In March and April 1623 he wrote to his parents in England. He asked them to pay off his indenture so he could come home, or else to send food. Since he left the ship, he wrote, he had eaten nothing but peas and loblollie. Loblollie was gruel, a thin porridge of grain boiled in water. He also wrote, "when we are sick there is nothing to comfort us."

By February 1624 a muster list had been made, a count of the living and the dead at each settlement. The list records Frethorne, spelled "Frethram," among the dead at Martin's Hundred.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-frethorne" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Runaways, and a sentence for life" -->
Servants who ran away and were caught faced the whip and more years of work. On July 9, 1640, the General Court of Virginia, the colony's highest court, judged three runaway servants of a planter named Hugh Gwyn. The judges ordered two of them, a Dutchman called Victor and a Scotsman named James Gregory, whipped with thirty lashes each. A lash is one stroke of the whip. Both men also had to serve extra years.

The third runaway was John Punch, a Black man. The judges ordered him to serve his master "for the time of his natural Life." For the same act, the same court gave the two white servants extra years and gave the Black servant his whole life.

On July 22, 1640, the same court ordered thirty lashes for other runaways. It ordered the letter R branded on three of them, Christopher Miller, Peter Wilcocke and a Black man named Emanuel. To brand a person was to press a red-hot iron into the skin. The burn left a scar in the shape of the iron, and it lasted for life. The court records name the letter and the place on the body, but they do not describe the iron used. Emanuel was also made to work in shackles, iron rings locked around the legs.

In March 1643 the members of the General Assembly, the colony's lawmakers, wrote these punishments into law. A runaway had to serve double the time he had been gone. A servant who ran away a second time was to be "branded in the cheek with the letter R."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, a boy who learned a trade moved up through three ranks: apprentice, journeyman and master. Sailors and dock workers made up the other large group of people who worked for wages. Officers of the British navy could seize those men and force them to serve at sea. In the colonies around Chesapeake Bay and in the Lowcountry along the southern coast, the number of enslaved workers grew fast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ladder of a trade" -->
A trade such as printing, shoemaking, carpentry or smithing had three ranks. A boy started as an apprentice, bound to a master to learn the work. When his time was up, he became a journeyman. A journeyman was a skilled worker who earned wages, paid by the day. If he saved enough money and had some luck, he could become a master with a shop of his own.

In the towns, enslaved men also worked at skilled trades. Owners "hired out" some of these men to other employers. The employers paid wages for the work, and the owners kept the wages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-work-workers" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A runaway apprentice. As a teenager he was bound to work in his older brother's print shop, and he ran away.
> **When and where:** Boston, New York and Philadelphia, 1718 to 1723.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a two-part documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS in April 2022. It is about Franklin's life.

Benjamin Franklin ran away from his apprenticeship at 17. In 1718, when he was 12, he was bound as an apprentice to his brother James, a printer in Boston. Under his indenture he had to work in James's shop until he was 21.

Franklin later wrote that his brother beat him. He also admitted that he himself may have been "too saucy and provoking." In September 1723, with about four years left on his indenture, he ran away. He went first to New York and then to Philadelphia. He reached Philadelphia on October 6, 1723, with almost no money.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-work-workers" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sailors, press gangs and the Knowles Riot" -->
Sailors and dock workers were paid wages, and they faced a danger that other workers did not. Officers of Britain's Royal Navy could impress them. To impress a man was to seize him and force him to serve on a navy ship. The groups of sailors sent to grab men were called press gangs.

In November 1747, press gangs working for Commodore Charles Knowles seized 46 men in Boston, among them boatmen, sailors and even carpenters. Beginning on November 17, a crowd made up mostly of working people rioted in Boston for three days. They took some of Knowles's officers as hostages. The crowd traded the officers back only after the seized men were set free. The fight is called the Knowles Riot.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="work-workers" label="1750 to 1800" state="thin" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the late 1700s, hired workers in Philadelphia began to act together against their employers. In 1786 journeymen printers there went on strike and won a minimum wage. In 1794 journeymen shoemakers in the same city formed a trade union that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The printers' strike of 1786" -->
In a strike, workers stop work together until the employer meets their demands. In 1786 the journeymen printers of Philadelphia struck against a cut in their wages. Labor historians date the start of the strike to June 2, 1786. The printers won. Their employers agreed to pay at least $6 a week.

The printers also set up a fund of money to support one another while they were out of work. It is the first strike fund on record. Their walkout is often called the first strike in the United States by hired workers against an employer. Historians call it the first strike that can be proved from the records. Journeymen tailors in New York had struck earlier, in 1768, and there were earlier colonial work stoppages too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The shoemakers' union of 1794" -->
Shoemakers in those days were called cordwainers. In May 1794, journeymen shoemakers in Philadelphia formed the Federal Society of Journeymen Cordwainers. A trade union is a group of workers in one trade who join together to bargain with employers over pay and hours. The Federal Society was the first trade union in the United States that lasted.

Its members wanted every employer to pay the same standard wage. When an employer paid less, the members called a "turn-out." A turn-out was their word for a strike.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
