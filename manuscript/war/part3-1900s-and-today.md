## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 24 minutes**

Most of the Americans who fought in the two world wars were drafted. World War II was the first American war in which more of them died in battle than of disease, accidents and infections.

**Who they fought.** Members of Congress declared war on Germany in April 1917. German submarines had sunk American ships, and German officials had tried to draw Mexico into an alliance against the United States. American soldiers then fought on the side of the Allies, the countries already at war with Germany, among them Britain. In World War II the United States fought Japan, Germany and Italy, after Japanese pilots attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941 and Germany and Italy declared war four days later.

**The two wars.** About 4.7 million Americans served in World War I, from 1917 to 1918, and 116,516 died, more of them of disease and accidents than in battle. More than 16 million served in World War II and 405,399 died, in counts that run from 1941 to 1946. These are the Defense Department's counts.

**The draft.** In World War I, 2.8 million men were drafted, which means they were ordered into the armed forces by law, and 2 million volunteered. In World War II about 10 million of the 16 million who served were drafted.

Military leaders kept Black service members in separate units until after President Harry Truman ordered equal treatment in 1948, and nearly every unit was mixed by about 1953. In 1945, American airmen dropped the first two atomic bombs used in war, on two cities in Japan. An atomic bomb gets its huge power from the tiny centers of atoms. One bomb could kill tens of thousands of people, and unlike other bombs it also gave off radiation, invisible energy that sickened and killed people for weeks afterward.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was the water cure?" -->
The water cure was a torture American soldiers used on Filipino prisoners during the war in the Philippines. Soldiers held a man down and poured water into his mouth and nose until his stomach swelled. Then they pressed or stood on his stomach to force the water out, and started again.

**Igbaras.** Soldiers did this to Tobeniano Ealdama, the mayor of the town of Igbaras, on the island of Panay, on November 27, 1900. Captain Edwin Glenn, an army lawyer, oversaw it. Ealdama said later, "My stomach and throat pained me, and also the nose where they passed the salt water through." That night Glenn ordered the town's 400 to 500 houses burned.

A sergeant who had been there, Charles Riley, told a Senate committee about it on April 14, 1902. Army officers tried Glenn and found him guilty. His punishment was a $50 fine and one month in which he was not allowed to do his job. He retired in 1919 as a brigadier general.

**Samar.** Filipino fighters killed 48 of the 74 men in an American company at Balangiga, on the island of Samar, in late 1901. Brigadier General Jacob Smith then ordered Marine Major Littleton Waller to turn the island into a "howling wilderness." Smith told him, "The more you kill and burn, the better it will please me." Smith said he thought Filipino boys of ten were old enough to fight.

Waller had eleven Filipino guides and carriers shot without a trial. At his court-martial, a trial run by military officers, the officers voted 11 to 2 to find him not guilty. At Smith's own court-martial in 1902, officers reprimanded him, which means they scolded him officially. President Theodore Roosevelt then ordered Smith to retire from the army at once. The historian Paul Kramer writes, "The scale of abuses in the Philippines remains unknowable."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The draft of 1917 and the men who refused" -->
Members of Congress passed the Selective Service Act, the law for the World War I draft, on May 18, 1917. Men had to register on three days in 1917 and 1918, and by the end about 24 million men had filled out draft cards, about 23 percent of everyone living in the country in 1918.

Some drafted men refused to fight because their religion forbade it, and they were called conscientious objectors.

**The Hutterite brothers.** Three brothers, David, Joseph and Michael Hofer, and Joseph's brother-in-law Jacob Wipf were drafted in 1918. They were Hutterites, members of a Christian community in South Dakota that does not fight in wars. They refused to put on uniforms or obey military orders. Officers at a court-martial at Camp Lewis sentenced them to 20 years in prison.

**Alcatraz.** Soldiers took the four men, chained in pairs, to the military prison on Alcatraz Island on July 25, 1918. Guards put them in cold, wet cells in the basement. At first they got half a glass of water a day and no food.

Guards chained them standing to the cell bars with their hands crossed and pulled up high, so only their toes touched the floor. This was called high cuffing, and it strained their arms and shoulders until they swelled. After five days their arms were too swollen to put on their jackets. Duane Stoltzfus, a historian at Goshen College, writes that guards were reported to have beaten them with knotted whips as well.

Guards moved the four in chains to Fort Leavenworth, in Kansas, on November 19, 1918, after the fighting in Europe had stopped. Joseph Hofer died there on November 29, and Michael Hofer on December 2. Officials had Joseph's body dressed in the army uniform he had refused to wear.

The prison doctor listed pneumonia, a sickness that fills the lungs with fluid, during the flu outbreak in the prison. The Hutterite church's record states that they died "as a result of cruel mistreatment by the United States military." On December 6, 1918, Secretary of War Newton Baker ordered an end to chaining prisoners to cell bars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Houston, 1917" -->
Army leaders sent 654 Black soldiers of the 3rd Battalion, 24th Infantry, to guard the building of Camp Logan outside Houston, Texas, in July 1917. People in Houston insulted them with racial slurs every day.

Houston police arrested a Black soldier on August 23, 1917, after he questioned the arrest of a Black woman. When Corporal Charles Baltimore asked about it, police beat him, shot at him and held him. A false report that he had been killed spread through the camp.

About 150 soldiers took rifles and marched into the city. About 20 people died. Townspeople, police officers and National Guardsmen were among them, and so were soldiers of the 24th whom other soldiers of the 24th shot by accident. The Department of Veterans Affairs history of that night does not say who shot each of the others.

**The trials.** Army officers charged 118 soldiers with disobeying orders, mutiny (refusing orders as a group), trying to kill and murder. The officers found only eight not guilty. At sunrise on December 11, 1917, they hanged the first 13 men sentenced to death. They did it in secret, within a day of the sentence, and did not tell the War Department.

After protests, War Department officials ordered that every death sentence be reviewed first. At later trials, officers sentenced 16 more men to death, and President Woodrow Wilson changed ten of those sentences to prison terms. In all, army officers hanged 19 soldiers. Most of the others got life in prison. Only Black soldiers were sentenced to death.

The Secretary of the Army canceled all the convictions in 2023 and changed the men's records to honorable discharges, which show that a soldier left the army in good standing. The Army's reviewers found that the soldiers "were wrongly treated because of their race and were not given fair trials."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Meuse-Argonne, the flu and the Choctaw code talkers, 1918" -->
More than a million American soldiers fought in the Meuse-Argonne, in northern France, from September 26 to November 11, 1918. By the National Archives' count, 26,277 of them were killed and 95,768 were wounded. An Air Force Times report on the battle's 100th anniversary called it "America's deadliest battle ever."

**The flu.** Influenza, the flu, spread through the army camps and troopships in 1918. Most of the soldiers it killed in army hospitals died of the pneumonia that followed it, and some died within hours. A sailor on one troopship to France remembered 15 burials at sea in one day.

Eric Durr, writing on the Army's website, counts about 45,000 soldiers dead of flu and pneumonia by the end of 1918. That is more than the 26,277 killed in the Meuse-Argonne. A narrower count, of soldiers admitted to army hospitals for flu, is 24,664 deaths.

**The Choctaw code talkers.** German soldiers were listening in on American telephone lines. On October 26, 1918, officers of the 142nd Infantry had Choctaw soldiers send orders over the phone in the Choctaw language, and the Germans could not understand them. The soldiers made up code words. Artillery, the big guns, was "big gun," and a machine gun was "little gun shoot fast."

Nineteen Choctaw soldiers are honored today as these code talkers. Their work was kept quiet until the late 1980s. Some of them had signed their enlistment cards as "natural born citizens." In 1901 members of Congress had changed the law to make the Choctaw citizens of the United States, along with four other nations in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma: the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Seminole and Creek. Many other Native people did not become citizens until a law of 1924.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-gunther" name="Henry Gunther" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Henry Gunther

> **Who:** A drafted bank clerk from Baltimore, whom General John Pershing's army listed as the last American soldier killed in World War I.
> **When and where:** Born in Baltimore, Maryland, in June 1895. Company A, 313th Infantry, 79th Division. Killed near Chaumont-devant-Damvillers, France, at 10:59 a.m. on November 11, 1918.

**He charged a German machine gun one minute before the fighting stopped.**

Henry Gunther kept the accounts at the National Bank of Baltimore when he was drafted in September 1917, and he was engaged to be married. By the time his regiment reached France in July 1918 he was a supply sergeant. Then army censors, who read soldiers' mail, opened a letter he had written to a friend. He complained about life in the trenches and told his friend not to join. He lost his rank and became a private again.

German and Allied leaders signed the armistice, the agreement to stop fighting, early on November 11, 1918. It would take effect at 11 a.m., and a runner brought word of it to Gunther's unit. At 10:59 Gunther charged a German machine gun with his bayonet locked onto his rifle. The German soldiers fired, and a bullet hit him in the left temple, the side of his head. He died there. The records do not say why he charged.

Army officials later gave him back his sergeant's rank and awarded him the Distinguished Service Cross, an army medal for bravery in battle. His body came home in 1923, and he is buried in Most Holy Redeemer Cemetery in Baltimore.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-gunther" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bonus Army, 1932" -->
**By 1919:** The fighting in Europe was over, and 116,516 of the Americans who served had died, about 45,000 of them of flu and pneumonia.

Members of Congress voted World War I veterans a bonus, an extra payment for their war service, in 1924, but set the payment date for 1945. In 1932, during the Great Depression, thousands of veterans came to Washington and camped there to ask for the money early. Counts of the veterans run from about 10,000 to more than 20,000. Counting their wives and children, a count in George Mason University's *Histories of the National Mall* reaches about 43,000 people.

Police clearing the camps on July 28, 1932, shot two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson, and both died. Hushka was a butcher from Lithuania who had sold his shop to join the army and became a citizen while he served. He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

**Tanks and tear gas.** The same day, President Herbert Hoover ordered the army to clear the camps. General Douglas MacArthur, the army's chief of staff, sent soldiers with six tanks, and they drove the veterans out with tear gas and bayonets.

A National Park Service history states that the soldiers used torches to set the camps on fire, and accounts differ on who set the fires in the main camp, at Anacostia. Members of Congress voted to pay the bonus in 1936, over President Franklin Roosevelt's veto, his refusal to sign the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The draft of 1940 and the objectors" -->
President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Selective Training and Service Act on September 16, 1940, the first draft in the country's history in a time of peace. Every man aged 21 to 45 had to register. By 1945, 50 million men aged 18 to 45 had registered and 10 million had been drafted.

About 43,000 men registered as conscientious objectors. To be accepted as one, a man usually had to belong to a church that taught peace, such as the Quakers, the Mennonites or the Church of the Brethren. Of the 43,000, about 25,000 served in the armed forces without weapons, most of them as medics, and about 12,000 did unpaid work in Civilian Public Service camps. About 6,000 refused to cooperate with the draft at all and went to prison, and more than 4,400 of those were Jehovah's Witnesses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="World War II, 1941 to 1945" -->
Japanese Navy pilots attacked the US Navy base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on December 7, 1941. They killed 2,403 Americans, counting the missing: 2,008 sailors, 109 Marines, 218 soldiers and 68 civilians. A Japanese bomb of 1,760 pounds hit the forward deck of the USS *Arizona* and set off an explosion. Of her crew, 1,177 died. More than 900 of them are still inside the sunken ship.

**A mess attendant.** Doris Miller was a mess attendant, a sailor who cooked and served food, on the USS *West Virginia* that morning. It was one of the few jobs Navy leaders let Black sailors do. Miller carried his dying captain. Then he fired a machine gun he had never been trained to use. Admiral Chester Nimitz gave him the Navy Cross, a navy medal for bravery in battle, in May 1942.

The crew of a Japanese submarine torpedoed his ship, the *Liscome Bay*, on November 24, 1943. The torpedo set off the bombs stored below deck, and the ship sank in about 23 minutes. About three of every four men aboard died, Miller among them. His body was never found.

**Bataan.** Major General Edward King Jr. surrendered about 75,000 American and Filipino soldiers on the Bataan Peninsula, in the Philippines, on April 9, 1942. Japanese soldiers marched them about 65 miles. The guards starved and beat the prisoners and stabbed with bayonets men who were too weak to walk.

By a Department of Veterans Affairs estimate, Japanese soldiers killed 650 American and 16,500 Filipino soldiers during and after the march, and other counts run higher. After the war, an American military court tried the Japanese commander, Lieutenant General Homma Masaharu. The court sentenced him to death, and American soldiers shot him on April 3, 1946.

**D-Day and Iwo Jima.** Soldiers of the Allies, the countries fighting Germany, landed in Normandy in France on June 6, 1944, called D-Day. Researchers at the National D-Day Memorial Foundation have found the names of 4,436 Allied soldiers, sailors and airmen killed that day. Of them, 2,519 were American, and the rest came from Britain, Canada and Norway.

On the Pacific island of Iwo Jima, from February 19 to March 26, 1945, 6,821 Americans were killed and 19,217 were wounded. Of about 21,000 Japanese defenders, 216 were captured. The rest were killed.

More than 350,000 American women served in uniform, in new women's branches of the Army, Navy and Marines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Soldiers in separate units" -->
Black soldiers, sailors and airmen served in separate units. Black pilots trained at Tuskegee, and the 332nd Fighter Group, made up of these pilots, guarded American bombers flying over Europe.

**The bomber story.** Reporters began to write in March 1945 that the group never lost a bomber. That story is false. Daniel Haulman, an Air Force historian, counted the bombers lost: enemy planes shot down 27 bombers that the 332nd was guarding. The other fighter groups in the same air force lost 46 each, on average.

**Port Chicago.** Two ships being loaded with ammunition blew up at the Port Chicago Navy base in California on July 17, 1944. The explosion killed 320 men, and 202 of them were Black sailors. Navy officers had given the dangerous loading work to Black sailors without enough training or equipment.

Afterward, 258 sailors refused to go back to loading under the same conditions. Navy officers convicted fifty of them of mutiny. Thurgood Marshall, a lawyer for the NAACP, a group that works for Black Americans' rights, worked to get a higher court to look at their case again. In 2024, Navy Secretary Carlos Del Toro cleared the names of all the men who were punished.

**Japanese Americans.** After Japan's attack, Army leaders would not let Japanese Americans join the army. President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, an order from the president that officials had to follow, on February 19, 1942.

Under it, federal officials forced more than 120,000 people of Japanese descent from their homes and locked them in camps behind fences and guard towers. Two thirds of them were American citizens. None of them was charged with a crime.

Army leaders dropped the ban on joining in 1943. From the camps, 1,208 people volunteered, fewer than 6 of every 100 who could. In Hawaii, where officials did not lock up Japanese Americans in large numbers, more than 10,000 volunteered. About 14,000 men served in the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, an army unit of Japanese American soldiers. They received 9,486 Purple Hearts, the medal for being wounded or killed in battle, and 21 Medals of Honor, the country's highest award for bravery in battle.

More than 375 Navajo Marines sent messages in a code built from the Navajo language. In it, a fighter plane was a "hummingbird" and a submarine was an "iron fish." Japanese code breakers never broke it. At Iwo Jima six Navajo code talkers sent more than 800 messages without an error. Military officials kept the code secret until 1968.

**Truman's order.** President Truman signed Executive Order 9981 on July 26, 1948. It required "equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." According to a National Park Service history, the labor leader A. Philip Randolph, who had threatened a protest march, pushed Truman to sign it. Military leaders were slow to obey it, and nearly every unit was mixed by about 1953.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-o-davis-jr" name="Benjamin O. Davis Jr." movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin O. Davis Jr.

> **Who:** Commander of the Black fighter pilots trained at Tuskegee, and the first Black general in the Air Force.
> **When and where:** Born in Washington, DC, December 18, 1912. West Point, 1932 to 1936. Commanded the 99th Fighter Squadron and the 332nd Fighter Group in Italy. Died July 4, 2002.

**For four years his West Point classmates spoke to him only on duty.**

Benjamin O. Davis Jr.'s father, Benjamin O. Davis Sr., was one of only two Black officers in the Army. The son entered West Point in 1932. For four years his white classmates spoke to him only when their duties required it. He never had a roommate, and he ate his meals in silence. He finished 35th of 276 in the class of 1936.

The Army had no Black flying units, so Army leaders made him an infantry officer, an officer of foot soldiers. When Army leaders opened pilot training for Black men at Tuskegee, he trained there and earned his pilot's wings in March 1942. He led the 99th Fighter Squadron and then the 332nd Fighter Group in combat.

Years later, Davis told the historian Alan Gropman that he himself doubted the claim that the group never lost a bomber. In 1998 President Bill Clinton made him a four-star general.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-o-davis-jr" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-inouye" name="Daniel Inouye" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Inouye

> **Who:** A Japanese American soldier from Hawaii who lost his right arm in Italy, and later a US senator.
> **When and where:** Born in Honolulu, September 7, 1924. 442nd Regimental Combat Team. Wounded near San Terenzo, Italy, April 21, 1945. Died December 17, 2012.

**With his left hand he pulled a live grenade from his right.**

Daniel Inouye wanted to become a surgeon. When he first tried to join the army, he was turned away under the rule that barred Japanese Americans. He enlisted in 1943, after Army leaders dropped the ban.

He led an attack up a ridge in Italy against three German machine guns on April 21, 1945. A bullet hit him in the stomach, and he kept going. Then, in his own words, a German "rifle grenade smashed into my right elbow and exploded. I looked at my dangling arm and saw my grenade still clenched in a fist that suddenly didn't belong to me anymore."

He pulled the live grenade out of his right hand with his left and threw it at the last machine gun. Then he was shot in the leg and passed out.

Surgeons at a field hospital cut off his right arm on May 1. Doctors had already given him so much morphine, a painkiller, that they could give him nothing to put him to sleep for the operation. He was given other people's blood 17 times. He left the army in 1947 as a captain and represented Hawaii in the US Senate for 50 years. On June 21, 2000, President Clinton gave him the Medal of Honor, with 19 other Japanese American veterans.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="daniel-inouye" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chester-nez-war" name="Chester Nez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Chester Nez

> **Who:** A Navajo Marine, one of the first 29 code talkers, who fought on four Pacific islands.
> **When and where:** Born in Chi Chil Tah, New Mexico, 1921. Guadalcanal, Bougainville, Guam and Peleliu, 1942 to 1944. Died in Albuquerque, June 4, 2014.

**Punished at school for speaking Navajo, he built a code in it.**

At his boarding school at Fort Defiance, school staff punished students, often by hitting them, for speaking Navajo. Marine recruiters came to the reservation in April 1942, and Chester Nez joined. He and 28 other Navajo Marines built the code at Camp Elliott in California.

He went into combat on Guadalcanal in November 1942, then on Bougainville. On Guam, in July and August 1944, he was wounded in the foot. He fought again on Peleliu in September 1944.

At home after the war he had nightmares and memories of combat that kept coming back, what doctors now call post-traumatic stress disorder. A Navajo healing ceremony for warriors, the Enemy Way, helped him. He finished high school, studied art at the University of Kansas on the G.I. Bill, a law under which federal officials paid for veterans' schooling, and worked 25 years for the Veterans Administration. He was the last of the first 29 when he died at 93.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chester-nez-war" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tokyo, March 1945" -->
**By March 1945:** Allied soldiers had been fighting in France since D-Day, and Marines were fighting on Iwo Jima.

Major General Curtis LeMay commanded the American B-29 bombers attacking Japan, and in early 1945 he changed their method. Instead of dropping ordinary bombs from high up in daylight, the planes flew low at night and dropped bombs made to start fires. Many were filled with napalm, gasoline thickened into a jelly, which sticks to whatever it lands on, including skin, and keeps burning.

B-29 crews in 279 planes dropped more than 1,665 tons of these bombs on Tokyo's neighborhoods of wooden houses on the night of March 9 to 10, 1945. The fires joined into one huge fire, called a firestorm, that used up the oxygen in the air. People suffocated or burned to death. Sixteen square miles of the city burned, and more than a million people lost their homes.

Historians at the National WWII Museum estimate 110,000 dead, and other estimates start at 80,000. That is close to the 116,516 Americans who died in all of World War I. Most of the dead were civilians. LeMay and his commander, General Hap Arnold, called the raid a success. American bomber crews firebombed many more Japanese cities after Tokyo.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The atomic bombs, August 1945" -->
Scientists and engineers of the Manhattan Project, the secret program to build the bomb, built the first atomic bombs at a cost of about $1.9 billion.

**The order.** General Thomas Handy, the acting Army chief of staff, gave the order on July 25, 1945, at the direction of the Secretary of War, Henry Stimson, and the Army chief of staff, George Marshall. He told General Carl Spaatz to have the 509th Composite Group, a bomber unit, drop the first bomb on a Japanese city as soon as the weather allowed. President Harry Truman approved.

The crew of the bomber *Enola Gay*, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, dropped a bomb made with uranium, a heavy metal, on Hiroshima at 8:16 a.m. on August 6, 1945. On August 9, Major Charles Sweeney's crew dropped a bomb made with plutonium, a different heavy metal, on Nagasaki. Japan's leaders surrendered on August 14.

Scientists at the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, run by Japan and the United States together, estimate that 90,000 to 166,000 people in Hiroshima and 60,000 to 80,000 in Nagasaki died within four months. The exact numbers are not known. A US study in 1946, the Strategic Bombing Survey, gave lower counts: 70,000 to 80,000 dead in Hiroshima and more than 35,000 in Nagasaki.

Most of the dead were killed by burns from the flash of heat and by injuries from the blast. The same US study found that 15 to 20 percent died of radiation sickness.

**Radiation sickness.** The bombs gave off invisible energy called radiation, which killed the cells that make blood and line the gut. People with radiation sickness ran fevers within two days. After about two weeks their hair fell out, and they bled from the gums and nose and under the skin. Deaths from radiation began about a week after the bombs and were highest three to four weeks later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coming home: the G.I. Bill and Isaac Woodard" -->
President Franklin Roosevelt signed the G.I. Bill on June 22, 1944. Under it, federal officials paid for veterans' schooling and promised banks that the Veterans Administration would repay a veteran's home loan if the veteran could not. By 1956, 7.8 million of the 16 million World War II veterans, about one in two, had used it for school or job training. From 1944 to 1952, Veterans Administration officials made that promise on nearly 2.4 million home loans.

**Who got the loans.** Local white bank officers and real estate agents decided who got the loans. Reporters at *Ebony* magazine found in 1947 that of 3,229 of these home loans in 13 Mississippi cities, only two went to Black veterans.

**Isaac Woodard.** Police in South Carolina beat a Black veteran blind on the day he left the Army. Sergeant Isaac Woodard, of Winnsboro, South Carolina, left the Army on February 12, 1946, after four years of service. On the bus home from Camp Gordon, Georgia, he argued with the driver after asking for a rest stop. At Batesburg, South Carolina, the driver had police take him off the bus.

Woodard said that Police Chief Lynwood Shull and Officer Elliot Long beat him with blackjacks, which are short clubs, and tried to gouge out his eyes. Shull later said, "I hit him across the front of the head." A local judge fined Woodard $50. Doctors at the veterans' hospital in Aiken found bleeding in both eyeballs. He was blind for the rest of his life.

Federal officials charged Shull with violating Woodard's civil rights. In November 1946, an all-white jury found Shull not guilty after thirty minutes. Chapter 25, Rights and Movements, tells how his case reached President Truman.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 15 minutes**

American forces fought two long wars in Asia between 1950 and 2000, in Korea and in Vietnam. Drafted men fought in Vietnam until the draft ended in 1973, and after that everyone who joined the armed forces had volunteered.

**The dead.** By the Defense Department's counts, 36,574 Americans died in Korea, from 1950 to 1953, and 58,220 died in Vietnam. Many more people died in those wars: at least 2.5 million in the Korean War, most of them Koreans, and by Vietnam's own count about 3 million fighters and civilians in the Vietnam War.

About 697,000 American troops went to war against Iraq in 1991. The fighting lasted from January 17 to February 28, and 383 Americans died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Korea, 1950 to 1953" -->
North Korean troops invaded South Korea on June 25, 1950, and American troops went to fight for the South. About 120,000 Chinese soldiers surrounded about 30,000 American and allied troops at the Chosin Reservoir in North Korea in November 1950. The temperature fell to 20 degrees below zero. The fighting at Chosin ended on December 13. Of about 8,000 Marines in the fighting, 836 were killed, and the survivors called themselves "The Frozen Chosen."

**No Gun Ri.** American soldiers of the 7th Cavalry Regiment fired on South Korean refugees, people fleeing from danger, in the last week of July 1950. The refugees had gathered at and under a railroad bridge near the village of No Gun Ri. Survivors say about 400 people died, and Korean officials have confirmed at least 163 dead or missing.

Army officers who reviewed the shooting said in January 2001 that they could not count the dead and found no order to shoot. That month President Bill Clinton said that "things were done which should not have been done." He expressed regret and did not apologize.

The historian Sahr Conway-Lanz found a letter in 2006 from the US ambassador, John Muccio, dated July 25, 1950. It said that refugees who kept coming toward American lines after warning shots "will be shot." The Army's report did not include the letter.

By the Korean War Legacy Foundation's count, 38 percent of the Americans taken prisoner in Korea died in captivity, more than one in three. The fighting stopped with an armistice signed on July 27, 1953. At least 2.5 million people died in the war, most of them Korean soldiers and civilians. No single official count exists.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The hydrogen bomb tests, 1952 and 1954" -->
Radioactive dust from American bomb tests after World War II sickened people who were not at war: Marshall Islanders, American service members and Japanese fishermen. American scientists and military officers tested nuclear weapons, the atomic bombs and the stronger bombs that came after them, in the Marshall Islands, in the Pacific, on the land of the Marshallese people.

**The first hydrogen bomb.** American scientists set off the first hydrogen bomb, a far more powerful kind of nuclear weapon, on Elugelab Island in Enewetak Atoll, a ring of small coral islands around a lagoon, on November 1, 1952. It was more than 450 times as powerful as the bomb dropped on Nagasaki. It destroyed the island and left an underwater crater 6,240 feet wide.

**Castle Bravo.** The test called Castle Bravo, at Bikini Atoll on March 1, 1954, was 1,000 times as powerful as the bomb dropped on Hiroshima, and two and a half times as powerful as its planners expected. A cloud 100 miles wide dropped fallout on Marshall Islanders, American service members, and the crew of a Japanese fishing boat. Fallout is radioactive dust and ash that falls back to the ground after a nuclear blast. It burns the skin it lands on and damages the body from inside when it is breathed in or swallowed.

The military had made no plans to move the people living nearby. On Rongelap, about 110 miles away, the fallout looked like snow, and children played in it. About three days after the blast, American crews took more than 230 people off Rongelap, Rongerik and Utirik, among them 28 American service members. The fallout left Rongelap unsafe to live on.

Twenty-three Japanese fishermen became sick, and one died. The physicist Theodore Taylor said later, "obviously we didn't irradiate (expose to radiation) the natives on purpose. Obviously, we didn't know what the hell we were doing."

**What followed.** American officials made the exposed Marshall Islanders the subjects of a secret medical study called Project 4.1. They told the people working on it not to discuss its purpose, "due to possible adverse public reaction," meaning for fear of how the public would react. The United States later gave the exposed people medical care. A screening from 1993 to 1997 found 57 cases of thyroid cancer, a cancer of a gland in the neck, among Marshallese people born before 1954.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The war in Vietnam, and who had to go" -->
Marines landed at Da Nang, South Vietnam, on March 8, 1965. By April 1969, about 543,000 American troops were in Vietnam. The deadliest year was 1968, when 16,899 Americans died there. The last American combat troops left on March 29, 1973. The war ended on April 30, 1975, when North Vietnamese troops and their allies in the South took Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam.

**Who had to go.** Counts of the men drafted during the Vietnam years run from about 1.8 million to 2.2 million. Full-time college students could get a student deferment, which put off their draft call while they stayed in school. Until 1969, local draft boards, the officials who chose which men in an area were drafted, called the oldest eligible men first.

**The lottery.** Selective Service officials, who ran the draft for the whole country, held a lottery instead on December 1, 1969. They put 366 birthdays in blue plastic capsules and drew them one at a time. The first date drawn was September 14, so men born on that day were called first. For 1970, the draft reached number 195.

Many men refused to go. Officials accused 209,517 men of breaking the draft law, and fewer than 9,000 were convicted. Some men fled to Canada. In 1972 more men were recognized as conscientious objectors than were drafted. The last draft call came on December 7, 1972, and the law allowing the draft ran out on June 30, 1973. Since July 1980, young men have had to register for the draft again, but no one has been drafted.

**The pardon.** Jimmy Carter pardoned, or officially forgave, the men who had broken the draft law between 1964 and 1973 on January 21, 1977, his first full day as president. His pardon left out crimes that involved force or violence, and it did not cover men who had deserted the armed forces after joining.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ron-kovic" name="Ron Kovic" movie="Born on the Fourth of July (1989)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ron Kovic

> **Who:** A Marine from Long Island, paralyzed by a bullet in Vietnam, who came home and spoke against the war.
> **When and where:** Born July 4, 1946. Grew up in Massapequa, New York. Wounded in Vietnam on January 20, 1968.
> **Movie:** *Born on the Fourth of July* (1989), directed by Oliver Stone and based on Kovic's memoir. Kovic helped write the screenplay.

**The second bullet went through his shoulder and into his spine.**

Ron Kovic joined the Marines and then volunteered for a second tour in Vietnam, a second stretch of service there. On January 20, 1968, he was shot in the right foot. Then a second bullet went through his right shoulder. It collapsed one lung and cut into his spinal cord, the bundle of nerves inside the backbone that carries messages between the brain and the body.

From the middle of his chest down, he could no longer move. In the hospital at Da Nang, doctors told him he would never walk again.

He spent months at the Veterans Administration hospital in the Bronx, New York. *New Mobility* magazine describes the hospital as short of staff, with out-of-date equipment and conditions that were sometimes abusive. Patients called it "the Bronx Zoo."

He gave his first speech against the war in 1969. In 1972, at a protest at President Richard Nixon's campaign office in Los Angeles, he was thrown out of his wheelchair and arrested. The *New Mobility* account does not name who threw him. His book *Born on the Fourth of July* came out in 1976.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ron-kovic" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="muhammad-ali-war" name="Muhammad Ali" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Muhammad Ali

> **Who:** The heavyweight boxing champion, who refused to be drafted and won his case in the Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** Refused induction in Houston, Texas, April 28, 1967. Supreme Court decision, June 28, 1971.

**When an officer called his name, he would not step forward.**

Muhammad Ali was a minister of the Nation of Islam, a Muslim religious group. He asked his draft board to list him as a conscientious objector. The board said no, and so did the appeal board.

He went to the army's induction center in Houston, where new soldiers were sworn in, on April 28, 1967. When the officer called him, he stayed where he was. The officer warned him that refusing was a crime that could bring five years in prison and a $10,000 fine. Boxing officials took away his heavyweight title that day.

He was found guilty in a federal court of refusing the draft on June 20, 1967, and sentenced to five years in prison and a $10,000 fine. His lawyers appealed, and he did not box again for more than three years. On June 28, 1971, all eight justices who heard the case overturned his conviction. They ruled that the appeal board had never given a reason for turning down his claim.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="muhammad-ali-war" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="My Lai, March 16, 1968" -->
American soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, under Captain Ernest Medina, killed the unarmed people of My Lai, a village in South Vietnam, on March 16, 1968. The counts of the dead differ. The Army's own investigation, led by Lieutenant General William Peers, found at least 175 killed and possibly more than 400. Another Army count gives 347. A memorial in the village lists 504 names, from babies of one year old to a person of 82.

**What the soldiers did.** The Peers report describes it. Soldiers of the 1st Platoon herded 60 to 70 villagers into an irrigation ditch and shot them. In another part of the village, soldiers of the 2nd Platoon made 10 to 20 women and children squat in a circle, fired grenades into them, and then shot the wounded with rifles.

Soldiers raped women and girls, forcing them into sex by violence or threat. The investigators found that one platoon leader watched several rapes that day and did nothing to stop them. Soldiers also burned homes, killed farm animals and destroyed crops.

**The cover-up.** Officers at every level of the division took steps that kept the killings secret, and the story did not become public until late 1969. The Peers investigation named 30 men who knew about the killings. Fourteen of them were charged with crimes. Only one was convicted.

**Calley.** Lieutenant William Calley was tried at a court-martial. On March 29, 1971, a panel of six army officers found him guilty of murdering 22 people. Two days later, the same officers sentenced him to life in prison. Three days after the guilty verdict, President Richard Nixon ordered him held under house arrest, kept under guard in his own quarters.

Lieutenant General Albert Connor, the commander who reviewed the case, cut the life sentence to 20 years in August 1971. Secretary of the Army Howard Callaway cut it to 10 in April 1974. Calley was freed early on parole, under rules he had to follow, in November 1974. No one was convicted of rape.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hugh-thompson" name="Hugh Thompson Jr." movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Hugh Thompson Jr.

> **Who:** An Army helicopter pilot who landed between American soldiers and Vietnamese villagers at My Lai to stop the killing.
> **When and where:** Born in Atlanta, Georgia, April 15, 1943. My Lai, South Vietnam, March 16, 1968. Died in Pineville, Louisiana, January 6, 2006.

**He set his helicopter down between the soldiers and the villagers.**

Hugh Thompson Jr. flew a small scout helicopter with two crewmen, Glenn Andreotta and Lawrence Colburn. Over My Lai that morning, he dropped green smoke next to wounded villagers as a signal that they needed help. When he came back, they were dead. He watched Captain Medina shoot a wounded young woman, and when he saw an irrigation ditch full of bodies he radioed, "There's something wrong here."

He landed, and Lieutenant Calley told him to mind his own business. Then Thompson saw soldiers chasing about ten villagers, some of them children, toward a shelter. He set his helicopter down between the soldiers and the villagers. He told Colburn and Andreotta to fire their machine guns at the soldiers if the soldiers shot at him or the villagers.

He got 11 people out of the shelter, and two other helicopter pilots flew them to safety. Andreotta climbed into a ditch of about 100 bodies and pulled out a living boy, Do Ba. Thompson flew the boy to a hospital and reported the killings. Medina then told his men to stop.

Andreotta was killed in combat three weeks later. In 1969 Mendel Rivers, the congressman who led the House Armed Services Committee, said Thompson was the one soldier at My Lai who should be punished, for threatening American soldiers. Thompson got death threats.

In March 1998, Army leaders gave all three men the Soldier's Medal, Andreotta after his death. It is the Army's highest award for bravery outside combat with an enemy. That year Thompson and Colburn went back to My Lai and met survivors.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hugh-thompson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the Vietnam War cost" -->
Vietnamese officials published their first count of the war's dead on April 3, 1995. They said 1.1 million fighters of North Vietnam and of the Viet Cong, the communist fighters in South Vietnam who fought on the North's side, had died over 21 years, and nearly 2 million civilians in the North and the South. Between 200,000 and 250,000 South Vietnamese soldiers died, by the US military's estimate.

**Operation Homecoming.** American aircrews flew 591 American prisoners of war home from North Vietnam from February to April 1973. Many had been held in Hoa Lo prison in Hanoi, which the prisoners called the "Hanoi Hilton." The historian Andrew Lipps, quoted in a Defense Department news story, writes of the "mental and physical torture" they went through.

**Agent Orange.** American forces sprayed plant-killing chemicals over South Vietnam. One of them, Agent Orange, was tainted with a poison called dioxin. Today officials of the Department of Veterans Affairs treat a list of illnesses in veterans who were exposed to Agent Orange as caused by it, so the veterans do not have to prove it. The list includes several cancers, such as bladder cancer, lung cancer and prostate cancer, and also Parkinson's disease, type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington, designed by Maya Lin, opened in 1982. It lists 58,281 names. The count is higher than the Defense Department's 58,220 because the two lists count some deaths differently.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Women and gay service members" -->
Under a law signed on June 12, 1948, women could serve as permanent members of the armed forces. Under the same law, women could make up no more than 2 percent of the forces and could not serve in combat units. Members of Congress removed the cap in 1967. In July 1976, 119 women entered the Military Academy at West Point, and 62 of them graduated in 1980.

Under a 1993 law called "Don't Ask, Don't Tell," gay, lesbian and bisexual service members could serve only if they kept it secret. Military officials put more than 13,000 of them out of the armed forces under it before it ended in 2011.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gulf War, 1991" -->
About 100,000 Iraqi soldiers invaded Kuwait on August 2, 1990. American and allied pilots began bombing Iraq on January 17, 1991. The ground attack began on February 24 and lasted 100 hours, and President George H. W. Bush declared a cease-fire on February 28. About 697,000 American troops went to the region, and 383 died, 148 of them in battle.

No official US count of the Iraqi dead exists. In 2003, Carl Conetta of the Project on Defense Alternatives, a research group, estimated that American and allied forces killed 20,000 to 26,000 Iraqi soldiers and about 3,500 Iraqi civilians.

**Gulf War illness.** At least one in four of the American troops came home sick. In 2008 scientists on a committee advising the Department of Veterans Affairs found that 175,000 to 210,000 Gulf War veterans had an illness. Its signs were memory problems, headaches, pain all over the body, and stomach trouble. Doctors had found no treatment that worked, and few of the sick veterans had gotten better.

The committee's scientists linked the illness to pills the soldiers took to protect them from nerve gas, and to pesticides, the chemicals used to kill insects. Other scientists, on panels of the Institute of Medicine, weighed the evidence differently.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 13 minutes**

Every American who fought in the wars after the attacks of September 11, 2001, had volunteered. American forces fought in Afghanistan for almost 20 years and in Iraq for almost 9.

**Who served.** Researchers at the Pew Research Center found in 2011 that in the ten years after 9/11 about one American in 200 was on active duty, serving full-time in the military, at any one time. At the height of World War II it had been nearly one in 11.

**The dead.** A 2020 report to Congress, using Defense Department records up to July 2020, lists more than 2,400 American service members dead in the Afghanistan war and about 4,500 in the Iraq war. More than 53,000 were wounded in action in the two wars together.

Far more people in those countries died. Researchers in the Costs of War project at Brown University study the wars after 9/11 in Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen and Pakistan. They estimate that more than 940,000 people in those countries were killed in the fighting itself from 2001 to 2023, and more than 432,000 of them were civilians. They estimate that 3.6 to 3.8 million more died because of what the fighting destroyed: the ways people earned a living, hospitals and health care, roads and water systems, and the land.

Counts of the Iraqi civilian dead differ widely. A 2006 survey published in the medical journal *The Lancet* estimated about 600,000 violent deaths, and US and Iraqi officials disputed it. Chapter 23, America and the World, explains why the counts are so far apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Afghanistan and Iraq, 2001 to 2021" -->
American bombing of Afghanistan began on October 7, 2001, less than a month after the September 11 attacks. The invasion of Iraq began on March 20, 2003. The last line of American military vehicles, carrying about 500 troops, left Iraq for Kuwait on December 18, 2011.

**Leaving Afghanistan.** American officials signed a deal with the Taliban in February 2020 and agreed to take American troops out of Afghanistan. The Taliban were the armed group American forces had fought there since 2001. President Joe Biden announced in April 2021 that the last of them would be out by September. Taliban fighters took Kabul, Afghanistan's capital, on August 15, 2021, and several thousand more American troops came to the airport to fly American embassy staff and some Afghans out.

**Abbey Gate.** A bomber from ISIS-K, a terror group in Afghanistan that was also fighting the Taliban, set off explosives he was wearing in a crowd at Abbey Gate, an entrance to the Kabul airport, on August 26. He killed 13 American service members: 11 Marines, a Navy medic and an Army soldier. Another 45 were wounded. US Justice Department officials said in 2025 that about 160 Afghan civilians were killed, and other reports say about 170.

**The drone strike.** Three days after the Abbey Gate bombing, on August 29, 2021, American forces fired a missile from a drone, an aircraft with no pilot aboard, at a car in Kabul. Officers believed it carried an ISIS-K bomb. The driver was Zemari Ahmadi, a 43-year-old aid worker, and the car was at his family's home. The missile killed ten civilians, seven of them children. The next night, at 11:59 p.m. on August 30, the last American plane left Kabul.

General Mark Milley first called it a "righteous strike." On September 17, 2021, General Frank McKenzie, the head of the military's Central Command, called it "a tragic mistake." On December 13, 2021, Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin decided that no one would be punished.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Abu Ghraib, 2003 and 2004" -->
American military police guarding prisoners at Abu Ghraib prison near Baghdad committed what an Army report calls "sadistic (taking pleasure in hurting people), blatant (done openly), and wanton (done for no reason) criminal abuses" of them, from October to December 2003. Army Major General Antonio Taguba investigated and wrote that report in 2004.

**What the guards did.** The report lists guards punching, slapping and kicking prisoners, jumping on their bare feet, keeping them naked for days, and forcing them into sexual poses to photograph them. Guards poured the liquid from broken glow sticks on prisoners.

Guards used dogs without muzzles to frighten prisoners, and in at least one case a dog bit a prisoner and badly hurt him. Guards also threatened prisoners with a loaded pistol, and attached wires to one prisoner to make him think he would be tortured with electricity.

The CBS program *60 Minutes II* broadcast the guards' photographs on April 28, 2004. Army officers at courts-martial convicted Specialist Charles Graner, who was sentenced to 10 years in prison, Private First Class Lynndie England, sentenced to 3 years, and other low-ranking soldiers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Haditha and Kandahar, 2005 and 2012" -->
A roadside bomb killed Lance Corporal Miguel Terrazas, a 20-year-old Marine, in the Iraqi town of Haditha on November 19, 2005. Marines of Staff Sergeant Frank Wuterich's squad then shot five unarmed Iraqi men who had been ordered out of a car. Wuterich told his men to "shoot first and ask questions later." They broke into nearby houses with grenades and rifle fire. In about 45 minutes they killed 24 Iraqi men, women and children, among them an old man in a wheelchair. No weapons were found.

Military prosecutors charged eight Marines. They dropped the charges against six, and one was found not guilty at a court-martial. Wuterich pleaded guilty to one charge of neglecting his duty in January 2012. The judge sentenced him to 90 days, a pay cut and demotion to private, but under his plea deal, an agreement to admit a crime in return for a lighter punishment, he served no time in prison.

**Kandahar.** Army Staff Sergeant Robert Bales left his base in Kandahar, Afghanistan, on the night of March 11, 2012, and went into two villages, Alkozai and Najiban. He killed 16 unarmed Afghan civilians and wounded six more. Seventeen of the 22 people he shot were women or children, and eleven of the dead came from one family. Bales pleaded guilty, and on August 23, 2013, he was sentenced to life in prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pat-tillman" name="Pat Tillman" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pat Tillman

> **Who:** A professional football player who left the Arizona Cardinals to join the Army Rangers, and was killed by soldiers of his own platoon.
> **When and where:** Enlisted 2002. 75th Ranger Regiment. Killed in eastern Afghanistan, April 22, 2004. A committee of the House of Representatives held a hearing on his death on April 24, 2007.

**He called out "Cease fire" and his own name until he stopped.**

Pat Tillman walked away from a $3.6 million contract with the Arizona Cardinals after September 11, 2001. In 2002 he and his brother Kevin joined the Army together. Pat became a Ranger, a soldier of a specially trained Army unit, in the 75th Ranger Regiment, and served tours in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Soldiers in a truck from Pat's own platoon fired on the hillside where his group was on April 22, 2004, after what Kevin Tillman called "a small harassing ambush." The soldier beside him said later that they yelled "Cease fire. Friendlies." Tillman threw a smoke grenade, and the shooting stopped.

When the shooting started again, Tillman was hit. He called out "Cease fire" and his own name over and over until he stopped. Three bullets struck his forehead, so close together that Army medical examiners judged he was shot with an M-16 rifle from about 10 yards. An Afghan soldier with them was killed too.

**The first account.** His family was told that enemy fighters had killed him. In the words of Congressman Henry Waxman, who led the hearing, "the military told the world" the same thing. Army commanders awarded him the Silver Star, a medal for bravery in battle, with a written account of a battle with the enemy.

Kevin Tillman told Congress that Pat's uniform, equipment and notebook were destroyed, and his testimony does not name who destroyed them. On May 29, 2004, five weeks after the shooting, an Army general announced that Tillman had "probably" been killed by his own side. Kevin Tillman called the Army's first account "utter fiction."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pat-tillman" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wounds that do not show, and veterans at home" -->
Military doctors diagnosed 535,544 service members with a traumatic brain injury, damage to the brain from a blow or a blast, from 2000 through 2025, according to a Defense Department count published in June 2026. About 83 percent were mild, most of them concussions, injuries in which a blow shakes the brain, and many happened in training or daily life, not in combat.

**Veteran suicides.** A total of 6,398 veterans killed themselves in 2023, an average of 17.5 a day, according to a report the Department of Veterans Affairs released in early 2026. That is nearly as many as the about 6,900 service members who died in the Afghanistan and Iraq wars together. Sixty-one percent of them had not received care from the department in the last year of their lives. The country had about 18 million veterans in 2023, about 6 of every 100 adults, the Pew Research Center found.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tammy-duckworth" name="Tammy Duckworth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tammy Duckworth

> **Who:** An Army helicopter pilot who lost both legs when her Black Hawk helicopter was hit in Iraq, and later a US senator.
> **When and where:** Born in Bangkok, Thailand. Illinois Army National Guard. Wounded in Iraq, November 12, 2004.

**"The explosion vaporized my right leg," she wrote.**

Tammy Duckworth became an Army officer in 1992 and learned to fly helicopters. In 2004 she went to Iraq as a Black Hawk pilot with the Illinois Army National Guard, a part-time state army force that can be sent to war.

A rocket-propelled grenade, a small rocket fired from a tube carried on a fighter's shoulder, hit the front of her helicopter on November 12, 2004. In her memoir she wrote, "The explosion vaporized my right leg. It blew my left leg up into the bottom of the instrument panel." It also tore through her right arm.

Dan Milberg, the other pilot aboard, landed the damaged helicopter. Matt Backues, another member of the crew, dragged her, unconscious, across the desert. She lost both legs and part of the use of her right arm, and she recovered at Walter Reed Army Medical Center.

She was an assistant secretary of Veterans Affairs from 2009 to 2011. Illinois voters elected her to the US House in 2012 and to the Senate in 2016. In 2018 she became the first senator to give birth while in office.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tammy-duckworth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who may serve" -->
"Don't Ask, Don't Tell" ended on September 20, 2011, and gay, lesbian and bisexual service members could serve openly. Defense Secretary Ashton Carter opened every combat job to women on December 3, 2015. About 220,000 jobs, roughly one in ten, had been closed to them.

**Transgender service members.** The justices of the Supreme Court let President Donald Trump's ban on transgender service members take effect on May 6, 2025, while lawsuits went on. On June 1, 2026, judges of a federal appeals court in Washington ruled 2 to 1 that the ban likely broke the Constitution, and they blocked the discharge of the service members who had sued.

A few weeks later a judge of a lower court extended that protection to all transgender troops then serving, about 4,240 people by the count of the Gender Justice League, a group that works for transgender people's rights. New people were still not allowed to join. On August 28, 2026, Justice Department lawyers asked the Supreme Court to lift the lower court's order.

**The Wounded Knee medals.** Twenty soldiers of the 7th Cavalry received the Medal of Honor for Wounded Knee in 1890, where soldiers of the regiment killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, almost half of them women and children. Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin ordered a review of those medals in 2024. In September 2025, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced that the soldiers would keep them. A bill called the Remove the Stain Act, which would take the medals back, had not passed as of September 2026.

**The department's name.** President Trump signed an order on September 5, 2025, letting officials call the Defense Department the "Department of War." Members of Congress set its legal name. Members of the House voted 216 to 212 on July 22, 2026, to change the legal name, but as of late September 2026 the change had not become law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2025 and 2026: boats, Venezuela and Iran" -->
US military crews began firing on boats in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific on September 2, 2025. President Trump's officials said the boats were carrying drugs. By September 19, 2026, the Associated Press counted at least 231 people killed in 69 strikes. In at least one case, the Associated Press reported, military officials gave no evidence that the boat carried drugs.

Lawyers for the American Civil Liberties Union are suing for the families of two men from Trinidad, Chad Joseph and Rishi Samaroo, who were killed in a strike on October 14, 2025. The group says they were fishermen and farm workers.

**Maduro.** Soldiers of the Army's Delta Force and officers of the CIA, the American spy agency, captured Venezuela's president, Nicolás Maduro, and his wife, Cilia Flores, in the capital, Caracas, on January 3, 2026. They flew the two to a Navy ship, the USS *Iwo Jima*, and then took them to New York. The next day federal officials made public the charges against them, crimes tied to smuggling drugs. On January 5, in a federal court in New York, both said they were not guilty.

By the Council on Foreign Relations' account, American forces killed 32 Cuban officers in the attack. Members of Congress had not voted to approve the raid beforehand.

**Iran.** American and Israeli forces began bombing Iran on February 28, 2026, and Iranian forces fired back at American troops. By the Pentagon's public count on September 22, 2026, 19 American service members had died and more than 820 had been wounded. Five officials told the *Washington Post* that at least 22 service members had died in the Middle East since the war began, not all of them in the fighting. Chapter 23 gives a count of the people killed in Iran. The war was still going on in late September 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- What killed most American soldiers before World War II? Disease. In the war with Mexico seven of every eight American dead died of something other than enemy fire, and in World War I flu and pneumonia killed more soldiers than the war's deadliest battle did.
- Who was made to fight, and who could get out of it? Drafts filled the armies of the Civil War, both world wars and Vietnam. In 1863 a drafted man could pay $300 and go home, and in the Vietnam years college students could put off the draft. Since 1973 everyone who serves has volunteered.
- Were soldiers who killed people who could not fight back ever punished? Rarely. No one was charged for Gnadenhutten or tried for Sand Creek, and Calley, the only man convicted for My Lai, went free on parole in 1974.

**What changed:** The fighters changed from militia farmers, who went home when the fighting ended, to millions of drafted men in the world wars, to a small army of volunteers, about one American in 200.

**Five dates to keep**
- May 26, 1637: English soldiers burn the Pequot town at Mystic.
- April 19, 1775: The shooting starts at Lexington.
- July 1 to 3, 1863: The battle of Gettysburg.
- August 6, 1945: An American bomber crew drops an atomic bomb on Hiroshima.
- June 30, 1973: The law allowing the draft runs out.

**Not known:** Nobody knows how many Filipino civilians died in the war of 1899 to 1902, where the counts run from 200,000 to 1,000,000, or how many of them American soldiers killed.

**Where to go:** The Amos Humiston monument stands on North Stratton Street in Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, the only one on the battlefield to a single enlisted soldier.
