## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People fought wars in North America long before Europeans came. No one in North America north of Mexico wrote those wars down, so what is known comes from what people left in the ground. Archaeologists are scientists who study the past by digging up what people left behind. They have found skeletons with wounds, houses burned where they stood, and ditches and walls built around towns.

From that evidence, archaeologists can tell that a town was attacked, about when it happened, and what a weapon did to a bone. No name survives from these wars, and no word that any of the fighters said, including their reasons for fighting.

On the Great Plains, signs of violence go back to about 250 B.C. After about the year 1250, there was more fighting on the Plains. From then on, attackers often burned villages, and skeletons regularly show cut marks from scalping and from bodies being cut apart. Scalping means cutting the skin and hair off the top of a person's head.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Walls, ditches and stone weapons" -->
Farming villages along the Missouri River, in what are now North and South Dakota, often had a ditch dug around them and a palisade. A palisade is a fence of tall logs set upright in the ground. Some of these villages held up to 1,000 people.

Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, was the largest town north of Mexico. Between about 1050 and 1150, perhaps 15,000 to 20,000 people lived in its center. Its people built a wall of upright logs about two miles long around the middle of the town. They began it about 1100 and rebuilt it three times over about 200 years, so four walls stood there one after another. Staff at Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site estimate about 20,000 logs for each one. Towers called bastions stuck out from the wall at even distances. Archers could stand in them and shoot arrows at attackers.

Archaeologists disagree about what the Cahokia wall was for. Bastions are built for fighting. Some stretches of the wall were put up in a hurry and cut right through neighborhoods where people lived. Other archaeologists, in lesson pages from the University of Illinois, read the wall another way. They think it may have marked off the town's powerful families from ordinary townspeople, or insiders from outsiders.

Wounds show which weapons people used. At Norris Farms, in central Illinois, archaeologists studied a cemetery of 264 people from a village of about the year 1300. At least 43 of them, about 16 in every 100, had died by violence. Counting only the adults, at least one death in three came from fighting. Arrows had hit some of them. Others had been struck from the front, the side and the back with heavy weapons such as stone axes. The bones do not show who the attackers were.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crow Creek, about 1325" -->
At Crow Creek, on the Missouri River in South Dakota, archaeologists can count the dead from a single attack. The town there belonged to ancestors of the Arikara Nation. In 1978, archaeologists from the University of South Dakota dug into the ditch that ran around the town. They found the bones of at least 486 men, women and children. They dated the bones by measuring the carbon left in them, a method called radiocarbon dating. By that method, the attack happened about 1325.

Douglas Bamforth, an archaeologist at the University of Colorado, calls the 486 dead nearly the whole town. P. Willey, the scientist who studied the bones, thinks they were about 60 in every 100 of the people who lived there. Bamforth thinks the killings came from a fight over food. He says the likely reason was too many people living in the area for the food they could get, at a time when the weather was getting worse.

Many of the people at Crow Creek had been scalped, had their heads cut off, or had their bodies cut apart. Scalping, cutting the skin and hair off the top of a person's head, leaves cut marks on the skull. Skull fractures, meaning cracked skulls, were common.

The bones also show that these people were badly underfed before they were killed. Animals had chewed many of the bones. That means the bodies lay in the open for some time before anyone buried them in the ditch.

The bones do not show who the attackers were. Fewer young women are in the grave than a whole town would have had. Willey thinks the most likely reason is that the attackers carried young women away as captives.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, small armies from Spain marched into the homelands of Native nations in what is now the southern United States. They brought horses and trained attack dogs, and they fought with swords and lances. Before they came, no one north of Mexico had written war down. Spanish leaders and soldiers wrote reports about what they did. Those reports record only the Spanish side.

In 1568, Native people destroyed every fort one Spanish captain had built inland. Spanish soldiers and French colonists also fought each other on the coast of Florida. There the Spanish commander had his men kill about 245 French prisoners who had already surrendered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="De Soto's march, 1539 to 1543" -->
Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay, in Florida, in May 1539. He brought about 600 men, 220 horses, packs of dogs and a herd of pigs. For the next three years his army crossed the Southeast. Along the way, de Soto took hostages to force the towns to let his army pass. He held the leader of the Coosa towns hostage to get through those towns, and his men ate the food the Coosa had stored.

The National Park Service's page on de Soto states what his men did to towns that did not cooperate. De Soto's men threw people to the dogs, burned people alive, raped Native people, cut off people's noses and hands, and enslaved men and women to carry the army's loads as it moved on. Throwing a person to the dogs meant setting trained attack dogs on a captive to bite and tear the person, often to death. Rape means forcing a person to have sex.

De Soto died of a fever on May 21, 1542. His men sank his body in the Mississippi River. They hid his death so the nations they had been fighting would not learn that he could die. In June 1543 the rest of the army went down the Mississippi in seven boats. Only 311 of the Spanish survived to reach the Gulf of Mexico. They brought with them enslaved Native people, and no one recorded how many.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mabila, October 18, 1540" -->
Mabila was a town with a palisade around it. Its leader was a man the Spanish called Tascalusa. The battle there, on October 18, 1540, began when a Spanish officer decided that a Mabila man had not shown him enough respect. The argument ended with the officer cutting off the man's arm. An article on the battle at HistoryNet, a history magazine website, names the officer as Captain Baltasar de Gallegos.

The battle that followed lasted about nine hours. At the end the Spanish burned the town while people were still inside it. The expedition's own record-keeper wrote, "We killed them all either with fire or the sword."

The counts of the dead do not agree. The National Park Service's page lists more than 200 Spanish dead and about 2,000 people of Mabila. A University of Alabama news report states about 20 Spanish killed and 250 wounded, with thousands of Mabila dead. No one has ever found where Mabila stood, though searchers from the University of Alabama have looked for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tiguex towns, 1540 to 1541" -->
In the winter of 1540 to 1541, far to the west, the Spanish commander Coronado and his army fought the Tiguex towns on the Rio Grande in New Mexico. The Tiguex were Pueblo people, who lived in towns of houses several stories tall. They spoke the Tiwa language and lived in twelve or more towns near present-day Albuquerque. Tiwa is still spoken today at the pueblos of Isleta, Sandia, Taos and Picuris.

Coronado's soldiers took over the town of Coofor and made its people leave. The army's horses and mules ate the corn stalks the Tiguex needed to burn for heat in winter. Coronado then demanded about 300 or more pieces of cloth from the towns. His men did not give the towns time to decide. People had to take off their own cloaks and hand them over, and soldiers took better cloaks off any Tiguex man they saw wearing one.

Then a Spanish soldier went into a Tiguex house and raped the wife of the man who lived there, or tried to. Pedro de Castañeda, a soldier on the expedition who later wrote its history, says so. The husband and the town's leaders complained to Coronado. The husband could not pick out the soldier in a line of men, but he knew the soldier's horse. The owner of the horse denied it, and the husband got no justice. Castañeda knew the soldier's name and chose not to write it down.

The next day Tiguex men killed a Native man who guarded the army's horses and drove off many of the horses. Coronado sent Don García López de Cárdenas to surround the town where the soldier had raped the woman, or tried to. After a day and a night, Cárdenas's men and their Native allies from Mexico smoked the defenders out of the cellars. The defenders made the sign of the cross to ask for peace. Two Spanish soldiers on the roof, Pablo de Melgosa and Diego López, made the sign back. The men put down their weapons, and the Spanish promised them they would not be punished.

Coronado had ordered Cárdenas not to take anyone alive and to make an example of them to frighten the other towns. Cárdenas ordered 200 stakes set up to burn the prisoners alive. Castañeda wrote that when the prisoners saw the Spanish tying men to the stakes and starting to burn them, about 100 men in a tent fought back. Spanish foot soldiers and horsemen killed them. Castañeda gives no exact count of the people burned. A history from the New Mexico Office of the State Historian names the town the Spanish took as Arenal, across the river from Coofor, and states that they killed dozens of captives there. At his trial, Cárdenas told a different story. He said some soldiers had speared men in a tent without his order.

The fighting went on for about three months. The Tiguex shut themselves inside their houses of several stories and shot arrows from the roofs and through slots in the walls. The Spanish charged on horseback and set fires to force people out. By the count on a National Park Service page, the Spanish killed hundreds of Tiguex, and the Tiguex killed a smaller number of Spanish and Mexican soldiers. The Spanish burned villages and enslaved Tiguex women and children.

Spanish officials later put Cárdenas on trial. A royal prosecutor, the lawyer who brought the case for the king, charged him with killings, with rapes committed by his men, with setting dogs on Native people, and with taking food and clothing. In 1549 the judges of the Council of the Indies, the king's council for Spain's colonies, found him guilty. They looked at the case again in 1551 and said again that he was guilty. They first ordered him to serve 30 months as a soldier at Oran, in North Africa, and to pay 800 ducats, which were Spanish coins. He asked for a lighter punishment and got one. He had to serve 12 months as a soldier in Spain, pay 200 ducats, and stay out of the Americas for 10 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Caroline and Matanzas, 1565" -->
Europeans also fought each other here. In 1564, about 200 French colonists built Fort Caroline near present-day Jacksonville, Florida. They were Huguenots, which means French Protestants. Protestants were Christians who had broken away from the Catholic Church, the church led by the pope in Rome. The land belonged to the Timucua, who had lived in northern Florida for at least 1,000 years. King Philip II of Spain claimed Florida, and he was Catholic.

The Spanish founded St. Augustine, farther south, on September 8, 1565. On September 20 their commander, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, marched 500 soldiers overland and attacked Fort Caroline at dawn. His men killed most of the settlers they found. In his own letter to the king, Menéndez counted 132 people at the fort whose throats his men cut. He counted 10 more killed the next day. He spared about 50 women and children and sent them by ship to Havana, in Cuba.

A hurricane had already wrecked the French ships. About 127 French survivors came ashore at an inlet south of St. Augustine with no food and no weapons, and they surrendered to Menéndez. He told them to give up their Protestant faith. They refused. His soldiers killed 111 of them. Menéndez spared 16 men. A few said they were Catholic. Some were sailors from Brittany who had been forced onto the ships. Four were craftsmen the Spanish settlers at St. Augustine needed. Menéndez wrote to King Philip on October 15, 1565, "I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife."

A second group of French survivors came to the same inlet with their commander, Jean Ribault. They surrendered, refused the same demand, and Menéndez's men killed 134 of them. The Florida Museum of Natural History dates the first killing to September 29, 1565. It dates the second to October 11, and the National Park Service dates it to October 12. In his letter Menéndez gave his reason: the French "spread the Lutheran sect," meaning Protestant teaching, in land the king of Spain claimed. Spanish speakers have called the inlet Matanzas ever since. The word means "slaughters."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The forts that were destroyed, 1568 and 1586" -->
Between 1566 and 1568, Captain Juan Pardo built a chain of small Spanish forts inland. He built Fort San Juan in January 1567 at the Native town of Joara, in the upper Catawba Valley of North Carolina. Thirty soldiers held it. In all, Pardo left about 120 men in forts across the Carolinas and east Tennessee.

The soldiers demanded more food than the towns around them could give, and they attacked the people they lived among. By May 1568, Native people had attacked every one of the forts. They destroyed them all and killed nearly every soldier. Archaeologists have found Fort San Juan. It is the Berry site in Burke County, North Carolina.

In 1586, English sailors under Francis Drake burned St. Augustine. Drake was a privateer, a sea captain allowed by his government to attack Spanish ships and towns. He arrived with 21 ships between May 28 and 30, 1586. The town had about 150 soldiers. Drake's men burned the town and its wooden fort and took about 2,000 pounds in money from the treasury.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s, English colonists on this coast fought wars against the Powhatan in Virginia, the Pequot in Connecticut, and Metacom's Wampanoag and their allies in New England. In every colonial war of this time, Native allies fought beside the colonists. In 1637, for example, Mohegan and Narragansett fighters joined the English against the Pequot.

The English colonies had no army. They had a militia instead. A militia is made up of ordinary men, mostly farmers. They leave their work to fight when their leaders call them, and they go home when the fighting ends. On December 13, 1636, the lawmakers of Massachusetts Bay, meeting as the General Court, ordered every healthy man between 16 and 60 to join the militia. Ministers and judges did not have to. The lawmakers organized the men into three regiments, large units of soldiers. Leaders of today's Army National Guard date its beginning to that order.

Colonial soldiers attacked the Pequot town at Mystic before dawn in 1637. In Virginia, from 1622 on, English soldiers burned Powhatan corn in the fields and seized the corn people had stored.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia, 1622 to 1646" -->
The English colony in Virginia stood on the land of the Powhatan, a group of nations led by one paramount chief, or head chief. In 1622 the Powhatan leader Opechancanough judged that the English would keep taking more Powhatan land unless they were stopped.

On March 22, 1622, he sent fighters to the English plantations, or large farms, along the James River in one planned attack. The men came to houses and fields as if to trade or share a meal, then attacked. They killed as many as 347 colonists, out of about 1,200 in the colony. A Powhatan man who worked for a settler named Richard Pace warned him. Pace warned Jamestown in time. The English accounts of the warning do not give the Powhatan man's name.

In May 1623, Opechancanough agreed to meet English leaders on the Pamunkey River to make peace. Captain William Tucker led the English party of about 12 men. The English offered poisoned drinks to toast the agreement. When the Powhatan leaders fell sick, the English shot them. Some of the English cut off scalps.

The English accounts of the dead do not agree. On June 9, 1623, a settler named Robert Bennett wrote to his brother that "some tooe hundred" were thought to have been poisoned, and that the English killed about 50 more. In 1624, Governor Francis Wyatt and his council answered that no one had been poisoned at a feast and that only nine were shot. Their own report says the men took poison along, to be used if nothing else worked. Neither English account names the poison.

From 1622 on, the English also went after the Powhatan food supply. In their own words, they waited "till their corne was ripe," then raided through the fall and winter to "surprize their corne," meaning to seize it. They agreed to pauses in the fighting so both sides could plant, then attacked again after the harvest. In July 1624, English soldiers burned fields with enough corn, by their own count, to feed 4,000 men for a year. The war lasted until 1632.

Opechancanough attacked again on April 18, 1644, and killed about 400 colonists. In 1646, Virginia's governor, Sir William Berkeley, led soldiers who captured him and took him to Jamestown. Within two weeks, one of his English guards shot him dead in his cell. Encyclopedia Virginia's history of Opechancanough does not name the guard.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pequot War, 1636 to 1638" -->
The Pequot War grew out of two killings of English traders. Pequot men killed the trader John Stone and his crew on the Connecticut River in 1634. In July 1636, Manisses men from Block Island killed the trader John Oldham. The Manisses were a different nation from the Pequot. In August 1636, John Endecott of Massachusetts Bay led 90 soldiers to Block Island and burned the villages there. Then his men sailed to Pequot land and burned a Pequot village too. On April 23, 1637, Pequot fighters attacked Wethersfield, Connecticut, killed nine men and women, and took two girls captive.

On May 26, 1637, Captain John Mason of Connecticut and Captain John Underhill of Massachusetts Bay reached the Pequot town at Mystic before dawn. Mohegan and Narragansett fighters came with them. The English set fire to the palisade and the houses inside it. They shot the people who tried to get out.

Historians at the Mashantucket Pequot Museum count more than 400 Pequot killed in one hour, and other counts go as high as 700. Most of the Pequot fighting men were away with their sachem, or leader, Sassacus. So most of the dead were old men, women and children. The English lost two dead and twenty wounded.

In July 1637 the English fought the surviving Pequot in a swamp near present-day Fairfield, Connecticut, for about a day and a night. Sassacus escaped west, and Mohawk fighters killed him later that month. The Pequot museum's history of the war states that some captive Pequot women were killed after the English argued over who would get them. The museum's history does not name who killed them. The English sold other captives into slavery in New England and the Caribbean.

John Winthrop was the governor of Massachusetts Bay. On July 13, 1637, he wrote in his journal that the colony's leaders had sent 15 Pequot boys and 2 women away as slaves. A ship captain, William Peirce, carried them. Massachusetts leaders held a day of thanksgiving on October 12, 1637, for defeating the Pequot and bringing the soldiers home safe. In February 1638 Peirce came back on the ship *Desire* with cotton, tobacco and enslaved Africans.

In 1638, leaders of the Connecticut colony at Hartford and Mohegan and Narragansett leaders signed the Treaty of Hartford, a written agreement between them. Under it, they divided the surviving Pequot between the Mohegan and the Narragansett. The treaty's words barred the Pequot from their homeland and forbade them ever to call themselves Pequot again. Pequot survivors held together. By 1651, Robin Cassacinamon had led some of them back to 500 acres of their land. In 1666, leaders of the Connecticut colony set aside a reservation for the Pequot at Mashantucket. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation to live on. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation is in Connecticut today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="King Philip's War, 1675 to 1676" -->
King Philip's War was fought in New England in 1675 and 1676. The English called the Wampanoag sachem Metacom by the name King Philip. A National Park Service article calls it one of the deadliest wars in American history, when the number killed is compared with how many people lived in New England. One estimate, published by HistoryNet, counts about 9,000 people killed out of about 80,000 in New England. About two out of every three of the dead were Native people, and many of them died of starvation.

The war started with a death and a trial. In January 1675, John Sassamon, a Christian Native man, was found dead under the ice of a pond. Days before, by the accounts of the time, he had warned Plymouth's governor, Josiah Winslow, that Metacom's men were planning an attack. On June 1, 1675, English judges at Plymouth tried three Wampanoag men for his murder before an all-English jury. Six Native men sat with the jury to advise it. On June 8, 1675, Plymouth's officials hanged two of them, Tobias and Mattashunannamo. They held back the third, Wampapaquan, for a month and then shot him to death. The fighting began that June at the English town of Swansea.

On December 19, 1675, about 1,000 English soldiers attacked a fortified Narragansett winter village in a frozen swamp in Rhode Island. About 1,000 Narragansett men, women and children were living there. Governor Josiah Winslow of Plymouth, Major Samuel Appleton of Massachusetts and Major Robert Treat of Connecticut led the soldiers of their colonies. The soldiers took the fort and burned every house in it. The National Park Service counts 300 Narragansett men and almost 400 women and children killed. James Oliver, an English officer, wrote that the English lost about 68 dead and 150 wounded.

Native fighters struck back across New England. By one count, they attacked 52 of the 90 English towns and burned 17 of them to the ground. In late March 1676, Narragansett fighters came to Providence. Roger Williams of Providence tried to bargain with them, and they turned him down and burned the town.

On August 12, 1676, a Native man fighting with the colonists shot Metacom dead at Mount Hope. The colonists put his head on display at Plymouth. They sold his wife and his nine-year-old son into slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deer Island and the captives" -->
Some Native people in Massachusetts were Christians and allies of the English. In the autumn of 1675, Massachusetts Bay's leaders did not trust them, so they moved them to Deer Island in Boston Harbor. The first were Nipmuc people from the town that is now South Natick. Between 500 and 1,100 people were held there, most of them women and children. Through the freezing winter they had little shelter and too little food, and many starved.

The colonists let the survivors go in May 1676. A National Park Service page states that very few survived. Mass Humanities, a history group, states that about half of some 900 people held there died of hunger and cold. Some people were smuggled off the island and sold as slaves in the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean, or in Tangier, in North Africa. The National Park Service page gives no number and does not name the men who sold them. Massachusetts colonists also executed about 45 Native people in public on Boston Common.

Governor Josiah Winslow of Plymouth and Governor John Leverett of Massachusetts Bay signed papers that allowed Native men, women and children to be sold into slavery for life. A colonial record notes one sale: "that day wee sold Capt Davenport 47 Indians young & old for 80 pd," meaning 80 pounds for 47 people. Ships carried captives to Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier in North Africa. Descendants of captives sent from Rhode Island to Bermuda have since reconnected with Native nations in New England and Canada.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Beaver Wars and the Pueblo Revolt" -->
Other wars of the 1600s had no English colonists in them. In the Beaver Wars, fighters from the Haudenosaunee nations, also called the Iroquois, raided villages of the Wendat, also called the Huron, for years, to control the trade in furs. French and Dutch traders armed their Native trading partners with guns, and the guns made the raids deadlier. Between 1642 and 1649, the raids broke the Wendat apart. Haudenosaunee fighters destroyed the Wendat homeland, Wendake, in 1649 and 1650. They killed many Wendat and adopted many others into Haudenosaunee nations. About 500 Wendat moved east to the Quebec City region.

On August 10, 1680, people from the Pueblo towns of the Rio Grande rose together against Spanish rule in New Mexico. They surrounded Santa Fe, took it, and drove the Spanish out. They killed 401 settlers and 21 Franciscan friars, who were Catholic priests, and let most other Spanish people leave. The Pueblos governed themselves for 12 years. Diego de Vargas began the Spanish return in 1692, and armed resistance went on for years after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, European rulers fought two wars that were also fought in North America. Here they are called Queen Anne's War and King George's War. Queen Anne's War, from 1702 to 1713, was the American part of a European war over who would become king of Spain. King George's War, from 1744 to 1748, was the American part of a war over who would rule Austria. Colonial militia and Native fighters did most of the fighting here, and the Native fighters had their own reasons.

The leaders of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, an alliance of Native nations with the biggest fighting force in the northeast, chose to stay out of these wars. At Montreal on August 4, 1701, about 1,300 representatives from more than 30 Native nations made peace with New France, the French colony in Canada. There the Haudenosaunee leaders promised to take neither side between France and Britain. They worked to keep that promise for about fifty years. French and British commanders both had to plan around it.

In the north, the common act of war was the winter raid. Fighters came at a sleeping town before dawn, killed some of the people and took others captive. Then they marched the captives hundreds of miles to Canada on foot. A captive might be adopted into a Native nation, sold back to family for money, or killed on the trail for not keeping up.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deerfield, February 29, 1704" -->
Before dawn on February 29, 1704, about 250 fighters attacked Deerfield, a town in western Massachusetts. Their commander was Jean-Baptiste Hertel de Rouville, a French lieutenant, or junior officer, born in Canada. He led 47 French Canadian soldiers and about 200 Abenaki, Kanien'kehaka and Wendat fighters. Kanien'kehaka is the Mohawk people's name for themselves. The historians of Deerfield's museum write that these nations fought to keep English settlers out of their homelands.

About 300 people lived in Deerfield. More than half were killed or taken captive. Deerfield's history museum, the Pocumtuck Valley Memorial Association, which keeps the town's old records, lists 48 killed and 111 captured. A Library of Congress history page lists 57 killed and 112 captured.

In the house of the town's minister, John Williams, the attackers killed his six-week-old daughter Jerusha and his six-year-old son John. They also killed Parthena, a Black woman Williams had bought and held as a slave. Her husband Frank, also enslaved by Williams, was taken captive.

The attackers drove the captives across the frozen Deerfield River and marched them 300 miles to New France in the middle of winter. Each captive belonged to the man who had taken them, and those men killed captives who could not keep up. Williams wrote that on one day they killed two women "so faint they could not travel." Mary Brooks, a young woman, told Williams that her falls on the ice had caused a miscarriage. That means she had lost the baby she was carrying. She said she was too weak to walk far and knew they would kill her. They killed her that day. Williams did not record the names of the men who killed them. Of the more than 100 captives, only about half ever came back to New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### John Williams

> **Who:** Deerfield's minister, captured with five of his children in the 1704 raid. He came home and wrote the best-known captivity story of colonial America.
> **When and where:** Born in Roxbury, Massachusetts, December 16, 1664. Minister at Deerfield from 1686. Captured February 29, 1704. Back in New England late in 1706. Died 1729.

John Williams finished his studies at Harvard College in 1683 and became Deerfield's minister in 1686. On February 29, 1704, the attackers took him, his wife Eunice Mather Williams and five of their children.

Soon after the march began, at the Green River in what is now Greenfield, Massachusetts, Eunice Mather Williams fell while crossing and went under the icy water. She walked on a short way. At the foot of a mountain, the man who had taken her captive killed her with one blow of a hatchet. Williams did not record his name.

Williams and four of his children came home. His daughter Eunice did not. He returned late in 1706 and preached about his captivity in Boston on December 5 that year. In 1707 he went back to his church in Deerfield. His book about the raid and the march, *The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion*, was printed in seven editions before 1795.

### Eunice Kanenstenhawi Williams

> **Who:** John Williams's daughter, taken from Deerfield at seven. She became a member of the Kanien'kehaka nation at Kahnawake and chose to stay there all her life.
> **When and where:** Born in Deerfield, September 17, 1696. Taken February 29, 1704. Died at Kahnawake, near Montreal, in 1785, aged 89.

Eunice Williams was seven when the attackers carried her out of Deerfield. At Kahnawake, a Kanien'kehaka town near Montreal, a family adopted her in a formal ceremony. She was first named A'ongote, which means "she was taken and placed." Later she was named Kanenstenhawi, "she brings in the corn," and was accepted as a full member of the nation. She was also baptized a Catholic, with the name Marguerite. Baptism is a Christian ceremony with water that makes a person a member of a church.

Her father and English officials tried again and again to bring her back. She refused. At 16 she married François-Xavier Arosen, a man of her town. They had at least three children. Two daughters, Catherine and Marie, were born in 1736 and 1739, and a son, John. The Pocumtuck Valley Memorial Association's history says John seems to have died at Lake George, in New York.

She visited her Williams brothers and sisters in New England four times, in 1740, 1741, 1743 and 1761. At her brother Stephen's house in Longmeadow, Massachusetts, she slept outside in the orchard instead of inside the English house. Each time, she went home to Kahnawake. New Englanders called her the "unredeemed captive," the one they never bought back.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuscarora War, 1711 to 1713" -->
The Tuscarora War was fought in North Carolina. The historians Elizabeth A. Fenn, Peter H. Wood and others, in the account NCpedia publishes, name three Tuscarora complaints. The Tuscarora objected to the way white traders dealt with them and to the enslaving of Native people. Most of all, they objected to settlers taking their land. In 1710, English settlers founded the town of New Bern near the Tuscarora.

On September 22, 1711, the Tuscarora began a series of planned attacks on the settlers. Soldiers from South Carolina, fighting with Native allies, defeated them by March 1713. By then about 1,400 Tuscarora were dead, and the winners had captured and enslaved about 1,000.

About 1,500 surviving Tuscarora walked north. In 1722 they became the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Yamasee War, 1715 to 1717" -->
The Yamasee War in South Carolina began over debt and the slave trade in Native people. Yamasee towns stood along the Savannah River and around Port Royal. Traders from Charleston let the Yamasee take goods and pay later, hoping to take Yamasee land when the debts could not be paid. By 1715, paying off the debt would have taken at least two years of work from every Yamasee man. The traders also enslaved Native people and whipped them, and settlers were moving onto Yamasee land. The Yamasee themselves had raided Apalachee towns in Florida and sold the people they captured to Carolina merchants as slaves.

Thomas Nairne was the colony's agent for dealing with Native nations. On April 14, 1715, he and two other colonists, William Bray and Samuel Warner, met Yamasee leaders at Pocotaligo Town. They hoped to stop the fighting before it started. The next morning, Good Friday, Yamasee men killed Bray and Warner. They held Nairne for several days and tortured him to death by sticking burning splinters of wood into his skin. Across the South, Creek, Choctaw, Apalachee and other men killed the traders who worked among them. By June 1715, about nine in every ten of those traders were dead.

The first Yamasee attacks, on plantations near Port Royal, killed about 100 colonists. Over the whole war, about 400 settlers were killed, and colonists had to leave half the farmland in the colony. The South Carolina Encyclopedia and NCpedia give no count of the Yamasee dead or the other Native dead.

South Carolina's governor, Charles Craven, called up every white man and armed some enslaved Black men as well. About 300 families who owned plantations escaped by ship. Craven's forces beat the Yamasee at Port Royal and at the Salkehatchie River and drove them south of the Savannah River. By the spring of 1716, most Yamasee had moved to Spanish Florida. In 1716 Carolina officials talked the Lower Cherokee into fighting the Creek. That began a war between the Cherokee and the Creek that lasted about forty years. Carolina officials ended their own war by 1718.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Louisbourg, 1745" -->
King George's War started in this part of the world in 1744, after a group of English scalp hunters killed or wounded several Penobscot people. Scalp hunters were men who hunted Native people for their scalps. In 1745, New England soldiers laid siege to Louisbourg, a French fortress on Cape Breton Island, in what is now Canada. A siege is an attack in which an army surrounds a fort or town and cuts it off until it gives up.

Massachusetts leaders raised an army of their own colonists to take it. The soldiers sailed from Boston on March 24, 1745. On April 30 nearly 2,000 of them landed near the fortress. Colonel William Pepperrell commanded them. Commodore Peter Warren, a British navy commander, waited offshore with a group of warships. Up to about 4,300 New Englanders served there over the whole siege, but only about half that many at any one time. The acting French governor, Louis Du Pont Duchambon, asked on June 15 what conditions he would get if he surrendered. He and his soldiers surrendered the fortress on June 17, 1745.

Far more New Englanders died holding Louisbourg than died taking it. During the siege, about 100 were killed by French fire and 30 died of illness. Then the soldiers spent the winter in wrecked, dirty buildings, and sickness spread. The counts of the dead do not agree. A HistoryNet account counts 561 burials between late November and mid-February. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography gives 1,200 to 2,000 deaths over the winter and spring.

In 1748, British officials gave Louisbourg back to France in the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, the agreement that ended the war. In return French officials gave back Madras, a city in India they had taken from Britain.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The French and Indian War began in 1754. For most of the next forty years, soldiers from the colonies and then from the new United States were at war. In the treaty that ended that first war, in 1763, French officials signed over Canada and all French land east of the Mississippi River to Britain. The Revolution lasted from 1775 to 1783 and ended with the thirteen colonies independent. From 1785 to 1795, US soldiers fought a group of Native nations north of the Ohio River.

In the Revolution, sickness killed more American soldiers than enemy weapons did. Historians at the American Battlefield Trust estimate that about 6,800 Americans were killed in battle in the Revolution. At least 17,000 more died of disease, and 8,000 to 12,000 of those were prisoners of war. The Defense Department's official list shows 4,435 American battle deaths. Its own note says its records from before World War I are often incomplete.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The French and Indian War, 1754 to 1763" -->
In the French and Indian War, British and colonial soldiers fought French soldiers and the Native nations allied with France. In 1754, French soldiers forced a young Virginia colonel, George Washington, to surrender at Fort Necessity in western Pennsylvania.

The next summer, the British general Edward Braddock marched about 1,450 men toward Fort Duquesne, the French fort where Pittsburgh stands now. On July 9, 1755, about 900 French and Canadian soldiers and Ottawa, Shawnee, Lenape and Mingo fighters caught his column, a long line of marching soldiers, near the Monongahela River. The Native fighters shot from the woods on both sides of the road. They and the French killed 456 of Braddock's men and wounded 422. The French side lost 23 killed. Braddock was badly wounded, and he died on the night of July 13.

At Fort William Henry, on Lake George in New York, the French general Montcalm surrounded the British with about 8,000 men, nearly 2,000 of them Native fighters. On August 9, 1757, the British commander, Lieutenant Colonel George Munro, surrendered. The terms let his men march away with their muskets but no ammunition. The next day, Native fighters allied with Montcalm killed and scalped sick and wounded men left in the fort. Then they attacked the column of British soldiers, and the women and children with it, on the road. The counts of the dead run from several dozen up to about 185 men, women and children. Early reports said 1,500, but a HistoryNet account finds it unlikely that more than 200 were killed. Montcalm got 500 captives released, and the Native fighters kept about 200 more.

On September 13, 1759, British soldiers took Quebec after a battle outside the city that lasted about twenty minutes. The British general James Wolfe was shot and died on the field. Montcalm was hit by a musket ball below the ribs and died the next morning.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Pitt and the smallpox blankets, 1763" -->
In 1763, the year the war with France ended, Native nations of the Ohio country went to war against the British in what is called Pontiac's War. On June 22, 1763, Shawnee, Lenape, Mingo and Seneca fighters surrounded Fort Pitt, the British fort at present-day Pittsburgh. Guyasuta was one of their leaders. Captain Simeon Ecuyer commanded the fort, with the trader and militia captain William Trent.

Smallpox is a disease that spreads from person to person and killed about one in three people who caught it. That spring the fort's officers had set up a hospital for soldiers sick with it. On June 24, two Lenape men, one named Turtleheart, came to the fort to warn the soldiers to leave. Trent wrote in his journal, "we gave them two Blankets and an Handkerchief out of the Small Pox Hospital. I hope it will have the desired effect." The officers meant to give the Lenape smallpox.

Two weeks later, the British commander in North America, General Jeffery Amherst, suggested the same thing in a letter to Colonel Henry Bouquet. "Could it not be contrived to Send the Small Pox among those Disaffected Tribes of Indians?" he wrote on July 8. Bouquet answered that he would try to spread it "by means of Blankets." On July 16 Amherst told him to try it, and to try "Every other method that can serve to Extirpate this Execreble Race." Contrived means arranged. Disaffected means unfriendly to the British. Execreble, usually spelled execrable, means hateful, and extirpate means to wipe out completely. Amherst also told Bouquet to take no Native prisoners.

Smallpox did spread among the Ohio nations in 1763 and 1764. No one can tell whether the blankets caused it. The disease was already in the area that spring, and it spreads mostly through the air people breathe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution, from Lexington to Yorktown" -->
The Revolution was fought between British soldiers and colonists who wanted independence. The shooting started at Lexington, Massachusetts, at about 5 in the morning on April 19, 1775. British soldiers killed eight militiamen on the town green and wounded ten. The fighting went on all day along 16 miles of road back to Boston. By night, 73 British soldiers and 49 militiamen were dead.

On June 17, 1775, British soldiers attacked the colonists' earth walls on Breed's Hill, in the battle named for nearby Bunker Hill. They charged three times in two hours. On the third charge they climbed the walls and fought with bayonets, the long blades fixed to the ends of their muskets. The colonists had run out of ammunition and fell back. British casualties, meaning the killed and wounded together, came to 1,054. The colonists lost about 450.

That June, the delegates, or representatives, from the thirteen colonies who met as the Continental Congress chose Washington to command a new Continental Army. About 231,000 men served in it during the war, but never more than 48,000 at one time. About 7,000 of them were Black.

Under the rules of the time, the delegates in Congress had no power to draft anyone. To draft means to order a person into the army by law. In 1778, the delegates asked each state to draft men from its militia for one year in the Continental Army. Officials in some states drafted more men than others, and the army stayed short of men. A drafted man could pay someone else to go in his place. Lawmakers in the colonies and the states wrote laws that kept enslaved men out of the militia altogether. Pennsylvania lawmakers made Quakers, whose religion forbids fighting, pay a fine if they would not join the volunteer militia companies called Associations. The fine was set to match the value of the time a man would have spent drilling, which means practicing marching and handling weapons with his company. Officials took property from Quakers who refused to pay.

At Saratoga, New York, on October 17, 1777, the British general John Burgoyne and his army surrendered 5,895 British and German soldiers to General Horatio Gates. In 1781, Washington and the French general Rochambeau led about 17,000 American and French soldiers to Yorktown, Virginia. There they surrounded the British general Charles Cornwallis. A French fleet held the sea behind him. About 8,000 British soldiers surrendered there on October 19, 1781.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Smallpox and Valley Forge" -->
On February 6, 1777, Washington ordered his whole army inoculated against smallpox. To inoculate a soldier, a doctor cut open a sore on a person sick with smallpox, then pushed the same blade under the healthy soldier's skin. The soldier then had a milder case of smallpox and could never catch it again.

More than 12,000 soldiers, with about 400 women and children, marched into winter camp at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, on December 19, 1777. They built about 1,500 log huts, each 14 feet by 16 feet. Four days later, Washington wrote to Congress that 2,898 of his men were "unfit for duty, because they are barefoot and otherwise naked."

By June 1778, nearly 2,000 people in the camp had died. Most died of influenza (the flu), typhus, typhoid or dysentery, and two out of three of those deaths came in March, April and May. Typhus is a fever spread by body lice. Typhoid and dysentery spread through dirty water and food. Dysentery is an infection of the gut that causes bloody diarrhea, and a person can lose so much water that they die.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The prison ships of New York" -->
The British held New York City from 1776 to the end of the war. Their officers kept captured American soldiers and sailors in prisons in the city and on ships in the harbor.

The British admiral in command at New York was Richard Howe. The ship called the *Jersey* held about 1,200 prisoners. The British officers who ran the ships gave the prisoners too little food and left the sick without care. By the National Park Service's account, smallpox, hunger and that neglect killed thousands. One survivor wrote that the ship's biscuits were eaten through by weevils, which are small beetles. The bread was sour and often moldy, and the meat was rotten with age. He also wrote that every morning a boat from each of the hospital ships carried the dead ashore, where the bodies "were all tumbled together into a hole dug for the purpose."

On land, the British held more than 4,500 Americans in the Sugar House, a sugar warehouse five stories tall. Another prison, the Provost, was run by Captain William Cunningham. His prisoners were packed so tightly that at night they could turn over only if they all turned at once. In January 1777, leaders of the Congress asked Washington to protest to Howe. Washington wrote to Howe about prisoners the British had sent back from New York. He said their "miserable emaciated Countenances," meaning faces so thin from hunger that the bones showed, proved what they said.

The counts of the dead do not agree. A National Park Service page gives more than 11,500 dead on the ships and in the prisons around New York. Historians at the American Battlefield Trust estimate 8,000 to 12,000 deaths among all American prisoners in the whole war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gnadenhutten, March 8, 1782" -->
Gnadenhutten was a town of Lenape and Mohican Christians in the Tuscarawas Valley, in what is now Ohio. They belonged to a Christian church called the Moravian church, and they refused to fight for either side in the Revolution. In February 1782, more than 150 of them went back to their old towns to gather the corn they had left in the fields.

About 160 Pennsylvania militiamen marched there under David Williamson, the officer they had chosen to command them. They blamed the Moravian Lenape for raids on Pennsylvania settlements, which the Lenape denied. The militiamen promised to take the people to Fort Pitt for safety and collected their weapons. Then they held a vote on whether to kill them. Only 16 or 18 of the militiamen voted against it.

The prisoners spent the night praying and singing hymns, with the men in one building and the women and children in another. On the morning of March 8, 1782, the militiamen tied them up. Taking turns, they struck each person on the head with a heavy hammer called a mallet, and then they scalped them. They killed 96 people. Eric Sterner, writing in the *Journal of the American Revolution*, counts 62 adults and 34 children. Another count gives 28 men, 29 women and 39 children.

A militiaman named Obadiah Holmes Jr., who opposed the killing, said another militiaman, Nathan Rollins, killed 19 people himself. Two boys lived. One of them survived being scalped. The militiamen burned the buildings with the bodies inside, burned the other Moravian towns, and carried off goods that needed 80 horses to move. People talked of bringing the killers to court, but no one charged any of the militiamen with a crime. Later, the Moravian missionary John Heckewelder gathered the bones and buried them at the town. A missionary is a person sent to spread a religion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### George Washington

> **Who:** A Virginia officer in the French and Indian War who went on to command the Continental Army for the whole of the Revolution.
> **When and where:** Fort Necessity, 1754. The Monongahela, July 9, 1755. Commander in chief from June 1775. Resigned at Annapolis, Maryland, December 23, 1783.
> **Movie:** *Washington* (History channel, 2020), a documentary series in three parts, with acted scenes.

As a young officer, George Washington was on the losing side twice in two years. At Fort Necessity in 1754, he surrendered to the French. He promised that he and his men would go back to Virginia and stay out of the region for a year.

A year later he rode with Braddock as a volunteer aide, a helper to the general. After the battle at the Monongahela, he wrote to his brother, "I had 4 Bullets through my Coat, and two Horses shot under" him.

In June 1775 the delegates of the Continental Congress chose him to command their army, and he held the command until the war ended. On December 23, 1783, he handed his commission, the paper that made him commander, back to Congress at Annapolis. The next day he left for his home at Mount Vernon.

### Joseph Plumb Martin

> **Who:** A Connecticut teenager who joined the army at 15, served as a private and then a sergeant, and later wrote down what it was like.
> **When and where:** Born in Becket, Massachusetts, November 21, 1760. Joined the army in June 1776 and was still serving at Yorktown in 1781. Died in Prospect, Maine, May 2, 1850, aged 89.

Joseph Plumb Martin was 15 when he joined the Connecticut troops in June 1776. He fought on Long Island that August, and later at White Plains, Germantown and Monmouth. At Valley Forge he went out with groups of soldiers searching the countryside for food for the army.

On the march into Valley Forge, he made himself shoes from a piece of raw cowhide. His other choice, he wrote, was to go barefoot, as hundreds of other men did, "till they might be tracked by their blood upon the rough frozen ground." A National Park Service article points out that his words do not make clear whether he saw the bleeding feet himself or repeated a story people told after the war.

At Yorktown he was a sergeant in the sappers and miners, soldiers who dug the trenches that let the American and French armies surround Cornwallis. He took part in the attack on Redoubt No. 10, a small British fort of earth.

After the war he farmed in Prospect, Maine, married Lucy Clewley, and raised five children. He lost a fight over his land with Henry Knox, a former general. By 1818, when officials approved him for a pension of $96 a year, he owned almost nothing. A pension is money paid every year to a former soldier. In 1830, at 70, he published his memoir, a book about his own life. It was printed again in 1962 as *Private Yankee Doodle*.

### Deborah Sampson

> **Who:** A Massachusetts woman who joined the army as a man named Robert Shurtleff, fought in the light infantry, was wounded, and later won a soldier's pension.
> **When and where:** Born in Plympton, Massachusetts, December 17, 1760. Enlisted May 1782. Discharged October 1783. Died 1827.

At 10, Deborah Sampson was bound out as an indentured servant to a farmer's family in Middleborough. An indentured servant owed a family a set number of years of unpaid work. In May 1782, at 21, she joined the army as a man in the 4th Massachusetts Regiment under the name Robert Shurtleff. She served 17 months near West Point, New York, in a light infantry company, a unit of lightly armed foot soldiers.

She was wounded in combat. The accounts differ on how, where on her body, and when. The Paul Revere House museum says it was in a fight with a Loyalist raiding party. Loyalists were colonists who fought for the British king. The National Women's History Museum describes a sword cut to her forehead and a musket ball in her left thigh. The story that she cut a musket ball out of her own thigh, so no army surgeon would find out she was a woman, comes from a book Herman Mann printed about her in 1797. The New England Historical Society calls Mann's book "a poor history," because he added a great deal that he imagined. The historian Alfred F. Young found accounts that put the wound in her shoulder or her chest.

A doctor treating her for a fever in Philadelphia found out she was a woman. General Henry Knox gave her an honorable discharge in October 1783. That means she left the army with a record that she had served well. In 1785 she married Benjamin Gannett of Sharon, Massachusetts, and they had three children.

In 1804, Paul Revere visited her at Sharon and wrote to her congressman to support her claim for a soldier's pension. She went on the federal pension list in 1805. In 1806 she wrote to Revere asking to borrow ten dollars. She died in 1827. In 1837, members of Congress voted to pay her husband, Benjamin Gannett, who had outlived her, for her service. He died before the money reached him.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Clair and Fallen Timbers, 1791 to 1794" -->
North of the Ohio River, several Native nations joined in a confederacy, an alliance of nations, and fought US soldiers from 1785 to 1795. In 1791, General Arthur St. Clair led about 2,000 men north from the Ohio River. They were a mix of militia, volunteers signed up for six months, and regular soldiers. On the way, volunteers whose six months were up went home. About 60 militiamen ran off. St. Clair sent 300 regular soldiers back to guard his food supplies. About 1,400 men were left on November 3.

The next day, on the Wabash River, more than 1,000 fighters under the Miami leader Little Turtle attacked St. Clair's camp. More than 900 of the army's soldiers, and the women and children traveling with them, were killed or wounded. That count comes from a US Army history article, and other counts split the dead and wounded differently. Most were left where they fell.

Members of Congress held their first special investigation ever into what went wrong, and they cleared St. Clair. President Washington then put General Anthony Wayne in charge of a new force called the Legion of the United States. Its soldiers were drilled, meaning they trained by practicing marching and fighting together over and over. Wayne's soldiers beat the Native confederacy at Fallen Timbers, in what is now Ohio, on August 20, 1794. The fight took less than an hour and a half. US Army historians trace today's regular army back to the Legion.
