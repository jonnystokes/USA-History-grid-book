## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 13 minutes**

US soldiers went to war with Britain and with Mexico between 1800 and 1850, and they fought Native nations again and again on the nations' own land, from the Creek in Alabama to the Sauk in Illinois and the Seminole in Florida.

The army's officers now trained at a school of their own. President Thomas Jefferson signed the law that set up the US Military Academy at West Point, New York, on March 16, 1802. US soldiers fought the British from 1812 to 1815 and Mexico from 1846 to 1848, and they marched into Mexico City in September 1847.

**Disease again.** In the war with Mexico and in the Second Seminole War, disease killed most of the soldiers who died. For the war with Mexico, Defense Department records list 1,733 American soldiers killed in battle and 11,550 dead of other causes. About seven of every eight American dead died of something other than enemy fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Madison asked for war in 1812" -->
President James Madison asked Congress for war with Britain on June 1, 1812, and listed three complaints. The first was impressment: British navy officers stopped American ships, took off sailors they said were British, and forced them to serve on British warships. The second was British orders that blocked trade by countries outside the war, including the United States. The third was that British officials encouraged Native nations to attack American settlers in the West.

The members of the House voted for war 79 to 49, and the senators voted 19 to 13, the closest votes on any declaration of war in US history. Madison signed the declaration on June 18, 1812.

**Washington burns.** British soldiers beat the American defenders at Bladensburg, Maryland, on August 24, 1814, and marched into Washington. Their commanders, Major General Robert Ross and Rear Admiral George Cockburn, had their men set fire to the Capitol, the President's House and the Treasury. Madison and many people in the city had already fled.

**Fort McHenry.** Three weeks later, British warships fired between 1,500 and 1,800 shells at Fort McHenry, which stood between the British navy and the city of Baltimore. The shelling lasted 25 hours, starting at 6 in the morning on September 13. The shells killed four men in the fort and wounded 24. Major George Armistead and his men held the fort, and the British ships sailed away.

Francis Scott Key watched the attack from an American ship in the harbor. He had gone to the British fleet to get a prisoner released, and the British would not let him go back to Baltimore until the battle was over. He wrote the verses that became "The Star-Spangled Banner."

**New Orleans.** American and British diplomats, officials sent to make the peace, signed a treaty to end the war at Ghent on December 24, 1814. The news did not reach New Orleans until March.

Before it came, on January 8, 1815, British soldiers under Edward Pakenham attacked General Andrew Jackson's lines outside the city. Jackson's force included frontiersmen from Tennessee and Kentucky, free Black soldiers, Choctaw fighters, and Louisiana militia, part-time citizen soldiers. Jean Lafitte's privateers, ship crews allowed by a government to attack enemy ships, fought with them too.

Of 3,000 British soldiers in the main attack, about 2,000 were killed, wounded or captured in less than 30 minutes. Pakenham rode forward to rally them, was hit by American fire, and died of his wounds. Counts of the British dead run from 285 to about 700. Jackson's side lost 13 killed.

For the whole war, Defense Department records list 2,260 Americans killed in battle and 4,505 wounded. They list no count of soldiers who died of disease.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Creek War, 1813 to 1814" -->
While US soldiers fought the British, a second war began in the South, on the land of the Creek, or Muskogee, people, in what is now Alabama and Georgia. It started as a war among the Creek themselves, between Creek towns friendly to the United States and a group from the Upper Towns called the Red Sticks.

Mississippi militia ambushed Red Sticks at Burnt Corn Creek on July 27, 1813, as the Red Sticks came home with ammunition from the Spanish at Pensacola. A month later, on August 30, Red Sticks attacked Fort Mims, a stockade, or fort with a wall of upright logs, north of Mobile. They killed 250 Creek and American settlers there. Tennessee's governor then sent Andrew Jackson with Tennessee militia into Creek country.

**Horseshoe Bend.** Jackson's army of about 3,300 men attacked the Red Sticks' walled town of Tohopeka, at Horseshoe Bend on the Tallapoosa River, on March 27, 1814. The army included Tennessee militia, US soldiers, and Cherokee and Lower Creek allies. About 1,000 Red Sticks under Chief Menawa held the town.

Jackson's army killed more than 800 of them. Soldiers counted 557 bodies on the battlefield, and soldiers led by a man named Coffee shot an estimated 300 more in the river as they tried to swim away. Jackson's side lost 49 killed and 154 wounded. Cherokee and Lower Creek fighters took about 350 Upper Creek women and children prisoner.

Menawa was wounded seven times. He lay among the dead until dark and escaped in a canoe. Later in 1814, in the Treaty of Fort Jackson, US officials made Creek leaders give up land. An Encyclopedia of Alabama article states more than 21 million acres, and a National Park Service page states 23 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Arikara towns and the Bad Axe, 1823 and 1832" -->
The Arikara, whose towns stood on the Missouri River in present-day South Dakota, fought US soldiers in 1823. Arikara men attacked a party of fur traders led by William Ashley below their towns on June 2, 1823, and killed twelve or thirteen of Ashley's men. Colonel Henry Leavenworth came in August with about 230 soldiers of the 6th Infantry, an army unit of foot soldiers. Fur trappers and about 750 Lakota and Yankton allies came with him.

They attacked the towns with cannon. After a peace was signed, the Arikara left their towns at night. As the soldiers marched away on August 15, two men of the Missouri Fur Company set the empty towns on fire. No reliable count of the Arikara dead survives.

**Black Hawk.** The Sauk leader Black Hawk led about 1,000 Sauk, Meskwaki and Kickapoo people back across the Mississippi River into Illinois in April 1832. US officials said the Sauk and Meskwaki had signed the land away in an 1804 treaty, and Black Hawk rejected that treaty. About 7,000 US soldiers and militiamen went out against his band. Abraham Lincoln served as a militiaman in this war.

**The Bad Axe.** By August the band, perhaps 500 men, women and children, was trying to cross back over the Mississippi near the Bad Axe River, in present-day Wisconsin. Soldiers aboard an army steamboat, the *Warrior*, under Captain Throckmorton, fired on them on August 1. The next day about 1,300 soldiers and militiamen under Brigadier General Henry Atkinson attacked the people still on the east bank.

A Wisconsin Historical Society marker put up in 1955 states that the people were "driven into the water by their pursuers." There, it states, "warriors, old people, women and children" were "shot down or drowned as they tried to escape." Illinois governor Thomas Ford later estimated 150 killed, as many drowned, and 50 taken prisoner.

About 70 people reached the far bank, where Dakota fighters, allies of the United States, captured or killed them. Neither the marker nor Ford's account names the men who fired on the people in the water.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Second Seminole War, 1835 to 1842" -->
US soldiers fought for seven years to force the Seminole out of Florida and west to Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. The Seminole had taken in people who escaped slavery in Georgia, South Carolina and Florida, and that turned slaveholders and army officers against them.

**Dade's column.** About 180 Seminole fighters led by Micanopy, Alligator and Jumper attacked Major Francis Dade's column of soldiers on December 28, 1835, on the road from Fort Brooke to Fort King. They killed almost all the soldiers in the column. A Florida Department of State history page states that one soldier survived, and other accounts count two or three.

**Osceola.** General Thomas Jesup ordered soldiers to seize the Seminole leader Osceola near St. Augustine in October 1837, while Osceola was meeting them under a white flag of truce, the sign that both sides agree not to fight while they talk. A National Park Service history describes a "public outcry" against Jesup's "dishonorable ploy," meaning a trick that broke the rules of honor. Soldiers then took Osceola and 237 of his people to Fort Moultrie in South Carolina. He died there as a prisoner on January 30, 1838.

More than 1,500 US soldiers had died in the war by 1842, most of them of disease, and US officials had spent more than $20 million on it. The National Park Service gives no count of the Seminole dead for the whole war. Its page gives one rate instead: for every four Seminole people the soldiers forced out of Florida, they killed one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Mexican War, 1846 to 1848" -->
Texas became part of the United States in 1845, and Mexican officials claimed the strip of land between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande. President James K. Polk sent soldiers under General Zachary Taylor into that strip in 1846. An article in the Colorado Encyclopedia states that Polk "goaded" Mexico into war this way, meaning he pushed Mexico into starting the fighting.

Mexican soldiers under Brigadier General Anastasio Torrejón surrounded a US patrol of cavalry, soldiers on horseback, under Captain Seth Thornton on April 25, 1846. The patrol surrendered. Mexican officials said Thornton's men were the invaders.

Polk told Congress that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil." House members voted for war 174 to 14 and senators 40 to 2, and Polk signed the declaration on May 13, 1846. Ulysses S. Grant, a young officer who fought in the war, later wrote that he regarded it "as one of the most unjust ever waged by a stronger against a weaker nation."

**Veracruz.** US soldiers won at Monterrey in September 1846 and at Buena Vista in February 1847. General Winfield Scott's guns fired more than 6,700 shells into the Mexican port city of Veracruz over four days in March 1847, and the city's defenders surrendered on March 29. One count, in a HistoryNet article, states about 100 Mexican civilians, 80 Mexican soldiers and 13 Americans dead there. Other counts run higher. Scott's soldiers then fought their way inland and took Mexico City in September 1847.

**Civilians.** American soldiers also killed Mexican civilians. Early in 1847, near Saltillo, Mexican ranchers dragged an Arkansas private, Samuel Colquitt, to death. Arkansas volunteers from Captain C. C. Danley's company and another company then found the civilians they believed were guilty and shot between 17 and 30 of them.

General Taylor was furious. He threatened to send the two companies back to the Rio Grande to do hard labor, and he did not, because the Mexican general Santa Anna's army arrived. No one counted all the Mexican civilians killed in the war.

**The surgeons.** Army surgeons had to cut off an arm or leg shattered in battle, an operation called amputation. In the spring of 1847 they used anesthetics in battle for the first time, drugs that put the patient to sleep so the patient feels no pain. Most of the men who died, though, died of dysentery, the gut infection that causes bloody diarrhea, or of yellow fever and malaria, fevers spread by mosquitoes.

**The treaty.** Mexican officials signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo on February 2, 1848. Under it they gave up 55 percent of Mexico's land, more than 525,000 square miles, to the United States, and US officials agreed to pay the Mexican government $15 million. That land became California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico and much of Colorado. In the 1840s, Comanche, Apache and Ute people controlled much of Mexico's northern lands, including part of this land.

About 78,700 Americans served in the war and 13,283 died, about one in six. By an estimate quoted on Smarthistory, an education website, about 25,000 Mexican soldiers died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-riley-san-patricios" name="John Riley" movie="One Man's Hero (1999)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Riley

> **Who:** An Irish-born US Army sergeant who crossed to the Mexican side before the war began and led a unit of deserters, the San Patricios, against the US Army.
> **When and where:** Company K, 5th US Infantry. Crossed the Rio Grande in the spring of 1846. Captured at Churubusco, near Mexico City, August 20, 1847.
> **Movie:** *One Man's Hero* (1999), a feature film about him, with Tom Berenger as Riley. It is a drama, not a documentary.

**Army officers hanged fifty of his men and branded his face.**

John Riley had trained West Point cadets to fire cannon. In the spring of 1846, before members of Congress declared war, he crossed the Rio Grande and joined the Mexican army, which made him a deserter, a soldier who leaves his army without permission. He organized other deserters into the San Patricio Battalion, a unit of a few hundred soldiers named in Spanish for Saint Patrick. Many of the men were Irish immigrants, and many were Catholic.

A National Park Service page lists their reasons. American officers treated Catholic soldiers with "anti-Catholic bigotry and brutal discipline," bigotry meaning hatred of people for who they are or what they believe. A Smithsonian article states that officers quite often forced Catholic soldiers to attend Protestant services.

Some men were against the war. Mexican officials offered land to foreigners who joined them. Some San Patricios were escaped slaves and free Black men, who saw Mexico, where slavery was against the law, as freer than the United States.

The San Patricios fought at Monterrey, Buena Vista, Cerro Gordo and Churubusco. At Churubusco on August 20, 1847, US soldiers captured between 75 and 80 of them. Army officers tried 72 as deserters in two mass courts-martial, or military trials, held 3 and 6 days after the capture. The men had no lawyers, and no one wrote down what was said.

**The hangings.** Army officers hanged fifty of them, at San Angel on September 10 and at Mixcoac on September 13, 1847. On September 13, Colonel William Harney hanged 30 of them within sight of Chapultepec Castle as the US flag went up over it. He left their bodies on the gallows to rot.

**The whip and the iron.** Riley had deserted before the war was declared, so the court could not sentence him to death. Instead the officers of the court ordered him whipped with fifty lashes, blows from a whip on his bare back that cut the skin. They also ordered him branded. A metal letter D, for deserter, was heated red-hot and pressed into the skin of his face near the eye, and it burned a scar in the shape of the letter that he carried for life.

Some accounts say it was done to both cheeks. One says the first D went on upside down and a second D was burned on the right way up. The Texas State Historical Association and *American Heritage* accounts do not name the men who held the whip and the iron.

Riley stayed in the Mexican army after the war. Records of where he went stop a few years later, and the date of his death is not known.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 23 minutes**

More American soldiers died in the Civil War, from 1861 to 1865, than in any other war the United States has fought. The Union was the United States government and the states that stayed loyal to it. The Confederacy was made up of Southern slave states whose leaders broke away.

**How many died.** Between 650,000 and 850,000 soldiers died in the Civil War. The historian J. David Hacker worked out that range in 2011 from census records, the government's counts of everyone in the country, from 1850 to 1880. His best estimate, about 750,000, is nearly twice the 405,399 Americans who died in World War II.

The long-used count was lower, about 620,000. The armies' own records count fewer still, because they are incomplete. The Union officer in charge of the draft, for example, counted only 133,821 Confederate dead in his final report.

**What killed them.** Disease killed more soldiers than bullets did. Union records list 140,414 Union soldiers killed in battle and 224,097 dead of disease and other causes. That is close to the rate an American Battlefield Trust article gives for the war: five deaths from disease for every three in battle.

Measles spread through the new army camps in 1861. In August that year Lee wrote, "The soldiers everywhere are sick." Army doctors recorded 79,462 cases of typhoid, an infection from bacteria in food or water that contains human waste. It causes high fever and stomach pain, and it can kill.

US soldiers fought the nations of the Plains and the West from the end of the Civil War until 1890. American sailors beat a Spanish fleet in Manila Bay, in the Philippines, in 1898, and American soldiers fought Spain in Cuba. A new war began in the Philippines in February 1899, between US soldiers and Filipino fighters led by Emilio Aguinaldo.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Civil War battles, 1861 to 1865" -->
Confederate gunners opened fire on Fort Sumter, in the harbor at Charleston, South Carolina, at 4:30 in the morning on April 12, 1861. Over 34 hours they fired more than 4,000 shots and shells. Major Robert Anderson and the Union soldiers who held the fort surrendered it the next afternoon.

**Antietam.** The two armies had about 23,000 casualties, soldiers killed, wounded or missing, in twelve hours of fighting at Antietam Creek in Maryland, on September 17, 1862. More soldiers were lost at Antietam than on any other single day of the war.

**Gettysburg.** The Union army under George Meade and the Confederate army under Robert E. Lee had 51,112 casualties between them at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, from July 1 to 3, 1863. About 7,000 of those men were killed. On the last day, Lee sent about 12,500 men to attack the Union soldiers on Cemetery Ridge, in the attack called Pickett's Charge. Close to 60 percent of them were killed, wounded or captured.

Ulysses S. Grant's army surrounded the river town of Vicksburg, Mississippi, for 46 days, in a siege that cut the town off until it gave up. On July 4, 1863, about 29,000 Confederate soldiers at Vicksburg marched out, stacked their rifles and surrendered to him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who had to fight in the Civil War?" -->
Officials on both sides drafted soldiers when too few men volunteered, and both drafts let richer men out. Members of the Confederate Congress passed their draft law first, on April 16, 1862. Under it, officials could draft any white man aged 18 to 35. On October 11, 1862, they passed the Twenty-Slave Law, under which any man who held twenty or more enslaved people did not have to serve. Drafted soldiers called it "a rich man's war."

**The Union draft.** Members of the US Congress passed the Enrollment Act on March 3, 1863. Army officers called provost marshals ran the draft. They drafted men in each district where too few had volunteered to fill the number of soldiers the army asked for. A drafted man could hire a substitute to go in his place, or pay a $300 fee and go home. In 1863, $300 could take the average person a whole year to earn.

Substitutes were often 18- or 19-year-olds too young to be drafted, or immigrants who were not yet citizens. People called it "a rich man's war and a poor man's fight." More than 20 percent of the men drafted did not report. Some fled west and some hid.

**The New York riots.** The draft began in New York City on Saturday, July 11, 1863. A rich man could pay his way out, and many New Yorkers were angry that poor men had to go instead. Many poor white workers, most of them Irish, also feared that if slavery ended, Black people would move to New York and take their jobs.

From Monday, July 13, to Thursday, July 16, mobs looted stores, burned a draft office, and attacked police and soldiers. Their main target was the city's Black people. The rioters lynched at least a dozen Black men, which means they killed them without any trial.

On Clarkson Street on July 13, a gang led by an Irish bricklayer, John Nicholson, beat a Black man named William Jones, who drove a cart for a living, hanged him and set his body on fire. The mobs burned homes and businesses. Rioters set fire to the Colored Orphan Asylum ("Colored" was a word used then for Black people), a home for Black children without parents, on Fifth Avenue with more than 200 children inside. All of the children got out.

The official count of the dead was 119, and the New-York Historical Society notes that many historians think more died. Union soldiers came from Gettysburg to stop the riots. Afterward many Black families moved to Brooklyn and New Jersey, and the number of Black people living in New York City fell by 20 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did surgeons cut off so many arms and legs?" -->
Soldiers on both sides fired the minié ball, a soft lead bullet shaped like a cone, and it often flattened when it hit a body. It splintered bone, tore muscle, and drove dirt and bits of cloth into the wound. A surgeon could not mend an arm or leg smashed that way, so he cut it off. Three of every four operations in the war were amputations, close to 60,000 in all.

**The operation.** The surgeon first put the soldier to sleep with chloroform or ether. He tied a tight band called a tourniquet around the arm or leg to slow the bleeding, then cut through the skin with a knife called a scalpel and through the muscle with a long knife. Then he sawed through the bone. Last, he tied off the blood vessels and bandaged the stump.

Soldiers whose limbs were cut off within two days of the wound lived more often than those operated on later. Infection, loss of blood and poor food killed many of the men operated on later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Amos Humiston

> **Who:** A sergeant in the 154th New York Infantry, killed at Gettysburg. He was identified only because of a photograph of his three children found in his hand.
> **When and where:** Born in Owego, New York, April 26, 1830. Killed at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, July 1, 1863. Identified in November 1863.

**A photograph of three children was all he had on him.**

Amos Humiston had made harnesses for horses and had sailed on a whaling ship from 1850 to 1854. He married Philinda Smith on July 4, 1854, and they had three children, Franklin, Alice and Frederick. He joined the army in July 1862 and was a sergeant by January 1863.

He was killed on the first day at Gettysburg. The records do not say what wound killed him. His body was found near the corner of Stratton and York Streets in the town. He had nothing on him to show who he was except a small photograph on glass, called an ambrotype, of three young children. He was buried as an unknown soldier.

**"Whose Father Was He?"** A doctor named John Francis Bourns had hundreds of copies of the photograph made. On October 19, 1863, editors at the *Philadelphia Inquirer* printed the story under that headline. In November, Philinda Humiston saw a copy and knew her children. Without the photograph, his grave in the Soldiers' National Cemetery would read "Unknown," like the graves of 979 other soldiers there.

In October 1866, his three children were among the first thirty children at a home for soldiers' orphans that opened in Gettysburg. A monument to Humiston went up on North Stratton Street in 1993. It is the only monument on the battlefield to a single enlisted soldier, meaning a soldier who was not an officer.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amos-humiston" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The prison camps" -->
**Why the camps filled.** Union and Confederate commanders traded captured soldiers under an agreement made in July 1862. In December 1862, Confederate President Jefferson Davis announced that captured Black soldiers and their white officers would not be traded. President Abraham Lincoln stopped the trades in the summer of 1863 because the Confederates would not include Black soldiers.

By August 1863, large trades had ended, and the prison camps filled up. By the end of the war, about 400,000 men had been held in more than 150 prisons.

**Andersonville.** Confederate officers opened a prison camp at Andersonville, Georgia, early in 1864. It was a walled pen of 26.5 acres, built for 10,000 men, and in August 1864 it held more than 33,000. One stream was the only water for most of them. About 19 feet inside the wall ran a line called the deadline, and guards had orders to shoot any prisoner who crossed it.

More than 45,000 Union soldiers were held there in 14 months. Almost 13,000 of them, more than one in four, died there under the Confederate officers who ran the camp, of disease, hunger, filth, crowding, and exposure to heat and cold.

A court of army officers found the camp's commander, Captain Henry Wirz, guilty of war crimes, acts that break the laws of war. The court sentenced him to death, and Union army officers hanged him in Washington on November 10, 1865. He was the only person put to death for war crimes in the Civil War.

**The Union camps.** Between 26,000 and 31,000 Confederate soldiers died in Union prison camps. At Elmira, New York, the barracks had no protection from the winter cold. Close to 3,000 Confederate prisoners died there, about one in four of the men held. An American Battlefield Trust article blames Colonel William Hoffman, the Union officer in charge of all prisoners.

At Camp Douglas, in Chicago, people from the US Sanitary Commission, a group that checked on the health of soldiers and their camps, reported standing water, filthy toilet trenches and "rotten bones." The camp's commanders cut the prisoners' food to make money for themselves. At Point Lookout, Maryland, more than 4,000 prisoners died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Pillow, April 12, 1864" -->
Black men who joined the Union army served in regiments, large army units, called the US Colored Troops. The Confederate general Nathan Bedford Forrest attacked Fort Pillow in Tennessee with about 1,500 men on April 12, 1864. The fort held 262 Black artillery soldiers, who fired the big guns, and 295 white Tennessee cavalrymen. Forrest's men took the fort and kept shooting soldiers who had surrendered. A witness told congressional investigators, "They killed all the men after they surrendered, until orders were given to stop."

Private George Shaw, a Black soldier, testified that a Confederate shot him after he surrendered, told him "You are fighting against your master," and threw him into the river. Shaw lived. He saw Confederate soldiers shoot three teenage boys who had escaped slavery in the forehead while the boys begged for their lives.

Forrest's men killed 182 of the Black soldiers that day or wounded them so badly that they died later, about 70 percent of them. A sign in the museum at the fort lists all 182 by name. About 20 percent of the white soldiers were killed. Forrest's men also killed some Black civilians, and no one knows how many. After Fort Pillow, Black soldiers went into battle shouting "Remember Fort Pillow."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Christian Fleetwood

> **Who:** A Black man born free in Baltimore, who carried his regiment's flag through the attack at New Market Heights and received the Medal of Honor.
> **When and where:** Born in Baltimore, Maryland, July 21, 1840. Enlisted in the 4th US Colored Infantry, August 1863. New Market Heights, outside Richmond, Virginia, September 29, 1864. Died September 28, 1914.

**Two flag bearers fell, and he picked up the flag.**

Christian Fleetwood's parents were free. He graduated from the Ashmun Institute in Oxford, Pennsylvania, in 1860, and helped start the *Lyceum Observer*, an early Black newspaper in the South. When he enlisted in August 1863, his officers made him sergeant major, the regiment's top sergeant, because he was so well educated.

His regiment charged at New Market Heights on September 29, 1864. Two soldiers carrying the flag were shot down. Fleetwood picked it up and carried it forward under fire. In his diary that day he wrote that the regiment "got used up" and that he "saved colors," meaning the regiment's flag.

He was one of 14 soldiers of the Colored Troops who received the Medal of Honor, an award for bravery in battle, for that day. Every officer in his regiment asked Secretary of War Edwin Stanton to make Fleetwood an officer. Stanton refused.

After the war Fleetwood worked as a clerk in the War Department in Washington. He also organized a battalion of the District of Columbia National Guard and served as its major.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="christian-fleetwood" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="grant-lee" name="Ulysses S. Grant and Robert E. Lee" movie="Grant (History, 2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ulysses S. Grant and Robert E. Lee

> **Who:** The two commanding generals at the end of the Civil War. Lee led the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia from June 1862. Grant led all the Union armies from March 1864.
> **When and where:** Lee was born at Stratford Hall, Virginia, on January 19, 1807, and died in Lexington, Virginia, on October 12, 1870. Grant was born in Point Pleasant, Ohio, on April 27, 1822, and died on July 23, 1885. They met at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, on April 9, 1865.
> **Movie:** *Grant* (History channel, 2020), a documentary in three parts about Grant, with acted scenes.

**Wesley Norris said Lee had salt water poured into his cuts.**

Both men trained at West Point, where Lee finished second in the class of 1829 and Grant 21st of 39 in 1843, and both fought in the Mexican War. In 1854 Grant quit the army so he would not be thrown out, after heavy drinking at remote army posts in the West.

**Wesley Norris.** From 1857 Lee was in charge of 189 enslaved people on the estates of his dead father-in-law. Three of them ran away in 1859. They almost reached the Pennsylvania line, but at Westminster, Maryland, they were caught and jailed, and Lee was told. No surviving record names the men who caught them.

One of the three, Wesley Norris, gave his account in 1866. A county constable named Dick Williams, he said, whipped him and his cousin fifty times each and whipped his sister twenty times while Lee watched. Then, Norris said, Lee ordered the overseer to wash their backs with brine, very salty water, poured into the cuts.

**Harpers Ferry.** In October 1859, the year of the escape, Lee led the 90 US Marines who ended John Brown's raid at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. Brown and 18 of his men had seized the US Armory and Arsenal there, where the government made and stored guns, to fight a war against slavery.

Lee resigned from the US Army on April 20, 1861, after Virginia's leaders voted to leave the Union. On December 29, 1862, he signed the paper freeing the people enslaved on his father-in-law's estates, because the will required it. At Gettysburg on July 3, 1863, he ordered Pickett's Charge.

Grant took Fort Donelson in February 1862, after telling the fort's commander he would accept "no terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender." He took Vicksburg on July 4, 1863. In March 1864 he became a lieutenant general and the commander of every Union army.

**Appomattox.** Lee surrendered to Grant in Wilmer McLean's parlor at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. Under Grant's terms, Lee's soldiers could go home once they signed a promise not to fight the United States again. The officers kept their side arms, the small weapons they carried, and their own horses and belongings. About 28,000 Confederate soldiers signed. Grant finished writing his memoirs a few days before he died of cancer in 1885.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bear River, Sand Creek and the Marias, 1863 to 1870" -->
**By 1865:** Lee had surrendered at Appomattox, and somewhere between 650,000 and 850,000 soldiers of the two armies were dead.

During and after the Civil War, US soldiers attacked Native camps in the West where families were living through the winter.

**Bear River.** Colonel Patrick Edward Connor led the 3rd California Volunteer Infantry against a Northwestern Shoshone winter camp on the Bear River, near present-day Preston, Idaho, on January 29, 1863. He had announced that he did not mean to take prisoners. The soldiers attacked in the early morning and began firing without a word.

People jumped into the freezing river. A woman named Anzee Chee hid under the riverbank with other women, and she drowned her own crying baby so the soldiers would not find them. Yeager Timbimboo, a 12-year-old boy, lived by lying still among the dead.

Soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 500 Shoshone, and Connor was promoted to brigadier general for the attack. An article in Smithsonian magazine states that the attack "is often described as" the deadliest killing of Native people by US soldiers, with a death toll believed to be higher than at Sand Creek or Wounded Knee.

**Sand Creek.** Colonel John Chivington led about 675 Colorado volunteer soldiers against the Cheyenne and Arapaho camp of Black Kettle at Sand Creek, in Colorado, on November 29, 1864. Black Kettle's camp flew a US flag and a white flag. The soldiers killed about 230 people by the National Park Service's count, most of them women, children and old people. Soldiers cut off scalps and other parts of the bodies.

Captain Silas Soule and Lieutenant Joseph Cramer would not let their men fire. Soule put the number of dead at three hundred. He also wrote that it was "hard to see little children on their knees have their brains beat out by men professing to be civilized."

After Soule and Cramer sent their letters, army officers held an investigation and members of Congress held two more. Members of Congress's Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War wrote that Chivington "deliberately planned and executed a foul and dastardly (cowardly) massacre," a killing of many people who could not defend themselves.

Chivington left the army and was never tried, and no officer or soldier was punished. About two months after Soule testified against Chivington, on April 23, 1865, someone shot Soule dead on a street in Denver. No one was ever brought to justice for his murder.

**The Marias.** General Philip Sheridan sent Major Eugene Baker of the 2nd Cavalry against Mountain Chief's band of Piikani, or Piegan Blackfeet, in Montana in January 1870. "Tell Baker to strike them hard," Sheridan ordered.

At daybreak on January 23, Baker found a camp on the Marias River. His scout, Joe Kipp, told him it was the peaceful band of Heavy Runner. By one account, Baker answered, "That makes no difference, one band or another of them. They are all Piegans and we will attack them." He ordered a sergeant to shoot Kipp if Kipp warned the camp.

Heavy Runner came out of his lodge waving his paper of safe passage, and the soldiers shot him first. They knocked down lodges, the band's tent homes, with people inside them, set them on fire, and burned the band's winter food.

Army officers counted 173 dead. A later estimate, reported by HISTORY, is that the soldiers killed 37 men, 90 women and 50 children, which makes 177. Other counts run to more than 200. Baker took about 140 women and children prisoner. When he found smallpox among them, he left them with no food or shelter. Neither Baker nor his men were ever punished.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The army in the West, 1866 to 1890" -->
From 1866 to 1890, US soldiers fought the nations of the Plains and the West. US officials wanted the nations on reservations, land set aside for them, and soldiers attacked the bands that would not go.

**The Buffalo Soldiers.** Members of Congress created six regiments of Black soldiers in 1866, later merged into four: the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the 24th and 25th Infantry. They became known as the Buffalo Soldiers, and army commanders posted them west of the Mississippi River. Counts of how many of them received the Medal of Honor in the wars against the Plains nations run from 17 to 23.

**Little Bighorn.** An army expedition under George Armstrong Custer reported gold in the Black Hills in 1874. An 1868 treaty had promised the hills to the Lakota, and US officials ignored it. In December 1875 they ordered the winter camps off the reservation to come onto it by January 31, 1876, and then sent the army after the camps that stayed out.

Lakota and Cheyenne fighters killed Custer, the commander of the 7th Cavalry, beside the Little Bighorn River in Montana on June 25 and 26, 1876. They killed every man in the five companies, or groups of soldiers, under his own command. In all, 268 US soldiers and scouts died in the two days.

**The Nez Perce.** General Oliver O. Howard ordered Chief Joseph's band of Nez Perce to leave their homeland in the Wallowa country of northeast Oregon in 1877 and move to the reservation. Fighting began that June. About 750 Nez Perce people, about 200 of them fighters, fled from US soldiers from June to October. They covered about 1,170 miles and fought the soldiers again and again as they went. They surrendered at Bear Paw, in Montana, on October 5, 1877, 40 miles from Canada.

**Wounded Knee.** Soldiers of the 7th Cavalry under Colonel James Forsyth surrounded a Lakota camp at Wounded Knee Creek in South Dakota on December 29, 1890, and began taking away the Lakota's guns. The soldiers had four Hotchkiss guns, small cannon on wheels that fired exploding shells one after another, on a bluff above the camp.

A rifle belonging to Black Coyote, a deaf man, went off. Some accounts say he fired it, and others say it went off when a soldier grabbed it. Soldiers of the 7th Cavalry then killed between 250 and 300 Lakota, and almost half of the dead were women and children.

At least 25 soldiers died, and an article in Smithsonian magazine states that other soldiers' bullets likely hit many of them. Twenty soldiers of the 7th Cavalry received the Medal of Honor for that day.

### Cathay Williams

> **Who:** A woman born into slavery who enlisted in the army as William Cathay and served two years with a Black infantry regiment in the West.
> **When and where:** Born in Independence, Missouri. Enlisted at St. Louis, November 1866. 38th US Infantry, in Kansas and New Mexico. Discharged October 14, 1868. Applied for a pension in 1891 and was refused.

**Her papers said William Cathay, age 22, 5 feet 9 inches.**

Cathay Williams was born enslaved. During the Civil War, Union soldiers of the 8th Indiana Infantry freed her and took her as "contraband," the army's word for enslaved people who came under Union control. She worked for the regiment, washing clothes and cooking, and followed it to the battle of Pea Ridge in Arkansas and to the Shenandoah Valley in Virginia.

She enlisted at St. Louis as William Cathay in November 1866. She was sent to the 38th Infantry, one of the new Black regiments. She caught smallpox soon after she enlisted and recovered. Over two years she marched about 1,000 miles with her company, from Fort Riley in Kansas to Fort Bayard in New Mexico.

An army doctor at her post found out she was a woman in 1868. She was discharged on October 14 with a certificate of disability, a paper saying she was not fit to serve. In 1876 she told a St. Louis newspaper, "I played sick... The post surgeon found out I was a woman."

She asked for a pension, the regular payment a former soldier can get, as a disabled veteran in 1891. She had spent eighteen months in a hospital in Trinidad, Colorado, and had lost some of her toes. Officials at the Pension Bureau turned her down, even though her discharge papers recorded illness. The date of her death is not known.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898, Cuba and the Philippines" -->
From 1898 on, American soldiers fought most of their wars overseas. The US Navy ship *Maine* blew up in the harbor at Havana, Cuba, on February 15, 1898, and 266 of the 354 men aboard died. People still argue over the cause. Later in 1898, a court of navy officers blamed a mine. In 1976, a study led by Admiral Hyman Rickover found that a fire in a coal bin most likely set off the explosives stored next to it.

**San Juan Hill.** The Black soldiers of the 10th Cavalry fought at Las Guasimas, Cuba, on June 24, 1898, and again in the attack on San Juan and Kettle Hills on July 1. About one in five of them became casualties in that attack. The fighting with Spain stopped on August 13, 1898.

Typhoid killed more American soldiers in 1898 than Spanish soldiers did. Defense Department records list 385 Americans killed in battle in the war with Spain and 2,061 dead of other causes, most of them of typhoid. In the army's camps in the United States there were 20,738 cases of typhoid and 1,590 deaths.

**The Philippines.** Private William Walter Grayson of the 1st Nebraska Volunteer Infantry was standing guard in Manila on February 4, 1899. He fired the shot counted as the first of a new war, between US soldiers and Aguinaldo's fighters. US soldiers captured Aguinaldo in 1901, and President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902, though fighting went on in places.

Historians at the State Department count more than 4,200 American and more than 20,000 Filipino fighters dead. They count as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians dead of violence, hunger and disease. Other estimates of the civilian dead run much higher, up to 1,000,000, and historians disagree about them. The State Department's history does not say how many of the civilians American soldiers killed.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** More American soldiers died in the Civil War than in any other war, and after it US soldiers spent twenty-five years fighting the nations of the West.
**In 1861:** Americans were fighting each other in a war at home in which between 650,000 and 850,000 soldiers died, most of them of disease.
**In 1900:** American soldiers were fighting across the Pacific, in the Philippines, where as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died by the State Department's count, and more by other counts.
**Next:** In the next fifty years more than 20 million Americans served in two world wars, and most of them were drafted.
