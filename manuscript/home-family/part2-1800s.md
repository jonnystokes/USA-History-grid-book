## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Middle-class families in towns and cities began, in the early 1800s, to treat the home as a place set apart from paid work. In the 1830s cooks began to trade the open hearth, the open fireplace where food cooked over the fire, for the iron stove. On farms, children's chores came first and school fitted around them. In the South, enslavers could sell any member of an enslaved family at any time. The law did not count enslaved people's marriages as real marriages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The parlor and the home apart from work" -->
Middle-class families were the families in between the rich and the poor. In the early 1800s, many of these families lived in towns and cities. They came to see the home as a place apart, and paid work happened somewhere else. In this view the home was the mother's to run. The parlor, the front room where the family received visitors, became the room for showing off the family's best things. Domestic means having to do with the home. These ideas are called the cult of domesticity. Here a cult means a set of beliefs that many people held strongly.

Catharine Beecher's 1841 book, *A Treatise on Domestic Economy*, described how to run a home of this kind. A treatise is a long book that explains one subject, and domestic economy meant running a home. Readers used the book as a handbook for these ideas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The stove takes the place of the fire" -->
Iron cookstoves spread through American kitchens from the 1830s. Foundries, the workshops where iron was melted and poured into molds, made stoves of many designs. By 1850 most middle-class kitchens in the cities had a stove in place of the open hearth. Poorer families and families far from towns changed over later.

For the woman who did the cooking, a stove meant less bending and lifting. It gave off less smoke, and no ash fell into the food. But a stove took hard work to clean. Stoves were also drafty and hard to control. In 1846 the writer Henry David Thoreau complained that the stove "concealed the fire." He wrote that cooking had become "merely a chemic process," by which he meant a kind of chemistry.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A child's day, and where school fit in" -->
On a farm, a child's chores came first. Country schools, called district schools, usually held two terms a year. The summer term ran from about May to August, for the younger children, and women usually taught it. The winter term ran from about November to April. Older boys could go then, because they were not needed for work on the farm. Men were often hired to teach the winter term. Children missed school when the weather was bad or when there was work to do at home.

The teacher often lived with the students' families, staying with one family and then the next. People called this boarding around.

At home, parents expected children to obey. Many parents hit their children with a rod, a stick or switch used for punishment. They pointed to a verse in the Bible's book of Proverbs: "He that spareth his rod hateth his son: but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes." It means that a father who does not use the rod hates his son. A father who loves his son punishes him early. In the 1800s some writers of advice books for parents began to urge calm, firm correction instead. One of them told parents never to punish a child in anger.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Home under slavery" -->
Enslavers could break up an enslaved family on any day. They sold enslaved people, and slave traders bought them and moved them. Between about 1790 and 1860, traders moved more than 1 million enslaved people from the Upper South to the Deep South. The Upper South was the more northern of the Southern states, and the Deep South was the states farther south. From 1820 they moved about 200,000 people every ten years. Traders marched them overland in chained lines called coffles, or shipped them by sea to New Orleans. New Orleans had the largest slave market in the country.

The Equal Justice Initiative is an American organization. By its count, the traders took nearly half of the people they moved away from a husband, a wife or a parent. The law did not count the marriages of enslaved people as real marriages, so an enslaver could sell a husband and a wife apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="catharine-beecher-home-family" name="Catharine Beecher" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Catharine Beecher

> **Who:** Wrote a book of instructions for running an American home, and argued that keeping house was a skill to be studied and taught in schools.
> **When and where:** Born September 6, 1800, in East Hampton, New York. Her *Treatise on Domestic Economy* came out in 1841. She died on May 12, 1878, in Elmira, New York.

Catharine Beecher wrote *A Treatise on Domestic Economy*, published in 1841. It was the first American book to describe every part of running a home. Economy here means managing a household. Printers issued it in about 15 editions and put out new copies nearly every year for years.

Beecher argued that housekeeping was a profession. By that she meant a skilled job that women should study and that schools should teach. In 1823 she founded the Hartford Female Seminary, a school for girls and young women, in Connecticut. Later she wrote another book about the home with her sister Harriet Beecher Stowe. It was called *The American Woman's Home*, and it came out in 1869.

### Buffalo Bird Woman (Waheenee)

> **Who:** A Hidatsa woman who grew up in her mothers' earth lodge and later told the story of her life to the anthropologist Gilbert Wilson.
> **When and where:** About 1839 to 1932. Born by the Knife River and raised at Like-a-Fishhook village on the Missouri River, in what is now North Dakota. Wilson interviewed her every summer from 1906 (some accounts say 1907) to 1918.

Buffalo Bird Woman grew up in an earth lodge in the 1840s. An earth lodge was a large round house with a frame of timber, covered over with packed earth. Her household held her father, his four wives, her grandmother and her great-grandmother. Late in her life she told the story of that household to Gilbert Wilson, an anthropologist, a scientist who studies how groups of people live. He interviewed her every summer from 1906 (some accounts say 1907) to 1918. She spoke Hidatsa and never learned English. Wilson published her story in 1921 as *Waheenee: An Indian Girl's Story*. Her son, Edward Goodbird, drew the pictures for Wilson's books.

She was born in an earth lodge near where the Knife River meets the Missouri. The National Park Service gives her birth year as about 1839. She said she was born "three years after the smallpox winter," and Wilson dated that winter to 1837 and 1838, which would make it about 1840 or 1841.

Smallpox is a deadly disease that spreads from person to person. In June 1837 the steamboat *St. Peters*, owned by the American Fur Company, carried passengers sick with smallpox up the Missouri River. Francis A. Chardon ran Fort Clark, a trading post near the Mandan and Hidatsa villages. At a trading post, traders swapped goods for furs. Chardon met the boat 30 miles downstream and heard that people on it were sick. On June 18 the boat landed at Fort Clark. Some accounts give June 19. In less than a day, workers from the boat and the fort unloaded goods and loaded furs, and villagers came and went from the boat.

About 2,000 Mandan people were alive in the spring of 1837. By October, smallpox had killed all but 138 of them. Buffalo Bird Woman's great-grandmother told her: "More than half of my tribe died in the smallpox winter. Of the Mandans only a few families were left alive. All the old people and little children died."

When she was ten days old, an old man named Nothing-but-Water gave her the name Good Way. Later her father gave her the name Waheenee-wea, Buffalo Bird Woman, because she was a sickly child. People believed a new name could move the gods to help her. Her father, Small Ankle, was a Hidatsa chief. He had four wives, and they were sisters. Her mother, Weahtee, was one of them. The others were Red Blossom, Stalk-of-Corn and Strikes-Many Woman. "I was taught to call all these my mothers," she said. "Such was our Indian custom." Her great-grandmother White Corn and her grandmother Turtle lived with the family too.

When she was four, the Mandan and Hidatsa chiefs moved their people together to one new village, at Like-a-Fishhook Point on the Missouri. The village stood by 1845. The chiefs made the move to protect their people from attacks by the Dakota and the Yanktonai. Women and dogs carried the family goods there, on their backs and on travois. A travois is a frame of two poles that a dog dragged along the ground. The people built about seventy summer earth lodges at the new village, most of them in the second summer. For the winter, families built small huts in the woods, and the June flood often swept them away.

In the first hard winter, the family camped at Many-Frogs Brook, three miles from the village. Smallpox broke out with the first snow. "My brother, my mother Weahtee, and her sister Stalk-of-Corn, died," she said. Her grandmother Turtle made her a doll of deerskin with eyes of bone beads.

The women of a household owned its earth lodge. Her mothers' lodge had a floor more than forty feet across. The fire burned in the center. A screen of upright split logs stood between the fire and the door and kept out drafts and dogs. Her father's bed, of cottonwood planks and buffalo robes, stood in front of the fire. The oldest person in the family usually slept there, because it was the warmest place. The rest of the family slept in covered beds against the back wall. The buffalo-skin bedding could not be washed, so her mothers aired it on the raised platform, called a stage, where they dried their corn. The family believed that "an earth lodge was alive and had a spirit."

Her first husband was Magpie. Her son, Edward Goodbird, was born in 1869. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation to live on. In the late 1880s, federal officials, the officials of the national government, carried out a law called the Dawes Act. They divided reservation land into allotments. An allotment was a plot of reservation land that federal officials assigned to one family. Families moved out onto their allotments, and by the late 1880s no one lived in Like-a-Fishhook village. In 1921 she said: "We no longer live in an earth lodge, but in a house with chimneys." She added that "my son's wife cooks by a stove." She still got up at daybreak to hoe her corn and sing to it. She died in 1932.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, American families lived in many kinds of homes. In New York, a family might live in a tenement flat of small rooms, with shared toilets out in the yard. A middle-class family might have a house with a parlor for guests, a dining room used only for meals, and a paid servant to help run it. On the plains, where few trees grew, settler families cut their houses out of the ground. State lawmakers began to require children to go to school, and federal officials took Native children away from their homes to distant schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement flat" -->
A tenement was a building of small apartments, called flats, rented to working families. Builders put up the tenement at 97 Orchard Street in New York in 1863. Over the years, about 7,000 people from more than 20 countries lived there. Families lived in small rooms. Many took in boarders, people who paid to sleep in the family's rooms. Families carried water into the flat by hand and shared privies, outdoor toilets, in the yard behind the building.

Lawmakers in New York began to change what a tenement had to include. Under the Tenement House Act of 1879, builders added air shafts, narrow gaps between buildings meant to let in air and light. In 1900 Governor Theodore Roosevelt appointed a group of people, called a commission, to study the housing laws. Lawrence Veiller, the commission's secretary, did most of the investigating and helped write a new law. New York's state lawmakers passed it on April 12, 1901. It required a window in every room and a toilet in every new apartment. Owners of old tenements had to give them better light and add one toilet for every two families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bigger houses and paid help" -->
Families who could pay for them built houses with more rooms, each with its own use. Running water inside the house came first to the cities and reached many farm homes decades later. In 1890, 24 of every 100 American households had running water, and 8 of every 100 had electricity.

Many middle-class homes also had paid help. The census is the count of the country's people taken every ten years. The census of 1870 was the first to record the jobs of working women in full, by a set method. The census takers counted 867,354 women in domestic service, which means paid work in another family's home. That was about 47 of every 100 women who worked for pay. It was by far the most common paid job for women.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sod houses and dugouts" -->
The plains had few trees, so settler families there could not build with logs. They built with sod instead. Sod is the top layer of prairie soil, held together by the roots of the grass. Settlers used a plow to cut about an acre of ground into strips 12 inches wide and 4 inches thick. They cut the strips into pieces about three feet long. Builders laid these sod bricks grass side down, turning each layer lengthwise and then crosswise, to make walls two feet thick. Most early sod houses had dirt floors. Women who lived in them complained about the dirt, the bugs, the snakes, the leaking roofs and the poor light.

Some families dug their first home into the side of a hill or a creek bank. A house like this is called a dugout, and it took less work to build than other kinds of house. In May 1874 Charles and Caroline Ingalls settled with their children in a dugout on Plum Creek, near Walnut Grove, Minnesota. Their crops failed three years in a row, and in 1876 they left. The hollow in the creek bank where the dugout stood is still kept today.

Walnut Grove lies on land the Dakota signed away under treaties in 1851. A treaty is a written agreement between nations. At Traverse des Sioux on July 23, 1851, three men bargained with the Sisseton and Wahpeton Dakota for about 24 million acres. They were Luke Lea, the US commissioner sent to make the treaty, Alexander Ramsey, the governor of Minnesota Territory, and Henry Sibley. The commissioners agreed to pay about 7.5 cents an acre. The Dakota believed US officials would take the land by force if they refused. After the treaty was signed, the fur trader Joseph R. Brown had Dakota leaders sign a third paper. No one read it aloud to them or translated it. That paper let traders take the annuity money, the yearly payments promised to the Dakota, for debts the traders claimed the Dakota owed them. Within ten years, more than 100,000 white settlers moved onto the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School laws and the boarding schools" -->
In 1852 Massachusetts lawmakers passed a law that required children to go to school. The law applied to children aged 8 to 14, for twelve weeks a year. By 1900, 32 states had such a law, and by 1918 every state had one. Mississippi was the last. Under these laws, a family that needed a child's wages or a child's work at home was required to send the child to school for part of the year.

Federal officials took Native children from their families to boarding schools far away. At the schools, staff cut the children's hair, took away their clothes and gave them English names. Staff beat children who spoke their own language, locked them up alone, held back their food or washed their mouths out with soap. Survivors and a federal investigation later reported these punishments. On November 1, 1879, Richard Henry Pratt, an Army officer, opened the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, a boarding school for Native children. In 1891 members of Congress gave the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the federal official in charge of Native affairs, the power to make and enforce rules forcing Native children to attend school. In 1893 they allowed officials to hold back food rations, clothing and payments owed to parents who refused to send their children.

In 2022 and 2024, officials of the US Department of the Interior reported on these schools. They counted 417 federal boarding schools for Native children in 37 states or territories, open between 1819 and 1969, and at least 973 children who died at those schools. Their report states that this count is low.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nathalie Gumpertz

> **Who:** A tenement mother of four who became a dressmaker after her husband disappeared.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, New York. Her husband Julius disappeared in 1874. A judge declared him dead in 1883.

Nathalie Gumpertz supported her four children by sewing dresses in her tenement flat. One morning in 1874, her husband Julius left for work and never came back.

Nathalie and Julius Gumpertz were immigrants from East Prussia who lived at 97 Orchard Street. They were living there during the Panic of 1873, a money crash in which many people lost their jobs. When Julius left, Nathalie had four young children.

She became a dressmaker and worked in the front room of the flat, the parlor. She made about three dresses a week and earned 8 to 9 dollars. In 1883 a judge declared Julius legally dead. Because of that, Nathalie could claim 600 dollars that he was due from his father's estate, the money and property his father left when he died. She then moved the family to upper Manhattan.

Later, genealogists working for the Tenement Museum in New York found that Julius had gone to Cincinnati. Genealogists are people who trace family histories.

### Rachel Calof

> **Who:** A young woman from Russia who came to North Dakota for an arranged marriage and later wrote down what keeping a homestead house was like.
> **When and where:** Near Devils Lake, North Dakota, 1894 to 1904. She wrote her memoir in 1936, and it was published in 1995.

In 1894, at the age of 18, Rachel Calof traveled from Russia to marry Abraham Calof. It was an arranged marriage, planned by other people rather than by the couple. Abraham was a homesteader, a settler who claimed land from the US government to live on and farm.

The couple lived in a shanty, a small, roughly built house, 12 feet by 14 feet with a dirt floor. In the winters, Abraham's parents and brothers, and the chickens, shared the one room with the couple to save fuel. Of all the hardships, she wrote, "the lack of privacy was the hardest to bear."

In 1936 she wrote a memoir, a book about her own life, in Yiddish, a language spoken by many Jews in Eastern Europe. It records her years on the homestead from 1894 to 1904. Indiana University Press published it in English in 1995 as *Rachel Calof's Story*.

The land around Devils Lake was Dakota land. Under a treaty of 1867, the land south of the lake became a reservation for the Sisseton and Wahpeton Dakota and the Cut Head band of Yanktonai Dakota. The Dakota name for the lake is Mni Wakan, Spirit Water. Settlers who were not Native illegally claimed 64,000 acres of the reservation. The Dakota accepted 345,000 dollars for that land. In 1904 federal officials opened 100,000 more acres of the reservation to settlers, at about 4 dollars and 50 cents an acre. By 1934 the Dakota owned a little more than 50,000 acres of the reservation. Whether the Calofs' own claim lay on land taken from the reservation is not known.
