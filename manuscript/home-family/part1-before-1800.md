## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the households of Native nations were built around kin. Kin are a person's relatives by birth or by marriage. Each nation built a kind of house that suited its climate and the way its families got their food. In many nations, several related families lived under one roof. Children learned to work by working beside adults, and old people lived in the same household as the young.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The longhouse" -->
In a Haudenosaunee longhouse, every family traced itself back through the mothers' side. The longhouse was a long building with a frame of wooden posts, covered with bark. One longhouse held many related families. Some accounts give 20 or more families in one house, and others give 30 to 60 people.

Each longhouse family began with one woman ancestor, and everyone in the house was related to her. The head of the house was the clan mother, the eldest respected woman of the clan. A clan is a large group of families who come down from the same ancestor. Women ran the longhouse and owned its everyday goods.

A woman's daughters and granddaughters lived in their longhouse all their lives. When they married, their husbands came to live with them. A son moved out when he married, into his wife's longhouse. He still belonged to his mother's longhouse, and his first loyalty stayed there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The earth lodge" -->
Among the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara nations on the upper Missouri River, women built the houses and owned them. Their house was the earth lodge, a dome made of timber and packed over with earth. An older woman directed the building, and women kept the lodge in repair.

One earth lodge held about 10 to 20 people. Some sources say up to 30. A lodge lasted about ten years. The large villages had more than 100 lodges each.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pueblos, wigwams and tipis" -->
Taos Pueblo is the northernmost of the nineteen Pueblos of what is now New Mexico. Its people built their homes of adobe, which is earth mixed with water and straw. They poured the adobe into forms or shaped it into bricks and dried them in the sun. The two main buildings, the North House and the South House, rise five stories high. People built them between about 1000 and 1450. Families have lived in them ever since. People have lived at Taos Pueblo without a break for more than 1,000 years.

In the Eastern Woodlands, the forests of the eastern part of the continent, many families lived in wigwams. A wigwam had a frame of saplings, which are thin young trees, covered with bark or with woven mats. On the Plains, the grasslands in the middle of the continent, families lived in tipis covered with animal hides. A family could take a tipi down and pack it up, so the household could move with the hunt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Few European families lived on this land in the 1500s. Most of the Europeans who came were soldiers, priests and sailors. Native households went on living in their own towns. The European newcomers carried new diseases, and those diseases spread among Native households. At St. Augustine in Florida, Spanish soldiers married Native women, and the town's first households mixed the two peoples.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, built in Seloy's town" -->
In September 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and about 800 Spanish colonists came ashore at Seloy, a large town of the Timucua people. Its principal chief was also named Seloy. The colonists included 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. Menéndez and his colonists made their first settlement inside Seloy's town. They used Seloy's council house, the building where the town held its meetings, as their first fort. Seloy's town covered more than 12 acres. Its remains lie under what is now the Fountain of Youth Park in St. Augustine.

The settlement Menéndez started became St. Augustine. People have lived there ever since. It is the oldest European settlement on the US mainland where people have lived without a break.

The Timucua traced their families and their inheritance through the mother's line. Fewer than half of the Spanish men in St. Augustine were married, and fewer than half of the married men had a Spanish wife. From 1565 on, many Spanish soldiers married Native women.

The records of the town's church, the Cathedral Parish, begin on January 24, 1594. They are the oldest church records that survive in the United States. The first marriage in them is dated February 2, 1594, and the first baptism June 25. A baptism is a church ceremony that welcomes a person, often a baby, into the church.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Teresa and Francisco Camacho

> **Who:** A Spanish soldier, his Native wife, her sister and her sister's son, living as one household.
> **When and where:** St. Augustine, Florida, 1580.

In 1580 one household in St. Augustine held four people. They were Francisco Camacho, his wife Teresa, Teresa's sister Catalina and Catalina's son Juan. Teresa was a Native woman. The record of the household does not state her nation.

Francisco came to Florida in the 1560s from San Lúcar de Barrameda, a town near Cádiz in Spain. He was a soldier in the town's regiment, a unit of the army. He also worked as a fisherman.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most English colonial families in the 1600s lived in a house with one room. The whole household cooked, ate, worked and slept in it. At one end was the hearth, the open fireplace. Its fire gave the room its heat and light, and the family cooked over it. Nearly everyone in the house worked, children included. Many boys left home for good at about 14 to learn a trade in another household.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth, built at Patuxet" -->
The English who founded Plymouth Colony in 1620 built their houses at Patuxet, a Wampanoag village on Plymouth harbor. Between 1616 and 1619, before they came, a disease killed most of the Native people living on the southeast coast of what is now Massachusetts. Estimates of the deaths run from one-third of the people to as many as 90 percent. Nobody knows for certain what the disease was. Researchers have proposed plague, yellow fever, smallpox and several other diseases.

In 1614 an English sea captain named Thomas Hunt captured 20 men from Patuxet and 7 from Nauset. He took them to Spain to be sold as slaves. One of the Patuxet men was Tisquantum, also known as Squanto. He made his way home to Patuxet in 1619. He found the houses empty and the cornfields grown over. When the English arrived the next year, he was one of the last of the Patuxet people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One room, then two" -->
The basic English house in Massachusetts had a single room, called the hall. Next to the hall stood the chimney bay, the part of the house that held the chimney, the front door and the stairs. The hearth in the hall was where the family cooked, kept warm, had light and did its work.

A family that could afford it added a second room, called the parlor. This plan is called the hall-and-parlor house. The hall was still for cooking and work. The parlor was the best room, and it usually held a bed. Colonists went on building one-room houses well into the 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Big families, short childhoods" -->
A New England mother had about 8 babies, on average, and about 6 of them lived to grow up. People in New England lived long lives for the time. In the Chesapeake, the colonies around Chesapeake Bay, far more children died. About 1 baby in 4 died as an infant. About another 1 in 4 died before the age of 20. Most children in the Chesapeake lost a mother, a father or both before they reached their teens.

A household also held people who were not family. Indentured servants lived and worked in the house as members of the household. An indentured servant had signed a contract to work for a master for a set number of years. During the 1600s, colonists also held more and more enslaved people inside their households.

At about 14, and sometimes younger, a boy could leave home for good. His parents and a master signed a written contract, called an indenture, that bound the boy to the master. The boy served the master until the age of 21, for as long as 7 years, and learned the master's trade. The master took the place of a parent. He had to feed the boy, give him a place to sleep, train him and teach him. About two-thirds of these contracts required the master to teach the boy reading or writing. At the end, the young man got freedom dues, a payment of clothes, tools or money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-ring" name="Mary Ring" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Ring

> **Who:** A widow in Plymouth Colony whose will and list of belongings survive.
> **When and where:** Plymouth Colony. She came in 1629 or 1630 and died in July 1631.

After Mary Ring died, people of the colony wrote down her belongings one by one. A list like this is called an inventory. Her inventory survives, and it records what one Plymouth household owned.

Mary Ring's husband, William Ring, died in the city of Leiden, in the Netherlands, sometime between 1620 and 1629. In 1620 the couple had planned to sail to America. They were among 20 people who could not go when their ship, the Speedwell, turned back. In 1629 or 1630 Mary Ring came to Plymouth as a widow with her three children, Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. She died in July 1631.

She wrote her own will and signed her name to it. A will is a paper that says who gets a person's belongings after death. Colony officials accepted the will in court on October 28, 1633.

Her inventory lists her books. They were a Bible, a psalm book, and books called *A Plea for Infants* and *Garland of Virtuous Dames*. A psalm book contains the songs from the Bible's Book of Psalms. Her clothes came in violet, red, blue, grey, white and black, and in a mixed color. Her will lists green cloth "to make a coat."

The inventory also lists money owed to her. Governor William Bradford owed her 2 pounds, and Edward Winslow owed her beaver fur. All three of her children married in the colony.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s, wealthy colonists began to build houses in which each room had its own use. Most families still lived in houses of one or two rooms. In nearly every household, women and children made much of what the family used, from cloth to candles, and much of that work went on at the hearth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Georgian house" -->
The Georgian house was built to match on both sides, with windows set in even rows. Its builders copied the proportions of ancient Greek and Roman buildings. A central hall ran through the middle of the house, with rooms opening off it. The formal rooms, the parlors and the dining room, were at the front. The rooms for work were farther back. Some Georgian houses had four chimneys, one to heat each of the four main rooms.

Separate rooms gave the people in a family privacy, the chance to be apart from one another. Only wealthy families could pay for a house like this.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The hearth as a workplace" -->
Cooks worked over the open fire in the hearth. Around that fire, a household made much of what it used. Its members spun and wove cloth, and they made candles and soap. They made butter and cheese, and they preserved food so it would keep. Most of this work was done by women and children.

The historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich described this work in northern New England in her 1982 book *Good Wives*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-kemble-knight" name="Sarah Kemble Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Kemble Knight

> **Who:** A Boston businesswoman who rode from Boston toward New Haven and New York and kept a journal of the households where she stayed.
> **When and where:** October 1704, on the road through Massachusetts, Connecticut and New York. She lived from 1666 to 1727.

Sarah Kemble Knight kept a journal of a trip she made on horseback in 1704. In it she wrote about the road, the inns and the households where she stayed the night. Few pieces of writing by American women survive from that time, and her journal is one of them.

Knight lived in Boston, where she ran a business and also taught. In October 1704 she set out from Boston for New Haven and New York on legal business. The journal records what she saw inside the houses where she stopped along the way.

Her journal stayed unprinted for more than 120 years. Theodore Dwight printed it in 1825.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1750 and 1800, families on the frontier lived in log cabins, and families in older settlements lived in farmhouses. Households of both kinds made most of what they used. Spinning, weaving, making candles and soap, and preserving food were the work of women and children. When men left home during the Revolutionary War, women ran the farms and kept the money accounts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the log cabin came from" -->
Settlers from Sweden and Finland brought the log cabin with notched corners to America. In a cabin like this, the builders cut notches near the ends of the logs so that the logs locked together at the corners. These settlers came to the colony of New Sweden on the Delaware River from 1638. Among them were the Forest Finns, who were skilled at building with logs. The Nothnagle Log House in Greenwich Township, New Jersey, was built in about 1638 to 1643, and it still stands.

New Sweden was built on Lenape land. In the spring of 1638, Peter Minuit's Swedish and Dutch crew sailed up the Delaware River. They made agreements with several sachems, or leaders, of the Lenape and Susquehannock nations. With the sachems' permission, the crew built Fort Christina. Several thousand Lenape lived in separate communities along the river, and people at the time counted them as the owners of the land beside it. The Lenape welcomed the colony because its people brought goods to trade. New Sweden lasted 17 years before the Dutch took control of it.

The English at Plymouth and at Jamestown did not build log cabins. The log cabin spread later. By the second half of the 1700s it was the usual first house of a family on the frontier.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="abigail-adams-home-family" name="Abigail Adams" movie="John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abigail Adams

> **Who:** Ran her family's farm, its money and her children's education through the Revolutionary War while her husband, John Adams, was away.
> **When and where:** Braintree, Massachusetts, the part of town now called Quincy, 1774 to 1784.
> **Movie:** *John Adams* (2008), a miniseries made for HBO. It is about John Adams and his family, and Abigail Adams is one of its main characters.

From 1774, Abigail Adams ran the family farm at Braintree, Massachusetts, and managed its money. Her husband, John Adams, was away for years at a time. First he served in the Continental Congress, the meeting of leaders from the colonies. Then he worked in Europe as a diplomat, a person who represents a country to other governments. Abigail taught the children herself. She and John wrote each other more than 1,100 letters, and those letters record how she ran the household.

In July 1776 she took her four children to Boston to be inoculated against smallpox. Caught the natural way, smallpox killed about 30 of every 100 people who got it. Inoculation meant giving a healthy person smallpox on purpose. A doctor cut the skin with a small blade called a lancet. Then he put in a thread soaked in pus from a smallpox sore. About 2 or 3 of every 100 people inoculated died.

On July 13, 1776, Abigail wrote to John from Boston: "I yesterday arrived and was with all 4 of our Little ones innoculated for the small pox." Their doctor was Dr. Bulfinch. She wrote that the children "stood the opperation Manfully." Nabby, who was 10, broke out in many painful sores. Johnny, who was 9, had a light case. Charles, who was 6, had to be inoculated three times before the smallpox took.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="abigail-adams-home-family" -->

### Martha Ballard

> **Who:** A midwife in Maine whose daily diary, kept for 27 years, records the work of an ordinary American household.
> **When and where:** Hallowell, Maine. The diary runs from January 1785 to 1812.
> **Movie:** *A Midwife's Tale* (1997), a documentary made for the PBS series *American Experience*. It is about Martha Ballard.

Martha Ballard wrote in her diary nearly every day for 27 years, from January 1785 to 1812. It contains about 10,000 entries over 9,965 days. She was a midwife in Hallowell, Maine. A midwife is a woman who helps other women give birth. The diary records 816 births that Ballard helped with. She gave each birth a number and wrote down a name for each one.

Around those births, the diary records the daily work of her own household. It describes her garden, spinning, weaving, quilting and brewing, along with the work her daughters did, the visitors who came and the weather each day.

In 1990 the historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich published a book about the diary, *A Midwife's Tale*. Ulrich received the Pulitzer Prize, one of the best-known American prizes for writing, for the book. The whole diary can be read online at DoHistory.org.
