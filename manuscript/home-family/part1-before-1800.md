## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 7 of 37 · Home and Family Life · 10 eras · about 60 minutes**

**The American home changed shape in every era, and again and again people with power decided which families could have one, and which could stay together.**

A Hidatsa woman named Buffalo Bird Woman told the story of her girlhood to a scientist, who published it in 1921. Home was her mothers' earth lodge on the Missouri River, with a floor more than forty feet across and a fire in the center. The women of the family owned it. Her father had four wives, all sisters, and she was taught to call all four her mothers. By 1921, she said, "We no longer live in an earth lodge, but in a house with chimneys."

This chapter describes American homes from the earth lodges and longhouses of the Native nations to the houses where parents worked and children went to school online in 2020. From era to era the houses change: the one-room colonial house, the log cabin, the city tenement, the sod house cut from the prairie, the bungalow and the suburban ranch house. As you read, watch four things: who lived together under one roof, who did the work of the house, whose land it stood on, and who decided which families could live there.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Who lived together under one roof, from the longhouse to today?
- Who did the work of keeping house, and did stoves, pipes and wires make it lighter?
- How did lenders and builders keep Black families out of new neighborhoods, and what did those families do?

**Five minutes?** Read "New home loans, and the red lines" in 1900 to 1950, then the story of Clyde Ross in 1950 to 2000.
**Can skip:** "Where the log cabin came from", "Bungalows, and houses sold by catalog", "The TV in the living room". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 3 minutes**

A Native household was built around kin, a person's relatives by birth or by marriage, and in many nations several related families lived under one roof. Each nation built the kind of house that suited its climate and the way its families got their food. Children learned to work by working beside adults, and old people lived in the same household as the young.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was in charge of a longhouse?" -->
Women ran the longhouse among the Haudenosaunee, several Native nations joined together in one union. The head of each house was the clan mother. She was the eldest or most respected woman of her clan, a large group of families who come down from the same ancestor. Women owned the house's everyday goods. The women of a longhouse and their children all came down from one woman who lived long ago, and every family traced itself back through the mothers' side.

The longhouse was a long building with a frame of wooden posts, covered with bark, and it held many related families. Two families lived in each section of the house, one on each side of an aisle, and they shared one fire in the middle. By different counts, one house held 30 to 60 people, or 20 families or more.

A woman's daughters and granddaughters lived in the longhouse all their lives. When they married, their husbands came to live with them. A son moved out when he married, into his wife's longhouse, but he still belonged to his mother's.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The earth lodge" -->
Among the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara nations on the upper Missouri River, women built the houses and owned them. Their house was the earth lodge, a dome made of timber and packed over with earth. An older woman directed the building, and women kept the lodge in repair.

One earth lodge held about 10 to 20 people, most often sisters and their families. Markers put up by the State Historical Society of North Dakota state that most lodges were 35 to 45 feet across, and that one lodge took about 150 trees to build. A lodge lasted about ten years, and then the women built it again. The large villages had more than 100 lodges each.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pueblos, wigwams and tipis" -->
Taos Pueblo is a town of many homes joined together and stacked on top of one another, the kind of Native town called a pueblo. It is the northernmost of the nineteen Pueblos of what is now New Mexico. Its people built their homes of adobe, earth mixed with water and straw. They poured the adobe into molds or shaped it into bricks and dried them in the sun.

The two main buildings, the North House and the South House, rise five stories high. By the Pueblo's own estimate, people built them between about 1000 and 1450, and families have lived in them ever since. People have lived at Taos Pueblo without a break for more than 1,000 years.

In the Eastern Woodlands, the forests of the eastern part of the continent, many families lived in wigwams. A wigwam had a frame of saplings, thin young trees, covered with bark or with woven mats. On the Plains, the grasslands in the middle of the continent, families lived in tipis covered with animal hides. A family could take a tipi down and pack it up, so the household could move with the hunt.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

Most of the Europeans who came to this land in the 1500s were soldiers, priests and sailors, so European families were few, and Native households went on living in their own towns.

The newcomers carried diseases that Native people had never had before, and those diseases made Native people sick and killed many of them. About 200,000 Timucua people lived in what is now northern Florida and southern Georgia before the Spanish came. After one outbreak of disease after another, only about 2,000 were left by the 1650s, 1 for every 100 who had lived there. No record counts how many died in the 1500s alone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, built in Seloy's town" -->
Chapter 6 tells how Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine in September 1565, inside Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. The town's chief, also named Seloy, at first offered Menéndez his council house, the building where the town held its meetings, and the Spanish used it as their first fort. Within months the Timucua were attacking the newcomers to drive them away. Seloy's town covered more than 12 acres, and its remains lie under what is now the Fountain of Youth Park in St. Augustine.

Few of the newcomers came as families. Of the 800 or so colonists, 26 were women, and an unknown number were enslaved Africans, people the Spanish owned as property and forced to work without pay.

Most Spanish men in St. Augustine had no wife. Most of the men who did marry had wives who were not Spanish, and from 1565 on, many Spanish soldiers married Native women. The Timucua traced their families and their inheritance through the mother's line.

Church officials in the town wrote down the names of the church's members. Their lists are called the Cathedral Parish records, after the parish, the group of people who belong to one church. The records begin on January 24, 1594, and they are the oldest church records that survive in the United States. The first marriage in them is dated February 2, 1594. The first baptism, the church ceremony that welcomes a person, often a baby, into the church, is dated June 25.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Teresa and Francisco Camacho

> **Who:** One of the mixed Spanish and Native households of early St. Augustine.
> **When and where:** St. Augustine, Florida, 1580.

**A Spanish soldier, his Native wife, her sister and her son.**

One household in St. Augustine in 1580 held four people: Francisco Camacho, his wife Teresa, Teresa's sister Catalina and Catalina's son Juan. Teresa was a Native woman.

Francisco had come to Florida in the 1560s from San Lúcar de Barrameda, a town near Cádiz in Spain. He was a soldier in a regiment, a unit of the army, and he also worked as a fisherman.

**Not known:** Teresa's nation. The account of this household does not name it.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

At Plymouth in 1620, English colonists built their houses on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag village. The usual English house in Massachusetts had one room, where the family lived and worked, and many households also held servants, enslaved people and boys from other families learning a trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Patuxet's houses stood empty" -->
An English sea captain named Thomas Hunt kidnapped 20 Native people from Patuxet, a village of the Wampanoag nation on Plymouth harbor, in 1614. He also took 7 from Nauset, another Native community, and carried them all to Spain to sell them as slaves.

Then, between 1616 and 1619, a disease killed most of the Native people on the southeast coast of what is now Massachusetts. Estimates of the deaths run from one-third of the people to as many as 90 percent.

One of the kidnapped Patuxet men was Tisquantum, also known as Squanto. He made his way home in 1619 and found the houses empty and the cornfields grown over. The English who founded Plymouth Colony arrived the next year and built their houses at Patuxet. By then he was one of the last of the Patuxet people.

**Not known:** what the disease was. Researchers have proposed several deadly diseases, among them plague, yellow fever and smallpox.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One room, then two" -->
The basic English house in Massachusetts had a single room, called the hall. Next to it stood the chimney bay, the part of the house that held the chimney, the front door and the stairs. The family cooked, kept warm, had light and did its work at the hearth, the open fireplace in the hall that gave the room its heat and light.

The next step up was the hall-and-parlor house, with a second room called the parlor. The hall was still for cooking and work. The parlor was the best room, and it usually held a bed. Colonists went on building one-room houses well into the 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Births, deaths, servants and apprentices" -->
A New England mother had about 8 babies, on average, and about 6 of them lived to grow up. People in New England lived long lives for the time. In the Chesapeake, the colonies around Chesapeake Bay, about half of all children died before the age of 20. About 1 baby in 4 died as an infant, and about another 1 in 4 before 20. Most children in the Chesapeake lost a mother, a father or both before they reached their teens.

A household also held people who were not family. Indentured servants, who had signed a contract to work for a master for a set number of years, lived and worked in the house as members of the household. Through the 1600s, colonists owned more and more enslaved people, and they forced them to work in their households without pay.

**Apprentices.** At about 14, and sometimes younger, a boy could leave home for good. His parents and a master signed a written contract called an indenture, and under it the boy had to live with the master and work for him. He served until the age of 21, for as long as 7 years, and learned the master's trade.

The master took the place of a parent. In the indenture he promised to feed the boy, give him a place to sleep, train him in the trade and give him some schooling. About two-thirds of these contracts required the master to teach the boy reading or writing. At the end, the young man got freedom dues, a payment of clothes, tools or money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-ring" name="Mary Ring" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Ring

> **Who:** A widow in Plymouth Colony whose will and list of belongings record what one household owned.
> **When and where:** Plymouth Colony, from 1629 or 1630. She died in July 1631.

**Governor Bradford owed her 2 pounds, and Winslow owed her beaver.**

After Mary Ring died in July 1631, her belongings were written down one by one, in a list called an inventory. It survives, and it records what one Plymouth household owned.

The inventory lists her books: a Bible, a psalm book with the songs from the Bible's Book of Psalms, and books called *A Plea for Infants* and *Garland of Virtuous Dames*. Her clothes came in violet, red, blue, grey, white and black, and in a mixed color. Her will lists green cloth "to make a coat."

The inventory also lists money owed to her. Governor William Bradford owed her 2 pounds in English money, written in the record as "Due from Gov." Edward Winslow owed her beaver fur, which the record spells the old way: "Bever due from mr Wynslow."

She wrote her own will, the paper that says who gets a person's belongings after death, and signed her name to it. The colony's court officials proved the will on October 28, 1633, more than two years after she died, which means they agreed it was real, so it could be carried out. By then both of the men she had named to oversee the will had died, and her son Andrew was still a child. Another colonist, Thomas Prence, took on the job of looking after Andrew's share.

Her husband, William Ring, died in the city of Leiden, in the Netherlands, sometime between 1620 and 1629. The Rings belonged to a church of English Separatists, people who wanted to worship apart from the Church of England. The church had moved to Leiden, and in 1620 some of its members sailed to America and founded Plymouth.

The Rings had planned to go too, but they were among 20 people who could not sail when their ship, the Speedwell, turned back. Mary Ring came to Plymouth as a widow in 1629 or 1630 with her three children, Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. All three married in the colony.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 2 minutes**

Each room had its own use in the new houses that wealthy colonists built in the early 1700s, but most families still lived in one or two rooms. In every kind of house, women and children made much of what the household used, from cloth to candles, and much of that work went on at the hearth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A room for every use: the Georgian house" -->
Wealthy colonists built a style of house now called Georgian, with both sides built to match. Its builders took its balanced look from ancient Greek and Roman buildings. A central hall ran through the middle of the house, with rooms opening off it. The formal rooms, the parlors and the dining room, were at the front, and the rooms for work were farther back. Some Georgian houses had four chimneys, one to heat each of the four main rooms.

In a house with separate rooms, the people in a family could be apart from one another and have some privacy. Houses like this belonged to wealthy families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a household made at the fire" -->
Around the open fire in the hearth, a household spun and wove cloth and made candles, soap, butter and cheese. Its members preserved food so it would keep, and the cooks worked over the same fire. Women and children did most of this work. The historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich described it in northern New England in her 1982 book *Good Wives*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-kemble-knight" name="Sarah Kemble Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Kemble Knight

> **Who:** A Boston businesswoman whose journal of a ride to New York records the households where she stayed.
> **When and where:** On the road from Boston to New Haven and New York and back, October 1704 to early 1705.

**A bed "as hard as it was high," warmed with a hot stone.**

Sarah Kemble Knight lived in Boston, where she ran a business and taught. In October 1704 she set out on horseback for New Haven and New York on legal business, and she kept a journal of the trip. In it she wrote about the road, the inns and the households where she stayed the night.

She stayed in New York for about two weeks and started home on December 21. Near Eastchester, New York, her party got lost in a great storm of wind and snow. One woman would hardly let them through her door, but the woman's son invited them in.

He showed Knight a bed "as hard as it was high," and he warmed it with a hot stone at her feet. The family had nothing in the house but milk. They boiled it and sweetened it with molasses for her.

Her journal stayed unprinted for about 120 years, until Theodore Dwight printed it in 1825.

**Why this story is here:** Few pieces of writing by American women survive from that time, and her journal is one of them.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 4 minutes**

When men left home during the Revolutionary War, women ran the households. A household still made most of what it used, and women and children still did most of that work. On the frontier, the edge of the colonists' settlements on land where Native nations lived, families made their homes in log cabins. Families in the older settlements lived in farmhouses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the log cabin came from" -->
The log cabin was the usual first house of a frontier family by the second half of the 1700s. Settlers from Sweden and Finland had brought it to America more than a hundred years earlier. To build one, they cut notches near the ends of the logs so that the logs locked together at the corners.

These settlers came to the colony of New Sweden on the Delaware River from 1638. Among them were the Forest Finns, who were skilled at building with logs. The Nothnagle Log House in Greenwich Township, New Jersey, built in about 1638 to 1643, still stands. A common story says the first English colonists lived in log cabins, but the English at Plymouth and at Jamestown did not build them.

**Whose land.** New Sweden was built on Lenape land. Peter Minuit's Swedish and Dutch crew sailed up the Delaware River in the spring of 1638 and made agreements with several sachems, or leaders, of the Lenape and Susquehannock nations. With the sachems' permission, the crew built Fort Christina.

Several thousand Lenape lived in separate communities along the river, and people at the time counted them as the owners of the land beside it. The Lenape welcomed the colony because its people brought goods to trade. New Sweden lasted 17 years before the Dutch took control of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="abigail-adams-home-family" name="Abigail Adams" movie="John Adams (2008 HBO miniseries)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abigail Adams

> **Who:** Ran her family's farm, its money and her children's education through the Revolutionary War while her husband, John Adams, was away.
> **When and where:** Braintree, Massachusetts, in the part that is now the city of Quincy, 1774 to 1784.
> **Movie:** *John Adams* (2008), a miniseries made for HBO. It is about John Adams and his family, and Abigail Adams is one of its main characters.

**Charles, who was 6, was given smallpox on purpose three times.**

Abigail Adams ran the family farm at Braintree, Massachusetts, and managed its money from 1774, while her husband, John Adams, was away for years at a time. He served first in the Continental Congress, the meeting of leaders from the colonies, and then in Europe as a diplomat, a person who represents a country to other governments. Abigail taught the children herself, and she and John wrote each other more than 1,100 letters.

**The inoculation.** She took all four children to Boston in July 1776 to be inoculated, which meant giving a healthy person smallpox on purpose so that the person got a milder case. A doctor cut the skin with a small blade called a lancet. Then he put in a thread soaked in pus from a smallpox sore.

Caught the natural way, smallpox killed about 30 of every 100 people who got it. Of every 100 people inoculated, about 2 or 3 died. People who lived through smallpox usually did not catch it again, so inoculation gave them that protection at far less risk.

Abigail wrote to John from Boston on July 13, 1776: "I yesterday arrived and was with all 4 of our Little ones innoculated for the small pox." Their doctor was Dr. Bulfinch. She wrote that the children "stood the opperation Manfully," which meant they were brave about it.

Nabby, who was 10, broke out in many painful sores, while 9-year-old Johnny had a light case. Charles was 6, and he had to be inoculated three times before he caught the smallpox. Thomas, the fourth child, showed no sickness at first and had to be inoculated again.

Her years of running the farm alone ended in 1784, when she and Nabby sailed to Europe to join John.

### Martha Ballard

> **Who:** A midwife in Maine whose daily diary, kept for 27 years, records the work of an ordinary American household.
> **When and where:** Hallowell, Maine. The diary runs from January 1785 to 1812.
> **Movie:** *A Midwife's Tale* (1997), a documentary made for the PBS series *American Experience*. It is about Martha Ballard.

**She wrote down 816 births, each one with a name.**

Martha Ballard was a midwife, a woman who helps other women give birth, in Hallowell, Maine. She wrote in her diary nearly every day for 27 years, from January 1785 to 1812: about 10,000 entries over 9,965 days. The diary records 816 births that she helped with.

Around those births, the diary records the daily work of her own household: her garden, spinning, weaving, quilting and brewing, the work her daughters did, the visitors who came and the weather each day.

The historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, who wrote *Good Wives*, published a book about the diary, *A Midwife's Tale*, in 1990. Ulrich won the Pulitzer Prize, a yearly American prize for writing, for the book. The whole diary can be read online at DoHistory.org.
