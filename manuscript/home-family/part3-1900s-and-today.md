## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Running water and electric power reached most American homes between 1900 and 1950. City homes got them first, and farms waited decades longer. In the Great Depression, the hard times of the 1930s, families who could not pay their home loans lost their houses. Federal officials then changed home loans so that a family could pay for a house month by month over many years. The same officials rated Black neighborhoods "Hazardous" and usually refused to back loans there. In 1947 the first families moved into Levittown on Long Island, New York. It was a new suburb, a town of houses built at the edge of a city.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Water, wires and washday" -->
Without running water or electric power, washing a family's clothes by hand took more than one day. The clothes were sorted and the stains treated. Then they soaked in warm soapy water for one or two days. Next came scrubbing on a washboard, a ridged board set in a tub of warm water and soap. The clothes were boiled in a pot with soap and soda to kill lice, and stirred with a wooden stick called a dolly. After rinsing, they hung out to dry, with the whites in the sun to bleach them. Last came the ironing, with flatirons heated over the coals and held with a rag around the handle.

Water pipes and electric wires reached homes unevenly. By 1940, 74 of every 100 American homes had running water and 83 had electric lights. By 1950, 83 of every 100 households had running water and 94 had electricity. The Census Bureau counted a home as having complete plumbing only if it had hot and cold running water, a bathtub or shower, and a flush toilet. In 1940 nearly half of American homes lacked complete plumbing, and more than one home in three had no flush toilet. In about ten states, the share without complete plumbing was near 70 percent or higher. Most of the homes without running water were in the country. In 1940 more than half of American homes were heated with coal, and another quarter with wood.

Farms got electric power last. In 1935 fewer than 11 of every 100 farms had it. About half had it by 1945, and about 9 in 10 by 1950.

Families also changed how they kept food cold. In 1930, 14 of every 100 households had a modern refrigerator. The number rose to 44 in 1940 and to 80 in 1950. Households without one used an icebox, a cabinet cooled by a block of ice. They also relied on milk delivered each day, on canned food and on stores that sold fresh food.

The historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan studied what the new machines did to a housewife's work. In her 1983 book *More Work for Mother*, she argued that the machines mostly replaced work that servants, children and men had done before. At the same time, families expected clothes washed more often, houses kept cleaner and more cooking done. So, in Cowan's argument, the housewife's own hours of work did not fall. Other scholars have written papers testing her argument. Census counts record one part of the change. The number of domestic servants fell by 32 percent between 1940 and 1950.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bungalow and the mail-order house" -->
The small family house of this era was the bungalow. From 1908 to 1940, Sears, Roebuck and Company sold whole houses by catalog. A buyer chose a design from the catalog. The house came by railroad in boxcars, as a kit of about 25 tons of materials with more than 30,000 parts. Many buyers put the house together themselves with relatives, friends and neighbors. Others hired local carpenters. The first Sears house catalog, in 1908, offered 44 designs priced from 360 dollars to 2,890 dollars. Nobody kept an official count of how many kit houses Sears sold. The usual estimate is 70,000 to 75,000, and some estimates run as high as 100,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sickness in a tenement flat" -->
The Rogarshevskys were a Jewish family from Lithuania who lived at 97 Orchard Street in New York from about 1908. On July 17, 1918, Abraham Rogarshevsky died there of tuberculosis. Tuberculosis is a disease caused by a germ. It usually attacks the lungs, and it spreads through the air when a sick person coughs or talks. Tuberculosis was then known as "the tailor's disease." After Abraham died, his widow, Fanny, became the building's janitor, the worker who cleaned the building and kept it in repair. She also took in boarders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The mortgage remade" -->
Before the 1930s, a home loan was hard to pay off. A mortgage is a loan to buy a house. If the borrower does not repay it, the lender can take the house. The usual mortgage ran for 5 to 10 years. Each month the borrower paid only the interest, the fee for borrowing the money. At the end, the borrower owed the whole amount at once. Buyers also had to pay about half the price up front, as a down payment. In the Great Depression many borrowers could not pay. Lenders then foreclosed, which means they took the houses back.

In June 1933 members of Congress passed the Home Owners' Loan Act. Under it, federal officials set up the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, or HOLC. HOLC officials replaced families' old loans with new loans that ran for 15 years at a fixed rate. The family paid the same amount every month, and each payment paid off part of the loan itself. In 1934 members of Congress set up the Federal Housing Administration, or FHA. FHA officials insured home loans, which means they promised to repay the lender if the family could not. Those loans ran 20 to 25 years and needed only a small down payment. After the Second World War the 30-year loan became the usual kind. A family could now buy a house by paying a set amount each month for many years.

Between 1935 and 1940, HOLC staff graded the neighborhoods of American cities. They worked with local bank loan officers, city officials, real estate agents and appraisers, people whose job is to judge what a property is worth. Each neighborhood got a letter and a color. A was green, for "Best." B was blue, for "Still Desirable." C was yellow, for "Definitely Declining." D was red, for "Hazardous." FHA officials used maps of their own. They rated neighborhoods where Black people lived "D" and usually refused to insure loans there. Drawing a red line around a neighborhood to cut it off from loans is called redlining.

Researchers at the National Community Reinvestment Coalition, a group that works for fair lending, studied 115 of the HOLC maps. Of the neighborhoods graded "Hazardous," they found that 74 of every 100 are low or moderate income today. Nearly 64 of every 100 are neighborhoods where most people belong to racial or ethnic minority groups.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Depression households" -->
The Great Depression changed who lived together. Couples waited to marry. In 1929 there were 10.1 marriages each year for every 1,000 Americans. By 1932 there were 7.9. For the first time in US history, parents had too few babies to replace their own generation. Families doubled up in one home. Young married couples moved back in with their parents, and old parents moved in with their grown children. By 1940, 1.5 million married women lived apart from their husbands.

In Chicago, Black families who had moved north from the South settled in a South Side neighborhood called Bronzeville. Landlords there split apartments into one-room units called kitchenettes. Bronzeville held more than 80,000 of them. A whole family lived in one room and shared a bathroom down the hall with other families. The writer Richard Wright recorded this life in his 1941 book *12 Million Black Voices*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanking and the new advice" -->
Advice to parents changed in the 1920s and 1930s, as experts on child development and psychology became popular. Psychology is the study of the mind and of how people behave. In 1928 John B. Watson published *Psychological Care of Infant and Child*, and it sold 100,000 copies within months. Watson told parents: "Never hug and kiss them. Never let them sit in your lap." He advised them to "shake hands with them in the morning."

Between 1924 and 1939, parents wrote to Angelo Patri, a popular expert on raising children, to ask for advice. In 2004 three researchers published a study of 147 of these letters that mentioned corporal punishment, which means hitting a child as a punishment. Most came from middle-class parents. The parents wrote more about whether hitting worked than about whether it harmed the child. The reasons they gave most often were that the child had disobeyed or been rude. One letter in four described an argument about hitting with another adult close to the family.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The teenager, and the war at home" -->
A new stage of life got its own name in these years. The stage grew partly because more children stayed in high school instead of going to work. Writers had printed "teen ager," as two words, as early as 1913. A 1941 article in *Popular Science* printed "teen-agers," and this is usually named as the first use in print as a single word. The word spread in the late 1940s, when advertisers used it to sell goods to young people.

During the Second World War, individuals and families moved to cities with war factories to take well-paid jobs. More Americans moved from one part of the country to another in these years than in any earlier period. Richmond, California, grew from fewer than 24,000 people to more than 100,000 during the war. About six million women took jobs in the war industries, many of them jobs that women had not held before. At home, federal officials limited how much of twenty kinds of goods each family could buy, and families shopped with ration books.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### The Baldizzi family

> **Who:** A Sicilian immigrant family who lived through the Great Depression in a tenement flat. Museum staff later rebuilt their apartment from the daughter's memories.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, New York, 1928 to 1935.

Adolfo and Rosaria Baldizzi raised their children, Josephine and Johnny, in a flat at 97 Orchard Street from 1928 until 1935, when the building's owner forced the last families out. Adolfo came to the United States from Palermo, in Sicily, in 1923. He was a trained woodworker who made fine furniture. During the Depression he could find only odd jobs. Rosaria sewed the linings into coats in a garment factory. In the evenings the family sat at the kitchen table to play cards and listen to the radio. Adolfo built a corner cabinet for the kitchen himself.

In 1934 and 1935, New York City officials required owners of old tenements to make costly repairs, such as fireproofing. The owner of 97 Orchard Street chose to empty the apartments rather than pay for the work. The owner evicted the families who still lived there, which means the owner forced them to leave. Next the owner boarded up the upper windows and sealed off the floors where people had lived. The Baldizzis had to move.

After the Tenement Museum was founded in 1988, Josephine Baldizzi came back to 97 Orchard Street and recorded hours of memories of her childhood there. Museum staff rebuilt the family's apartment from what she remembered.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="baldizzi-family" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="levittown-family" name="Theodore and Patricia Bladykas" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Theodore and Patricia Bladykas

> **Who:** The first family to move into Levittown, with their 14-month-old twin daughters, Patricia Ann and Betty Ann.
> **When and where:** October 1, 1947, 67 Bellmore Road, Levittown, Long Island, New York.

Theodore and Patricia Bladykas were the first family to move into a house in Levittown. They moved in on October 1, 1947, with their twin daughters, Patricia Ann and Betty Ann, who were 14 months old. A photographer from the newspaper *Newsday* took their picture that day. About 300 families moved into Levittown on that first day.

The builder was the company Levitt and Sons, run by William Levitt. The first houses rented for 60 dollars a month, and a renter could buy the house for 7,500 dollars after a year. A ranch house model from 1949 sold for 7,990 dollars. By 1951 Levitt and Sons had built 17,447 houses there. Many of the fathers who moved in were veterans just back from the Second World War.

William Levitt and his company rented and sold these houses to white families only. Clause 25 of the lease said that no "person other than members of the Caucasian race" could live in the house. Caucasian here means white. The only exception was for servants, who also had to be white. In 1948, in the case *Shelley v. Kraemer*, the justices of the US Supreme Court ruled that courts could not enforce rules like this. William Levitt dropped the written clause and kept refusing to sell to Black families. His sales contracts forbade buyers to resell their houses to African Americans. He said that if a Black family bought a house in Levittown, white families would stop buying there. In 1954 he told the *Saturday Evening Post*: "We can solve a housing problem, or we can solve a racial problem. But we cannot combine the two."

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950 the house in the suburbs became the common picture of American family life. Home ownership rose from 43.6 of every 100 households in 1940 to 61.9 in 1960. Lenders, sellers and white neighbors kept Black families out of many of the new suburbs. In Levittown, Pennsylvania, a mob attacked the house of the first Black family to move in. Inside their homes, families planned their evenings around the new television set. Later in the century, more mothers took paid jobs and the divorce rate more than doubled. Some parents began to teach their children at home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Suburbia as the norm" -->
Between 1946 and 1964, 76 million babies were born in the United States. People call these years the baby boom. Builders put up more towns like Levittown, including a second Levittown in Pennsylvania from 1952. The ranch house, a long, low house of one story, spread through the new suburbs.

The 1948 Supreme Court ruling did not end whites-only housing. Builders, sellers and lenders kept up redlining and whites-only sales for many years after it. Activists campaigned against these practices through the Fair Housing Act of 1968 and after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The TV in the living room" -->
Most American families bought their first television set in the 1950s. About 9 of every 100 households had a set in 1950. By 1955 the figure was 64.5, and by 1960 it was 87. Families began to plan their evenings around the programs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The household changes shape" -->
The divorce rate more than doubled between 1960 and 1980. In 1960 there were 9.2 divorces each year for every 1,000 married women. By 1980 there were 22.6. The rate reached its peak in 1981 and then began to fall.

More mothers took paid jobs. In 1948 about 17 of every 100 married mothers were in the labor force, which means they worked for pay or were looking for paid work. By 1985 the figure was 61, and by 1995 it was about 70. Counting all mothers with children under 18, the share rose from 47 percent in 1975 to a peak of 73 percent in 2000, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. With both parents at work, more families paid for day care.

Some children went home after school to an empty house. People called them latchkey children. Counts of these children disagreed. A 1987 Census Bureau study found about 2.1 million children aged 5 to 13 regularly home alone. Advocacy groups, which campaign for a cause, estimated 6 to 7 million. In the fall of 1995 the Census Bureau counted 6.9 million children aged 5 to 14, or 18 of every 100, who regularly looked after themselves. On average they spent six hours a week that way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School comes home" -->
In the 1970s and 1980s some parents began to teach their children at home instead of sending them to school. This is called homeschooling. It grew from two separate starting points. John Holt wanted to change how schools taught, and in August 1977 he started a newsletter called *Growing Without Schooling*. On the religious side, Raymond Moore argued for keeping young children at home, in his 1981 book *Home Grown Kids*. In many states, homeschooling was against the law, or nearly impossible to do legally, into the 1980s. Families taught at the kitchen table anyway. In some families one parent gave up a paid job to teach, and families formed groups to help each other.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Daisy and William Myers

> **Who:** The first Black family in Levittown, Pennsylvania: a war veteran, a teacher and their three small children, who stayed in their house while crowds attacked it.
> **When and where:** 43 Deepgreen Lane, Levittown, Pennsylvania, from August 13, 1957, to 1961.
> **Movie:** Crisis in Levittown (1957), a documentary by Lee Bobker and Lester Becker, filmed while the events were happening.

On August 13, 1957, William and Daisy Myers and their three small children moved into 43 Deepgreen Lane in Levittown, Pennsylvania. They were the first Black family to live in Levittown. William had served in the Army in the Second World War. Daisy was a teacher. Lewis Wechsler, a white Jewish friend, helped them arrange to buy the house.

Crowds of 200 to 1,000 people gathered on Deepgreen Lane for more than a week. People in the crowds threw rocks through the windows of the house, and they threw Molotov cocktails, which are bottles filled with fuel and set on fire. Members of the crowds also burned crosses, one of them at the Wechsler house. Callers threatened the family by telephone. Deliveries of milk, bread and heating oil to the house stopped, and the family's insurance company canceled its fire insurance. On the seventh night more than 500 people gathered before state troopers broke up the crowd.

Members of the Levittown Betterment Committee passed around a petition against letting Black families live in Levittown, and they organized the attacks on the family. A judge ordered the Betterment group to stop. Pennsylvania Governor George Leader sent the state police, and after about six weeks the troopers had ended the open violence. A court convicted two people of burning the cross on the Wechsler lawn.

Neighbors, Quaker families and church members brought the Myers family food and flowers. The family stayed in the house until 1961, when they moved to York, Pennsylvania. Daisy Myers told the story in her memoir, *Sticks 'n Stones*, published in 2005.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="daisy-william-myers" -->

### Clyde Ross

> **Who:** A Second World War veteran and soup-factory worker who could not get a mortgage and bought his Chicago house on contract.
> **When and where:** North Lawndale, on Chicago's West Side, from 1961.

Clyde Ross bought a house in Chicago in 1961 on terms that let the seller take back everything if he missed a single payment. He was born in 1923 near Clarksdale, Mississippi. He was drafted in the Second World War and served on Guam. In 1947 he moved to Chicago and took a job as a taster at Campbell's Soup.

Ross tried to get an ordinary mortgage for a house in another neighborhood. A loan officer told him there was no money to lend. The records do not name the officer or the bank. FHA officials rated neighborhoods where Black people lived "D," colored them red on their maps, and usually refused to back loans there.

In 1961 Ross bought a house in North Lawndale for 27,500 dollars. The seller was a speculator, a person who buys property to sell it again at a profit, and had paid 12,000 dollars for the house. Ross bought on contract. Under the contract the seller kept the deed, the paper that shows who owns a house, until Ross made the last payment. If Ross missed a single payment, he would lose his 1,000-dollar down payment, every monthly payment he had made, and the house. Another account gives the year as 1958 and the price as 27,000 dollars. By one estimate from the time, 85 of every 100 Black families who bought homes in Chicago bought them on contract.

In 1968 Ross joined the Contract Buyers League, a group of Black homeowners on Chicago's South and West Sides, and became its vice president. About 500 families in the league stopped making their payments together, as a strike against the contracts. By July 1971, 155 contracts had been rewritten, saving each family an average of 14,000 dollars. About 70 league families lost their homes to eviction. The accounts do not name who had each family put out. In 2014 the journalist Ta-Nehisi Coates told Ross's story in *The Atlantic* magazine. Ross was 91, and he still lived in the house and owned it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clyde-ross-home-family" -->

### David and Micki Colfax and their sons

> **Who:** Parents who built a ranch house by hand in California and taught their four sons at home.
> **When and where:** Near Boonville, Mendocino County, California, from 1973.

David and Micki Colfax taught their four sons at home on a ranch in California, where the family built its own house. Their eldest son, Grant, started public school in St. Louis. After six weeks his parents took him out, because they found the lessons "very rote and uncreative." Rote learning means learning by repeating things over and over.

In 1973 the family moved to land near Boonville, in Mendocino County, California. The land had no house, no electricity and no running water. One account says the land was 40 acres, and another says 50. Everyone, the young sons included, helped build the house, the barns and the sheds. The family raised goats, sheep, chickens and pigs and sold them to restaurants in San Francisco.

Lessons fit around the work of the ranch. "Some days we did a lot of homework, some days we had none," Reed Colfax said later. "It depended on the weather and what had to be done on the ranch." The boys studied four or five days a week, summers included. Grant, Drew and Reed went on to Harvard University and graduated in 1987, 1990 and 1992. The youngest son, Garth, was still being taught at home in 1989. That year about 500,000 American children were taught at home by their parents, according to a count reported in *The Harvard Crimson*. David and Micki Colfax wrote a book about their method, *Homeschooling for Excellence*.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
New American houses kept growing larger until 2015, while households kept growing smaller. In the crash that began in 2007, lenders took back millions of homes from families who could not pay their loans. In 2020, during the pandemic, a worldwide outbreak of disease, many homes became an office and a school at the same time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="More house, fewer people" -->
The median new single-family house, the one in the middle when all are ranked by size, grew from 983 square feet in 1950 to a peak of 2,467 square feet in 2015, by Census Bureau figures. Households shrank over the same years. By the Census Bureau's count, the average household held 3.67 people in 1940 and 2.50 people in 2025.

The kinds of households changed too. In 2025, 29 of every 100 households were one person living alone, up from 20 in 1975. Married couples made up 47 of every 100 households in 2025, down from 66 in 1975. In the same year, 58 of every 100 young adults aged 18 to 24 lived in their parents' home. These figures also come from the Census Bureau. In the second quarter of 2026, 65 of every 100 households owned their home, according to the Census Bureau.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2008 at the kitchen table" -->
Between 2007 and 2010, lenders foreclosed on about 3.8 million homes. Estimates for the whole crisis run from about 7.7 million to 10 million homes lost, because researchers counted in different ways. These figures come from Federal Reserve History, a website of the Federal Reserve, the country's central bank, and from the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago.

Many of the lost homes had been bought with subprime loans. A subprime loan is a loan made at a higher cost to a borrower with weaker credit, a weaker record of paying back debts. The biggest subprime lender was Countrywide Financial. Angelo Mozilo founded the company and ran it for 39 years. In 2009 the Center for Public Integrity, a news organization, reported that Countrywide made at least 97.2 billion dollars in high-interest loans between 2005 and 2007.

Foreclosure filings reached their peak in 2010, at nearly 2.9 million homes. In 2025 there were 367,460, according to ATTOM, a private company that collects property records.

Some families who lost their homes moved in with relatives. In 1971, 7 of every 100 Americans lived in a household of several generations, such as grandparents, parents and children together. By 2021 the share was 18 of every 100, or 59.7 million people, according to the Pew Research Center, which studies American life. The number grew fastest during the years of the crash.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homelessness, counted" -->
Each January, local volunteers count the people who are homeless on a single night. The US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), the federal housing department, reports the count. In January 2024 volunteers counted 771,480 people, the highest number on record and 18 percent more than a year before. The number of homeless people in families with children rose 39 percent in that year.

In January 2025 the count was 745,652 people. HUD officials called the change a drop of 3 percent. The National Low Income Housing Coalition, a group that works for affordable homes, gave the drop as 3.4 percent. People in families with children fell 11 percent, to 230,366. The number of people counted as chronically homeless, homeless for a long time, reached 155,750, the highest on record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="2020: office and school at once" -->
In 2020 many homes became a workplace and a classroom at once. The share of workers who worked mainly from home tripled, from 5.7 percent in 2019 to 17.9 percent in 2021, according to the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. In 2025 nearly 22 percent of workers worked from home at least part of the time, according to Census data reported by the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis.

Homeschooling rose too. Here homeschooling means that the family taught the children, not that a school taught them online. In the spring of 2020, 5.4 percent of households with school-age children were homeschooling. By the fall of 2020 the figure was 11.1 percent, according to the Census Bureau's Household Pulse Survey. Among Black households it rose from 3.3 to 16.1 percent.

Many children did their schoolwork online, and some homes had no fast internet connection. The Pew Research Center reported in 2020 that 35 percent of households with children aged 6 to 17 and incomes under 30,000 dollars a year had no broadband, the fast home internet used for online classes. Among households with incomes over 75,000 dollars, 6 percent had none.

Some of the rise in learning at home lasted. In a survey of the National Center for Education Statistics, parents reported that 5.2 percent of children aged 5 to 17 learned at home in the 2022-23 school year, counting children in full-time online school. In 2018-19 the figure had been 3.7 percent. The 2020 survey and this survey asked different questions, so the exact trend cannot be measured. The share learning at home in 2022-23 was higher than before the pandemic and much lower than in the fall of 2020.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="solon-familia-foreclosure" name="Pierre Solon and Katty Familia" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pierre Solon and Katty Familia

> **Who:** Immigrant parents, from Haiti and the Dominican Republic, who lost their Boston house to foreclosure.
> **When and where:** Hyde Park, Boston, Massachusetts, about 2006 to 2010.

Pierre Solon and Katty Familia lost their house in Boston in 2009. Their monthly loan payments were larger than everything they earned. Solon came from Haiti and Familia from the Dominican Republic. In about 2006 they bought a green wooden house in Boston's Hyde Park neighborhood for 584,000 dollars. They borrowed the whole price. The loans came from Fremont Investment and Loan, a subprime lender. Their lawyer, Nadine Cohen of Greater Boston Legal Services, said the two monthly mortgage payments came to almost 5,000 dollars a month more than the couple's income.

Each parent worked two jobs. Then each lost one. "Sometimes we don't even have food," Katty Familia said. The couple fell more than 150,000 dollars behind. Wells Fargo, the bank that collected their payments, offered lower monthly payments on a debt that would have grown to more than 600,000 dollars. The couple turned the offer down. Wells Fargo's staff then foreclosed, and late in 2009 the family moved out with their children.

In November 2010 Wells Fargo sold the house at auction to an investor for 115,000 dollars. Two weeks later that investor sold it to another investor for 270,000 dollars. The Massachusetts Attorney General sued Fremont for deceptive lending, which means lending by misleading borrowers. Fremont went bankrupt and closed. In 2010 the couple ran a day care center in Boston. "I worked 20 years in this country for this," Katty Familia said. The reporter Anthony Brooks told their story on the radio program *Morning Edition*, on NPR, in December 2010.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="munoz-family-2020" name="The Munoz family" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The Munoz family

> **Who:** A Las Vegas family whose four school-age children did school online at the dining room table while both parents went out to work.
> **When and where:** Las Vegas, Nevada, August 2020.

In August 2020, four of the five Munoz children in Las Vegas, Nevada, started the school year on laptops at the dining room table. Both parents worked in the food industry, away from home. While they were at work, the family's 10th-grade son helped the younger children. He worked with a fourth-grader with reading and a new middle-schooler with writing. When he had trouble, he emailed the teachers.

The father, Johnathan Munoz, recorded the family's first week as an audio diary for NPR's radio program *Weekend Edition Sunday*. On the second day he said, "it was a challenge because we couldn't log in." The middle-schooler said that one teacher kept marking her absent. Another teacher did not allow a brother or sister to be seen in the background.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="munoz-family-2020" -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="2000-today" -->
