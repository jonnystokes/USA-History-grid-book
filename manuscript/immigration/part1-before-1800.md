## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 4 of 37 · Immigration to America · 10 eras · about 95 minutes**

**People came here to stay from almost every country on earth, and from the 1600s on, the people who had come earlier kept deciding who could join them, by law and sometimes by mob violence.**

The poet Emma Lazarus wrote a poem in 1883 to help raise money for the stone base of the Statue of Liberty. It includes the lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free." Nobody read it aloud when the statue opened in 1886. In 1903 her friend Georgina Schuyler had the poem mounted on a bronze plaque inside the base. One year before Lazarus wrote those lines, President Chester A. Arthur had signed a law that shut Chinese workers out of the country.

This chapter follows the people who came to stay, from a Spanish fort in Florida in 1565 to the record number of people held in immigration detention in 2026. Watch why they came: for land and work, to worship in their own way, and to escape famine, riots against Jews, revolution and war. Watch, too, who decided whether they could stay. Lawmakers wrote the rules, from citizenship for white people only in 1790 to the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the quotas of 1924. Mobs in Philadelphia, Louisville and Rock Springs used fire and guns. The forced arrival of Africans appears here only where it meets those laws. Chapter 24, Slavery and Freedom, tells it in full.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why did people leave home to come here?
- Who did Americans try to keep out, and how?
- Who could become a citizen, and how did that change?

**Five minutes?** Read "Angel Island" in 1900 to 1950.
**Can skip:** "The Dutch and the Swedes" in the 1600s, "Jewish communities" in 1700 to 1750, "The Revolution and the Loyalists" in 1750 to 1800. The chapter still makes sense without them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 3 minutes**

No one from across the ocean came to settle here for good before 1500. Hundreds of Native nations lived in the land that is now the United States, and their people spoke hundreds of different languages. Every group of newcomers who came later arrived on land where people were already living.

Nobody knows how many people lived north of Mexico in 1492. Estimates run from about 1 million to as many as 18 to 20 million. Chapter 2, Native Nations, tells who they were.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The land bridge and the Norse" -->
The first people to reach this land came on foot from Asia, long ago. They walked across the ice-age land bridge, a stretch of dry ground that joined Asia to North America.

Chapter 1 tells the Norse voyages in full. What matters here is that the Norse did not stay. Around the year 1000, Norse sailors from Greenland, the people often called Vikings, built a camp at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, in what is now Canada. Wood they cut there with metal blades dates to 1021. Before Columbus, it is the only camp that anyone from across the ocean is known to have built in North America.

Native people had lived at that spot long before the Norse came. Five Native groups had lived there, the oldest about 6,000 years earlier. The Norse stayed only a short time, from a few years to about twenty by different estimates, and built no settlement that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

Europeans came to these coasts in the 1500s to conquer and explore, and almost none of them stayed. Most were soldiers, explorers, sailors and Christian missionaries, people sent to bring others into their religion. Native nations held almost all of the land for the whole century. Of three European attempts to settle, only the Spanish town of St. Augustine lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Which settlement lasted?" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, lasted. It is the oldest town that Europeans founded on the US mainland, and people have lived in it without a break since it began. The Spanish navy commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded it on September 8, 1565. He named it for Saint Augustine because he had first sighted land on that saint's feast day, a day the church sets aside to honor a saint. St. Augustine is 42 years older than Jamestown and 55 years older than Plymouth.

**Seloy.** Menéndez landed at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. Counts of the people who came with him differ. The National Park Service gives about 600 soldiers and settlers. The Florida Museum of Natural History gives about 800, including 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans.

The newcomers used the council house of the Timucua chief, also named Seloy, as their first fort. It was the building where the town's leaders met. Timucua fighters attacked again and again to drive the Spanish away. In May 1566 Menéndez moved the settlement across the bay to Anastasia Island, and by about 1572 the Spanish had moved back to the place where the town stands today.

**Fort Caroline.** Two other attempts to stay failed. René de Laudonnière founded Fort Caroline, a French settlement near where Jacksonville, Florida, stands today, in 1564. The French there met Timucua people, and one of their leaders was Athore. The French settlers were Huguenots, French Protestants, members of the Christian churches that split away from the Catholic Church led by the pope in Rome. In 1565 Menéndez destroyed Fort Caroline and killed most of the French defenders. The Spanish were Catholics, and the fight was partly over religion.

**Roanoke.** Nobody knows what happened to the 117 English settlers who landed on Roanoke Island in 1587. The island lies off the coast of what is now North Carolina, and it was the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples. Sir Walter Raleigh organized the colony, a settlement ruled from a faraway country, and John White led it. On August 18, 1587, a settler named Eleanor Dare gave birth there to a daughter, Virginia Dare, the first English child recorded as born in the Americas.

The settlers sent White back to England for supplies. Before he left, they agreed on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of their new place on a tree or a post. If they were in trouble, they would carve a cross above the name. White could not get back for three years. In 1588 Queen Elizabeth I took every English ship for her navy, to fight the Spanish Armada, a fleet of Spanish warships.

**What White found.** White went ashore on August 18, 1590, Virginia Dare's third birthday. He found a wall of upright logs, the houses gone, and no people. Someone had carved CRO on a tree and CROATOAN on a post, with no cross. Croatoan was a nearby island, where a man named Manteo was born, and White wrote that its people were friends of the English. A storm blew his ship out to sea before he could search there. No one found the settlers.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 9 minutes**

Most English people who went to Virginia and Maryland in the 1600s paid for the voyage with years of their own work. People from Europe began coming to stay in these years. Some came so that they could worship in their own way. Others came to make money by farming, trading or growing tobacco.

The colonists settled along the Atlantic coast, on land where Native nations already lived. English colonists built Jamestown on Powhatan land and Plymouth on the site of a Wampanoag town. Dutch colonists built New Amsterdam on Lenape land, and Quakers under William Penn settled Pennsylvania on Lenape land too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown and indentured servitude" -->
**Jamestown.** English colonists founded Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607, and it was the first English settlement that lasted. The Virginia Company of London paid for it. Many people owned shares in the company, hoping to split a profit. The colonists built their fort inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan, a union of Native towns and peoples under one head chief. Jamestown Island was a hunting ground of the Paspahegh, one of those peoples, and Paspahegh fighters attacked the new settlement on May 26, 1607.

Many colonists died of disease and hunger in the first years. In the fall of 1609 the Powhatan leader, also called Powhatan, cut off the food trade with the colonists and surrounded their fort. About 500 colonists were alive at the start of that winter. About 61 were alive by spring.

**Tobacco.** John Rolfe began growing tobacco for sale overseas in the 1610s. Once tobacco sold well, more settlers came to Virginia to grow it, and they took more Powhatan land without paying for it. On March 22, 1622, fighters from an alliance of Virginia Indian nations, led by Opechancanough, attacked the English settlements and killed as many as 347 colonists.

Most early English arrivals in the Chesapeake, the country around the Chesapeake Bay in Virginia and Maryland, came as indentured servants. An indentured servant signed a contract, called an indenture, and gave up four to seven years of work in exchange for the trip across the ocean. The contract often promised food, a place to live, and a payment at the end called freedom dues.

Servants made up about 80 to 90 percent of the European immigrants to the Chesapeake in the 1600s, eight or nine of every ten. Across all the British colonies over the whole colonial period, the share was lower: about one-half to two-thirds of white immigrants came this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Africans brought by force, 1619" -->
An English privateer, a privately owned armed ship whose crew attacked the ships of enemy countries, brought more than twenty Africans to Virginia in late August 1619. The ship was the *White Lion*, and it landed them at Point Comfort, near the mouth of the James River.

**Where they came from.** These Africans had been seized in wars around the kingdoms of Kongo and Ndongo, in West Central Africa, in what is now Angola. In 1618 and 1619 Luis Mendes de Vasconçelos, the Portuguese governor of Angola, led two attacks on the people of Ndongo.

He fought alongside the Imbangala, African fighters paid to fight for others, and together they captured thousands of people. Their captors marched them to the Portuguese port of Luanda and loaded them onto the Portuguese ship *San Juan Bautista*, with about 350 captives bound for Mexico.

Two English privateers, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, attacked that ship in the Gulf of Mexico. Their crews took 50 to 60 of the Africans between them. The *White Lion* carried its share, more than twenty people, to Virginia. They are the first Africans recorded in English Virginia, and their landing began the forced arrival of Africans in the English mainland colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne

> **Who:** An English boy who came to Virginia as an indentured servant and wrote home begging for help.
> **When and where:** Arrived in December 1622 on the *Abigail*. Martin's Hundred, Virginia. Listed among the dead there by February 1624.

**"I never ate anything but peas and loblollie," he wrote home.**

Richard Frethorne is one of very few ordinary people in the early colonies whose own words survive. He came to Virginia as an indentured servant, landing in December 1622 from a ship called the *Abigail*, and he worked at a place called Martin's Hundred.

In March and April 1623 he wrote letters to his parents in England. He begged them to buy out his indenture, which meant paying to free him from the rest of his years of work. If they could not do that, he asked them to send food. "I never ate anything but peas and loblollie (a thick porridge)," he wrote. In another line he wrote, "there is nothing to comfort us."

His name was on a list of the dead at Martin's Hundred by February 1624. The list does not give the cause of his death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth and the Puritans" -->
Farther north, the *Mayflower* carried about 102 passengers across the Atlantic in 1620. They founded Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts, on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. A disease had killed many of Patuxet's people between 1616 and 1619. The Pilgrims among the passengers were English Separatists. They wanted to worship apart from the Church of England, the official church of their country, and before crossing the ocean they had first moved to Leiden, in Holland.

A larger group followed ten years later. In 1630 John Winthrop led a fleet of 11 ships to Massachusetts Bay with about 700 passengers. His flagship, the ship that carried the fleet's leader, was the *Arbella*. These colonists were Puritans, English Protestants who disagreed with how the Church of England was run.

**Laud's courts.** King Charles I made William Laud Archbishop of Canterbury, the head of the Church of England, in 1633. Laud wanted every church to follow the same rules and ceremonies. Judges in a royal court called the Star Chamber and in a church court called the High Commission took church jobs away from many Puritan ministers, the men who led church services, or banned them from preaching.

Three Puritan writers, William Prynne, Henry Burton and John Bastwick, wrote against Laud. In 1637 judges sentenced them to have their ears cut off and their cheeks branded. Branding means burning the skin with a hot iron to leave a mark that never goes away.

About 20,000 Puritans came to New England over the 1630s, in what is called the Great Migration. They were one part of a larger movement. About 80,000 people left England in that decade, and roughly 20,000 went to each of four places: Ireland, the West Indies, the Netherlands and New England. The West Indies are the islands of the Caribbean Sea.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** An English woman who followed her minister to Boston, was put on trial and banished three years later, and was killed by Siwanoy fighters in 1643.
> **When and where:** Crossed on the *Griffin* in the fall of 1634. Boston, then Rhode Island, then land the Dutch claimed.

**She asked the court why she was banished, and got no answer.**

Anne Hutchinson crossed the Atlantic to follow her minister, John Cotton, the preacher who led her church, to Boston. Three years after she landed, the leaders of the Massachusetts colony put her on trial and banished her, which means they ordered her to leave and never come back.

She sailed on the *Griffin* in the fall of 1634 with her husband, William, and their children. About 200 passengers were on board.

**The trial.** In November 1637 the colony's lawmakers and judges, sitting as the General Court of Massachusetts, tried her at Newtown, now Cambridge. Governor John Winthrop led the questioning. At the end he told her, "you are banished from out of our jurisdiction (the land the court ruled) as being a woman not fit for our society." She answered, "I desire to know wherefore (why) I am banished?" Winthrop replied, "Say no more, the court knows wherefore and is satisfied."

She moved to Portsmouth, Rhode Island, in 1638. Later she moved again, into territory the Dutch claimed, near what is now New York City. The Dutch governor there, Willem Kieft, was fighting the Native nations nearby, in a war now called Kieft's War. On the night of February 25, 1643, Kieft's men killed at least 80 and perhaps as many as 120 Munsee people.

**Her death.** Siwanoy fighters attacked Anne Hutchinson's household near Pelham Bay in August or September 1643. They killed her, her servants, and all but one of the children who were with her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dutch and the Swedes" -->
Dutch settlement began earlier than the Puritan fleet, in the 1620s, between Virginia and New England. Dutch colonists founded a colony called New Netherland. Its main town was New Amsterdam, on the island of Manhattan, in Lenapehoking, the homeland of the Lenape people. From 1638 to 1655, Swedish and Finnish settlers ran a small colony called New Sweden along the Delaware River, until the Dutch took it over in 1655.

Four English warships arrived at New Amsterdam in 1664. The Dutch governor, Peter Stuyvesant, surrendered without a fight, and the papers of surrender were signed in late August. The English renamed the colony New York, after James, the Duke of York, the younger brother of King Charles II of England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Jewish refugees, 1654" -->
Twenty-three Jewish refugees, people fleeing danger at home, landed at New Amsterdam in 1654 on a ship called the *Ste. Catherine*. They came from Recife, in Brazil. The Dutch had held that part of Brazil, and the refugees left after Portuguese forces took it back. Another Jewish man, Jacob Barsimson, had arrived earlier that year.

Governor Peter Stuyvesant tried to force the refugees out of the colony. Leaders of the Dutch West India Company, the trading company that ran New Netherland and gave the governor his orders, overruled him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Catholics, Quakers and Huguenots" -->
Other groups crossed the ocean for their religion too. Catholics settled in Maryland from 1634. Quakers, members of a Protestant group also called the Society of Friends, settled in Pennsylvania under William Penn from 1681. The Lenape people lived on both sides of the Delaware River there.

**The Huguenots.** King Louis XIV of France canceled the Edict of Nantes in 1685. King Henry IV had made that law in 1598, and under it French Protestants could worship in the towns they held. Huguenots fled France, and most went to other countries in Europe. Only about 1,500 to 2,000 had reached the American colonies by 1700. They settled in places such as New York, Massachusetts, and Charleston, South Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 6 minutes**

Scots-Irish and German newcomers came by the thousands in these fifty years, and many other arrivals came bound to work for years or for life. The Scots-Irish came from the north of Ireland, and the Germans from the lands along the Rhine River. Small Jewish communities grew in the port towns. The colonists still lived in a thin strip along the Atlantic coast, and the land farther inland was still Native land.

Bound workers could not leave their work until their time was up, and some never could. Indentured servants had signed contracts for years of work. Merchants sold British convicts as laborers. Slave traders brought enslaved Africans by force.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Germans and Scots-Irish left home" -->
German newcomers left to escape war, taxes and failed crops, and they came for cheap land. They came from southwest Germany and from the lands along the Rhine River, including the Palatinate, a region along the Rhine whose people were called Palatines. About 2,100 Palatines reached New York in 1709 and 1710.

A larger number of Germans went to Pennsylvania, mostly between about 1725 and 1775. About 12,000 German speakers had come to Pennsylvania by 1749. By the time of the Revolution, Germans made up about one-third of the people in that colony. The name Pennsylvania Dutch comes from *Deutsch*, the German word for German.

The Scots-Irish were Presbyterians, members of a Protestant church, from Ulster in the north of Ireland. Their ancestors were Scots who had settled in Ulster. Between 1710 and 1775 about 200,000 of them came to the land that later became the United States.

**The Test Act.** They left to escape high rents and a law called the Test Act, which Ireland's lawmakers passed in 1704. Under it, only people who took communion, a church ceremony of bread and wine, in the Church of Ireland could hold a public job. That rule shut Presbyterians out of public jobs. Marriages performed by Presbyterian ministers did not count under the law, so the law said the children of those marriages were born outside a lawful marriage.

Pennsylvania was the main place where the Scots-Irish landed. Many then moved south along the Appalachian backcountry, the land in and near the Appalachian Mountains, far from the coast. That backcountry was Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" name="John Peter Zenger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Peter Zenger

> **Who:** A boy from the German Palatinate who grew up to be a New York printer, jailed and tried over what his newspaper printed.
> **When and where:** Arrived in New York in June 1710. Died July 28, 1746.

**His paper missed one issue in his almost nine months in jail.**

John Peter Zenger reached New York in June 1710, at the age of 13. His father, Nicolaus, had died on the voyage. The family came from Rumbach, in the Palatinate, and had joined the Palatine migration in late 1709, traveling first to England. John Peter landed with his widowed mother, Johanna, and his two siblings. In 1711 he began work as an apprentice, a young worker who learns a trade from a master, to the New York printer William Bradford.

**The newspaper.** In 1733 Zenger became the printer of the *New-York Weekly Journal*, a newspaper started by men who opposed the governor, William Cosby. Cosby had copies of the paper burned. Two judges on Cosby's side, James De Lancey and Frederick Philipse, ordered Zenger's arrest, and on November 17, 1734, the sheriff took him to jail.

**The charge.** He was charged with seditious libel, the crime of printing words that blame a public official or the government. His bail, money paid to a court so that a person can leave jail until the trial, was set at 400 pounds. Zenger did not have that much money, so he stayed in jail until his trial, almost nine months later. His wife, Anna, and his apprentices kept printing the paper, and only one issue was missed. On August 4, 1735, a jury found Zenger not guilty.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jewish communities" -->
Most of the Jews in the colonies were Sephardic Jews, whose families had come from Spain and Portugal. After the first group landed at New Amsterdam in 1654, Jewish communities formed at Newport, Rhode Island, and at Charleston, New York and Savannah, Georgia. On July 11, 1733, 42 Jews landed at Savannah.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts sent as bound labor" -->
**The Transportation Act.** British lawmakers passed the Transportation Act in 1718. Under it, merchants shipped about 50,000 convicts, people found guilty of crimes, from Britain to the American colonies. Some counts put the number as high as 60,000, and more than 20,000 went to Virginia. Officials of the British treasury, the office that handled the government's money, paid the merchants for each convict. The first merchant to get this contract was Jonathan Forward.

In the colonies the merchants sold the convicts as bound laborers, workers who had to work for a buyer for a set number of years. Most had to work seven years, and some had to work fourteen. Buyers included merchants, tradesmen, shipbuilders and ironworks owners, because a convict cost less than an indentured servant or an enslaved African. The shipping went on until 1775.

Convicts made up roughly a quarter of the people who emigrated from Britain in the 1700s, leaving their own country to live in another. Indentured servants kept coming alongside them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forced arrival through Charleston" -->
The number of enslaved Africans that slave traders brought to the mainland colonies rose sharply in these years. Rice planters in the South Carolina Lowcountry, the low coastal land, bought large numbers of West and Central Africans. Some planters chose Africans who already knew how to grow rice. Charleston, South Carolina, was the main mainland port where enslaved Africans arrived.

**Sullivan's Island.** Many were held first at a quarantine station on Sullivan's Island, at the mouth of Charleston's harbor, where new arrivals were kept apart so that any disease they carried would not spread. They were kept in buildings called pest houses and checked for disease there. The histories of the island do not name the people who kept them. Then slave traders took them to Charleston and sold them at public auction.

The historian Peter Wood wrote in 1974 that Sullivan's Island "might well be described as the Ellis Island of black Americans." Ellis Island was the New York station where millions of European immigrants later arrived. Published estimates put at least 200,000 Africans through Charleston harbor and say that about 40 percent of African Americans today have an ancestor who came that way. Nic Butler, a historian at the Charleston County Public Library, argues that the share credited to Sullivan's Island is too high.

Across the whole slave trade, slave traders forced about 12.5 million Africans onto ships. About 10.7 million lived through the crossing, and about 1.8 million died on the slave traders' ships on the way. Fewer than 4 of every 100 people who lived through the crossing were brought to mainland North America. About 305,000 came straight from Africa, and close to 388,000 came in all, counting those brought by way of the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 5 minutes**

After the Revolution, members of Congress decided who could become a citizen, a full legal member of the new country, and they limited it to free white people. Fewer people crossed the Atlantic to settle during the American Revolution, from 1775 to 1783, than before it. In the 1790s thousands of refugees arrived from a revolution in the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Hamilton (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** An orphaned clerk from the Caribbean who came to New York as a teenager to go to school, and stayed.
> **When and where:** Born on Nevis. Sailed from St. Croix in late 1772 or early 1773. King's College, New York.
> **Movie:** *Hamilton* (2020), the filmed Broadway stage musical with its original cast, released on Disney+ on July 3, 2020. It is a dramatization, not a documentary.

**A letter about a hurricane paid his way to school.**

Alexander Hamilton was an orphan, both of his parents dead, working on the Caribbean island of St. Croix as a clerk, a worker who keeps written records for a business. He was born on the nearby island of Nevis on January 11. The year is disputed: accounts of his life give either 1755 or 1757.

A hurricane struck St. Croix on August 31, 1772. Hamilton wrote a letter describing the storm, dated September 6, and a newspaper called the *Royal Danish American Gazette* printed it on October 3. After reading it, men on the island raised money to send him to school in North America.

He sailed in late 1772 or early 1773. Accounts differ on the date. He studied at King's College in New York, and he stayed. Later he worked for the new national government at the Treasury, the department that handles the government's money.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution and the Loyalists" -->
Fewer immigrants crossed the Atlantic during the Revolutionary War, from 1775 to 1783, than in the years before it. The shipping of British convicts to the colonies ended in 1775. During and after the war, tens of thousands of Loyalists, colonists who sided with the British king against the Revolution, left the country, many of them for Canada.

Under the peace of 1783, the western border of the United States was the Mississippi River. Spanish and French officials claimed the land beyond the river. Most of the land away from the Atlantic coast was still Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who could become a citizen in 1790?" -->
Only "a free white person" of "good character" could. Members of Congress wrote those words into the Naturalization Act of 1790, the national government's first law on naturalization, the legal process by which a person born in another country becomes a citizen. To qualify, a person had to live in the United States for two years, including at least one year in the state where they applied, and swear loyalty to the Constitution. Children born abroad to citizens were included as citizens.

Under this law, an immigrant who was enslaved or was not white could not become a citizen. Members of Congress did not fully remove the limit to white people until 1952, 162 years later.

The men who wrote the Constitution in 1787 also set a date for arrivals by force. In Article I, Section 9, they barred Congress from stopping the bringing in of enslaved people before 1808. So for about twenty more years, slave traders could legally bring captives into the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-toussaint" name="Pierre Toussaint" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pierre Toussaint

> **Who:** A man born enslaved in Saint-Domingue and brought to New York by the family that enslaved him. He became a leading hairdresser and was freed in 1807.
> **When and where:** Brought to New York in 1787. Freed July 2, 1807. Died in New York, June 30, 1853.

**He was one of New York's leading hairdressers, and still enslaved.**

The Bérard family took Pierre Toussaint, whom they enslaved, from Saint-Domingue to New York in 1787. Saint-Domingue was a French colony in the Caribbean. The Bérards were fleeing fighting and disorder there, four years before the revolution in the colony began. Toussaint had been born enslaved in Saint-Domingue in about 1766, though some recent accounts give 1781.

In New York he trained under a hairdresser and became one of the city's leading hairdressers. When the husband of Marie Elisabeth Bérard died, Toussaint supported her. He was still enslaved by her family at the time.

**Freedom.** Marie Elisabeth Bérard married again, to a Mr. Nicolas, and she died in 1807. On her deathbed she made her second husband promise to free Toussaint. Mr. Nicolas kept the promise, and Toussaint was free on July 2, 1807. If he was born in about 1766, he was about 41, though one account says 45. He took the surname Toussaint to honor Toussaint Louverture, a leader of the revolution in Saint-Domingue.

Toussaint used his money to care for orphans and to fund early Catholic charities in New York, groups that helped the poor. During an outbreak of yellow fever, a deadly disease spread by mosquitoes, in New York in 1798, he nursed sick people who were kept apart from others. In 1811 he married Juliette Noel, an enslaved woman, after he paid for her freedom.

**After his death.** Cardinal John O'Connor, the head of the Catholic Church in New York, had Toussaint's remains moved in 1990 to the crypt of St. Patrick's Cathedral, a burial room beneath the church. Pope John Paul II declared him Venerable in 1996, a title the pope gives to a person whom church leaders are studying as a possible saint.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Refugees from Saint-Domingue" -->
Enslaved Africans in Saint-Domingue, a French sugar colony, rose up on August 21, 1791, against the French planters who enslaved them and against French rule. The fighting went on until January 1, 1804, when Saint-Domingue became the independent country of Haiti.

Refugees fled to cities in the United States. They included white planters and free people of color, meaning people of African or mixed descent who were not enslaved. They also included enslaved people, whom their owners brought with them. That first year a ship called the *Charming Sally* carried 15 refugees to Philadelphia. By 1794 more than 3,000 refugees had come to that city, and nearly 20,000 refugees from Saint-Domingue came to the United States across the 1790s and the 1800s.
