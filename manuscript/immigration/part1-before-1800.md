## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, hundreds of Native nations lived in the land that is now the United States, and their people spoke hundreds of different languages. No one from across the ocean had come to settle there for good. Every group of newcomers who came later arrived on land where people were already living.

Nobody knows how many people lived north of Mexico in 1492, and experts give very different numbers. William Denevan, a geographer who studies the history of people and places, puts the number at about 3.8 million, and many experts use his figure. A book of world population maps published in 1978 gave only about 1 million. Some later researchers argued for as many as 18 to 20 million. The historian Donald Fixico gives a range from a few million to 15 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The land bridge and the Norse" -->
The first people to reach this land came on foot from Asia, long ago. They walked across the ice-age land bridge, a stretch of dry ground that joined Asia to North America.

Before Columbus, only one group from across the ocean is known to have built a camp in North America, and it did not stay. Around the year 1000, about 500 years before Columbus sailed, Norse sailors from Greenland built a camp at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, in what is now Canada. Two old Icelandic stories, called sagas, say that Leif Erikson, the son of Erik the Red of Greenland, led a voyage to a place he named Vinland. Scientists counted the tree rings in wood the Norse cut at the camp with metal blades. The trees were cut down in 1021. It is the only exact date known for Europeans in the Americas before Columbus.

Native people had lived at that spot long before the Norse came. Five Native groups had lived there, the oldest about 6,000 years earlier. The Norse stayed only a short time and then left. Historians disagree on how short: their guesses run from a few years to about twenty. The Norse built no settlement that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Europeans reached these coasts in the 1500s, but almost none of them stayed. Most were soldiers, explorers, sailors and missionaries. A missionary is a person sent to bring other people into a religion, and these missionaries were Christian. They came to conquer land and to explore it. Native nations held almost all of the land for the whole century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, and the failures" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, is the oldest town that Europeans founded in the continental United States, the part of the country on the North American mainland. People have lived in it without a break since it began. The Spanish admiral Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded it on September 8, 1565. He named the town for Saint Augustine, because he had first sighted land on that saint's feast day. A feast day is a day the church sets aside to honor a saint. St. Augustine is 42 years older than the English town of Jamestown and 55 years older than Plymouth.

Menéndez landed at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. Counts of the people who came with him differ. One count gives about 600 soldiers and settlers. The Florida Museum of Natural History gives about 800, including 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. The newcomers used the council house of the Timucua chief, also named Seloy, as their first fort. Timucua fighters attacked again and again to drive the Spanish away. In May 1566 Menéndez moved the settlement across the bay to Anastasia Island, and by about 1572 the Spanish had moved back to the place where the town stands today.

Two other attempts to stay failed. In 1564 René de Laudonnière founded Fort Caroline, a French settlement near where Jacksonville, Florida, stands today. The French there met Timucua people, whose leader was Athore. The French settlers were Huguenots, which means French Protestants. Protestants are Christians whose churches split away from the Catholic Church, the church led by the pope in Rome. In 1565 Menéndez destroyed Fort Caroline and killed most of the French defenders. The Spanish were Catholics, and the fight between them and the French was partly over religion.

In 1587 about 117 English settlers landed on Roanoke Island, off the coast of what is now North Carolina. The island and the coast around it were the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples. Sir Walter Raleigh organized the colony, a settlement ruled from a faraway country, and John White led it. On August 18, 1587, a settler named Eleanor Dare gave birth there to a daughter, Virginia Dare. Virginia Dare is the first English child recorded as born in the Americas.

The settlers sent White back to England for supplies. Before he left, they agreed on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of their new place on a tree or a post. If they were in trouble, they would carve a cross above the name. In 1588 Queen Elizabeth I took every English ship for her navy, to fight the Spanish Armada, a fleet of Spanish warships. White could not get back until 1590. He went ashore on August 18, 1590, Virginia Dare's third birthday. He found a wall of upright logs, the houses gone, and no people. Someone had carved CRO on a tree and CROATOAN on a post, with no cross. Croatoan was a nearby island, where a Croatoan man named Manteo was born, and White wrote that its people were friends of the English. A storm blew White's ship out to sea before he could search there. Nobody knows what happened to the settlers.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People from Europe began coming to stay in the 1600s. Some came so that they could worship in their own way. Others came to make money by farming, trading or growing tobacco. Most English people who went to Virginia and Maryland paid for the voyage with years of their own labor.

The colonists settled along the Atlantic coast, on land where Native nations already lived. Jamestown was built on Powhatan land, Plymouth on the site of a Wampanoag town, New Amsterdam on Lenape land, and Pennsylvania on Lenape land too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown and indentured servitude" -->
In 1607 English colonists founded Jamestown, Virginia, the first English settlement that lasted. The Virginia Company of London paid for it. It was a joint-stock company, which means many people bought shares in it, hoping to split a profit. The colonists built their fort inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan, a union of Native towns and peoples under one head chief. Jamestown Island was a hunting ground of the Paspahegh, one of those peoples, and Paspahegh fighters attacked the new settlement on May 26, 1607.

In the first years many colonists died of disease and hunger. In the fall of 1609 the Powhatan leader, also called Powhatan, cut off trade with the colonists and surrounded their fort. About 500 colonists were alive at the start of that winter, and about 61 were alive by spring. In the 1610s John Rolfe began growing tobacco for sale overseas. Once tobacco sold well, more settlers came to Virginia to grow it, and they took more Powhatan land without paying for it. On March 22, 1622, Powhatan fighters led by the head chief Opechancanough attacked the English settlements and killed as many as 347 colonists.

Most early English arrivals in the Chesapeake came as indentured servants. The Chesapeake is the country around the Chesapeake Bay, in Virginia and Maryland. An indentured servant signed a contract, called an indenture, and gave up four to seven years of work in exchange for the trip across the ocean. The contract often promised food, a place to live, and a payment at the end called freedom dues.

In the Chesapeake in the 1600s, about 80 to 90 percent of European immigrants came as indentured servants. Across all the British colonies over the whole colonial period, the share was lower: about one-half to two-thirds of white immigrants came this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Africans brought by force, 1619" -->
In late August 1619, an English privateer called the *White Lion* brought more than twenty Africans to Point Comfort, near the mouth of the James River in Virginia. A privateer was a privately owned armed ship whose crew attacked the ships of enemy countries. These Africans had been seized in wars around the kingdoms of Kongo and Ndongo, in West Central Africa, in what is now Angola. No surviving record names the men who captured them. Their captors marched them to the Portuguese port of Luanda and loaded them onto the Portuguese ship *San Juan Bautista*, with about 350 captives bound for Mexico.

Two English privateers, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, attacked that ship in the Gulf of Mexico. Their crews took 50 to 60 of the Africans between them. The *White Lion* carried its share, more than twenty people, to Virginia. They are the first Africans recorded in English Virginia, and their landing began the forced arrival of Africans in the English mainland colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" name="Richard Frethorne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Richard Frethorne

> **Who:** An English boy who came to Virginia as an indentured servant and wrote home begging for help.
> **When and where:** Arrived in December 1622 on the *Abigail*. Martin's Hundred, Virginia. Listed among the dead there by February 1624.

Richard Frethorne is one of very few ordinary people in the early colonies whose own words survive. He was an English boy who came to Virginia as an indentured servant. He arrived in December 1622 on a ship called the *Abigail*, and he worked at a place called Martin's Hundred.

In March and April 1623 he wrote letters to his parents in England. He begged them to buy out his indenture, which meant paying to free him from the rest of his years of work. If they could not do that, he asked them to send food. "I never ate anything but peas and loblollie," he wrote. Loblollie was a thick porridge. In another line he wrote, "there is nothing to comfort us."

By February 1624 his name was on a list of the dead at Martin's Hundred. No surviving record gives the cause of his death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth and the Puritans" -->
Farther north, the *Mayflower* carried about 102 passengers across the Atlantic in 1620. They founded Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts, on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. A disease had swept through Patuxet between 1616 and 1619 and killed most of its people. The Pilgrims among the passengers were English Separatists. They wanted to worship apart from the Church of England, the official church of their country. Before crossing the ocean, they had first moved to Leiden, in Holland.

A larger group followed ten years later. In 1630 John Winthrop led a fleet of 11 ships to Massachusetts Bay with about 700 passengers. His flagship, the ship that carried the fleet's leader, was the *Arbella*. These colonists were Puritans, English Protestants who disagreed with how the Church of England was run.

King Charles I made William Laud Archbishop of Canterbury, the head of the Church of England, in 1633. Laud wanted every church to follow the same rules and ceremonies. In the king's courts and the church courts, many Puritan ministers, the men who led church services, lost their church jobs or were forbidden to preach. In 1637 judges sentenced the Puritan writers William Prynne, Henry Burton and John Bastwick to have their ears cut off and their cheeks branded, burned with a hot iron to leave a mark that never goes away, for writing against Laud.

About 20,000 Puritans came to New England over the 1630s, in what is called the Great Migration. They were one part of a larger movement. About 80,000 people left England in that decade. Roughly 20,000 went to each of four places: Ireland, the West Indies, the Netherlands and New England. The West Indies are the islands of the Caribbean Sea.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** An English woman who followed her minister to Boston, was put on trial and banished three years later, and was killed by Siwanoy fighters in 1643.
> **When and where:** Crossed on the *Griffin* in the fall of 1634. Boston, then Rhode Island, then land the Dutch claimed.

Anne Hutchinson crossed the Atlantic to follow her minister, the preacher who led her church. Three years after she landed, the leaders of the Massachusetts colony put her on trial and banished her. Banished means ordered to leave and never come back.

She was born in 1591 in Alford, England. In the fall of 1634 she sailed on the *Griffin* with her husband, William, and their children. About 200 passengers were on board. The Hutchinsons were following their minister, John Cotton, to Boston.

In November 1637 the General Court of Massachusetts, the colony's lawmakers and judges, tried her at Newtown, now Cambridge. Governor John Winthrop led the questioning. At the end he told her, "you are banished from out of our jurisdiction as being a woman not fit for our society." She answered, "I desire to know wherefore I am banished?" Jurisdiction meant the land the court ruled, and wherefore means why.

In 1638 she moved to Portsmouth, Rhode Island. Later she moved again, into territory the Dutch claimed, near what is now New York City. There the Dutch governor, Willem Kieft, had started a war with the Native nations nearby. On the night of February 25, 1643, Kieft's men killed at least 80 and perhaps as many as 120 Munsee people. Later that year, in August or September, Siwanoy fighters attacked the Hutchinson home. They killed Anne Hutchinson, her servants, and all but one of the children who were with her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dutch and the Swedes" -->
Dutch settlement began earlier than the Puritan fleet, in the 1620s, between Virginia and New England. Dutch colonists founded a colony called New Netherland. Its main town was New Amsterdam, on the island of Manhattan, in Lenapehoking, the homeland of the Lenape people. From 1638 to 1655, Swedish and Finnish settlers ran a small colony called New Sweden along the Delaware River. In 1655 the Dutch took over New Sweden.

In 1664 four English warships arrived at New Amsterdam. The Dutch governor, Peter Stuyvesant, surrendered without a fight, and Dutch and English leaders signed the papers of surrender in late August 1664. The English renamed the colony New York, after James, the Duke of York, the younger brother of King Charles II of England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Jewish refugees, 1654" -->
Twenty-three Jewish refugees landed at New Amsterdam in 1654 on a ship called the *Ste. Catherine*. A refugee is a person who flees home to escape danger. They came from Recife, in Brazil. The Dutch had held that part of Brazil, and the refugees left after Portuguese forces took it back. Another Jewish man, Jacob Barsimson, had arrived earlier that year.

Governor Peter Stuyvesant tried to expel the refugees, which means to force them out of the colony. Leaders of the Dutch West India Company, the trading company that ran New Netherland and gave the governor his orders, overruled him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Catholics, Quakers and Huguenots" -->
Other groups also crossed the ocean for their religion. Catholics settled in Maryland from 1634. Quakers, members of a Protestant group also called the Society of Friends, settled in Pennsylvania under William Penn from 1681. The Lenape people lived on both sides of the Delaware River there.

In 1685 King Louis XIV of France revoked the Edict of Nantes. That law, made by King Henry IV in 1598, had let French Protestants worship in the towns they held. Revoked means Louis canceled it. Huguenots fled France, and most went to other countries in Europe. By 1700 only about 1,500 to 2,000 had reached the American colonies. They settled in places such as New York, Massachusetts, and Charleston, South Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s the colonists still lived in a thin strip along the Atlantic coast. The land farther inland was still Native land.

Large numbers of newcomers who were not English came in these years. About 200,000 Scots-Irish came between 1710 and 1775. About 2,100 Germans reached New York in 1709 and 1710, and about 12,000 German speakers had come to Pennsylvania by 1749. Small Jewish communities grew in port towns. Many other arrivals came bound to work. They included indentured servants, British convicts sold as laborers, and enslaved Africans whom slave traders brought by force.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans and Scots-Irish" -->
German newcomers came from southwest Germany and from the lands along the Rhine River, including the Palatinate. The Palatinate is a region along the Rhine, and people from there were called Palatines. People left to escape war, taxes and failed crops, and they came for cheap land. About 2,100 Palatines reached New York in 1709 and 1710.

A larger number of Germans went to Pennsylvania, mostly between about 1725 and 1775. By 1749 about 12,000 German speakers had come to Pennsylvania. By the time of the Revolution, Germans made up about one-third of the people in that colony. The name Pennsylvania Dutch comes from *Deutsch*, the German word for German.

The Scots-Irish were Presbyterians, members of a Protestant church, from Ulster in the north of Ireland. Their ancestors were Scots who had settled in Ulster. Between 1710 and 1775 about 200,000 of them came to the land that later became the United States.

They left to escape high rents and a law called the Test Act. Ireland's lawmakers passed it in 1704. Under it, only people who took communion, a church ceremony of bread and wine, in the Church of Ireland could hold a public job, so Presbyterians could not. Marriages performed by Presbyterian ministers did not count under the law. Pennsylvania was the main place where the Scots-Irish landed. Many then moved south along the Appalachian backcountry, the land in and near the Appalachian Mountains, far from the coast. That backcountry was Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" name="John Peter Zenger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Peter Zenger

> **Who:** A boy from the German Palatinate who reached New York at 13, after his father died on the crossing, and trained as a printer.
> **When and where:** Arrived in New York in June 1710. Died there July 28, 1746.

John Peter Zenger reached New York in June 1710, at the age of 13. His father, Nicolaus, had died on the voyage.

John Peter was born in 1697 in Rumbach, in the Palatinate. In late 1709 his family joined the Palatine migration and traveled first to England. They sailed for New York in April 1710. John Peter landed with his widowed mother, Johanna, and his two siblings. In 1711 he began work as an apprentice to the New York printer William Bradford. An apprentice is a young worker who learns a trade by working for a master for several years.

In 1733 Zenger became the printer of the *New-York Weekly Journal*, a newspaper started by men who opposed the governor, William Cosby. Cosby had copies of the paper burned. Two judges on Cosby's side, James De Lancey and Frederick Philipse, ordered Zenger's arrest, and on November 17, 1734, the sheriff took him to jail. He was charged with seditious libel, the crime of printing words that blame a public official or the government. His bail was set at 400 pounds, more money than he had, so he stayed in jail until his trial, almost nine months later. His wife, Anna, and his apprentices kept printing the paper, and only one issue was missed. On August 4, 1735, a jury found Zenger not guilty. He died on July 28, 1746.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-peter-zenger-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jewish communities" -->
Most of the Jews in the colonies were Sephardic Jews, whose families had come from Spain and Portugal. After the first group landed at New Amsterdam in 1654, Jewish communities formed at Newport, Rhode Island, and at Charleston, New York and Savannah, Georgia. On July 11, 1733, 42 Jews landed at Savannah.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts sent as bound labor" -->
Under a British law of 1718, the Transportation Act, merchants shipped about 50,000 convicts, people found guilty of crimes, from Britain to the American colonies. Some counts put the number as high as 60,000, and more than 20,000 went to Virginia. The British government paid the merchants for each convict. The first merchant to get this contract was a Londoner named Jonathan Forward. In the colonies the merchants sold the convicts as bound laborers, workers who had to work for a buyer for a set number of years. Most had to work seven years, and some had to work fourteen. Buyers included merchants, tradesmen, shipbuilders and ironworks owners, because a convict cost less than an indentured servant or an enslaved African. The shipping went on until 1775.

Convicts made up roughly a quarter of the people who emigrated from Britain in the 1700s. To emigrate means to leave your own country to live in another. Indentured servants also kept coming alongside the convicts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forced arrival through Charleston" -->
In these years slave traders brought many more enslaved Africans to the mainland colonies than before. Rice planters in the South Carolina Lowcountry, the low coastal land, bought large numbers of West and Central Africans. Some planters chose Africans who already knew how to grow rice. Charleston, South Carolina, was the main mainland port where enslaved Africans arrived. Many were held first at a quarantine station on Sullivan's Island, at the mouth of Charleston's harbor. Quarantine means keeping new arrivals apart so that any disease they carry does not spread. Quarantine workers kept them in buildings called pest houses and checked them for disease. Then slave traders took them to Charleston and sold them at public auction.

In 1974 the historian Peter Wood wrote that Sullivan's Island "might well be described as the Ellis Island of black Americans." Ellis Island was the New York station where millions of European immigrants later arrived. Some estimates say at least 200,000 Africans passed through Charleston harbor, and that about 40 percent of African Americans today have an ancestor who came that way. Some historians say these numbers are too high.

Across the whole slave trade, slave traders forced about 12.5 million Africans onto ships. About 10.7 million lived through the crossing, so about 1.8 million died on the way. Fewer than 4 percent of the people who lived through the crossing were brought to mainland North America. About 305,000 came straight from Africa, and close to 388,000 came in all, counting those brought by way of the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Fewer people crossed the Atlantic to settle during the American Revolution, from 1775 to 1783, than before it. After the war, members of Congress passed the national government's first law on who could become a citizen, a full legal member of the country. They limited it to free white people. In the 1790s thousands of refugees arrived from a revolution in the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Hamilton (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** An orphaned clerk from the Caribbean whose writing about a hurricane led men on St. Croix to pay for his schooling in North America. He came as a teenager and stayed.
> **When and where:** Born on Nevis. Sailed from St. Croix in late 1772 or early 1773. King's College, New York.
> **Movie:** *Hamilton* (2020), the filmed Broadway stage musical with its original cast, released on Disney+ on July 3, 2020. It is a dramatization, not a documentary.

Alexander Hamilton came to North America from the Caribbean as a teenager, to go to school. Men on the island of St. Croix paid for it.

He was born on the Caribbean island of Nevis on January 11. The year is disputed. Some sources give 1755, and others give 1757. He was an orphan, which means both of his parents had died. He worked on St. Croix as a clerk, a worker who keeps written records for a business.

On August 31, 1772, a hurricane struck St. Croix. Hamilton wrote a letter describing the storm, dated September 6, 1772. It was printed in a newspaper called the *Royal Danish American Gazette* on October 3. After reading it, men on the island raised money to send him to school in North America.

He sailed in late 1772 or early 1773. Accounts differ on the date. He studied at King's College in New York, and he stayed. Later he worked for the new national government at the Treasury, the department that handles the government's money.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution and the Loyalists" -->
The Revolutionary War lasted from 1775 to 1783. During those years fewer immigrants crossed the Atlantic than in the years before. The shipping of British convicts to the colonies ended in 1775. During and after the war, tens of thousands of Loyalists left the country, many of them for Canada. Loyalists were colonists who sided with the British king against the Revolution.

Under the peace of 1783, the western border of the United States was the Mississippi River. Spanish and French officials claimed the land beyond the river. Most of the land away from the Atlantic coast was still Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Naturalization Act of 1790" -->
In 1790 members of Congress passed the Naturalization Act. Naturalization is the legal process by which a person born in another country becomes a citizen. The act was the first federal law on who could become a citizen. It allowed naturalization only for "a free white person" of "good character." To qualify, a person had to live in the United States for two years, including at least one year in the state where they applied, and swear loyalty to the Constitution. Children born abroad to citizens were included as citizens.

Under this law, an immigrant who was enslaved or was not white could not become a citizen. Members of Congress did not fully remove the limit to white people until 1952, 162 years later.

The Constitution, written in 1787, also set a date for arrivals by force. Its writers barred Congress from stopping the bringing in of enslaved people before 1808. Under that part of the Constitution, Article I, Section 9, slave traders could legally bring captives into the country for twenty more years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-toussaint" name="Pierre Toussaint" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pierre Toussaint

> **Who:** A man born enslaved in Saint-Domingue and brought to New York by the family that enslaved him. He became a leading hairdresser and was freed in 1807.
> **When and where:** Brought to New York in 1787. Freed July 2, 1807. Died in New York, June 30, 1853.

In 1787 the Bérard family took Pierre Toussaint, whom they enslaved, from Saint-Domingue to New York. Saint-Domingue was a French colony in the Caribbean. Toussaint was born enslaved there in about 1766, though some recent accounts give 1781. The Bérards were fleeing unrest in the colony. Unrest means fighting and disorder. They came four years before the revolution in Saint-Domingue began.

In New York he trained under a hairdresser and became one of the city's leading hairdressers. Marie Elisabeth Bérard, of the family that enslaved him, lost her husband, and Toussaint supported her. He was still enslaved at the time.

Marie Elisabeth Bérard had married again, to a Mr. Nicolas, and she died in 1807. Toussaint was freed on July 2, 1807, after her death. Some accounts say that on her deathbed she made her husband promise to free him. If Toussaint was born in about 1766, he was about 41 when he was freed. One account says he was 45. He took the surname Toussaint to honor Toussaint Louverture, a leader of the revolution in Saint-Domingue.

Toussaint used his money to care for orphans and to fund early Catholic charities in New York, groups that helped the poor. During a yellow fever outbreak in New York in 1798, he nursed sick people who were kept apart from others. Yellow fever is a deadly disease spread by mosquitoes. In 1811 he married Juliette Noel, an enslaved woman, after he paid for her freedom.

He died on June 30, 1853. In 1990 Cardinal John O'Connor, the head of the Catholic Church in New York, had his remains moved to the crypt of St. Patrick's Cathedral in New York, a burial room beneath the church. In 1996 Pope John Paul II declared him Venerable, a title the pope gives to a person whom church leaders are studying as a possible saint.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pierre-toussaint" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Refugees from Saint-Domingue" -->
On August 21, 1791, enslaved Africans in Saint-Domingue rose up against the French planters who enslaved them and against French rule. Saint-Domingue was a French sugar colony. The fighting went on until January 1, 1804, when it became the free country of Haiti. Refugees fled to cities in the United States. They included white planters and free people of color, meaning people of African or mixed descent who were not enslaved. They also included enslaved people, whom their owners brought with them.

That first year a ship called the *Charming Sally* carried 15 refugees to Philadelphia. By 1794 more than 3,000 refugees had come to that city. Nearly 20,000 refugees from Saint-Domingue came to the United States across the 1790s and the 1800s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->
