## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, nobody crossed the ocean to settle for good in the land that is now the United States. Hundreds of Native nations already lived here, and their people spoke hundreds of different languages. Every group of newcomers who came later arrived on land where people were already living.

Nobody knows how many people lived north of Mexico in 1492, and scholars give very different numbers. The geographer William Denevan puts the number at about 3.8 million, and many scholars cite his figure. A world population atlas published in 1978 gave only about 1 million. Some later scholars argued for as many as 18 to 20 million. The historian Donald Fixico gives a range from a few million to 15 million. No number is settled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The land bridge and the Norse" -->
The first people to reach this land came on foot from Asia, long ago. They walked across the ice-age land bridge, a stretch of dry ground that joined Asia to North America.

Only one group crossed the ocean to this land before 1500, and it did not stay. Around the year 1000, Norse sailors from northern Europe built a camp at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, in what is now Canada. They lived there for about ten years and then left. They built no settlement that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Europeans reached these coasts in the 1500s, but almost none of them stayed. Most were soldiers, explorers, sailors and missionaries. A missionary is a person sent to bring other people into a religion, and these missionaries were Christian. They came to conquer land and to explore it. Native nations held almost all of the land for the whole century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, and the failures" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, is the oldest town that Europeans founded in the continental United States, meaning the states other than Alaska and Hawaii. People have lived in it without a break since it began. The Spanish admiral Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded it on September 8, 1565, on Native land. He landed with about 600 soldiers and settlers. He named the town for Saint Augustine, because he had first sighted land on that saint's feast day, the day the church sets aside to honor him. St. Augustine is 42 years older than the English town of Jamestown and 55 years older than Plymouth.

Two other attempts to stay failed. In 1564 René de Laudonnière founded Fort Caroline, a French settlement near where Jacksonville, Florida, stands today. Its settlers were Huguenots, which means French Protestants. In 1565 Menéndez destroyed Fort Caroline and killed most of the French defenders. The fight between the Spanish and the French there was partly over religion.

In 1587 English settlers landed on Roanoke Island, off the coast of what is now North Carolina. Sir Walter Raleigh organized the colony, and John White led it. On August 18, 1587, a settler named Eleanor Dare gave birth there to a daughter, Virginia Dare. Virginia Dare is the first English child recorded as born in the Americas. White went back to England for supplies. A war between England and Spain kept him from returning until 1590. When he came back, the settlers were gone, and someone had carved the word CROATOAN on a post. Nobody knows what happened to them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s people from Europe began coming to stay. Some came so that they could worship in their own way. Others came for land, for trade, or to grow tobacco. Most English people who went to Virginia and Maryland paid for the voyage with years of their own labor.

Every one of these colonies was built on land where Native people already lived. The newcomers settled along the Atlantic coast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown and indentured servitude" -->
In 1607 English colonists founded Jamestown, Virginia, the first English settlement that lasted. The Virginia Company of London paid for it, and its investors hoped to make a profit. It was a joint-stock company, which means many people bought shares in it and would split any profit. The Powhatan people already lived on the land around Jamestown. In the first years many colonists died of disease and hunger, and colonists and Powhatan fought each other. In the 1610s John Rolfe began growing tobacco for sale overseas. Once tobacco sold well, more settlers came to Virginia to grow it.

Most early English arrivals in the Chesapeake came as indentured servants. The Chesapeake is the country around the Chesapeake Bay, in Virginia and Maryland. An indentured servant signed a contract, called an indenture, and gave up four to seven years of work in exchange for the ship's passage. The contract often promised food, a place to live, and a payment at the end called freedom dues.

In the Chesapeake in the 1600s, about 80 to 90 percent of European immigrants came as indentured servants. Across all the British colonies over the whole colonial period, the share was lower: about one-half to two-thirds of white immigrants came this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An arrival that was not chosen" -->
In late August 1619, an English privateer called the *White Lion* brought more than twenty Africans to Point Comfort, near the mouth of the James River in Virginia. A privateer was a privately owned armed ship whose crew attacked the ships of enemy countries. Captors whom the sources do not name had seized these Africans in wars in West Central Africa, in what is now Angola. They were marched to the Portuguese port of Luanda and loaded onto the Portuguese ship *San Juan Bautista*, with about 350 captives bound for Mexico. In the Gulf of Mexico, the crews of the *White Lion* and another English privateer, the *Treasurer*, attacked that ship and took 50 to 60 of the Africans. This landing began the forced arrival of Africans in the English mainland colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Richard Frethorne

> **Who:** An English boy who came to Virginia as an indentured servant and wrote home begging for help.
> **When and where:** Arrived in December 1622 on the *Abigail*. Martin's Hundred, Virginia. Listed among the dead there by February 1624.

Richard Frethorne is one of very few ordinary people in the early colonies whose own words survive. He was an English boy who came to Virginia as an indentured servant. He arrived in December 1622 on a ship called the *Abigail*, and he worked at a place called Martin's Hundred.

In March and April 1623 he wrote letters to his parents in England. He begged them to buy out his indenture, which meant paying to free him from the rest of his years of work. If they could not do that, he asked them to send food. "I never ate anything but peas and loblollie," he wrote. Loblollie was a thin porridge. In another line he wrote, "there is nothing to comfort us."

By February 1624 his name was on a list of the dead at Martin's Hundred. The sources do not give the cause of his death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-frethorne-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth and the Puritans" -->
In 1620 about 102 passengers crossed the Atlantic on the *Mayflower* and founded Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts. The Pilgrims among them were English Separatists. They wanted to worship apart from the Church of England, the official church of their country. Before crossing the ocean, they had first moved to Leiden, in Holland.

A larger group followed ten years later. In 1630 John Winthrop led a fleet of 11 ships to Massachusetts Bay with about 700 passengers. His flagship, the ship that carried the fleet's leader, was the *Arbella*. These colonists were Puritans, English Protestants who disagreed with how the Church of England was run. King Charles I made William Laud Archbishop of Canterbury, the head of the Church of England, in 1633. Puritans at home then came under more religious pressure.

About 20,000 Puritans came to New England over the 1630s, in what is called the Great Migration. They were one part of a larger movement. About 80,000 people left England in that decade. Roughly 20,000 went to each of four places: Ireland, the West Indies, the Netherlands and New England. The West Indies are the islands of the Caribbean Sea.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" name="Anne Hutchinson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Hutchinson

> **Who:** An English woman who followed her minister to Boston, was put on trial and banished three years later, and was killed in 1643.
> **When and where:** Crossed on the *Griffin* in the fall of 1634. Boston, then Rhode Island, then land the Dutch claimed.

Anne Hutchinson crossed the Atlantic to follow her minister. Three years after she landed, she was put on trial and banished from the colony she had joined. Banished means ordered to leave and never come back.

She was born in 1591 in Alford, England. In the fall of 1634 she sailed on the *Griffin* with her husband, William, and their children. About 200 passengers were on board. The Hutchinsons were following their minister, John Cotton, to Boston.

In November 1637 she was tried in Massachusetts and banished. The sources used here do not name her judges. In 1638 she moved to Portsmouth, Rhode Island. Later she moved again, into territory the Dutch claimed. In 1643 fighters of the Siwanoy, a Native nation, attacked there and killed her and several of her children. The sources used here do not give the reason for the attack.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-hutchinson-immigration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dutch and the Swedes" -->
Dutch colonists settled New Netherland in the 1620s. Its capital was New Amsterdam, on the island of Manhattan. From 1638 to 1655, Swedish and Finnish settlers ran a small colony called New Sweden along the Delaware River. In 1655 New Sweden came under Dutch control.

In 1664 four English warships arrived at New Amsterdam. The Dutch governor, Peter Stuyvesant, surrendered without a fight, and the papers of surrender were signed in late August 1664. The English renamed the colony New York, after the Duke of York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jewish-refugees-1654" name="The first Jewish refugees (1654)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### The first Jewish refugees (1654)

> **Who:** 23 Jewish refugees from Recife, Brazil, the first Jewish group to arrive at New Amsterdam, and Jacob Barsimson, who arrived earlier the same year.
> **When and where:** New Amsterdam, 1654.

In 1654, 23 Jewish refugees landed at New Amsterdam on a ship called the *Ste. Catherine*. A refugee is a person who flees home to escape danger. They came from Recife, in Brazil. The Dutch had held that part of Brazil, and the refugees left after Portuguese forces took it back. Another Jewish man, Jacob Barsimson, had arrived earlier that year.

Governor Peter Stuyvesant tried to expel the refugees, which means to force them out of the colony. Officials of the Dutch West India Company, who had power over the governor, overruled him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jewish-refugees-1654" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Catholics, Quakers and Huguenots" -->
Other groups also crossed the ocean for their religion. Catholics settled in Maryland from 1634. Quakers, members of a Protestant group also called the Society of Friends, settled in Pennsylvania under William Penn from 1681.

In 1685 King Louis XIV of France revoked the Edict of Nantes, the law that had let French Protestants worship in France. Revoked means he canceled it. Huguenots fled France. Most went to other countries in Europe. By 1700 only about 1,500 to 2,000 had reached the American colonies. They settled in places such as New York, Massachusetts, and Charleston, South Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
