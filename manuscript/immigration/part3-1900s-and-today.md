## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The great wave of newcomers to the United States peaked in the early 1900s. In 1907 alone, officials at Ellis Island admitted 1,004,756 people. Then members of Congress passed quota laws in 1921 and 1924. A quota is a fixed number of people allowed in. Under the 1924 law, no more than about 165,000 immigrants could come each year, and almost no one from Asia could come at all. On the West Coast, officials at Angel Island held Chinese arrivals anywhere from a few days to many months.

From 1929 to 1936, officials pushed somewhere between 500,000 and 2 million people of Mexican descent out of the country. By the two larger counts, about 6 in 10 of them were US citizens. In 1939 officials in Cuba, the United States and Canada refused to let a ship full of Jewish refugees land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ellis Island's busiest years and the melting pot" -->
Ellis Island's busiest year was 1907. Officials there admitted 1,004,756 immigrants that year. On the busiest single day, April 17, 1907, 11,747 people came through. From 1892 to 1954, about 12 million immigrants passed through Ellis Island. Older claims of about 17 million are too high.

On October 5, 1908, a play by Israel Zangwill called *The Melting Pot* opened in Washington, D.C. Theodore Roosevelt watched the first night from his box and praised it. After that, the phrase "melting pot" spread. A melting pot is a pot in which different metals melt together into one. People used the phrase to mean that newcomers from many lands would blend together into one nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-capra" name="Frank Capra" movie="Frank Capra's American Dream (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frank Capra

> **Who:** A boy from Sicily who crossed to America in the cheapest part of a ship in 1903. He grew up to win three Academy Awards for directing films.
> **When and where:** Born May 18, 1897, in Bisacquino, Sicily. Came through Ellis Island in 1903, then went on to Los Angeles.
> **Movie:** *Frank Capra's American Dream* (1997), a documentary made for television. Kenneth Bowser directed it, and Ron Howard hosted it.

Frank Capra came to the United States from Sicily in 1903 as a young boy. As a grown man he directed films, and in the 1930s he won three Academy Awards as best director.

He was born Francesco Rosario Capra on May 18, 1897, in the town of Bisacquino. Sicily is an island that belongs to Italy. His family crossed the Atlantic on the ship *Germania*. They traveled in steerage, the cheapest space on a ship, below the decks. They passed through Ellis Island and then rode a train across the whole country to Los Angeles.

In 1946 he made the film *It's a Wonderful Life*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-capra" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="doukenie-bacos" name="Doukenie Bacos" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Doukenie Bacos

> **Who:** A Greek girl who reached Ellis Island at 16. She was traveling underage and was afraid officials would send her back.
> **When and where:** Born December 18, 1904, to a Greek family in Thrace. Sailed from Piraeus, Greece. Reached Ellis Island January 5, 1921.

Doukenie Bacos reached Ellis Island at the age of 16. She was traveling underage, which means she was younger than an adult. She was afraid the officials would send her home.

Her full name was Doukenie Babayanie Bacos. She was born on December 18, 1904, to a Greek family in Thrace, a region of southeastern Europe now divided among Greece, Turkey and Bulgaria. She sailed on the ship *King Alexander* from Piraeus, the port city of Athens. She left on December 22, 1920, and reached Ellis Island on January 5, 1921. Her interview opens with the year 1919, and National Park Service staff later marked that year as a mistake.

Officials at Ellis Island held her for three days. Her uncle, who had paid for her ticket, was a bachelor, a man who had never married. The officials would not hand a girl her age to a bachelor alone, so he had to find a family for her to live with. She was afraid they would send her back, and she later said she had thought of falling into the river and dying rather than go home. On the third day the officials let her go. That night her uncle came with a woman he called her "aunt," and Doukenie went to live with that family. In 1991 she told her story to the Ellis Island Oral History Project, which records interviews with people who came through the island. Her interview is number 49 in its EI series.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="doukenie-bacos" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Angel Island" -->
Angel Island Immigration Station opened in 1910 on an island in San Francisco Bay. The officials who worked there mainly enforced the Chinese Exclusion Act and other laws against Asian immigrants. They held many Asian arrivals for days or months. The National Park Service states that some stays lasted several years, while the longest stay in other records is 22 months. Officials refused entry to about 18 out of every 100 people who arrived at Angel Island. At Ellis Island the figure was 1 to 3 out of every 100.

Guards locked the detainees, the people being held, in crowded dormitories. A dormitory is a large room full of beds. No detainee could leave it without a guard. Officers read every letter the detainees sent or received. No one could visit a detainee until the officials had decided the case.

Doctors from the Public Health Service examined the arrivals. They turned people's eyelids inside out to look for trachoma, an eye infection that can cause blindness. They took samples of blood, spit, urine or stool, which means bowel movements. They tested Asian arrivals for worms that live inside the body, such as hookworm. When officials doubted a person's age, doctors made the person strip naked. The doctors then examined the person's hair, skin, teeth, voice and genitals, the private parts of the body.

Under the exclusion laws, most Chinese people could get in only by showing a family tie to a US citizen. Some claimed a tie that was false. They were called "paper sons" and "paper daughters," because the family tie existed only on paper. To catch them, a group of officials called a Board of Special Inquiry questioned each Chinese applicant about home villages and relatives. The board was made up of two inspectors, a translator and a stenographer, a clerk who wrote down every word. One inspector said in 1934 that "well over ninety percent" of his time went to questioning arriving Chinese people.

One encyclopedia states that some detainees killed themselves, before leaving or on the voyage back to China. It lists no names and no count.

Detainees carved poems into the wooden walls of the barracks where they were held. The poems were almost lost before a park ranger, Alexander Weiss, found them in 1970, and then they were saved.

Counts of the Chinese people who passed through the station differ. One count is about 175,000 between 1910 and 1940, and the National Park Service states 250,000.

On August 12, 1940, a fire burned the station's main office building, and the station closed soon after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-puey-you" name="Lee Puey You" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lee Puey You

> **Who:** A Chinese woman who came as a "paper daughter" in 1939. Officials held her on Angel Island for about 20 months and then sent her back to China.
> **When and where:** Arrived at Angel Island in 1939. Deported to China in November 1940. Came back in 1947 and settled in San Francisco.

Officials held Lee Puey You on Angel Island for about 20 months, one of the longest stays known for a Chinese detainee. Then they refused to let her in and deported her, which means they forced her to leave the country.

She arrived in 1939 as a "paper daughter." Inspectors asked her about 170 questions. In November 1940 officials sent her back to China. She later said she "cried a bowlful of tears."

In 1943 members of Congress repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act. In 1947 Lee Puey You came back to the United States as a war bride, meaning a woman engaged or married to a man who served in the war. She married and settled in San Francisco. Judy Yung later wrote about her in an article called "A Bowlful of Tears Revisited."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-puey-you" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The quota laws of 1921 and 1924" -->
In 1921 members of Congress passed the Emergency Quota Act, the first law to put number limits on immigration. The yearly quota for each nationality was 3 percent of the people born in that country who lived in the United States in 1910. That count came from the census, the official count of everyone living in the country.

In 1924 members of Congress passed the Immigration Act of 1924, which set much lower limits. It is also called the Johnson-Reed Act, after the two lawmakers behind it. Representative Albert Johnson of Washington State was chairman of the House committee on immigration. David Reed was a senator from Pennsylvania. President Calvin Coolidge signed the law on May 26, 1924.

Under the 1924 law, each country's quota was 2 percent of the people born there who lived in the United States in 1890. Lawmakers picked 1890 because it came before the large wave from southern and eastern Europe. Italy's yearly quota fell from more than 42,000 to fewer than 6,000. Lawmakers also shut out anyone "ineligible for citizenship," meaning anyone who could not become a citizen under US law. At that time the law let only white people and people of African birth or descent become citizens. In 1922 and 1923 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that immigrants from Japan and from India could not count as white. Under that rule, almost no one from Asia could come in.

Lawmakers capped the total at about 165,000 people a year. Under the 1921 law it had been about 355,000. About 86 of every 100 quota places went to northern and western Europe. About 9 went to southern and eastern Europe. With those limits, the great rush of newcomers from Europe ended.

In December 1923, in his first message to Congress, Coolidge had said, "America must be kept American. For this purpose, it is necessary to continue a policy of restricted immigration." The State Department's Office of the Historian states: "The most basic purpose of the 1924 Immigration Act was to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity." Homogeneity means sameness.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eugenics and the men behind the 1924 law" -->
The men who shaped the 1924 law argued for it with eugenics. Eugenics was a false belief that a nation could improve its people by controlling who had children and who came into the country. Its believers ranked groups of people as "better" or "worse" by birth.

Harry H. Laughlin ran the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, New York. He testified, meaning he spoke under oath at a hearing, to Johnson's committee three times between 1920 and 1924. He claimed that immigrants from southern and eastern Europe had higher rates of "feeblemindedness, insanity, criminality, and dependency." He meant mainly Italians and Jews. Those were old words for low intelligence, mental illness, crime and needing public charity. He said that too many of the people in mental hospitals came from southern and eastern Europe. His own figures showed high rates for German and Irish immigrants as well. Johnson named Laughlin the committee's "expert eugenics agent."

In 1922 Laughlin also wrote a model law for forced sterilization. A model law is a sample law for lawmakers to copy. Sterilization is an operation after which a person can never have a baby. For a woman, a doctor cut or tied the two tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb, or cut out a piece of each tube, so that no egg could reach the womb again. For a man, a doctor cut the tubes that carry sperm. The operation could not be undone.

In part because of Laughlin's work, lawmakers in dozens of states passed laws that let officials force people to be sterilized against their will. By the 1960s doctors in the United States had sterilized more than 62,000 people this way, about 60 out of every 100 of them women. In Virginia, after the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 8 to 1 that the state could do it, Dr. John H. Bell cut out pieces of both of Carrie Buck's tubes on October 19, 1927. In 1933 the Nazis, the party of Adolf Hitler that then ruled Germany, passed a forced sterilization law based in part on the American practice. About 400,000 people were sterilized in Nazi Germany. In 1936 the University of Heidelberg, in Germany, gave Laughlin an honorary degree.

Madison Grant was a New York lawyer. In 1916 he wrote a book called *The Passing of the Great Race*. In it he claimed that a "Nordic race" from northwestern Europe was better than all other people. Grant helped Johnson write the 1924 law. Hitler wrote to Grant and called the book his "Bible."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mexican arrivals and the Mexican Repatriation" -->
The Mexican Revolution was a war in Mexico that lasted from 1910 to 1920. During those years about 350,000 people fled north across the border into the United States. More kept coming in the 1920s.

From 1929 to 1936, officials pushed people of Mexican descent out of the United States. This is called the Mexican Repatriation. Repatriation means sending people back to their own country. Most of the people sent to Mexico had been born in the United States, so they were being sent out of their own country. It happened during the Great Depression, the years when many people in the United States had no work.

Secretary of Labor William N. Doak, who served under President Herbert Hoover, led a national campaign to remove Mexicans. He claimed they were taking jobs from other workers. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel, head of the city's unemployment relief committee, planned a publicity campaign. Relief was public money for people without work.

Officials raided Mexican American neighborhoods and public places. County welfare officers told families that their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico. Officials of the Los Angeles County welfare office ran the trains, and the first one left on March 23, 1931. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks bound for the border. Families lost their property. In a 2005 California law, state lawmakers wrote that this property "often was sold by local authorities" as "payment" for the cost of removing the families.

On February 26, 1931, immigration agents blocked the exits of La Placita, a park in Los Angeles, while about 400 people were inside. Accounts of what happened next differ. According to the California Migration Museum, the agents arrested about a dozen people and deported a few. According to a History.com article, they rounded up more than 400 people and deported them.

Counts of the people removed run from about 500,000 to about 2 million. The California Migration Museum states about 1 million, following the historians Francisco Balderrama and Raymond Rodríguez. By that count, about 60 of every 100 were citizens born in the United States. The 2005 California law states about 2 million, and about 1.2 million of them had been born in the United States. Officials pushed about 35,000 people out of Los Angeles, about a third of the Mexican people living there.

California lawmakers apologized for the removals in that 2005 law. In February 2012 the members of the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors, who run the county, apologized and put up a plaque at LA Plaza. No apology from the federal government is on record.

In August 1942, US and Mexican officials started the Bracero Program. Under it, Mexican farmworkers came to the United States on contracts to work for a set time and then go home. Bracero is a Spanish word for a man who works with his arms, from *brazo*, the word for arm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The St. Louis and the first refugee law" -->
On May 13, 1939, the German ship *St. Louis* left Hamburg with 937 passengers. Almost all of them were Jewish refugees, people fleeing their country to escape danger. The Nazi government of Germany had taken over Austria in March 1938, and attacks on Jews grew that year. On the night of November 9 and 10, 1938, Nazi leaders set off attacks on Jews across Germany. Nazis burned more than 1,400 synagogues, wrecked thousands of Jewish-owned shops and broke into Jewish homes. German police locked about 26,000 Jewish men in concentration camps, prison camps for people the Nazis targeted, just because they were Jewish. That night is called Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass. Afterward Nazi officials seized property that Jews owned. The ship sailed for Havana, Cuba. Cuba's president, Federico Laredo Brú, canceled their papers to land, and only 28 passengers were let off at Havana. The passengers asked President Franklin D. Roosevelt for help, and he never answered them. State Department officials told them they must "await their turns on the waiting list." Canadian officials refused them too.

On June 6 the ship turned back to Europe. The passengers went to four countries: 288 to Britain, 224 to France, 214 to Belgium and 181 to the Netherlands. Of the 620 who went back to the European mainland, 87 moved on to other countries before the German army invaded in 1940. Of the rest, 254 were killed in the Holocaust, and 278 survived. The Holocaust was the murder of Jews and other people by the Nazis, the party that ruled Germany, during World War II.

In 1948 members of Congress passed the Displaced Persons Act. It was the first refugee law in the United States. Displaced persons were Europeans who had lost their homes in the war. Under the law, 200,000 people could come at first. They were counted against the yearly quotas. In 1950 lawmakers raised the total. Between 1948 and 1952 about 350,000 to 400,000 European refugees settled in the United States. One count gives 393,542.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1954 federal officers rounded up Mexicans and Mexican Americans and sent them to Mexico. Many of the people they sent were US citizens. In 1965 members of Congress ended the quota system that had favored northern and western Europe since the 1920s. After that, the main sources of immigration shifted to Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Caribbean.

Refugees came from Cuba after 1959 and from Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia after 1975. Counts credited to the United Nations refugee agency give at least 200,000 Vietnamese who died at sea while escaping by boat. About 2.7 million people who had lived in the country for years without legal permission got legal status under a 1986 law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The braceros and Operation Wetback, 1954" -->
The Bracero Program lasted until 1964. Over those years, Mexican farmworkers signed about 4.5 million short-term contracts to work in the United States. The workers came for the length of a contract and were expected to go home afterward.

In 1954 federal officers carried out a mass deportation of Mexicans. Officials named it "Operation Wetback." "Wetback" is a slur, an insulting name, for Mexicans who crossed the border without papers. The word suggested that they had swum across the Rio Grande, the river along the border between Texas and Mexico.

Attorney General Herbert Brownell Jr., the head of the US Justice Department, pushed for the operation. He served under President Dwight D. Eisenhower. General Joseph M. Swing ran it. He was the commissioner, or head, of the Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS), the federal office in charge of immigration. Swing announced the operation on June 9, 1954. INS officials planned it with officials of the Mexican government. Officers started in California and Arizona and moved into Texas in the middle of July.

Border Patrol officers, the federal agents who guard the border, ran the roundups like military operations, and newspapers covered them heavily. Officials meant the coverage to scare people into leaving on their own. On the first day in Texas, July 15, 1954, officers caught 4,800 people. Officers sent people south by truck, bus, train and plane. From Port Isabel, Texas, they also put people on two ships, the *Emancipation* and the *Mercurio*, bound for Veracruz, Mexico. About a quarter of the people removed from Texas went by ship. A History.com article states that the ships were later compared to slave ships.

Seven deportees jumped from the *Mercurio*. The *Handbook of Texas* says a mutiny followed, meaning the people on board rose up against the crew. Other accounts say the seven drowned. After protests in Mexico, officials stopped sending people by ship.

The historian Mae M. Ngai reports that in July 1955 officers rounded up workers in 112-degree heat, and 88 braceros, Mexican farm workers hired under contract, died of sunstroke as a result. Sunstroke is an illness in which the body gets so hot that it can no longer cool itself. A labor official told Ngai that more would have died without help from the Red Cross.

Ngai also quotes a Mexican labor leader. He reported that deportees were carried into Mexico on trucks "like cows" and unloaded in the desert, fifteen miles down the highway from the border. US immigration officials also left deportees in Mexicali, a Mexican border city where the summer heat averages 108 degrees. No record says whether the 88 were left in the desert, and no record names the officers who ran that roundup. Other deportees died of disease and other causes while officers held them. No full count of the dead is on record, and no record shows that any official was charged for the deaths.

INS officials claimed that as many as 1,300,000 people had left. That count included people who, officials said, had fled out of fear. An INS report gave nearly 1.1 million people caught. One encyclopedia explains that most of those people had been caught before the operation began, because the year the report counted ended on June 30, 1954. The real number of arrests was far lower. In the San Antonio district, officers caught slightly more than 80,000 people. In Texas in July 1954, officers held 42,000 people, and more than 63,000 left on their own. The historian Kelly Lytle Hernández puts the true number deported at "likely closer to 300,000." Many of the people deported were US citizens of Mexican descent. No one knows how many.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="antonio-garcia-bracero" name="Antonio García" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Antonio García

> **Who:** A Mexican farmworker who joined the Bracero Program at seventeen, after having his military ID card changed to say he was eighteen.
> **When and where:** Born June 13, 1937, in Tepehuanes, Durango, Mexico. Worked on contracts in Texas, California, Colorado and New Mexico into the early 1960s. Told his story in Chicago in 2003.

To get into the Bracero Program at seventeen, Antonio García had his military ID card changed to say he was eighteen. He then worked in the United States on and off for years as a contract farmworker.

He was born on June 13, 1937, in Tepehuanes, in the Mexican state of Durango. He grew up in Nuevo Ideal and left school after fourth grade. He signed up at the contracting centers in Chihuahua and in Empalme, Sonora, where Mexican workers signed contracts to work in the United States. His first contract was for work in the cotton fields of Texas. Within a week he was seriously ill. He kept working on contracts into the early 1960s, in California, Colorado, New Mexico and Texas.

On March 12, 2003, Claudia Morales Haro recorded his life story in Chicago. The recording is item 162 in the Bracero History Archive, which contains more than 700 such interviews. His record does not say whether he settled in the United States for good.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="antonio-garcia-bracero" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1965 law" -->
In 1965 members of Congress passed the Immigration and Nationality Act, also called the Hart-Celler Act. With it, lawmakers ended the national-origins quotas, the rules that had limited immigration country by country since the 1920s. Under the new law, people got in mainly through family ties to people already here or through their job skills. Lawmakers also ended the bars against Asians and against southern and eastern Europeans.

For the first time, lawmakers also set a limit on the number of immigrants from the Western Hemisphere, the half of the world that includes North and South America. That limit included Mexico. Over the following decades, the main sources of immigration shifted away from Europe, toward Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cubans and the Mariel boatlift" -->
In 1959 Fidel Castro led a revolution in Cuba. Through the 1960s, many Cubans came to the United States, especially to Florida.

In 1980 Castro briefly let people leave. From April to October 1980, about 125,000 Cubans crossed by boat from the port of Mariel to Florida. This was the Mariel boatlift.

About a month before the boatlift began, members of Congress passed the Refugee Act of 1980. With it, lawmakers created a permanent system for admitting refugees. They defined a refugee the same way the United Nations does: a person outside their own country who cannot go back because of a real fear of being harmed for their race, religion, nationality, group or political beliefs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="reinaldo-arenas" name="Reinaldo Arenas" movie="Before Night Falls (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Reinaldo Arenas

> **Who:** A Cuban novelist whom Cuban government officials persecuted and jailed for his writing and for being gay. He left Cuba in the Mariel boatlift.
> **When and where:** Born July 16, 1943, near Holguín, Cuba. Left through the port of Mariel in May 1980. Lived in New York. Died December 7, 1990.
> **Movie:** *Before Night Falls* (2000), a drama based on his life story. Julian Schnabel directed it. Javier Bardem played Arenas, a role that earned an Oscar nomination.

Reinaldo Arenas was a novelist whom Cuban government officials put in prison for his writing and for being gay. Gay means loving people of the same sex. In May 1980 he left Cuba through the port of Mariel. He was one of about 125,000 Cubans in the boatlift.

He was born on July 16, 1943, near Holguín, in Cuba. Cuban officials persecuted him, which means they punished him again and again for who he was and what he wrote. In 1973, according to records at Princeton University Library, officials charged him with "ideological deviation." That means holding ideas that Cuba's rulers banned. The second charge was publishing his books in other countries without permission. Other accounts differ. One says the court convicted him of being "extravagant" and "immoral." The Mexican writer Rafael Lemus writes that officials accused him in 1973 of abusing children, that he hid for four months, and that he was held from January 1974.

Arenas escaped from prison and tried to leave Cuba by floating away on a tire inner tube. Cuban officers caught him and locked him in El Morro, an old fort in Havana used as a prison. Officers of State Security, the political police of Fidel Castro's government, moved him to their headquarters, called Villa Marista. Lemus writes that at Villa Marista officers pumped steam into the prisoners' closed cell until it became a steam room. There officers forced Arenas to sign a paper saying he was sorry for being gay and for his books. He was released in 1976, after he signed a promise not to write again. No record names any of the officers who held him.

In the United States he lived and wrote in New York. He wrote the story of his own life, called *Before Night Falls*. It was published in 1992, and in English in 1993.

He became sick with AIDS, a disease caused by a virus that destroys the body's defenses against other illnesses. On December 7, 1990, at the age of 47, he killed himself.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="reinaldo-arenas" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Refugees from Southeast Asia and the boat people" -->
In 1975 North Vietnam's army took Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam, at the end of the Vietnam War. After that, refugees fled Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. They included the Hmong, an ethnic group from the mountains of Laos. Many Vietnamese escaped in small boats, and they became known as the boat people.

Storms, hunger, disease and pirates killed many of them at sea. Counts credited to the United Nations refugee agency run from 200,000 to 400,000 dead. The journalist Barry Wain estimated 30,000 dead by 1981. No one knows the exact number.

According to a UN refugee official in 1986 and a later study, the pirates were Thai fishermen in the Gulf of Thailand. Robert C. McCabe, a researcher at Maynooth University in Ireland, writes that some fishermen turned to piracy after big commercial fishing boats had cut the catch in their villages. No record names a single pirate.

Pirates robbed and raped refugees, murdered some and set others adrift. Rape means forcing a person into sex. In 1981 alone, the UN refugee agency counted 452 boats that reached Thailand with 15,479 refugees. Pirates had attacked 349 of those boats, on average three times each. They kidnapped 228 women, and 881 people were dead or missing.

In June 1982 officials of the UN refugee agency and of the Thai government started a program against the pirates. Twelve countries paid for it, and the United States gave $1.2 million of the $3.67 million. By October 1987, 96 suspects had been arrested. No record names the officers who arrested them. Judges gave sentences of two to fifty years in prison, and one pirate was sentenced to death in December 1986.

From 1975 to 1997, US officials accepted 402,382 Vietnamese refugees from camps in Southeast Asia and Hong Kong, more than any other country. A different count gives nearly 281,000 Vietnamese refugees settled in the United States from 1981 to 1990 alone. About 500,000 more Vietnamese came to the United States by 1994 through the Orderly Departure Program, a legal way to leave Vietnam without the boat trip.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tung-trinh" name="Tung Trinh" movie="Bolinao 52 (2008)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tung Trinh

> **Who:** One of 52 survivors of a refugee boat from Vietnam that drifted at sea for 37 days in 1988.
> **When and where:** Left Vietnam by boat in 1988. Brought ashore at Bolinao, in the Philippines. Later settled in Orange County, California.
> **Movie:** *Bolinao 52* (2008), a documentary directed by Duc Nguyen. It won two regional Emmy awards in 2009.

In 1988 Tung Trinh was one of 110 people on a small boat that left Vietnam. The boat's engine stopped working, and the boat drifted at sea for 37 days.

More than 20 ships passed the boat during those days. On June 10, 1988, one of them, the USS Dubuque, a ship of the United States Navy, stopped. Its sailors handed over some food, water and maps, but they did not take the people aboard. They guessed 50 to 70 people were on the boat, not 110. About 20 people had died before the Dubuque came, and the rest died after it left.

Of the 110 people on board, 58 died. During the drift, some of the people on the boat ate human flesh to stay alive. Eating human flesh is called cannibalism.

The Navy removed the Dubuque's commanding officer from his command. A military court found him guilty of breaking orders to help people in danger at sea, and he received a letter of reprimand, a written scolding.

Filipino fishermen found the boat and brought the 52 survivors to Bolinao, in the Philippines. Tung Trinh later came to the United States and settled in Orange County, California.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tung-trinh" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sergey-brin" name="Sergey Brin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sergey Brin

> **Who:** A Jewish boy from Moscow whose family left the Soviet Union in 1979. In 1998 he started Google with Larry Page.
> **When and where:** Born 1973 in Moscow. Arrived in the United States October 25, 1979, at six.

Sergey Brin came to the United States from the Soviet Union on October 25, 1979, when he was six. In 1998 he and Larry Page started the company Google.

He was born in Moscow in 1973. The Soviet Union was a communist country, where the government owned the businesses and only one party was allowed, and Moscow was its capital. His family was Jewish. In the Soviet Union his parents were held back in their careers and harassed because they were Jews. The accounts of his life do not name the people who did it.

In 1979 the family got permission to leave. About 51,000 Soviet Jews left that year, the most in any single year. In the United States his father, Michael, taught mathematics at the University of Maryland. His mother, Eugenia, became a researcher at NASA, the US space agency.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sergey-brin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1986 law" -->
On November 6, 1986, President Ronald Reagan signed the Immigration Reform and Control Act, called IRCA. About 2.7 million people who had lived in the country for years without legal permission got legal status under it. To qualify, they had to show they had lived in the country without a break since before 1982. Certain farmworkers could also qualify.

The same law, for the first time, let the government punish employers who knowingly hired workers without legal permission. These punishments are called employer sanctions. After 1986 the number of people living in the country without legal permission grew again.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
About 47.8 million people living in the United States in 2023 had been born in another country, by the Census Bureau's count. That was 14.3 percent of the population, the highest share since 1910. More of them came from Mexico than from any other country.

The US Border Patrol counted 895 migrant deaths along the border with Mexico in the year ending September 2022, the most on record. In April 2018 Attorney General Jeff Sessions announced a "zero tolerance" policy: every adult who crossed the border illegally was to be charged with a crime. Border Patrol agents then took thousands of children from their parents. In January 2026 immigration officers held more than 71,000 people in detention, the most ever, according to a report by two human rights groups. These figures are as of September 2026 and will change.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who comes now" -->
In 2023 the Census Bureau's American Community Survey counted about 47.8 million foreign-born people in the United States. Foreign-born means born in another country. They made up 14.3 percent of everyone living here. That was the highest share since 1910, when it was 14.7 percent. The highest share ever recorded was 14.8 percent, in 1890. The lowest was 4.7 percent, in 1970.

The Pew Research Center counted where the immigrants living here in 2023 were born. About 11 million came from Mexico, 22 of every 100 immigrants. India came next with 3.2 million, then China with 3 million, the Philippines with 2.1 million and Cuba with 1.7 million. About 52 of every 100 immigrants were born in Latin America, and 27 of every 100 in Asia. The Pew researchers found that immigration from Mexico has slowed since about 2007.

The number of immigrants from Africa south of the Sahara Desert roughly tripled from 2000 to about 2.5 million in 2024, the Migration Policy Institute found. Communities of people born in Jamaica, Haiti, the Dominican Republic and other Caribbean countries also grew.

Pew and the Department of Homeland Security both publish estimates of how many people live in the country without legal permission. Their estimates reached about 12 million in 2007, then fell to about 11 million, and rose again in the 2020s. The estimates are uncertain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homeland Security and DACA" -->
After the attacks of September 11, 2001, members of Congress passed the Homeland Security Act of 2002. With it, lawmakers created the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which began work in 2003. Lawmakers ended the old INS and gave its immigration work to three new DHS agencies: US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and Customs and Border Protection (CBP). ICE officers arrest and hold immigrants inside the country. CBP officers, including the Border Patrol, work at the borders.

On June 15, 2012, DHS officials started DACA, short for Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals. Under DACA, some people who were brought to the country as children can apply to have their removal from the country deferred, or put off, and to work legally. DACA has faced challenges in court again and again. In September 2017 Sessions advised ending it, and Elaine Duke, the acting Secretary of Homeland Security, ended it. On June 18, 2020, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the way officials had ended DACA broke federal law, so DACA stayed. On January 17, 2025, a federal appeals court ruled that its work permits were unlawful and limited that ruling to Texas. About 455,000 people held DACA status on March 31, 2026, according to the National Immigration Forum, an immigrant rights group, using USCIS figures.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaths at the border" -->
Every year hundreds of migrants die trying to cross from Mexico into the United States. Border Patrol officials count these deaths by the federal budget year, which runs from October to September. Their count was 281 in 2018, 300 in 2019, 254 in 2020, 568 in 2021 and 895 in 2022. The 2022 count is the highest on record. For 2023, a report of April 2025 from the Government Accountability Office, which checks federal agencies for Congress, gives 704 deaths and more than 5,800 rescues.

In 2022 the government's own auditors found that the Border Patrol's counts were too low. Border Patrol officials had not collected complete records of migrant deaths. They had left out deaths found by sheriffs, by medical examiners, the doctors who find out why a person died, and by other agencies. The auditors later reported that Border Patrol officials had made the changes they asked for.

People die of heat and thirst in the desert, drown in the Rio Grande, or die during or after chases by Border Patrol agents. Some die in custody, meaning while officers are holding them. Investigators inside CBP counted 171 deaths in the 2022 budget year in which CBP officers were involved "in some way." Of those people, 52 died in CBP custody.

The International Organization for Migration (IOM), a United Nations agency, keeps a second count. Its staff recorded 686 deaths and disappearances on the US-Mexico border in 2022, and they called it "the world's deadliest migration land route." Of those people, 307 died crossing the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts, most of them from heat. The IOM calls its figures minimums, so the real numbers are higher, because Texas county coroners, the officials who look into deaths, and Mexican search teams do not report every death to it.

For 2025, the IOM counted 409 migrant deaths in all of North and South America, the lowest count since 2014. Its staff linked the drop to fewer people trying to cross the US-Mexico border and the Darién, a jungle between Colombia and Panama. In February 2026 they said the 2025 figures would not be final until the middle of 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Children taken from their parents, 2018" -->
On April 6, 2018, Attorney General Jeff Sessions announced a "zero tolerance" policy. The Attorney General is the head of the Justice Department. Sessions told federal prosecutors along the southwest border to charge with a crime every adult whom DHS officers sent to them for crossing the border illegally. That included parents who came with their children. Officers sent the parents to criminal custody, and Border Patrol agents took their children away from them.

Inside DHS, three agency heads proposed charging those parents in a memo dated April 23, 2018. They were Kevin K. McAleenan, the head of CBP, Thomas Homan, the acting head of ICE, and L. Francis Cissna, the head of USCIS. They recommended sending every adult who crossed illegally to be charged, "including those initially arriving or apprehended with minors." Minors are children. Sessions urged Kirstjen Nielsen, the Secretary of Homeland Security, to approve the memo's plan. She was the head of DHS, and she approved it on May 4, 2018.

Border Patrol leaders told their commanders along the Mexican border to begin on May 5. McAleenan left out parents with children under five who were crossing illegally for the first time. Agents asked a federal refugee office to take each child, and ICE officers held the parents. In a later court case, government lawyers wrote that there was "no prescribed specific course of action for reunification." That means no one had set a plan for putting the families back together.

On May 7, 2018, Sessions said in a speech, "If you are smuggling a child, then we will prosecute you and that child will be separated from you as required by law." In 2017, under a trial program in El Paso, Texas, officials had already separated about 280 families.

Michael Horowitz, the Justice Department's inspector general, later investigated. An inspector general is an official who checks the work of a government department. In January 2021 Horowitz reported that Sessions and a small number of other Justice Department officials knew, when the policy was issued in April 2018, that DHS "would change its long-standing practice" and separate families. He found that the Attorney General's office "was a driving force" in that change. He also wrote that the department's "single-minded focus on increasing immigration prosecutions came at the expense of careful and appropriate consideration of the impact of child separations."

Officials deported hundreds of parents without their children before a court ordered the families put back together in June 2018. Counts of the children differ, because each measures something different. Staff of a federal group, the Family Reunification Task Force, counted 3,924 children taken from their parents between January 20, 2017, and January 20, 2021. In February 2023 the task force said that more than 600 had been reunited by then. It said that 998 were still separated and that the true number still apart was "likely greater than 1,000."

On December 11, 2023, a federal judge approved a settlement in a lawsuit brought by the American Civil Liberties Union, called *Ms. L. v. ICE*. A settlement is an agreement that ends a lawsuit. It covers an estimated 4,500 to 5,000 children and their parents. By March 20, 2024, the task force had confirmed 1,120 separated children as part of that group. No final count of children returned to their parents is on record. No official has been charged for the separations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Arrests, detention and deportation, 2025 and 2026" -->
Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. swore in Donald Trump as president on January 20, 2025, in the Rotunda, the round central hall of the US Capitol. Detention means holding people in custody while officials decide whether to deport them.

In January 2026 ICE officers held more than 71,000 people in detention, a record. Human Rights Watch and Physicians for Human Rights gave that figure in a report of June 25, 2026. ABC News reported on July 21, 2026, that ICE officers held 65,765 people, citing figures ICE had posted.

The same report counted 52 people who died in ICE custody from January 20, 2025, to June 4, 2026. Thirty-nine of them died in the first year. Of those 39, seven were apparent suicides, meaning they appeared to have killed themselves, and five died of injuries. The others died of strokes, heart failure, long-term illness and failure to breathe. The death rate reached about 8.4 for every 10,000 people held, which the report describes as "nearly four times" the rate under the previous president.

Removal is the government's word for deportation. ABC News reported that ICE had posted 356,389 removals in the 2026 budget year through about July 21, 2026. For the 2025 budget year, the immigration researcher Austin Kocher counted about 329,018 ICE removals. On January 20, 2026, DHS officials claimed "more than 675,000 deportations" and "an estimated 2.2 million self-deportations" in the year since January 20, 2025. Self-deportation means leaving the country on one's own, without being deported by officers. Kocher notes that DHS officials have not published how they count these numbers. He found that ICE's own removals made up only about 54 percent of an earlier DHS claim of 605,000. In that same year, by the DHS count, officers caught 90,084 people along the southwest border.

Federal immigration officers shot and killed at least four people from January 2025 to late January 2026, PBS NewsHour reported. One was Silverio Villegas González, a citizen of Mexico. On September 12, 2025, ICE officers stopped his car in Franklin Park, a suburb of Chicago. He had just dropped his children at school. One officer shot him in the neck, and he died. ICE officer Jonathan Ross shot Renée Nicole Good, a US citizen, in Minneapolis on January 7, 2026. CBP officers shot Alex Pretti in Minneapolis on January 24, 2026. As of that report, no officer had been charged.

DHS officials said that Villegas González drove his car at officers and dragged one of them, and that the officer fired "fearing for his own life." In body-camera video, an agent described his own injuries as "nothing major." Federal officials have not released the name of the officer who shot him, and federal prosecutors declined to charge that officer. In spring 2026, at the request of the Franklin Park police, investigators of the Illinois State Police opened their own case. It was still open in September 2026, the Chicago Sun-Times and WBEZ reported.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="giannis-antetokounmpo" name="Giannis Antetokounmpo" movie="Rise (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Giannis Antetokounmpo

> **Who:** A basketball player born in Greece to Nigerian parents. He grew up a citizen of no country and came to the United States when a National Basketball Association (NBA) team picked him in 2013.
> **When and where:** Born December 6, 1994, in Athens, Greece. Came to the United States in 2013, at 18, to play for the Milwaukee Bucks.
> **Movie:** *Rise* (2022), a Disney+ drama about the Antetokounmpo family, made with the family. It is a dramatization, not a documentary.

Giannis Antetokounmpo grew up stateless, meaning no country counted him as its citizen. In 2013, at the age of 18, he came to the United States to play basketball for the Milwaukee Bucks.

He was born in Athens on December 6, 1994. His parents, Charles and Veronica Antetokounmpo, were immigrants from Nigeria with no legal status in Greece. Under Greek law, a child does not become a citizen just by being born in Greece. As boys, Giannis and his brothers sold goods on the street.

Greek officials made him a citizen in 2013. A few weeks later, in June 2013, the Bucks' managers picked him 15th in the NBA draft, the yearly event in which teams choose new players. He was named the league's Most Valuable Player in 2019 and in 2020. In 2021 he won the NBA championship with the Bucks and was named the most valuable player of the finals. His parents and brothers joined him in the United States.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lost-boys-of-sudan" name="Peter Dut and Santino Chuor, Lost Boys of Sudan" movie="Lost Boys of Sudan (2003)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Peter Dut and Santino Chuor, Lost Boys of Sudan

> **Who:** Two Dinka teenagers whose parents were killed when government forces attacked their villages in Sudan's civil war. They lived for years in a refugee camp in Kenya. They were among the "Lost Boys" resettled in the United States.
> **When and where:** From Sudan. Kakuma refugee camp, Kenya. Houston, Texas, in their first year in the United States, during the resettlement that began in 2001.
> **Movie:** *Lost Boys of Sudan* (2003), a documentary that follows these two young men. Megan Mylan and Jon Shenk made it. It was nominated for an Emmy and won the Truer Than Fiction Award at the Independent Spirit Awards.

Peter Nyarol Dut and Santino Majok Chuor were two of about 4,000 young refugees from Sudan who were resettled in the United States starting in 2001. They were called the "Lost Boys of Sudan."

They were Dinka, an ethnic group of southern Sudan. Sudan's civil war was a war inside one country, between the government in the north and rebels in the south. Military forces of the central government attacked the boys' villages, destroyed them and killed their families, according to the notes for the PBS film about them. Those notes do not state which soldiers or fighters killed each boy's parents. Researchers at Human Rights Watch report that in these years Sudan's rulers gave modern weapons to militias called the muraheleen. The militia fighters burned Dinka villages, stole cattle and food, and carried off women and children as slaves. Human Rights Watch does not say that these militias attacked the two boys' own villages. The rebel army of the south, the Sudan People's Liberation Army, also took many of the older Lost Boys as soldiers. The two boys survived a long walk out of Sudan and then spent years in Kakuma, a refugee camp in Kenya.

The documentary follows their first year in the United States, which began in Houston. After a time, Peter left Houston for Kansas to go to high school.
