## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Immigration to the United States reached its highest point in the early 1900s. In 1907 alone, officials at Ellis Island admitted 1,004,756 people. Then members of Congress passed quota laws in 1921 and 1924. A quota is a fixed number of people allowed in. Under the 1924 law, no more than about 165,000 immigrants could come each year, and almost no one from Asia could come at all. On the West Coast, officials at Angel Island held Chinese arrivals for days, for months and sometimes for years.

From 1929 to 1936, officials pushed somewhere between 500,000 and 2 million people of Mexican descent out of the country. Most of them were US citizens. In 1939 officials in Cuba, the United States and Canada refused to let a ship full of Jewish refugees land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ellis Island's busiest years and the melting pot" -->
Ellis Island's busiest year was 1907. Officials there admitted 1,004,756 immigrants that year. On the busiest single day, April 17, 1907, 11,747 people came through. From 1892 to 1954, about 12 million immigrants passed through Ellis Island. Some older books give about 17 million, a figure that is too high.

On October 5, 1908, a play by Israel Zangwill called *The Melting Pot* opened in Washington, D.C. Theodore Roosevelt watched the first night from his box and praised it. After that, the phrase "melting pot" spread. A melting pot is a pot in which different metals melt together into one. People used the phrase to mean that newcomers from many lands would blend into one people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-capra" name="Frank Capra" movie="Frank Capra's American Dream (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frank Capra

> **Who:** A boy from Sicily who crossed to America in the cheapest part of a ship in 1903. He grew up to win three Academy Awards for directing films.
> **When and where:** Born May 18, 1897, in Bisacquino, Sicily. Came through Ellis Island in 1903, then went on to Los Angeles.
> **Movie:** *Frank Capra's American Dream* (1997), a documentary made for television. Kenneth Bowser directed it, and Ron Howard hosted it.

Frank Capra came to the United States from Sicily in 1903 as a young boy. As a grown man he directed films, and in the 1930s he won three Academy Awards as best director.

He was born Francesco Rosario Capra on May 18, 1897, in the town of Bisacquino. Sicily is an island that belongs to Italy. His family crossed the Atlantic on the ship *Germania*. They traveled in steerage, the cheapest space on a ship, below the decks. They passed through Ellis Island and then rode a train across the whole country to Los Angeles.

In 1946 he made the film *It's a Wonderful Life*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-capra" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="doukenie-bacos" name="Doukenie Bacos" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Doukenie Bacos

> **Who:** A Greek girl who reached Ellis Island at 16. She was traveling underage and was afraid officials would send her back.
> **When and where:** Born December 18, 1904, to a Greek family in Thrace. Sailed from Piraeus, Greece. Reached Ellis Island January 5, 1921, by one date in her record.

Doukenie Bacos reached Ellis Island at the age of 16. She was traveling underage, which means she was younger than an adult. She was afraid the officials would send her home.

Her full name was Doukenie Babayanie Bacos. She was born on December 18, 1904, to a Greek family in Thrace, a region now divided among Greece, Turkey and Bulgaria. She sailed on the ship *King Alexander* from Piraeus, the port city of Athens. Her record gives January 5, 1921, as the day she reached Ellis Island. Other parts of the same record give December 22, 1919. The two dates do not agree.

She later said she had thought about jumping into the water rather than be sent back. In 1991 she told her story to the Ellis Island Oral History Project, which records interviews with people who came through the island. Her interview is number 49 in its EI series.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="doukenie-bacos" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Angel Island" -->
Angel Island Immigration Station opened in 1910 on an island in San Francisco Bay. The officials who worked there mainly enforced the Chinese Exclusion Act and other laws against Asian immigrants. They held many Asian arrivals for days or months, and some for years. The longest recorded stay was 22 months. Officials refused entry to about 18 out of every 100 people who arrived at Angel Island. At Ellis Island the figure was 1 to 3 out of every 100.

Guards locked the detainees, the people being held, in crowded dormitories. A dormitory is a large room full of beds. No detainee could leave it without a guard. Officers read every letter the detainees sent or received. No one could visit a detainee until the officials had decided the case.

Doctors from the Public Health Service examined the arrivals. They turned people's eyelids inside out to look for trachoma, an eye infection that can cause blindness. They took samples of blood, spit, urine or stool. They tested Asian arrivals for worms that live inside the body, such as hookworm. When officials doubted a person's age, doctors made the person strip naked. The doctors then examined the person's hair, skin, teeth, voice and genitals, the private parts of the body.

Under the exclusion laws, most Chinese people could get in only by showing a family tie to a US citizen. Some claimed a tie that was false. They were called "paper sons" and "paper daughters," because the family tie existed only on paper. To catch them, a group of officials called a Board of Special Inquiry questioned each Chinese applicant about home villages and relatives. The board was made up of two inspectors, a translator and a stenographer, a clerk who wrote down every word. One inspector said in 1934 that "well over ninety percent" of his time went to questioning arriving Chinese people.

One encyclopedia states that some detainees killed themselves, either on the island or on the voyage back to China. It gives no names and no count.

Detainees carved poems into the wooden walls of the barracks where they were held. The poems were nearly destroyed. In 1970 a park ranger, Alexander Weiss, found them, and they were saved. Counts of the Chinese people who passed through the station differ. One count gives about 175,000 between 1910 and 1940, and the National Park Service gives 250,000. On August 12, 1940, a fire burned the station's main office building, and the station closed soon after.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lee-puey-you" name="Lee Puey You" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lee Puey You

> **Who:** A Chinese woman who came as a "paper daughter" in 1939. Officials held her on Angel Island for about 20 months and then sent her back to China.
> **When and where:** Arrived at Angel Island in 1939. Deported to China in November 1940. Came back in 1947 and settled in San Francisco.

Officials held Lee Puey You on Angel Island for about 20 months, one of the longest stays known for a Chinese detainee. Then they refused to let her in and deported her, which means they forced her to leave the country.

She arrived in 1939 as a "paper daughter." Inspectors asked her about 170 questions. In November 1940 officials sent her back to China. She later said she "cried a bowlful of tears."

In 1943 members of Congress repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act. In 1947 Lee Puey You came back to the United States as a war bride, meaning a woman engaged or married to a man who served in the war. She married and settled in San Francisco. Judy Yung later wrote about her in an article called "A Bowlful of Tears Revisited."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lee-puey-you" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The quota laws of 1921 and 1924" -->
The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 set the first number limits on immigration. The yearly quota for each nationality was 3 percent of the people born in that country who lived in the United States in 1910. That count came from the census, the official count of everyone living in the country.

The Immigration Act of 1924 cut the numbers much further. It is also called the Johnson-Reed Act, after the two lawmakers behind it. Representative Albert Johnson of Washington State was chairman of the House committee on immigration. David Reed was a senator from Pennsylvania. President Calvin Coolidge signed the law on May 26, 1924.

Under the 1924 law, each country's quota was 2 percent of the people born there who lived in the United States in 1890. Lawmakers picked 1890 because it came before the large wave from southern and eastern Europe. Italy's yearly quota fell from more than 42,000 to fewer than 6,000. The law also shut out anyone "ineligible for citizenship," meaning anyone the law did not allow to become a citizen. Under that rule, almost no one from Asia could come in.

The total was capped at about 165,000 people a year. Under the 1921 law it had been about 355,000. About 86 of every 100 quota places went to northern and western Europe. About 9 went to southern and eastern Europe. With those limits, the great wave of immigration ended.

In December 1923, in his first message to Congress, Coolidge had said, "America must be kept American. For this purpose, it is necessary to continue a policy of restricted immigration." The State Department's Office of the Historian writes: "The most basic purpose of the 1924 Immigration Act was to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity." Homogeneity means sameness.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eugenics and the men behind the 1924 law" -->
The men who shaped the 1924 law argued for it with eugenics. Eugenics was a false belief that a nation could improve its people by controlling who had children and who came into the country. Its believers ranked groups of people as "better" or "worse" by birth.

Harry H. Laughlin ran the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, New York. He testified to Johnson's committee three times between 1920 and 1924. He claimed that immigrants from southern and eastern Europe, mainly Italians and Jews, had higher rates of "feeblemindedness, insanity, criminality, and dependency." Those were old words for low intelligence, mental illness, crime and needing public charity. He said that too many of the people in mental hospitals came from southern and eastern Europe. His own figures showed high rates for German and Irish immigrants as well. Johnson named Laughlin the committee's "expert eugenics agent."

In 1922 Laughlin also wrote a model law for forced sterilization, surgery that leaves a person unable ever to have children, which lawmakers in several states and in Nazi Germany later used as a pattern.

Madison Grant was a New York lawyer. In 1916 he wrote a book called *The Passing of the Great Race*. In it he claimed that a "Nordic race" from northwestern Europe was better than all other people. Grant helped Johnson write the 1924 law. Adolf Hitler, who later ruled Germany, wrote to Grant and called the book his "Bible."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mexican arrivals and the Mexican Repatriation" -->
The Mexican Revolution was a war in Mexico that lasted from 1910 to 1920. During those years about 350,000 people fled north across the border into the United States. More kept coming in the 1920s.

From 1929 to 1936, officials pushed people of Mexican descent out of the United States. This is called the Mexican Repatriation. Repatriation means sending people back to their own country. Most of the people sent to Mexico had been born in the United States, so they were being sent out of their own country. It happened during the Great Depression, the years when many people in the United States had no work.

Secretary of Labor William N. Doak, who served under President Herbert Hoover, led a national campaign to remove Mexicans. He claimed they were taking jobs from other workers. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel, head of the city's unemployment relief committee, planned a publicity campaign. Relief was public money for people without work.

Officials raided Mexican American neighborhoods and public places. County welfare officers told families that their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico. Officials of the Los Angeles County welfare office ran the trains, and the first one left on March 23, 1931. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks bound for the border. Families lost their homes and belongings. In a 2005 California law, state lawmakers wrote that this property "often was sold by local authorities" as "payment" for the cost of removing the families.

On February 26, 1931, immigration agents blocked the exits of La Placita, a park in Los Angeles, while about 400 people were inside. Accounts of what happened next differ. The California Migration Museum says the agents arrested about a dozen people and deported a few. A History.com article says they rounded up more than 400 people and deported them.

Counts of the people removed run from about 500,000 to about 2 million. The California Migration Museum gives about 1 million, following the historians Francisco Balderrama and Raymond Rodríguez. By that count, about 60 of every 100 were citizens born in the United States. The 2005 California law gives about 2 million, and about 1.2 million of them had been born in the United States. About 35,000 left Los Angeles, about a third of the Mexican people living there.

California lawmakers apologized for the removals in that 2005 law. The Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors apologized in February 2012 and put up a plaque at LA Plaza. The sources checked for this chapter record no apology from the federal government.

In August 1942, US and Mexican officials started the Bracero Program. Under it, Mexican farmworkers came to the United States on contracts to work for a set time and then go home. Bracero is a Spanish word for a man who works with his arms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The St. Louis and the first refugee law" -->
On May 13, 1939, the German ship *St. Louis* left Hamburg with 937 passengers. Almost all of them were Jewish refugees, people fleeing their country to escape danger. The ship sailed for Havana, Cuba. Cuba's president, Federico Laredo Brú, canceled their papers to land, and only 28 passengers were let off at Havana. The passengers asked President Franklin D. Roosevelt for help, and he never answered them. The State Department told them they must "await their turns on the waiting list." Canadian officials refused them too.

On June 6 the ship turned back to Europe. The passengers went to four countries: 288 to Britain, 224 to France, 214 to Belgium and 181 to the Netherlands. Of the 620 who went back to the European mainland, 254 were killed in the Holocaust, and 278 survived. The Holocaust was the murder of Jews and other people by the Nazis, the party that ruled Germany, during World War II.

In 1948 members of Congress passed the Displaced Persons Act. It was the first refugee law in the United States. Displaced persons were Europeans who had lost their homes in the war. Under the law, 200,000 people could come at first, counted inside the quotas. In 1950 lawmakers raised the total. Between 1948 and 1952 about 350,000 to 400,000 European refugees settled in the United States. One count gives 393,542.
