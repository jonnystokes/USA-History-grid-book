## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, the first large wave of people who chose to come here arrived, mostly from Germany and Ireland. Germans were the largest group of newcomers from about 1840. Between 1845 and 1855, during and after a famine in Ireland, more than 1.5 million Irish people reached American ports. New York was the main port where they landed. Large numbers also came ashore at Boston and at New Orleans.

At the start of these years, the legal shipping of enslaved Africans into the country came to an end.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of legal arrival from Africa" -->
On March 2, 1807, President Thomas Jefferson signed a law that banned bringing enslaved people into the United States from other countries. It is called the Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves. Importation means bringing people or goods in from abroad. The ban began on January 1, 1808, the earliest date the Constitution allowed.

After 1808 no one could legally bring an enslaved African across the ocean. Smugglers kept bringing in captives against the law. From then on, the number of enslaved people in the country grew mainly through children born into slavery here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans and the Forty-Eighters" -->
From about 1840 to 1890, more immigrants came from Germany than from any other country. Many Germans left home because their crops had failed. They came for farmland, most of all in the Midwest. Roughly three out of every five German immigrants settled there to farm. About 6 million Germans came to the United States between 1820 and World War I.

In 1848 and 1849, revolutions broke out in Germany. The people who fought in them wanted democratic government, in which the people choose their leaders. The revolutions failed, and some of the revolutionaries fled abroad. The ones who came to the United States are called the Forty-Eighters. There were only about 4,000 to 10,000 of them. Many were journalists or professionals, meaning people trained for skilled work such as law or medicine. Some of them later became leaders in their new country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jette-bruns" name="Jette Bruns" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jette Bruns

> **Who:** A German woman who settled in Missouri with her doctor husband and wrote letters home to her brother for more than fifty years.
> **When and where:** Born 1813 in Oelde, Westphalia. Arrived at the Westphalia Settlement, central Missouri, in 1836. Died 1899.

Jette Bruns came from Germany to a settlement in Missouri in 1836. For more than fifty years afterward she wrote letters home to her brother Heinrich. Her full name was Henriette Geisberg Bruns.

She was born in 1813 in Oelde, a town in Westphalia, a region of Germany. She arrived at the Westphalia Settlement in central Missouri with her husband, the doctor Bernhard Bruns. Their baby son came too, along with her two brothers and a maid.

Her husband Bernhard became mayor of Jefferson City, the capital of Missouri. He died in office during the Civil War, and his death left Jette with almost no money. She ran a boardinghouse, a house where people paid to sleep and eat, across from the Missouri state capitol. The capitol is the building where the state's lawmakers meet. She died in 1899.

Her letters survive. In 1988 the University of Missouri Press printed them, along with a short account of her life that she wrote after the Civil War. Adolf E. Schroeder edited them and translated them into English. The book is called *Hold Dear, As Always: Jette, a German Immigrant Life in Letters*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jette-bruns" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Irish famine" -->
From 1845 to 1852, a plant disease called potato blight struck Ireland's potatoes. People starved, and about 1 million people in Ireland died. This is called the Great Famine. A famine is a time when food runs so short that many people die of hunger.

Nearly 2 million people left Ireland in that decade, and most of them went to the United States. More than 1.5 million Irish people arrived at American ports between 1845 and 1855. Most of them were poor when they landed. They settled in cities of the Northeast, such as New York and Boston, and in other cities in Pennsylvania, Massachusetts and beyond.

Some Americans turned against the Irish newcomers and against Catholics, the members of the Christian church led by the pope in Rome.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="patrick-kennedy-bridget-murphy" name="Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy

> **Who:** Two people from County Wexford, Ireland, who married in Boston during the famine years. Patrick was the great-grandfather of President John F. Kennedy.
> **When and where:** Patrick left Ireland in 1848 or 1849. They married in September 1849. East Boston, Massachusetts.

Patrick Kennedy left Ireland during the Great Famine and made a new home in East Boston, Massachusetts. He was born in 1823 at Dunganstown, in County Wexford. Accounts differ on whether he left Ireland in 1848 or in 1849. In East Boston he worked as a cooper, a person who makes barrels.

In September 1849 he married Bridget Murphy. She also came from County Wexford, from a place called Owenduff. The couple had five children.

Patrick died of cholera on November 22, 1858, at the age of 35. Cholera is a disease people catch from dirty water or food. It causes heavy diarrhea, and a sick person can lose so much water from the body that they die within days.

Patrick's son Patrick Joseph Kennedy, known as P.J., had a son named Joseph P. Kennedy. Joseph's son was John F. Kennedy, who later became president of the United States.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1855 and 1890, about 11 million immigrants arrived through the port of New York alone. Germans still came in the largest numbers in the 1850s. Chinese immigrants began arriving on the West Coast in 1849. Toward the end of the century, more of the newcomers came from southern and eastern Europe than before.

In these years lawmakers also began to shut some immigrants out. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the first law in the United States to bar a group of people because of their nationality, meaning the country they came from.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans and the Know-Nothings" -->
More than 950,000 Germans came to the United States in the 1850s. About 220,000 of them arrived in 1854 alone.

In the 1850s, members of secret societies that opposed immigrants and Catholics formed a political party. Its official name was the American Party. Members who were asked about the group answered, "I know nothing." Other people then called them the Know-Nothings. In the 1854 elections in Massachusetts, Know-Nothing candidates won almost every race. Then the party's members split over slavery. By 1860 the party had faded, and many of its members had joined the new Republican Party.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-schurz" name="Carl Schurz" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Schurz

> **Who:** A German revolutionary who fled after the revolutions of 1848 and 1849 failed. In the United States he became a Union general, a senator and Secretary of the Interior.
> **When and where:** Born March 2, 1829, in the Rhine Province of Prussia, near Cologne. Settled in Watertown, Wisconsin, in 1852. Died May 14, 1906.

Carl Schurz was one of the Forty-Eighters. He came to the United States in 1852 as a refugee from the failed German revolutions.

He was born on March 2, 1829, near the city of Cologne, in the Rhine Province of Prussia. Prussia was a kingdom in what is now Germany. Schurz joined the revolutions of 1848 and 1849. When Prussian forces crushed them, he fled to France and then to London. In 1852 he crossed the Atlantic and settled in Watertown, Wisconsin.

He served as a Union general in the Civil War. From 1869 to 1875 he represented Missouri in the United States Senate, as the first senator born in Germany. President Hayes then chose him as Secretary of the Interior, one of the heads of government departments who advise the president. He held that job from 1877 to 1881, and he died in 1906.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-schurz" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last known slave ship" -->
Around July 8, 1860, a sailing ship called the *Clotilda* carried about 110 African captives to Mobile, Alabama. Bringing captives into the country had been against the law for 52 years. The businessman Timothy Meaher paid for the voyage. The *Clotilda* is the last documented slave ship to reach the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chinese immigrants in the West" -->
Chinese immigration to the United States began with the California Gold Rush, when crowds of people went to California to look for gold. About 325 Chinese people came in 1849. Three years later, in 1852, some 20,000 came. Between 1851 and 1860 about 40,400 arrived in all. Most came from Guangdong province, also called Canton, in southern China. By 1880 about 105,465 Chinese people lived in the United States.

Between 1865 and 1869, managers of the Central Pacific Railroad hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers. These workers built the western part of the first railroad to cross the country. Roughly 1,200 of them died. The sources for this chapter do not record why they died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Page Act and the Chinese Exclusion Act" -->
In 1875 members of Congress passed the Page Act, the first federal law to restrict immigration. Under it, "coolie" contract laborers could not come in. A contract laborer was a worker brought over already bound by a contract to work for someone. The word "coolie" was an insulting name used then for such workers from Asia. In practice, most Chinese women could not get in under the Page Act either. The stated reason for keeping them out was to stop prostitution, which means selling sex for money. That reason was a pretext, meaning a false excuse.

On May 6, 1882, President Chester A. Arthur signed the Chinese Exclusion Act. Exclusion means shutting people out. Under the act, Chinese laborers could not immigrate for 10 years. It was the first law in the United States to bar a group of people because of their nationality or their ethnicity, meaning the people they belonged to. Members of Congress later renewed the law and made it wider. They did not repeal it, which means cancel it, until 1943, 61 years after Arthur signed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jews from the Russian Empire, and others from southern and eastern Europe" -->
Italians, Poles, Hungarians and Greeks came in growing numbers late in the century. So did Jews from the Russian Empire.

In 1881 the ruler of the Russian Empire, Tsar Alexander II, was assassinated, which means he was murdered for political reasons. His killers are not named in the sources for this chapter. After that, pogroms spread across the empire. A pogrom is a violent riot in which a crowd attacks Jewish people. Those sources do not identify the attackers or state how many people they hurt or killed. In 1882 Russian officials issued rules called the May Laws. Under them, Jews in the Pale of Settlement were restricted in where they could live and work. The Pale was the region of the Russian Empire where Jews were allowed to live.

More than 2 million Jews from the Russian Empire came to the United States between 1881 and 1914. Over 1.5 million of them had arrived by 1910, most of them from Russia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Statue of Liberty and Emma Lazarus" -->
The Statue of Liberty, a gift from France, was dedicated in New York Harbor on October 28, 1886. Dedicated means opened with a ceremony.

Three years earlier, in 1883, the poet Emma Lazarus wrote a poem called "The New Colossus." She wrote it to help raise money for the statue's pedestal, the stone base it stands on. The poem includes the lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free." Nobody read the poem at the dedication in 1886, and people mostly forgot it.

Lazarus was born in New York in 1849, into a Sephardic Jewish family. She had worked with Jewish refugees from Russia who arrived in the 1880s. She died in 1887. Later her friend Georgina Schuyler had the poem cast on a bronze plaque, and in 1903 the plaque was mounted inside the pedestal. After that, people began to link the statue with the idea of America as a refuge, a safe place, for immigrants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Castle Garden and Ellis Island" -->
From August 1855 to April 1890, most immigrants who landed at New York passed through Castle Garden. It stood at Castle Clinton, in Battery Park, at the southern tip of Manhattan. It was the country's first official depot for immigrants, a building where officials received newcomers, checked them and recorded them. New York State and New York City officials ran it together. About 11 million immigrants arrived through New York in those years, and about 8 million of them passed through Castle Garden.

People had complained of corruption at Castle Garden, meaning officials were using their jobs to cheat people or take money. When it closed in 1890, federal officials took charge of immigration. On January 1, 1892, they opened Ellis Island, a federal immigration station on an island in New York Harbor, in its place.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="annie-moore" name="Annie Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Annie Moore

> **Who:** A 17-year-old from County Cork, Ireland, the first immigrant processed at Ellis Island. For about a century, another woman's life was told as hers.
> **When and where:** Arrived January 1, 1892, on the steamship *Nevada*. Lived on the Lower East Side of New York. Died in 1924.

Annie Moore was the first immigrant processed at Ellis Island, on the day it opened, January 1, 1892. She was 17 and came from County Cork, Ireland. She arrived on the steamship *Nevada* with her two younger brothers, Anthony, who was about 15, and Philip, who was about 12. She was given a $10 gold piece, a gold coin worth ten dollars.

For about a century, writers described the wrong woman as Annie Moore. That woman went west and died in Texas, and she had been born in Illinois. In 2006 the genealogist Megan Smolenyak offered $1,000 to anyone who could find the real Annie. A genealogist is a person who traces family histories through old records. Researchers who took up the challenge found her.

The real Annie Moore never left New York. She lived in a tenement, a crowded apartment building, on Monroe Street on the Lower East Side. She married a clerk at a fish market. She died in 1924, at the age of 50. A headstone was placed on her grave in 2008. Statues of her stand at Ellis Island and at Cobh, in Ireland.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="annie-moore" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="irving-berlin" name="Irving Berlin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Irving Berlin

> **Who:** A Jewish boy from the Russian Empire who arrived at Ellis Island at five and grew up to write "White Christmas" and "God Bless America."
> **When and where:** Born in 1888 in the Russian Empire (the place is uncertain). Arrived at Ellis Island September 14, 1893. Died September 22, 1989, at 101.

At the age of five, Irving Berlin arrived at Ellis Island. He later wrote the songs "White Christmas," "Easter Parade" and "God Bless America."

He was born Israel Beilin in 1888 in the Russian Empire. The place of his birth is uncertain. Recent research points to Tyumen, in Siberia, and older accounts give the region around Mogilev. His family used May 11 as his birthday, but one source gives May 23.

His memory of Russia was of his family's house burning during violence against Jews. The sources for this chapter do not record how the fire started. The family arrived at Ellis Island on September 14, 1893. In America their surname became Baline.

He wrote his songs under the name Irving Berlin. He wrote a first version of "God Bless America" in 1918. The singer Kate Smith first performed it for the public in 1938. Berlin died on September 22, 1989, at the age of 101.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="irving-berlin" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wong-kim-ark" name="Wong Kim Ark" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Wong Kim Ark

> **Who:** A cook born in San Francisco to Chinese immigrant parents. Officials refused to let him back into the country, and his case went to the Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** Born 1873, San Francisco. Refused landing August 1895. Supreme Court decision March 28, 1898.

In 1895 officials refused to let Wong Kim Ark back into the United States, the country where he was born. They acted under the Chinese exclusion laws. His case reached the Supreme Court, and he won. The justices ruled that anyone born in the United States is a citizen.

He was born in San Francisco in 1873. His parents were Chinese immigrants, and he worked as a cook. In August 1895 he was coming home from a visit to China on the steamship *Coptic*, and officials stopped him from landing. Their names are not given in the sources.

On October 2, 1895, a court issued a writ of habeas corpus for him. That is a court order that requires the people holding someone to bring that person before a judge and show a lawful reason for holding them. The case, *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, went on to the Supreme Court. On March 28, 1898, the justices decided it 6 to 2. They ruled that under the Fourteenth Amendment, an addition to the Constitution, everyone born in the United States is a citizen.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="wong-kim-ark" -->
