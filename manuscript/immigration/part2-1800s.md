## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 11 minutes**

The first large wave of people who chose to come here arrived in these years, mostly from Germany and Ireland, and some Protestant Americans met the Irish Catholics with mobs. Germans were the largest group of newcomers from about 1840.

One kind of arrival ended early in these years. Under a national ban that began on January 1, 1808, no one could legally bring an enslaved person into the country from abroad.

Protestants, Christians whose churches had split away from the Catholic Church led by the pope, formed mobs that attacked Catholics near Boston in 1834 and burned Irish Catholic homes in Philadelphia in 1844. Then more than 1.5 million Irish people reached American ports between 1845 and 1855, during and after a famine in Ireland, a time when food ran so short that many people died of hunger. New York was the main port where they landed, and large numbers also came ashore at Boston and at New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of legal arrival from Africa" -->
President Thomas Jefferson signed a law on March 2, 1807, that banned bringing enslaved people into the United States from other countries. It is called the Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves, importation meaning bringing people or goods in from abroad. The ban began on January 1, 1808, the earliest date the Constitution allowed.

No one could legally bring an enslaved African across the ocean after that. Smugglers kept bringing in captives against the law. From then on, the number of enslaved people in the country grew mainly through children born to enslaved mothers. Enslavers held those children as property from birth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans and the Forty-Eighters" -->
More immigrants came from Germany than from any other country from about 1840 to 1890. Many Germans left home because their crops had failed, and they came for farmland, most of all in the Midwest. Roughly three out of every five German immigrants settled there to farm. About 6 million Germans came to the United States between 1820 and World War I.

**The Forty-Eighters.** Revolutions broke out in Germany in 1848 and 1849. The people who fought in them wanted democratic government, in which the people choose their leaders. Soldiers from Prussia, a kingdom in what is now Germany, crushed the revolutions, and some of the revolutionaries fled abroad.

The ones who came to the United States are called the Forty-Eighters, after the year 1848. There were only about 4,000 to 10,000 of them. Many were journalists or professionals, people trained for skilled work such as law or medicine. Some of them later became leaders in their new country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jette-bruns" name="Jette Bruns" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jette Bruns

> **Who:** A German woman who settled in Missouri with her doctor husband and wrote letters home to her brother for more than fifty years.
> **When and where:** Born 1813 in Oelde, Westphalia. Arrived at the Westphalia Settlement, central Missouri, in 1836. Died 1899.

**She wrote home to her brother for more than fifty years.**

Henriette Geisberg Bruns, known as Jette, came from Oelde, a town in Westphalia, a region of Germany. In 1836 she arrived at the Westphalia Settlement in central Missouri with her husband, the doctor Bernhard Bruns. Their baby son came too, along with two of her brothers and a maid.

Bernhard became mayor of Jefferson City, the capital of Missouri, and he died in office during the Civil War. After he died, Jette had almost no money. She ran a boardinghouse, a house where people paid to sleep and eat, across from the Missouri state capitol, the building where the state's lawmakers meet.

**The letters.** Her letters home to her brother Heinrich survive. The University of Missouri Press printed them in 1988, along with a short account of her life that she wrote after the Civil War. Adolf E. Schroeder edited them and translated them into English. The book is called *Hold Dear, As Always: Jette, a German Immigrant Life in Letters*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jette-bruns" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs against Catholics, 1834 and 1844" -->
Some Protestant Americans turned against the Irish newcomers and against Catholics. Before the Irish famine, Protestant mobs attacked Catholics in two cities and burned their buildings.

**Charlestown, 1834.** A mob of mostly Protestant workmen burned a Catholic convent school, a school that nuns ran in their own home, in Charlestown, near Boston, on the night of August 11, 1834. The nuns and 47 girl students were inside, and only six of the girls were Catholic. One Boston newspaper put the mob at 50 to 60 men, and another put it at 150 to 200. About 2,000 people gathered to watch.

The mob broke down the doors and windows, set fire to the buildings and burned them to the ground. The men also broke open the tomb where several dead nuns were buried. The records of the attack list no deaths.

Police arrested 13 men. At trial, juries found 12 of them not guilty, including their leader, John R. Buzzell. He later wrote that the evidence against him was enough "to have convicted (found guilty) twenty men, but somehow I proved an alibi (proof that he was somewhere else)." The one man found guilty was 16 years old, and Governor John Davis pardoned him, forgiving the crime and ending the punishment.

Massachusetts lawmakers at first voted down a request from Catholic church leaders to be paid for the loss. In 1846 they voted the church $10,000. Church leaders put the loss at about $100,000, ten times as much, and refused the money.

**Philadelphia, 1844.** In Philadelphia the fighting began over schools. Teachers in the public schools made Catholic children read the Protestant version of the Bible. Francis Patrick Kenrick, the Catholic bishop of Philadelphia, the leader of the city's Catholic churches, objected. Nativists, people who wanted power kept for native-born Protestants, then claimed that Catholics were trying to throw the Bible out of the schools.

Nativists held a rally on May 6, 1844, in Kensington, a mostly Irish Catholic neighborhood. Shooting and fighting broke out between the nativists and the Irish Catholics. An 18-year-old nativist named George Shiffler was shot dead. The records do not say who fired the shot.

Nativist crowds burned Irish Catholic homes, a fire station and a market on May 7 and 8. They also burned two Catholic churches, St. Michael's and St. Augustine's, and the school of St. Augustine's. They burned a seminary, a school that trains people for church work, run by the Sisters of Charity, a group of Catholic nuns. One count gives about 30 homes burned and about 200 people driven from their homes.

**St. Philip de Neri.** Nativist crowds learned in July that the brother of the priest at a third church, St. Philip de Neri, had stored guns in it to defend it. They gathered there and brought a cannon. Soldiers of the state militia, a force of citizens called up to serve as soldiers, fired on the crowd under General George Cadwalader. The crowd attacked the soldiers with bricks, stones, bottles and guns.

Accounts give different counts of the dead. For the May riots, the counts run from at least 14 dead to 20 dead. One of the Catholics killed was Joseph Rice. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia lists four soldiers and "probably a dozen rioters" dead in July. EBSCO's reference articles give figures that add up to about 34 dead and at least 126 wounded in both riots together. There is no agreed total.

**Who was blamed.** A grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to put a person on trial, charged 19 of the rioters. Juries found only a handful of the 19 guilty. In June 1844 a grand jury blamed the Catholics who had been attacked. It said the May riots came from efforts "to exclude the Bible from the public schools." Catholics answered that the jury had called no Catholic to testify.

That October, Philadelphia voters elected Lewis Levin to Congress. Levin was a newspaper editor who had founded the nativist group that held the Kensington rally.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Irish famine" -->
A plant disease called potato blight rotted Ireland's potatoes from 1845 to 1852. The blight is a water mold, a tiny living thing that grows on the plant and makes it rot. At least two out of every three people in Ireland lived mainly on potatoes. People starved, and at least 1 million people in Ireland died. This is called the Great Famine.

**Food kept leaving.** Ireland had been part of the United Kingdom since 1801, ruled by the government and the lawmakers in London. Ships kept carrying food from Ireland to Britain while people starved. The historian Christine Kinealy counted almost 4,000 ships that carried butter, grain and farm animals from Ireland to British ports in 1847. Soldiers guarded some of those shipments. About 400,000 people in Ireland died that year. Accounts still differ about how much of the need that food could have met.

In an earlier food shortage, in the 1780s, British officials had banned grain from leaving Ireland, and the ban had worked. In 1846 and 1847, Prime Minister Lord John Russell, the head of the British government, and his ministers, the officials who ran its departments, refused to ban grain from leaving Ireland again. They also made Irish taxpayers pay for famine relief, the food and work given to people who were starving.

Charles Trevelyan was the senior official at the Treasury, the government's money department, who ran famine relief. In December 1846, writing to Lord Monteagle about Ireland, he said that the real problem was not the famine. He said it was "the selfish, perverse (stubbornly wrong) and turbulent (unruly) character of the people," meaning the Irish.

**The quarter-acre rule.** In 1847 members of the British Parliament, the lawmakers in London, added a rule to the law on help for the poor. The rule was named for one of them, William H. Gregory. Under it, a family holding more than a quarter of an acre of land could get no relief. To get food, a family had to give up its land.

**Evictions.** Landlords forced tenant families, families who rented the land they farmed, off their land and often pulled their houses down. This is called eviction. Police records list almost 250,000 people evicted from 1849 to 1854.

The historian James S. Donnelly Jr. judged that count too low. He also counted families forced to give up their land in other ways from 1846 to 1854, and he wrote that the total "would almost certainly exceed half a million persons." Lord Palmerston, a minister in the British government, cleared about 2,000 tenants from his land in County Sligo and paid their fares overseas. Major Denis Mahon evicted thousands of tenants in County Roscommon.

Most of the people who died in the famine died of disease, which people then called fever. Officials in Ireland did not record deaths then, so the numbers are estimates. They run from about 1 million to 1.5 million dead.

**The crossing.** At least 2.1 million people left Ireland from 1845 to 1855, and most of them went to the United States. People called the crowded ships of those years "coffin ships." The *Virginius* sailed to Canada in 1847 with 496 passengers, and a report in the Toronto *Globe* said that 158 of them had died, "nearly one third."

More than 1.5 million Irish people arrived at American ports between 1845 and 1855. Most of them were poor when they landed. They settled in cities of the Northeast, such as New York and Boston, and in other cities in Pennsylvania, Massachusetts and beyond.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="patrick-kennedy-bridget-murphy" name="Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Patrick Kennedy and Bridget Murphy

> **Who:** Two people from County Wexford, Ireland, who married in Boston during the famine years. Patrick was the great-grandfather of President John F. Kennedy.
> **When and where:** Patrick left Ireland in 1848 or 1849. They married in September 1849. East Boston, Massachusetts.

**He made barrels in East Boston and died of cholera at 35.**

Patrick Kennedy was born in 1823 at Dunganstown, in County Wexford, Ireland. He left during the Great Famine and made a new home in East Boston, Massachusetts. Accounts differ on whether he left in 1848 or in 1849. In East Boston he worked as a cooper, a person who makes barrels.

He married Bridget Murphy in September 1849. She also came from County Wexford, from a place called Owenduff. The couple had five children.

Patrick died of cholera, a disease people catch from dirty water or food, on November 22, 1858, at the age of 35. Cholera causes heavy diarrhea, and a sick person can lose so much water from the body that they die within days.

Patrick's son Patrick Joseph Kennedy, known as P.J., had a son named Joseph P. Kennedy. Joseph's son was John F. Kennedy, who later became president of the United States.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 21 minutes**

Chinese immigrants became the first people that lawmakers barred from the country because of where they came from. Newcomers from Europe kept arriving, about 11 million through the port of New York alone between 1855 and 1890. Germans still came in the largest numbers in the 1850s. Chinese immigrants began arriving on the West Coast in 1849. Toward the end of the century, Italians, Poles, Hungarians, Greeks and Jews from the Russian Empire came in growing numbers.

Mobs in the West killed Chinese immigrants and drove whole Chinese communities out of their towns from 1871 to 1887. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the first law in the United States to bar a group of people because of their nationality, meaning the country they came from.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Germans and the Know-Nothings" -->
More than 950,000 Germans came to the United States in the 1850s. About 220,000 of them arrived in 1854 alone.

Members of secret societies that opposed immigrants and Catholics formed a political party in the 1850s. Its official name was the American Party. Members who were asked about the group answered, "I know nothing," and the party came to be called the Know-Nothings. The Know-Nothings feared that Catholics were a danger to the Protestant faith and to democracy. In the 1854 elections in Massachusetts, Know-Nothing candidates won all but 3 of the 400 seats in the state's lawmaking body.

**Bloody Monday.** Know-Nothing mobs attacked immigrants in Louisville, Kentucky, on Monday, August 6, 1855. It was election day, and people call it Bloody Monday. Irish and German immigrants made up about a third of the city. In the weeks before the vote, George D. Prentice, editor of the *Louisville Journal*, wrote newspaper articles against immigrants.

Know-Nothing gangs stopped immigrants from voting. Mobs led by party members attacked the German neighborhoods east of downtown and the Irish neighborhoods to the west. They set fires and robbed homes and businesses, and they damaged more than 100 homes and businesses.

Accounts written later say the mob burned Quinn's Row, a row of houses owned by an Irishman named Quinn, and shot people as they ran from the flames. A study published in the 2020s in the Kentucky Historical Society's journal uses court statements from the 1850s to challenge many of the stories told about the riot.

The Kentucky Historical Society lists at least 22 dead. Other counts give 19 to 22, and Catholic leaders at the time put the number at well over 100. Most of the dead were Irish and German. A grand jury charged five people, and none was found guilty. No one paid the victims for their losses. The Catholic bishop, Martin John Spalding, asked Catholics to stay calm and not to take revenge.

The party's members then split over slavery. By 1860 the party had broken apart, and many of its members had joined the new Republican Party.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-schurz" name="Carl Schurz" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Schurz

> **Who:** A German revolutionary who fled after the revolutions of 1848 and 1849 failed. In the United States he became a Union general, a senator and Secretary of the Interior.
> **When and where:** Born March 2, 1829, in the Rhine Province of Prussia, near Cologne. Settled in Watertown, Wisconsin, in 1852. Died May 14, 1906.

**He fought a revolution in Prussia and became a United States senator.**

Carl Schurz was one of the Forty-Eighters. He grew up near the city of Cologne, in the Rhine Province of Prussia, a kingdom in what is now Germany. Schurz joined the revolutions of 1848 and 1849. When Prussian forces crushed them, he fled to France and then to London. In 1852 he crossed the Atlantic as a refugee and settled in Watertown, Wisconsin.

In the Civil War he served as a general in the Union army, the army of the United States government, which fought the Southern states that had broken away. From 1869 to 1875 he represented Missouri in the United States Senate, as the first senator born in Germany. President Hayes then chose him as Secretary of the Interior, one of the heads of government departments who advise the president. He held that job from 1877 to 1881.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-schurz" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last known slave ship" -->
A sailing ship called the *Clotilda* carried about 110 African captives to Mobile, Alabama, around July 8, 1860. Bringing captives into the country had been against the law for 52 years. The businessman Timothy Meaher paid for the voyage, reportedly to win a bet that he could smuggle captives in. The ship's captain, William Foster, set the *Clotilda* on fire near Twelve-Mile Island. It is the last documented slave ship to reach the United States.

**Kossola.** One of the captives was Oluale Kossola, born in about 1841 in what is now Benin, in West Africa. Accounts say soldiers of the kingdom of Dahomey captured him in a raid and took him to the coast. In Alabama, Meaher, his brothers and some friends enslaved the captives for the next five years, until slavery ended in 1865.

**Africatown.** After the end of slavery the survivors could not pay for the trip home. They built their own town near Mobile and called it Africatown. The writer Zora Neale Hurston interviewed Kossola in 1927, and her book about him, *Barracoon*, came out in 2018. The wreck of the *Clotilda* was found in the Mobile River and confirmed in 2019.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chinese immigrants in the West" -->
Chinese immigration to the United States began with the California Gold Rush, when crowds of people went to California to look for gold. About 325 Chinese people came in 1849. Three years later, in 1852, some 20,000 came. About 40,400 arrived in all between 1851 and 1860. Most came from Guangdong province, also called Canton, in southern China. By 1880 about 105,465 Chinese people lived in the United States.

**The railroad.** Chinese workers built the western part of the first railroad to cross the country. Managers of the Central Pacific Railroad had about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers on the job at any one time between 1865 and 1869, about nine out of every ten of the railroad's workers. Chinese workers earned about $35 a month and paid for their own food and housing. White workers got about $42 a month plus housing.

**The strike.** Thousands of Chinese workers went on strike in June 1867, which means they stopped work together to press for better terms. They asked for $40 a month, a 10-hour workday instead of 11 hours, and shorter shifts in the tunnels. Charles Crocker was the railroad director in charge of building. He cut off all food and supplies to their camps and brought in the sheriff and his deputies. After about eight days the workers went back to work.

Snow, explosions and disease killed Chinese railroad workers. In the winter of 1866 and 1867, 44 storms hit the Sierra Nevada mountains. At Strong's Canyon an avalanche, a mass of snow sliding down a mountainside, carried away the camp of two work crews of Chinese workers who were building tunnels there. One worker's body was not found until spring.

Workers blasted through solid granite rock with black powder, an early kind of gunpowder. From 1867 they also used nitroglycerin, a liquid explosive that can go off from a jolt. Smallpox, a disease that passes from person to person and covers the body in sores, broke out in 1868 and 1869.

**The count of the dead.** The railroad's managers kept no record of how many workers died. A figure of about 1,200 dead comes from one newspaper report in 1870. A reporter for the *Sacramento Reporter* wrote that a train had brought in "the accumulated bones of perhaps 1,200" Chinese men from along the line. Chinese aid societies paid to send such bones home to China. On the same day, the *Sacramento Union* reported about 50 dead.

**Known:** Chinese workers died of snow, explosions and disease, and the railroad's managers did not count them.
**Not known:** The real number. Researchers at a railroad history museum think about 100 to 150 Chinese workers died building the railroad, and other estimates run from hundreds to thousands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Page Act and the Chinese Exclusion Act" -->
Members of Congress passed the Page Act in 1875, the national government's first law to limit immigration. Under it, contract laborers from China, Japan and other Asian countries could not come in. These were workers brought over without their free consent, already bound to work for someone for a set time. The law called them by an insulting name used then for Asian laborers. A Chinese worker who came freely, with no such contract, could still come in.

**Chinese women.** Under the Page Act, no one could bring in women for prostitution, which means selling sex for money. Immigration officers at the ports decided which women those were. In practice they treated Asian women in general as suspects and kept out most Chinese women. The stated reason, stopping prostitution, was a pretext, meaning a false excuse.

**What changed in 1882.** President Chester A. Arthur signed the Chinese Exclusion Act, a law to shut Chinese workers out, on May 6, 1882. Under it, no Chinese laborer could come in for 10 years, whether he had a contract or not. It was the first law in the United States to bar a group of people because of their nationality or their ethnicity, the group a person is born into, with its own language and customs. Members of Congress later renewed the law and made it wider. They did not cancel it until 1943, 61 years after Arthur signed it.

**By 1882:** Chinese workers had built the western part of the first railroad across the country, and lawmakers had now shut new Chinese workers out for ten years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attacks on Chinese immigrants, 1871 to 1887" -->
Before and after the Exclusion Act, mobs in the West attacked Chinese immigrants. Juries and judges punished almost none of the attackers.

**Los Angeles, 1871.** A gunfight broke out between two groups of Chinese residents of Los Angeles on October 24, 1871. A local rancher, Robert Thompson, fired into a Chinese store. He was hit by return fire and died about an hour later. A mob of about 500 non-Chinese men then spent about two hours pulling Chinese people out of the Chinese part of town. They shot, beat and hanged them, and they robbed Chinese homes and stores.

They killed 18 Chinese men and boys, about one in ten of the city's Chinese people. Only one of the dead is thought to have taken part in the first gunfight. The mob hanged seven men from the wooden crossbar of a porch and three more from the side of a wagon.

The dead included Chee Long Tong, a doctor who healed people with herbs, and a teenager, Ah Loo. Dr. Tong offered the mob money and the diamond ring on his finger if they would spare his life. The geographer Reece Jones writes that a man in the crowd shot him in the mouth and cut off his finger to take the ring. The mob also hanged him.

A grand jury charged 25 men. By one account eight were found guilty of manslaughter, the crime of killing someone without planning to, and by another account nine. A judge sentenced them to two to six years in San Quentin prison. About a year later the judges of the California Supreme Court threw out the guilty verdicts because of a mistake in how the charges were written. None of the men was tried again.

**Rock Springs, 1885.** Between 100 and 150 armed white miners and railroad workers attacked the Chinese miners they worked beside in Rock Springs, in Wyoming Territory, on September 2, 1885. A territory was land the national government ran before it became a state. Managers of the Union Pacific Railroad had hired the Chinese miners at lower pay. Most of the attackers were immigrants themselves, from Britain, Ireland, Wales and Scandinavia.

The attackers shot Chinese miners, struck them with picks, hatchets and clubs, and burned all 79 houses in the Chinese part of town. Some Chinese people burned to death in their cellars. About 600 Chinese people fled. The Wyoming State Historical Society lists 28 Chinese killed and 15 wounded. Other counts put the dead at 40 to 50, because some who fled were never found.

President Grover Cleveland sent army troops, and the railroad's managers brought the Chinese miners back to work under army guard. Officials arrested 16 men, or 22 by some accounts. A grand jury charged none of them. It said that no one had been able to testify to "a single criminal act committed by any known white person that day."

**Eureka, 1885.** Other mobs drove Chinese people out of their towns. In Eureka, California, in February 1885, a stray bullet from a gunfight between two Chinese groups killed a city councilman, David Kendall. That night more than 600 white residents gathered and put up a gallows, a frame for hanging people. City leaders ordered every Chinese resident to leave within 24 hours. A committee of white residents put about 480 Chinese people, nearly all of the town's Chinese residents, on two steamships to San Francisco. No one was killed.

The Chinese families left behind their furniture, goods, shrimp boats and vegetable farms. Chinatown was torn down, and the accounts do not name who did it. A Chinese aid group, the Six Companies, sued the city in 1886, and the case was dropped. Rules keeping Chinese people out stayed in Eureka's city charter, its basic set of laws, until 1959.

**Tacoma and Seattle.** Mayor Jacob Weisbach of Tacoma, in Washington Territory, led a year-long campaign to drive the Chinese out. He called them "a curse." Tacoma's leaders ordered all Chinese residents to leave by November 3, 1885, and about 150 left before that date. On November 3, a mob forced about 200 more Chinese residents out of their homes in the rain and onto trains out of town. The mob then burned their houses. Officials charged 27 Tacoma men, Weisbach among them, and later dropped every charge.

Mobs in Seattle forced more than 300 Chinese residents to the docks in February 1886, and ships carried about 300 of them away. Of 13 men tried for the Seattle riot, none was found guilty.

**Hells Canyon, 1887.** A gang of white horse thieves shot and killed as many as 34 Chinese gold miners in May 1887. The miners were camped in Hells Canyon, on the Snake River in Oregon, and the gang took their gold dust. One of the gang, Frank Vaughan, confessed that they had agreed nearly a year earlier to murder the miners. Vaughan testified for the state and was not tried. Three of the accused men fled before trial. A jury found the three who stood trial not guilty.

A stone marker placed at the site in 2012 states, "No one was held accountable." Its words are in English, in Chinese and in Nez Perce, a Native American language.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Italians, Poles, Hungarians and Greeks" -->
Italians, Poles, Hungarians and Greeks came in growing numbers late in the century. Many of them were peasants, poor farm workers, who had lost their land as farming in Europe became a business. They came to earn enough money to go home and buy a piece of land. One Slavic steelworker put the plan this way: "A good job, save money, work all time, go home, sleep, no spend." Many did go home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jews from the Russian Empire" -->
Jews from the Russian Empire came running from riots and from laws aimed at them. Unlike many of the peasants who came to earn money, they came as families and planned to stay.

Revolutionaries murdered the ruler of the Russian Empire, Tsar Alexander II, in March 1881. In the towns, many people in the crowds believed a rumor that Jews had killed him. They also believed that a special order from the government allowed them to beat Jews.

Six weeks after the murder, on April 15, 1881, a fight in a tavern in the town of Elisavetgrad, in what is now Ukraine, turned into an attack on the town's Jews. It was the first of about 250 pogroms, violent riots in which a crowd attacks Jewish people and their homes, shops and synagogues, the buildings where Jews worship. The pogroms went on until 1884, in Kiev, Warsaw and many other places. Another count gives 259 pogroms from 1881 to 1883.

**Who attacked.** The historian John Klier describes many of the attackers as laborers who drifted from place to place looking for work. Railroad workers, factory workers and local business rivals of Jews also took part. Peasants joined in mostly to steal. The attackers robbed and wrecked Jewish homes and shops. They beat, raped and killed Jews. Rape means forcing a person into sex.

Counts of the dead differ. One count says at least 40 Jews were killed from April to December 1881. Klier counts about 50 deaths in the whole wave. About half of those dead were Jews, and about half were rioters killed while the tsar's officials put down the riots.

For many years, accounts said that officials of the tsar's government had planned the attacks. Later, the historians Hans Rogger, I. Michael Aronson and John Klier looked for evidence of that and found none. Some local officials helped stir the attacks up. Klier writes that more than a thousand rioters were punished in some way. Another account says the tsar pardoned most of the rioters who were brought to trial. The two accounts do not agree.

**The May Laws.** Nikolai Ignatiev, the tsar's minister in charge of the police and of other matters inside the country, proposed rules called the May Laws in May 1882. Tsar Alexander III approved them. Ignatiev blamed the pogroms on the Jews themselves. The rules were for Jews in the Pale of Settlement, the region of the Russian Empire where Jews were allowed to live.

Under the May Laws, Jews could not move to new homes outside towns. They could not buy or rent land in the countryside, and they could not trade on Sunday mornings or on Christian holidays.

More than 2 million Jews from the Russian Empire came to the United States between 1881 and 1914. Many of them were escaping the pogroms and the May Laws. Over 1.5 million of them had arrived by 1910, most of them from Russia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Statue of Liberty and Emma Lazarus" -->
The Statue of Liberty, a gift from France, was opened with a ceremony in New York Harbor on October 28, 1886. Emma Lazarus wrote the poem quoted at the start of this chapter, "The New Colossus," in 1883, to help raise money for the statue's pedestal, the stone base it stands on. She wrote it one year after lawmakers shut Chinese workers out of the country.

Lazarus was born in New York in 1849, into a Sephardic Jewish family, whose ancestors were Jews of Spain and Portugal. She had worked with Jewish refugees from Russia who arrived in the 1880s. She died in 1887. People mostly forgot her poem until 1903, when her friend Georgina Schuyler had it mounted on a bronze plaque inside the pedestal. After that, people began to link the statue with the idea of America as a refuge, a safe place, for immigrants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Castle Garden and Ellis Island" -->
Most immigrants who landed at New York from August 1855 to April 1890 passed through Castle Garden. It stood at Castle Clinton, in Battery Park, at the southern tip of Manhattan. It was the country's first official depot for immigrants, a building where officials received newcomers, checked them and recorded them. New York State and New York City officials ran it together. About 11 million immigrants arrived through New York in those years, and about 8 million of them passed through Castle Garden.

People had complained that officials at Castle Garden were dishonest. When it closed in 1890, officials of the national government took charge of immigration. On January 1, 1892, they opened Ellis Island, the national government's immigration station on an island in New York Harbor, in its place.

### Annie Moore

> **Who:** A 17-year-old from County Cork, Ireland, the first immigrant processed at Ellis Island. For about a century, another woman's life was told as hers.
> **When and where:** Arrived January 1, 1892, on the steamship *Nevada*. Lived on the Lower East Side of New York. Died in 1924.

**First through Ellis Island, she received a $10 gold coin.**

Annie Moore sailed from Ireland on the steamship *Nevada* with her two younger brothers, Anthony, who was about 15, and Philip, who was about 12. They reached New York on January 1, 1892, the day Ellis Island opened, and officials processed her before anyone else.

**The wrong Annie.** For about a century, writers described the wrong woman as Annie Moore. That woman went west and died in Texas, and she had been born in Illinois. In 2006 the genealogist Megan Smolenyak, a person who traces family histories through old records, offered $1,000 to anyone who could find the real Annie. Researchers who took up the challenge found her.

The real Annie Moore never left New York. She lived in a tenement, a crowded apartment building, on Monroe Street on the Lower East Side, and she married a clerk at a fish market. She died in 1924, at the age of 50. A headstone was placed on her grave in 2008. Statues of her stand at Ellis Island and at Cobh, in Ireland.

### Irving Berlin

> **Who:** A Jewish boy from the Russian Empire who arrived at Ellis Island at five and grew up to write "White Christmas" and "God Bless America."
> **When and where:** Born in 1888 in the Russian Empire (the place is uncertain). Arrived at Ellis Island September 14, 1893. Died September 22, 1989, at 101.

**He remembered his family's house in Russia burning.**

Irving Berlin was born Israel Beilin in 1888 in the Russian Empire. The place of his birth is uncertain. Recent research points to Tyumen, in Siberia, and older accounts give the region around Mogilev. His family used May 11 as his birthday, but one account gives May 23.

His memory of Russia was of his family's house burning during violence against Jews. No record says who set the fire. The family arrived at Ellis Island on September 14, 1893, and in America their surname became Baline.

He wrote his songs, among them "Easter Parade," under the name Irving Berlin. He wrote a first version of "God Bless America" in 1918, and the singer Kate Smith first performed it for the public in 1938. Berlin died on September 22, 1989, at the age of 101.

### Wong Kim Ark

> **Who:** A cook born in San Francisco to Chinese immigrant parents. Officials refused to let him back into the country, and his case went to the Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** Born 1873, San Francisco. Refused landing August 1895. Supreme Court decision March 28, 1898.

**Born in San Francisco, he was refused entry to his own country.**

Wong Kim Ark was born in San Francisco in 1873. His parents were Chinese immigrants, and he worked as a cook. In August 1895 he was coming home from a visit to China on the steamship *Coptic*.

John H. Wise, the official in charge of collecting taxes on goods coming into the port of San Francisco, refused to let him land. Wise acted under the Chinese exclusion laws. He said Wong was not a citizen, because his parents had not become citizens, and he held Wong on the ship.

**The court order.** On October 2, 1895, the national government's court in San Francisco issued a writ of habeas corpus for him, an order that the people holding him bring him before a judge and give a lawful reason for holding him. Judge William Morrow ruled that Wong was a citizen and ordered him freed.

Henry Foote, the national government's lawyer in San Francisco, had brought the case against Wong. Lawyers for the national government took it higher. The case, *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, went on to the Supreme Court. On March 28, 1898, the justices, the judges of the Supreme Court, decided it 6 to 2 in Wong's favor. They ruled that under the Fourteenth Amendment, an addition to the Constitution, everyone born in the United States is a citizen.

**If you remember one thing:** Lawmakers shut Chinese workers out in 1882, the first time a law barred people for the country they came from, and in 1898 the Supreme Court ruled that anyone born here is a citizen.
**One number:** 61 years. Members of Congress kept the Chinese Exclusion Act, renewed and widened, from 1882 until they canceled it in 1943.
**Next:** After 1900 more newcomers came through Ellis Island than ever before, and lawmakers answered with limits on how many could come from each country.
