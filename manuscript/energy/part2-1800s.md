## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 5 minutes**

Hard coal from northeastern Pennsylvania went into American stoves and furnaces in these years, and gas made from coal lit city streets. A tavern keeper showed in 1808 that the coal could heat a home, and boats, first on the rivers and then on canals, carried it to the cities. The country's first public gas street lamp was lit in Baltimore in 1817, with gas made from coal and sent through pipes.

The American whaling fleet reached its largest size in 1846, with 735 ships. Whale oil cost too much for most families, and most homes burned cheaper fuels.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone coal" -->
Northeastern Pennsylvania has a hard kind of coal called anthracite, which people then called stone coal. Two brothers named Gore, who were blacksmiths, had burned it in their forges, the fires where blacksmiths heat metal, since 1769. For use at home, though, most people thought anthracite was too difficult to burn.

**Fell's grate.** Jesse Fell ran a tavern in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania. He had believed for some time that a big enough pile of anthracite would keep burning once it was lit. He built a grate shaped like the letter L out of "Small Iron rods," to hold the coal up off the floor of the fireplace so air could flow in underneath.

Fell set his grate in the fireplace of the tavern's main room on February 11, 1808, and lit the coal. He later wrote that "on first lighting" he "found it to burn excellently well."

After Fell showed that anthracite could heat a home, the anthracite business grew. A company called Lehigh Navigation was formed in 1818, and anthracite floated down the Lehigh River to Philadelphia in boats called arks. Later, boats on canals carried the coal into the cities. From the 1820s to the 1840s, people put stoves and furnaces made to burn anthracite into homes and other buildings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gaslight" -->
Heating coal makes a gas that burns, a mix of gases that includes hydrogen and a gas called carbon monoxide. Rembrandt Peale, who ran a museum on Holliday Street in Baltimore, lit a room of his museum with gas made from coal in 1816.

**The first gas company.** The Gas Light Company of Baltimore received its charter, a legal paper that allowed the company to form, on June 17, 1816. It was the first gas company in the United States. Gas makers stored the gas in large tanks called gasometers and sent it through pipes. The company's first public gas street lamp, the first in the country, was lit at Market and Lemon Streets in Baltimore on February 7, 1817.

About 50 American cities and towns had gas works, plants that made gas, by 1850. Gaslight was expensive, and most Americans could not pay for it. It lit streets, shops and the homes of richer families in the larger cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whale oil, and who could pay for it" -->
The American whaling fleet counted 735 ships in 1846, the most in any year, out of about 900 whaling ships in the whole world. Other counts put the peak of the whaling business as late as 1852. New Bedford, Massachusetts, was the leading whaling port, and at its peak more than 400 whaling ships sailed from there.

**Who could pay.** Whale oil cost $1.30 to $2.50 a gallon, so mainly richer families burned it. Oil from sperm whales lit their homes, along with lighthouses and public halls. Most families burned cheaper fuels: tallow candles made of animal fat, lard oil made from pig fat at about 90 cents a gallon, and camphene at about 50 cents.

Camphene was one part turpentine, a liquid made from pine trees, mixed with four parts alcohol and a little camphor, added for its smell. Its fumes caught fire easily, and camphene lamps could start sudden fires.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nelson-cole-haley" name="Nelson Cole Haley" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nelson Cole Haley

> **Who:** A New Bedford harpooner whose book tells how a whaling crew turned a whale into lamp oil.
> **When and where:** At sea on the whaling ship *Charles W. Morgan*, 1849 to 1853.

**The crew boiled the whale's fat over a fire of whale scraps.**

Nelson Cole Haley hunted whales for the oil that Americans burned in their lamps. He grew up in New Bedford, Massachusetts, and his father died when he was a boy. In 1844, when he was twelve, he ran off to sea to become a whaleman, even though his mother did not want him to go.

**The chase.** Haley became a harpooner on the *Charles W. Morgan* in 1849, when he was seventeen. A harpooner threw the harpoon, a heavy iron spear, into the whale from a boat that the crew rowed out from the ship. Haley called the harpoon an iron.

He later wrote about driving it into the whale's blubber, the thick layer of fat under its skin. "I raised the iron, and with all the force I was capable of, I sent it to the socket into the vast mass of blubber and meat that was now only a foot or two clear of the boat."

He drove a second harpoon in beside the first. Then the second mate, one of the ship's officers, killed the whale with a lance, a long spear with a sharp blade, and the crew rowed the dead whale back to the ship.

**Whale into oil.** The next morning, men stood on the dead whale and peeled off strips of blubber. The crew hoisted the strips on board and cut them into chunks they called bibles, then boiled the chunks in two big metal pots on the try-works, a brick furnace built on the deck.

The heat melted the fat into oil, which the crew poured into barrels below deck. For fuel under the pots, they burned the crisp bits of blubber left over from the boiling.

Haley's voyage lasted four years, from 1849 to 1853. Whaling ships of that time stayed at sea three to five years on a voyage. Haley wrote a book about it, *Whale Hunt*, which the Mystic Seaport Museum first published in 1948, almost fifty years after he died.

The *Charles W. Morgan* was built in New Bedford in 1841 and made 37 voyages between 1841 and 1921, with sailors from fifty countries. It is the last wooden whaling ship left in the world, and it is kept at the Mystic Seaport Museum, in Mystic, Connecticut.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 12 minutes**

Americans began to buy two new kinds of energy in these years: kerosene for their lamps, made from oil, and electricity sent through wires. Edwin Drake's crew drilled a well in Pennsylvania that reached oil in 1859, and refiners made the oil into kerosene. Thomas Edison's workers began sending electric current to paying customers from a station in New York City in 1882.

Coal became the country's main fuel in these years. By the count of the US Energy Information Administration, coal supplied 9 percent of the energy Americans used in 1850 and 27 percent by 1870. Around 1885, Americans began to burn more energy from coal than from wood for the first time. Miners died digging it. A fire at the Avondale coal mine in Pennsylvania killed 110 people in 1869.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drake's well, 1859" -->
Edwin Drake's crew struck oil 69.5 feet down near Titusville, Pennsylvania, on August 27, 1859. It was the first well in the United States drilled on purpose to find oil and sell it. Writers at the American Oil & Gas Historical Society date the start of the American oil business to that day.

Drake worked for the Seneca Oil Company of New Haven, Connecticut, whose owners wanted oil to make kerosene, a cheap fuel for lamps. The driller, William Smith, known as Uncle Billy, cut through rock and shale, a soft rock made of hardened mud, at about three feet a day. A steam engine of six horsepower drove the drill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-drake" name="Edwin Drake" movie="Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake (1954)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Drake

> **Who:** The man whose 1859 well started the American oil business, though he lost his money afterward.
> **When and where:** Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859.
> **Movie:** *Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake* (1954). A 30-minute drama paid for by the oil industry's American Petroleum Institute, with Vincent Price as Drake. It first played in Titusville on March 29, 1954.

**The title "Colonel" was made up to impress the town.**

Edwin Drake drilled the first American well made to find oil. Afterward he lost his money and spent his last years ill.

People called him Colonel Drake, but he never held that rank. An investor named James Townsend made up the title and addressed letters to "Colonel E. L. Drake" to impress the people of Titusville.

**The drive pipe.** Water from Oil Creek kept seeping into the hole, and the sides kept caving in. Drake had a pipe driven down to the rock, and then the drill worked inside the pipe, which kept the water and loose ground out. The drill reached oil at 69.5 feet.

Drake never patented the drive pipe, which means he never got a government paper giving him the only right to make and sell it. His friends raised about $4,000 for him in the late 1860s. In 1873 people of Titusville won him a pension from the state of Pennsylvania, $1,500 paid to him every year. Drake died in November 1880 in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-drake" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Did kerosene save the whales?" -->
A common saying holds that kerosene saved the whales, but writers at the University of Houston's engineering college and at the website environmentalhistory.org call that claim a myth. Whaling was already shrinking before kerosene came, and in most homes kerosene replaced camphene, not whale oil.

**What families burned.** American families switched to kerosene for their lamps in the 1860s. Refiners made it by heating oil until parts of it boiled off as vapor, then cooling the vapor back into separate liquids. Kerosene sold for about 50 cents a gallon.

The whaling fleet had been largest in 1846, and whalers caught fewer whales through the 1850s. Whale oil cost $1.30 to $2.50 a gallon, so mainly richer families burned it. Most families burned camphene and lard oil.

**The tax.** During the Civil War, members of Congress passed the Internal Revenue Act to pay for the Union Army, the army of the Northern states. Under that law, alcohol was taxed at up to $2.08 a gallon from 1862 to 1864. The lawmakers meant to tax alcohol that people drank, but they did not leave camphene out of the law, and camphene was about three quarters alcohol.

Before the tax, camphene and kerosene each sold for about 50 cents a gallon. At the top rate, the tax on the alcohol in one gallon of camphene came to more than a dollar, while kerosene was taxed at 10 cents a gallon. People stopped buying camphene, and families switched to kerosene.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pearl Street, 1882" -->
Thomas Edison's workers started the first permanent central power station in the United States on September 4, 1882. It stood at 257 Pearl Street in Manhattan, in New York City, and it made electricity in one place for many buildings, sending it out through wires.

**Coal into current.** The station ran on coal. Four boilers burned coal to make steam, the steam ran engines, and the engines turned six dynamos, machines that turn motion into electric current. Wires carried the current to a district of about a quarter of a square mile around the station.

When the station started, Edison was standing in the office of the banker J. Pierpont Morgan, at the bank of Drexel, Morgan and Company. On the first day the station served 85 customers, who had about 400 lamps among them. Within a year it served 513 customers with about 10,000 lamps. It ran until January 2, 1890, and in those years it went dark only once, for three hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-edison-energy" name="Thomas Edison" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Edison

> **Who:** The inventor who built his electric system on direct current, a kind that could not travel far.
> **When and where:** Pearl Street, Manhattan, New York City, from September 4, 1882.

**His current could reach only a quarter of a square mile.**

Thomas Edison built his Pearl Street system to run on direct current, called DC, which flows in one direction only, like the current from a battery. The station sent DC out at a low voltage, the force that pushes electric current through a wire.

Low-voltage direct current loses a lot of its energy in the wires as it travels, so the Pearl Street station could serve only about a quarter of a square mile around it. The voltage of direct current could not easily be raised or lowered.

George Westinghouse sold a different kind of current, alternating current, called AC, which flows back and forth. Edison and his supporters said AC was deadly.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-edison-energy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The current war" -->
Alternating current, called AC, flows back and forth, changing direction many times each second. In the United States today it changes direction 60 times a second. A machine called a transformer can easily raise or lower the voltage of AC, which could not easily be done with direct current.

Nikola Tesla received patents for motors that ran on alternating current in May 1888. Two months later, George Westinghouse paid for the right to use them.

**The dogs.** Edison and his supporters attacked AC as deadly. Harold Brown, an electricity salesman, ran tests at Edison's laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey, in 1888, killing dogs with alternating current to show that it was dangerous. The dogs were strays, bought from boys in the neighborhood for 25 cents each. Edison's supporters used the word "Westinghoused" to mean killed by electricity.

**The chair.** New York lawmakers replaced hanging with death by electricity in 1888, a change Governor David B. Hill had proposed. New York State officials hired Harold Brown to build the first electric chair, and Edison secretly paid Brown to use alternating current in it. An electric chair kills a person by sending a strong electric current through the body.

The first man put to death in the electric chair was William Kemmler, of Buffalo, New York. Kemmler killed Matilda Ziegler, who lived with him as his wife, in their apartment in Buffalo, striking her more than 25 times on the head, neck and shoulders with a hatchet. At his trial in a Buffalo court in May 1889, he was found guilty of murder and sentenced to die by electricity in Auburn State prison.

His lawyer, W. Bourke Cockran, argued that death by electricity was a cruel and unusual punishment, which the New York State Constitution forbids. The judges of the New York Court of Appeals said it was unusual but not cruel, and the justices of the US Supreme Court refused to change that decision.

**The execution.** Prison officials at Auburn put Kemmler in Brown's chair on August 6, 1890, and sent a current through him for 17 seconds. A doctor said he was dead, but then Kemmler groaned and began to breathe again. The officials turned the current back on, and this time it took between one and four minutes to kill him.

During the execution his fist clenched so hard that blood ran from his palm, and the back of his coat briefly caught fire. The room filled with the smell of burning flesh, and several witnesses fainted. Newspaper writers called the execution "a disgrace to civilization." Westinghouse said, "It has been a brutal affair. They could have done better with an ax."

**Chicago and Niagara.** Westinghouse bid $399,000 to light the World's Columbian Exposition, the world's fair in Chicago, and General Electric's bid was $554,000. Westinghouse got the job. In May 1893 his company's AC system lit about 100,000 electric lamps at the fair, 250 times the 400 lamps Pearl Street lit on its first day.

Westinghouse also won the job of building the generators, machines that turn motion into current as Edison's dynamos did, for the Adams power plant at Niagara Falls. The Adams plant sent out its first alternating current, to factories in the town of Niagara Falls, on August 26, 1895. In November 1896 current from Niagara Falls lit Buffalo, New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" name="George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla" movie="The Current War (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla

> **Who:** The businessman who sold alternating current, and the inventor whose AC motors he bought the right to use.
> **When and where:** 1888 to 1896. Chicago, 1893. Niagara Falls and Buffalo, New York, 1895 and 1896.
> **Movie:** *The Current War* (2017). A Hollywood drama, not a documentary. Michael Shannon plays Westinghouse and Nicholas Hoult plays Tesla.

**Tesla's motors cost Westinghouse $2.50 for every horsepower they made.**

George Westinghouse bought the right to use Nikola Tesla's motors in July 1888, two months after Tesla received patents for them. He paid $60,000 in cash and in stock, or shares in the ownership of a company, plus $2.50 for every horsepower the motors produced.

Westinghouse then won the job of lighting the Chicago world's fair, with a bid $155,000 below General Electric's. His company's workers built the three generators for the Adams plant at Niagara Falls, each rated at 5,000 horsepower, and in November 1896 their current reached Buffalo.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Avondale, 1869: one way out" -->
A fire at the Avondale mine, near Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, killed 110 people on September 6, 1869. The Steuben Coal Company owned the mine. Mine operators then kept small fires burning at the bottom of their shafts, the deep holes that lead down into a mine. A fire like this, called a ventilating furnace, made a draft that moved fresh air through the mine.

**One way out.** Sparks from the ventilating furnace set fire to the wooden timbers in the shaft, and the flames spread up to the breaker, the wooden building where coal was broken and sorted. The breaker stood right over the shaft, and the shaft was the only way into or out of the mine. The men who ran the Steuben Coal Company had the mine and its breaker built that way.

The fire filled the mine with smoke and gas, and 108 men and boys underground died because they could not breathe. Five of the dead were boys between 12 and 17 years old. Two men who went down to rescue the miners also died.

Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a mine law in 1870. It required every mine to have more than one way out, and it forbade using a single passage both to bring fresh air in and to let bad air out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From charcoal to coal" -->
Hopewell Furnace in Pennsylvania burned charcoal from about 1771 until it closed in 1883. By then iron makers had built new ironworks that burned anthracite, the hard coal, and charcoal furnaces had fallen out of use. For more than a hundred years, workers at Hopewell had cut the forest around it for charcoal. Mark Bird, who built the furnace, enslaved some of those workers in its early decades, and the last record of slavery at Hopewell is from 1821.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lafayette-houck" name="Lafayette Houck" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lafayette Houck

> **Who:** One of the last colliers at Hopewell Furnace, who later taught the National Park Service how charcoal was made.
> **When and where:** Hopewell Furnace, Berks County, Pennsylvania, in the later 1800s, and again in 1936.

**Fifty-three years after the furnace closed, he showed how charcoal was made.**

Lafayette Houck came from a family of colliers. Their work at Hopewell began in 1818, when Henry Houck worked there as a master collier, in charge of the pits and of the helpers who worked them. In winter Henry also repaired wooden wagon wheels or chopped wood to sell. In the 1830s the furnace's owners paid about 20 colliers, and each one tended eight or nine pits.

**The watch.** Lafayette made charcoal the way Hopewell's colliers had since the 1700s, in smoldering piles of stacked wood. A Hopewell collier lived in a rough hut near his pits from May to October, with one or two helpers, and together they tended up to eight or nine pits at once. They watched the pits day and night, from the moment a pit was lit until a teamster, a man who drove a wagon, hauled away the last load of charcoal.

**Teaching the trade.** Hopewell closed in 1883, and federal officials bought the land in the 1930s. In 1936 Houck and his son, William, showed workers of the Civilian Conservation Corps, a government jobs program, how a collier lived and worked. Houck taught the National Park Service how charcoal was made, and he told them what he remembered about life at the furnace.

**Not known:** his exact age, his pay, and the years he began and stopped work as a collier. One National Park Service page gives his life as 1846 to 1939, and another says he was 82 in 1936, which would put his birth around 1854.
