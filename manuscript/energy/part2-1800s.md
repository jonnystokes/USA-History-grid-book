## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans began to heat their homes with coal dug out of the ground. In 1808 a tavern keeper in Pennsylvania showed that hard coal from northeastern Pennsylvania could heat a home. Boats, first on the rivers and then on canals, carried that coal to the cities. In 1817 the first public gas street lamp in the country was lit in Baltimore, with gas made from coal and sent through pipes.

The American whaling fleet reached its largest size in 1846, with 735 ships. Whale oil cost too much for most families, and most homes burned cheaper fuels.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone coal" -->
Northeastern Pennsylvania has a hard kind of coal called anthracite. People then called it stone coal. Two brothers named Gore, who were blacksmiths, had burned it in their forges since 1769. A forge is a blacksmith's fire for heating metal. For use at home, though, most people thought anthracite was too hard to burn.

Jesse Fell ran a tavern in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania. He had believed for some time that a big enough pile of anthracite would keep burning once it was lit. He built a grate shaped like the letter L out of "Small Iron rods." A grate is a rack that holds fuel up off the floor of a fireplace, so air can flow in underneath. On February 11, 1808, Fell set his grate in the fireplace of the tavern's main room and lit the coal. He later wrote that "on first lighting" he "found it to burn excellently well."

Fell's test showed that anthracite could heat a home. After that, more men went into the business of mining anthracite and selling it as house fuel. In 1818 a company called Lehigh Navigation was organized. Anthracite floated down the Lehigh River to Philadelphia in boats called arks. Later, boats on canals carried the coal into the cities. Through the 1820s, 1830s and 1840s, people put stoves and furnaces made to burn anthracite into homes and other buildings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gaslight" -->
Heating coal makes a gas that burns. The gas is a mix of gases that can burn, among them hydrogen and carbon monoxide. In 1816 Rembrandt Peale, who ran a museum on Holliday Street in Baltimore, lit a room of his museum with gas made from coal.

On June 17, 1816, the Gas Light Company of Baltimore received its charter, a legal paper that allowed the company to form. It was the first gas company in the United States. Gas makers stored the gas in large tanks called gasometers and sent it through pipes. On February 7, 1817, the company's first public gas street lamp, the first in the country, was lit at Market and Lemon Streets in Baltimore.

By 1850 about 50 American cities and towns had gas works, plants that made gas. Gaslight was expensive, and most Americans could not pay for it. It lit streets, shops and the homes of richer families in the larger cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whale oil, and who could pay for it" -->
In 1846 the American whaling fleet reached its largest size, 735 ships. There were about 900 whaling ships in the whole world, so most of them were American. New Bedford, Massachusetts, was the leading whaling port. At its peak, more than 400 whaling ships sailed from there.

Whale oil cost $1.30 to $2.50 a gallon, and most families burned cheaper fuels. Tallow candles were made of animal fat. Lard oil, made from pig fat, cost about 90 cents a gallon, and camphene cost about 50 cents. Camphene was one part turpentine, a liquid made from pine trees, mixed with four parts alcohol. Its fumes caught fire easily, and camphene lamps could start sudden fires.

Oil from sperm whales lit the homes of richer families. It also lit lighthouses and public halls.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nelson-cole-haley" name="Nelson Cole Haley" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nelson Cole Haley

> **Who:** A New Bedford boy who ran off to sea at twelve, became a harpooner on a whaling ship, and later wrote a book about his four-year voyage.
> **When and where:** At sea aboard the whaling ship *Charles W. Morgan*, 1849 to 1853. Born 1832, died 1900.

Nelson Cole Haley hunted whales for the oil that Americans burned in their lamps. He was born in New Bedford, Massachusetts, in 1832. His father died when he was a boy. In 1844, when he was twelve, he ran off to sea to become a whaleman, even though his mother did not want him to go.

In 1849, when he was seventeen, Haley became a harpooner on the whaling ship *Charles W. Morgan*. A harpooner threw the harpoon, a heavy iron spear, into the whale from a boat that the crew rowed out from the ship. Haley called the harpoon an iron. He later wrote about one chase: "I raised the iron, and with all the force I was capable of, I sent it to the socket into the vast mass of blubber and meat that was now only a foot or two clear of the boat." Blubber is the thick layer of fat under a whale's skin. He drove a second harpoon in beside the first. Then the second mate, one of the ship's officers, killed the whale with a lance, a long spear with a sharp blade.

The crew rowed the dead whale back to the ship. The next morning, men stood on the dead whale and peeled off strips of blubber, and the crew hoisted the strips on board. On deck, the crew cut the blubber into chunks they called bibles. They boiled the chunks in two big metal pots set on the try-works, a brick furnace built on the ship's deck. The heat melted the fat into oil, and the crew poured the oil into barrels below deck. For fuel under the pots, they burned the crisp bits of blubber left over from the boiling.

Haley's voyage lasted four years, from 1849 to 1853. Whaling ships of that time stayed at sea three to five years on a voyage. Haley wrote a book about the voyage, called *Whale Hunt*. The Mystic Seaport Museum first published it in 1948, almost fifty years after he died.

The *Charles W. Morgan* was built in New Bedford in 1841. It made 37 voyages between 1841 and 1921, with sailors from fifty countries. The ship is kept today at the Mystic Seaport Museum. It is the last wooden whaling ship left in the world.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Kerosene made from oil went into American lamps between 1850 and 1900, and some Americans began to buy electricity. In 1859 Edwin Drake's crew drilled a well in Pennsylvania that reached oil, and refiners made the oil into kerosene. In 1882 Thomas Edison's workers began sending electric current through wires to paying customers from a station in New York City.

Coal became the country's main fuel in these years. According to the US Energy Information Administration, coal supplied 9 percent of the energy Americans used in 1850 and 27 percent by 1870. Around 1885, Americans began to burn more energy from coal than from wood, for the first time. In 1869, 110 people died in a fire at the Avondale coal mine in Pennsylvania. Smoke and gas suffocated 108 men and boys underground, and two men who went down to rescue them also died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drake's well, 1859" -->
On August 27, 1859, near Titusville, Pennsylvania, Edwin Drake's crew struck oil 69.5 feet down. It was the first well in the United States drilled on purpose to find oil and sell it. Writers at the American Oil & Gas Historical Society date the start of the American oil business to that day.

Drake worked for the Seneca Oil Company of New Haven, Connecticut. The owners wanted oil to make kerosene, a cheap fuel for lamps. The driller, William Smith, known as Uncle Billy, cut through rock and shale, a soft rock made of hardened mud, at about three feet a day. A steam engine of six horsepower drove the drill. A horsepower is a unit of power based on the work one horse can do.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-drake" name="Edwin Drake" movie="Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake (1954)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edwin Drake

> **Who:** The man the Seneca Oil Company hired to drill for oil. His 1859 well started the American oil business.
> **When and where:** Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859. Died November 1880 in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.
> **Movie:** *Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake* (1954). A 30-minute film in color, paid for by the oil industry's American Petroleum Institute, with Vincent Price as Drake.

Edwin Drake drilled the first American well made to find oil. He lost his money afterward and spent his last years ill.

People called him Colonel Drake, but he never held that rank. An investor named James Townsend made up the title. He addressed letters to "Colonel E. L. Drake" to impress the people of Titusville.

Water from Oil Creek kept seeping into the hole, and the sides kept caving in. Drake had a pipe driven down to the rock, and then the drill worked inside the pipe. This drive pipe kept the water and loose ground out. On August 27, 1859, the drill reached oil at 69.5 feet.

Drake never patented the drive pipe. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the only right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. In the late 1860s his friends raised about $4,000 for him. In 1873, people of Titusville won him a pension of $1,500 a year from the state of Pennsylvania. A pension is money paid every year to someone who no longer works. Drake died in November 1880 in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.

The film about him, *Born in Freedom*, first played in Titusville on March 29, 1954.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-drake" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kerosene in the lamp" -->
In the 1860s, American families switched to kerosene for their lamps. Refiners made it from oil by distillation. In distillation, a liquid is heated until parts of it boil off as vapor, and the vapor is cooled back into separate liquids. Kerosene sold for about 50 cents a gallon.

Whaling was already shrinking before kerosene came. The whaling fleet was largest in 1846, and whalers caught fewer whales through the 1850s. Whale oil cost $1.30 to $2.50 a gallon, and mainly richer families burned it. Most families burned camphene and lard oil.

In 1862, during the Civil War, members of Congress passed the Internal Revenue Act to pay for the Union Army, the army of the Northern states. Under that law, alcohol was taxed at up to $2.08 a gallon from 1862 to 1864. The lawmakers meant to tax alcohol that people drank. They did not leave camphene out of the law, and camphene was about three quarters alcohol. Kerosene was taxed at 10 cents a gallon. With the tax added, a gallon of camphene cost far more than a gallon of kerosene. People stopped buying camphene, and families switched to kerosene.

People sometimes say that kerosene saved the whales. That claim is too simple. Whaling had been shrinking for more than ten years, and the fuel that kerosene replaced in most homes was camphene.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pearl Street, 1882" -->
On September 4, 1882, Thomas Edison's workers started the first permanent central power station in the United States. A central power station makes electricity in one place and sends it out through wires to many buildings. It stood at 257 Pearl Street in Manhattan, in New York City.

The station ran on coal. Four boilers burned coal to make steam. The steam ran engines, and the engines turned six dynamos. A dynamo is a machine that turns motion into electric current. Wires carried the current to a district of about a quarter of a square mile around the station.

On the first day, the station served 85 customers, who had about 400 lamps among them. Within a year it served 513 customers with about 10,000 lamps. When the station started, Edison was standing in the office of the banker J. Pierpont Morgan, at the bank of Drexel, Morgan and Company. The station ran until January 2, 1890. In those years it went dark only once, for three hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-edison-energy" name="Thomas Edison" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Edison

> **Who:** The inventor whose workers built the first permanent central power station in the United States. He built his system on direct current.
> **When and where:** Pearl Street, Manhattan, New York City, from September 4, 1882.

Thomas Edison built his first power system to run on direct current, called DC. Direct current flows in one direction only, like the current from a battery. The Pearl Street station sent DC out at a low voltage. Voltage is the force that pushes electric current through a wire.

Low-voltage direct current loses a lot of its energy in the wires as it travels. So the Pearl Street station could serve only about a quarter of a square mile around it. The voltage of direct current could not easily be raised or lowered.

George Westinghouse sold a different kind of current, alternating current, called AC, which flows back and forth. Edison secretly paid a salesman named Harold Brown to use AC in the first electric chair, a chair built to kill a person with electricity.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-edison-energy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The current war" -->
Alternating current, called AC, flows back and forth, changing direction many times each second. In the United States today it changes direction 60 times a second. A machine called a transformer can easily raise or lower the voltage of AC. Direct current could not easily be changed that way.

In May 1888 Nikola Tesla received patents for motors that ran on alternating current. In July 1888 George Westinghouse paid for the right to use Tesla's motors. He paid $60,000 in cash and in stock, which means shares in the ownership of a company, plus $2.50 for each horsepower that the motors produced. Westinghouse's company sold AC systems, and Edison's company sold DC systems.

Edison and his supporters attacked AC as deadly. Harold Brown, an electricity salesman, ran tests in 1888 at Edison's laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey. He killed dogs with alternating current to show that it was dangerous. The dogs were strays, bought from boys in the neighborhood for 25 cents each. Edison's supporters used the word "Westinghoused" to mean killed by electricity.

In 1888 New York lawmakers replaced hanging with death by electricity. Governor David B. Hill had proposed the change. New York State officials hired Harold Brown to build the first electric chair, and Edison secretly paid Brown to use alternating current in it. An electric chair kills a person by sending a strong electric current through the body.

The first man put to death in the electric chair was William Kemmler, of Buffalo, New York. In 1889 Kemmler killed Matilda Ziegler, who lived with him as his wife. He struck her more than 25 times on the head, neck and shoulders with a hatchet. A court found him guilty in May 1889 and sentenced him to death. His lawyer, W. Bourke Cockran, argued in court that death by electricity was a cruel and unusual punishment, which the New York State Constitution forbids. The judges of the New York Court of Appeals said it was unusual but not cruel. The justices of the US Supreme Court refused to change that decision.

On August 6, 1890, at Auburn prison in New York, prison officials strapped Kemmler into Brown's chair. The current ran through him for 17 seconds. His fist clenched so hard that blood ran from his palm. A doctor said he was dead. Then Kemmler groaned and began to breathe again. The officials turned the current back on. This time it took between one and four minutes to kill him. The back of his coat caught fire, and the room filled with the smell of burning flesh. Several witnesses fainted. Newspaper writers called the execution "a disgrace to civilization." Westinghouse said, "It has been a brutal affair. They could have done better with an ax."

In 1893 Westinghouse's company offered to light the World's Columbian Exposition, the world's fair in Chicago, for $399,000. General Electric offered to do it for $554,000. Westinghouse's company got the job, and in May 1893 his AC system lit about 100,000 electric lamps at the fair. Westinghouse's company also won the job of building the first generators for the Adams power plant at Niagara Falls. A generator is a machine that turns motion into electric current. On August 26, 1895, the Adams plant sent out its first alternating current, to factories in the town of Niagara Falls. In November 1896 alternating current from Niagara Falls lit Buffalo, New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" name="George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla" movie="The Current War (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla

> **Who:** The businessman whose company sold alternating-current systems, and the engineer whose AC motors he paid to use.
> **When and where:** 1888 to 1896. The Chicago world's fair, 1893. Niagara Falls and Buffalo, New York, 1895 and 1896.
> **Movie:** *The Current War* (2017). A Hollywood drama, not a documentary. It stars Cumberbatch as Edison, Shannon as Westinghouse and Hoult as Tesla, with Tom Holland as Samuel Insull. Gomez-Rejon directed it. It first showed at the Toronto International Film Festival on September 9, 2017, and opened widely in 2019.

George Westinghouse's company won the jobs that brought alternating current to the Chicago world's fair and to Niagara Falls. Its AC motors came from Nikola Tesla, who received patents for them in May 1888. In July 1888 Westinghouse bought the right to use those patents for $60,000 in cash and stock, plus a payment of $2.50 for every horsepower the motors produced.

Edison's supporters called AC deadly and used the word "Westinghoused" to mean killed by electricity. In 1893 Westinghouse's company offered to do the Chicago world's fair job for $155,000 less than General Electric, and it lit the fair with alternating current. Two years later, on August 26, 1895, the Adams plant at Niagara Falls, with generators built by Westinghouse's company, began making AC power. In November 1896 the current from Niagara reached Buffalo.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="westinghouse-tesla-energy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What coal cost the miners" -->
On September 6, 1869, a fire at the Avondale mine, near Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, killed 110 people. The Steuben Coal Company owned the mine. Mine operators then kept small fires burning at the bottom of their shafts, the deep holes that lead down into a mine. A fire like this, called a ventilating furnace, made a draft that moved fresh air through the mine.

Sparks from the ventilating furnace set fire to the wooden timbers in the shaft. The flames spread up to the breaker, the wooden building where coal was broken and sorted. The breaker stood right over the shaft, and the shaft was the only way into or out of the mine. The men who ran the Steuben Coal Company had the mine and its breaker built that way. The fire filled the mine with smoke and gas, and 108 men and boys suffocated underground. They could not get air to breathe, and they died. Five of the dead were boys between 12 and 17 years old. Two men who went down to rescue the miners also died.

In 1870 Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a mine law. It required every mine to have more than one way out. It also forbade using one single passage both to bring fresh air in and to let bad air out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From charcoal to coal" -->
Hopewell Furnace in Pennsylvania burned charcoal from about 1771 until it closed in 1883. According to the National Park Service, charcoal furnaces had fallen out of use by then. Iron makers had built new ironworks that burned anthracite, the hard coal, in their place. For more than a hundred years, workers at Hopewell had cut the forest around it for charcoal. Some of the people who worked at Hopewell in its early decades were enslaved. In 1780 Mark Bird, who built the furnace, enslaved 10 men, 4 women, 3 boys and 1 girl. The last record of slavery at Hopewell is from 1821.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lafayette-houck" name="Lafayette Houck" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Lafayette Houck

> **Who:** A collier, or charcoal maker, at Hopewell Furnace. He came from a family of colliers and was one of the last men who knew how the work was done.
> **When and where:** Hopewell Furnace, Berks County, Pennsylvania, in the later 1800s. He showed how to make charcoal in 1936.

Lafayette Houck was one of the last colliers at Hopewell Furnace. His family's work there began in 1818, when Henry Houck worked at Hopewell as a master collier. In winter Henry also repaired wooden wagon wheels or chopped wood to sell.

A collier's pit was a big pile of wood, stacked around a pole and covered with leaves and dust. The collier lit it, and the wood smoldered, burning slowly with very little air. In 10 days to two weeks the wood turned into charcoal. A Hopewell collier lived in a rough hut near his pits from May to October, with one or two helpers. Together they tended up to eight or nine smoldering pits at once. They watched the pits day and night, from the moment each one was lit until a teamster, a man who drove a wagon, hauled away the last load of charcoal. In the 1830s the furnace's owners paid about 20 colliers, and each one tended eight or nine pits.

Hopewell closed in 1883. In the 1930s the federal government bought the land. In 1936 Houck and his son, William, showed workers of the Civilian Conservation Corps, a government jobs program, how a collier lived and worked. Houck taught the National Park Service how charcoal was made. He also told them what he remembered about life at the furnace.

His age is uncertain. One National Park Service page gives his life as 1846 to 1939. Another says he was 82 years old in 1936, which would mean he was born around 1854. No surviving record gives his pay or the years he began and stopped work as a collier.
