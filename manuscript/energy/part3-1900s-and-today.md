## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1950, electric wires reached almost every American home. In 1890 about 8 in 100 households had electricity. In 1950, 94 in 100 had it, according to census figures gathered by Emanuela Cardia in a 2008 study. Farms were far behind. In 1935 fewer than 11 farms in 100 had it.

Coal still heated most homes. The 1940 census found that more than half of American homes were heated mainly with coal, and about a quarter with wood. In 1901 a single well in Texas began to pour out about 100,000 barrels of oil a day. In World War II, most of the oil burned by the Allies, the countries fighting Germany, Italy and Japan, came from the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spindletop, 1901" -->
On January 10, 1901, a well on Spindletop hill, about 3 miles south of Beaumont, Texas, struck oil 1,139 feet down. Oil shot more than 100 feet into the air. The well poured out an estimated 100,000 barrels a day until workers capped it nine days later, on January 19. In the whole year of 1900, all of Texas had produced 836,000 barrels. The Spindletop well could beat that in nine days.

Anthony F. Lucas led the drilling. According to the Texas State Historical Association, he was the leading American expert on salt domes, large masses of salt buried deep underground. The well stood on land owned by the McFaddin, Wiess and Kyle families. Several oil companies started at Spindletop or grew large there. Among them were the Texas Company, later called Texaco, and Gulf Oil.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What coal cost the miners, 1900 to 1950" -->
Coal miners died at work by the thousands in the early 1900s. The federal Mine Safety and Health Administration keeps a count for every year. In 1900, 1,489 coal miners were killed at work. The worst year was 1907, when 3,242 were killed. In 1930, 2,063 died, and in 1950, 643. From 1900 through 2025, the yearly counts add up to about 105,000 coal miners killed in accidents. The counts leave out miners who died of disease from their work.

On December 6, 1907, explosions in mines No. 6 and No. 8 at Monongah, West Virginia, killed 362 men by the official count. It is the worst mine disaster in American history. Later estimates run above 500, because boys and helpers who were not on the company's list also went underground. The dead included immigrants from Italy, Poland and Hungary, and Black workers. About 1,000 children lost a father.

Sources disagree about which company owned the mines. One names the Fairmont Coal Company, and another names the Consolidation Coal Company. The Fairmont company's president, C. W. Watson, said the explosion "could only have been caused by the careless use of a mining lamp." Experts at the time disagreed about the cause. Some blamed torn electrical wires, and others blamed lamps. One account of the cause begins with a loaded coal car that broke loose and ran back down into mine No. 6. No one is known to have been charged with a crime. After Monongah, members of Congress created the federal Bureau of Mines.

In 1914 Colorado National Guard soldiers killed striking coal miners and their families at Ludlow, Colorado. The coal mined there fed Colorado's steel mill and its railroads. In 1913 the coal miners went on strike. Most of them worked for the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, and its main owner was John D. Rockefeller Jr. The miners wanted the company to obey Colorado's mining laws and to accept their union, the United Mine Workers. The strikers and their families lived in tent camps. Governor Elias M. Ammons sent the Colorado National Guard, and the mine owners agreed to pay for it.

On April 20, 1914, fighting broke out at the tent camp at Ludlow. No one knows who fired the first shot. Guardsmen burned the tents. At least 19 people died: one guardsman, five miners, and thirteen women and children. The women and children had hidden in a pit dug under a tent, and the smoke and fumes suffocated them. Guardsmen killed three strike leaders who were under a flag of truce, a white flag that asks for a pause in fighting. They shot the strike leader Louis Tikas, a Greek American, three times in the back. In the days after, miners attacked mines across the region and killed mine bosses and guards. President Woodrow Wilson sent the US Army on April 28. The strike ended in December 1914, and the miners won none of the union's goals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-insull" name="Samuel Insull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Insull

> **Who:** Thomas Edison's private secretary, who later built one of the biggest groups of electric companies in the country. It collapsed in 1932.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1892 to 1932. Died in Paris in 1938.

Samuel Insull built a chain of electric companies that served millions of people, and the chain collapsed in 1932. He had worked for Thomas Edison as his private secretary. In 1892 Insull took over the Chicago Edison company, and he built it into Commonwealth Edison.

Insull tied together more than 85 companies through holding companies. A holding company is a company that owns shares, or pieces of ownership, of other companies. Insull's companies owned shares in one another, and much of the money behind them was borrowed. Many small investors bought shares in his companies.

In 1932, during the Great Depression, Insull's companies failed, and investors lost millions of dollars. Insull left the country for Europe. In 1934 he was brought back to the United States to stand trial. He was tried three times: for fraud, which means cheating people by lying, for breaking federal bankruptcy law, and for embezzlement. Embezzlement means stealing money that other people trusted you to handle. He was found not guilty all three times.

In 1932 Franklin Roosevelt, running for president, named Insull in a campaign speech. In 1935 members of Congress passed the Public Utility Holding Company Act. They wrote it to stop anyone from building another chain of electric companies like Insull's. Insull died in Paris in 1938.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-insull" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rivers into power, and who was moved" -->
In the 1930s federal officials built large dams to make electricity, and they moved tens of thousands of people off their land to do it. A hydroelectric dam holds back a river. Water rushing down through the dam turns machines that make electricity.

On May 18, 1933, President Franklin Roosevelt signed the law that created the Tennessee Valley Authority, or TVA. The TVA was a federal agency, and its board and staff built dams in the Tennessee Valley. Before the TVA, about 3 in 100 families in the valley had electricity. Work on the first TVA dam, Norris Dam, began in October 1933.

From 1933 to 1945, TVA officials bought or took about 1.3 million acres from their owners. They moved an estimated 82,000 people from their homes to build 16 hydroelectric dams. For Norris Dam alone, TVA officials moved more than 3,000 families. TVA lawyer John Snyder described how the land was taken: "a price is set and we buy at that price or we condemn." To condemn land is to take it by law without the owner's agreement, at a price the government sets. TVA's own economists estimated that condemning land got it for about 60 percent of what a private buyer would pay. The records do not name one official who made these choices. The TVA's board and staff made them together.

On October 9, 1936, the first generator at Hoover Dam began sending electricity over 266 miles of wire to Los Angeles. Some records date the start to October 26, 1936, the day that generator reached full operation.

Workers for the federal Bureau of Reclamation built Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia River. They finished it in 1941. The dam stands 550 feet high, and its builders gave it no fish ladders. A fish ladder is a set of rising pools that lets salmon swim upstream past a dam. The lake behind the dam flooded Kettle Falls, where Native people had fished for salmon for about 9,000 years. The catch there had averaged about 644,500 fish a year. In June 1940 the Colville Confederated Tribes held a three-day Ceremony of Tears at the falls. Between 8,000 and 10,000 people came, from the Spokane, Yakima, Nez Perce, Coeur d'Alene and other nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Osage oil" -->
Some of the oil Americans burned in the 1920s came from under the Osage Nation's land in Oklahoma. Under the Osage Allotment Act of 1906, the Osage kept the rights to the oil and minerals under their land, owned in common. Each Osage person with a share, called a headright, received part of the oil money. After a law passed in 1921, local judges put many Osage adults' money under the control of white guardians.

From about 1920 to 1925, husbands, guardians and others killed Osage headright holders to inherit their shares of the oil money. William Hale, a cattleman, planned the deaths of one family so that his nephew, Ernest Burkhart, could inherit their headrights. Burkhart was married to Mollie Burkhart. Her sister Anna Brown was shot in May 1921. Their mother, Lizzie Q. Kyle, died in July 1921, and poison was suspected. Their cousin Henry Roan was shot. On March 10, 1923, a bomb blew up the home of Mollie's sister Rita Smith. It killed Rita, her husband, Bill Smith, and Nettie Brookshire, who worked for them. Federal agents proved that Hale ordered the murders. In January 1929 he was convicted and sent to prison for life.

The counts of the dead differ. The Oklahoma Historical Society counts about 24 violent or suspicious deaths, and the FBI says at least two dozen. The Osage Nation counts more than 60 murders and suspicious deaths.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The night the lights came on" -->
Wires reached American farms mostly after 1935, when federal officials began lending money to farmers' cooperatives to build power lines. In 1935 fewer than 11 farms in 100 had electricity from a central power station.

On May 11, 1935, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 7037, which created the Rural Electrification Administration, or REA. An executive order is a rule that a president issues on his own authority, without a vote of Congress. On May 20, 1936, Roosevelt signed the Rural Electrification Act. Under that law, REA officials made long-term, low-cost loans to cooperatives owned by farmers. A cooperative is a business owned by the people who use it. The farmers' cooperatives used the loans to put up poles and string wires. About half of American farms had electricity by 1945, about 9 in 10 by 1950, and about 97 in 100 by 1960.

In 1930 a Tennessee farmer, whose name was not recorded, said: "The greatest thing on earth is to have the love of God in your heart, and the next greatest thing is to have electricity in your house." David Nye records the words in his book *Electrifying America*. In 1984 the national association of electric cooperatives named its history of the program *The Next Greatest Thing*.

REA staff also taught farm families how to use the new power. In the 1930s and 1940s, Louisan Mamer, an REA home electrification specialist, toured farm towns with a traveling show called the electric circus. She showed families electric appliances and how to cook with electricity.

In the Texas Hill Country, power first ran through the lines of the Pedernales Electric Cooperative on October 27, 1939. The cooperative had 1,800 miles of line and 422 first customers. Families coming home that night saw their farmhouses lit for the first time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pearl-yates" name="Pearl Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pearl Yates

> **Who:** A high school English teacher in the countryside of western North Carolina. Electricity reached her area in July 1939, and she told about it in an interview in 1984.
> **When and where:** Haywood County, North Carolina, 1939.

Pearl Yates walked from room to room with her small children when the electric lights came on in her home. "We'd stop and look and they were so pretty and we'd go look at another room and another room," she said. The power reached her part of Haywood County, North Carolina, in July 1939.

Before then, she cooked on a wood stove. Washing clothes took a whole day, in a large pot. Officials of the power company, Carolina Power and Light, would not run lines into the countryside where the Yates family lived. So people there formed a cooperative. Many neighbors did not trust it at first, because outsiders ran it and members had to pay money up front. Organizers went from door to door and signed many of them up.

On May 29, 1984, Bonnie Bishop interviewed Pearl Yates and her husband, Oral Yates. Oral Yates had worked as a teacher and for the railroad, and he had served in the North Carolina legislature. The interview was part of a set recorded for the 50th anniversary of the REA. It is kept in the Southern Oral History Program at the University of North Carolina.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pearl-yates" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil for a world war" -->
The Allies used about 7 billion barrels of oil in World War II, and about 6 billion of those barrels came from the United States. The American Oil and Gas Historical Society gives these figures.

Tanker ships carried oil from Texas along the coast, and German submarines were sinking them. So workers built two pipelines on land. The Big Inch, a pipe 24 inches wide, ran from East Texas to Illinois and on to the East. It was finished in August 1943. Together with a second line, the Little Big Inch, it carried more than 350 million barrels of oil east before the war in Europe ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Nuclear power plants began making electricity in the United States in the 1950s. In 1957 the first full-scale American plant built only for civilian power began sending electricity into a power company's lines in Pennsylvania. In 1979 a reactor at Three Mile Island, also in Pennsylvania, partly melted. After that, American power companies stopped ordering new reactors.

Americans kept burning more oil and natural gas after World War II, according to the US Energy Information Administration. In October 1973 the leaders of Arab oil-producing countries cut off oil sales to the United States. Drivers waited in long lines for gasoline. Members of Congress then passed new energy laws, and the federal Department of Energy opened in 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Electricity from the atom" -->
A nuclear power plant makes electricity from uranium. Inside the plant's reactor, atoms of uranium split in two, and each split gives off a large amount of energy as heat. This splitting is called fission. The heat boils water into steam. The steam spins the blades of turbines, and the turbines drive generators that make electricity.

On December 20, 1951, a test reactor in Idaho called EBR-1 made the first usable electricity ever produced from nuclear energy. It lit four 200-watt light bulbs. On July 17, 1955, the town of Arco, Idaho, about 1,200 people, ran on electricity from a nearby reactor called BORAX III for about an hour. Arco was the first town anywhere lit only by nuclear power.

On December 8, 1953, President Eisenhower gave a speech called "Atoms for Peace" to the General Assembly of the United Nations. Work on a nuclear power plant at Shippingport, Pennsylvania, began in 1954. On December 18, 1957, the plant sent its first electricity into the lines of Duquesne Light, a power company. On December 23 it reached its full power of 60,000 kilowatts. That is enough to light 300,000 of the 200-watt bulbs lit by EBR-1.

Shippingport was the first full-scale nuclear power plant in the United States built only to make electricity for civilian use, meaning not for the military. It was not the first to make nuclear electricity. EBR-1 and smaller American reactors came before it, and so did reactors built for more than one purpose. A British plant, Calder Hall, began sending power in 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Garrison Dam and the Three Affiliated Tribes" -->
In 1953 the Army Corps of Engineers finished Garrison Dam on the Missouri River in North Dakota. Its lake flooded the farmland along the river on the Fort Berthold Reservation. The reservation is the home of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara, called the Three Affiliated Tribes. Members of Congress paid for the dam in 1947. It was part of the Army Corps' Pick-Sloan Plan, which promised flood control and electricity.

The lake behind the dam, Lake Sakakawea, covered the river bottomlands where the tribes farmed. Bottomlands are the low, flat lands beside a river. Sources give from 153,000 to about 155,000 acres. Farms, homes, schools, a hospital, businesses, churches, cemeteries, roads and whole towns went under the water. The drowned towns included Elbowoods, Sanish and Van Hook. By 1954 the Elbowoods school building stood under 50 feet of water.

About 300 families had to move. By one count, that was more than 90 percent of the tribes' people. In 1948, at the signing of the contract for the tribes' land, tribal chairman George Gillette said: "The truth is, as everyone knows, our Treaty of Fort Laramie ... and our constitution are being torn to shreds by this contract." In 1950, 525 of the tribes' 991 adults voted to accept the terms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black lung and the 1969 law" -->
On November 20, 1968, an explosion at the Consol No. 9 coal mine in Farmington, West Virginia, killed 78 miners. Members of Congress then passed the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969, which took effect on March 30, 1970. Under it, federal inspectors had to check every underground coal mine four times a year and every surface mine twice a year. Inspectors could close a mine where they found a danger to life. The law also paid federal benefits to miners with black lung disease.

Black lung is a group of lung diseases caused by breathing coal mine dust. The body's defending cells try to break down the grains of dust and cannot. The cells burst and release chemicals that damage the lung. Over the years, scar tissue and hard lumps form in the lungs, and miners cough up dark, inky phlegm, the thick slime that comes up from the lungs. In the worst form, large masses of scar tissue fill the lungs, and the miner gasps for every breath. According to the US Department of Labor, more than 76,000 miners died of black lung from 1968 to 2017.

Deaths from mine accidents fell after the law. The Mine Safety and Health Administration counts 311 coal miners killed at work in 1968, 260 in 1970, 133 in 1980 and 38 in 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1973: the gasoline runs short" -->
In October 1973 the leaders of Arab oil-producing countries, acting together, announced an embargo on oil sales to the United States. An embargo is an order that stops trade. They acted because the US government was sending military supplies to Israel in the Yom Kippur War. The embargo lasted until March 1974. In about six months, the price of a barrel of oil rose from about $2.90 to $11.65, about four times as much.

Gas stations ran out of gasoline, and drivers waited in lines that stretched for blocks. Many states let drivers buy gas only on certain days, by license plate number. Cars with odd numbers could buy on odd-numbered days, and cars with even numbers on even-numbered days. Members of Congress set a national speed limit of 55 miles an hour, and it became law in January 1974. According to a Department of Energy report cited in a 2022 Wisconsin TV news story, gasoline cost an average of 39 cents a gallon in 1973 and 53 cents in 1974.

More energy laws followed. On December 22, 1975, the Energy Policy and Conservation Act became law. It created the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, a federal store of crude oil kept for emergencies. It also set the first fuel-economy standards for cars. New cars had to average 27.5 miles per gallon by 1985, about twice what they got then. The federal Department of Energy opened on October 1, 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="david-falconer" name="David Falconer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### David Falconer

> **Who:** A photographer for the Environmental Protection Agency's DOCUMERICA project. He photographed Oregon's gasoline shortage and wrote a caption for each picture.
> **When and where:** Oregon, October 1973 to May 1974.

David Falconer photographed the gasoline shortage in Oregon and wrote down what he saw. He worked for Project DOCUMERICA. Officials of the Environmental Protection Agency started the project in 1972 to photograph environmental problems around the country. About 120 photographers took part. The National Archives keeps Falconer's pictures and captions.

In October 1973 he photographed closed pumps on Interstate 5 at Sutherlin. His caption says, "By midday gasoline was becoming unavailable along interstate highways." That month in Lincoln City, gas stations closed early and stayed closed on weekends, and "No Gas Today" signs went up. In December 1973 in Portland, he photographed a man who had run out of gas and was standing in line with a gas can. The man's name was not recorded.

In February 1974 in Portland, drivers with even-numbered license plates lined up for gas on even-numbered days, Monday through Friday. Anyone could try on Saturday if gas was left, and all Portland stations closed on Sundays. Falconer wrote that Oregon was the first state to use this odd-even plan. Other records do not confirm his claim.

The embargo ended in March 1974. In May 1974 a gas station in downtown Portland still put up a sign saying its gas for the day was sold out.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-falconer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three Mile Island, 1979" -->
On March 28, 1979, the Unit 2 reactor at the Three Mile Island power plant in Pennsylvania partly melted down. It was the worst accident at a commercial nuclear power plant in American history. In a meltdown, the reactor's fuel gets so hot that it melts.

General Public Utilities ran the reactor. A small fault in the cooling system came first. Then a relief valve stuck open and let cooling water escape. The operators had no gauge that showed the valve was open. They believed the system held too much water, so they cut back the water flowing into it. The core lost its water and overheated. At least 45 percent of the core melted.

On March 30, Governor Dick Thornburgh advised pregnant women and preschool children living within 5 miles to leave. He told people within 10 miles to stay indoors. Officials gave out messages that conflicted with each other. About 140,000 people left on their own.

A small amount of radiation escaped. The average dose to people within 10 miles was 0.08 millisieverts, and no one got more than 1 millisievert. A millisievert is a unit that measures how much radiation a body takes in. Scientists at the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and more than 12 health studies since 1981, found no health effects they could detect. No deaths are linked to the accident. After Three Mile Island, American power companies stopped ordering new reactors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the uranium came from" -->
Uranium for the federal government, and later for power plants, was mined on Navajo land, and the miners were not warned about a gas that caused lung cancer. From 1944 to 1986, miners dug nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore from Navajo land, according to the Environmental Protection Agency. About 10,000 people worked in uranium, and about a quarter of them were Navajo. Until 1966 the federal Atomic Energy Commission was the only buyer. After that, uranium was also sold to fuel power plants.

Uranium gives off radon, a radioactive gas. Something radioactive gives off energy that can damage the cells of the body. Navajo miners say that no one taught them about the dangers of uranium mining. They say no one gave them protective gear or fresh air in the mines. In 1995 a federal committee, the Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments, reported that "at least several hundred miners" had died of lung cancer from breathing radon. The committee found that federal officials did not require the mines to be ventilated and did not warn the miners.

On July 16, 1979, four months after Three Mile Island, a dam holding radioactive waste from a United Nuclear Corporation uranium mill broke near Church Rock, New Mexico. About 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid and 1,100 tons of solid waste poured down the Rio Puerco, past about 1,700 Navajo people. A 2013 study estimated that the spill released 46 curies of radiation. It estimated 13 curies for Three Mile Island. A curie is a unit that measures radioactivity. A New Mexico state agency called the effects "quite limited." Governor Bruce King turned down the Navajo Nation's request for a federal disaster declaration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Panels on the White House roof" -->
President Carter held a ceremony for 32 new solar panels on the roof of the West Wing of the White House on June 20, 1979. The panels used sunlight to heat water. They were in use for about seven years. In 1986, while President Reagan was in office, workers took the panels down to repair the roof, and no one put them back.

In 1991 some of the panels went to Unity College in Maine. Workers there put 16 of them back up to heat water for the cafeteria. In 2009 one panel went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. Others are at the Carter Library and at a solar museum in Dezhou, China.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have changed the fuels they burn to make electricity. In 2016 natural gas passed coal as the largest source of American electricity for a full year, according to the US Energy Information Administration, or EIA. Drillers got more and more of that gas by fracking, which cracks rock deep underground to free the gas inside. Wind and solar power made a record share of the country's electricity in 2025, about 17 to 18 percent, by EIA figures.

In 2019 the United States produced more energy than it used, for the first time since 1957. In February 2021 a winter storm shut down power plants across Texas, and more than 4.5 million households lost power. In 2023 the first newly built American nuclear reactor in more than 30 years began running in Georgia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fracking" -->
After drillers began fracking shale, the United States became the world's largest producer of natural gas and then of crude oil. Fracking is short for hydraulic fracturing. Drillers pump water, sand and chemicals down a well at high pressure. The pressure cracks the rock, and the sand holds the cracks open so that gas or oil can flow out. Many of these wells turn and run sideways through the rock for thousands of feet. That is called horizontal drilling.

George Mitchell was a Texas oilman. Starting in the early 1980s, he and his company, Mitchell Energy, spent about twenty years trying to get gas out of a layer of shale in Texas called the Barnett Shale. In the late 1990s an engineer, Nick Steinsberger, used a cheaper way of fracking called slickwater fracturing. It cut the cost enough that selling the shale gas made money. In the 2000s drillers joined fracking with horizontal drilling.

American wells produced about 5 billion cubic feet of shale gas a day in 2007 and about 80 billion cubic feet a day in 2023, according to the EIA. In 2011 the United States became the world's largest producer of natural gas. In 2018 it passed Russia to become the world's largest producer of crude oil, the EIA reports. In 2025 American wells pumped a record 13.6 million barrels of crude oil a day, by EIA figures published in 2026.

People disagree about fracking. Supporters say it brought cheaper energy and jobs. They add that natural gas burns cleaner than coal, and power companies have replaced coal-burning plants with gas-burning ones. Critics worry that fracking fluid can pollute groundwater and that methane, the main gas in natural gas, leaks from wells and pipes. Earthquakes are a third concern. Drillers pump wastewater from oil and gas wells deep underground. In 2017, scientists at the US Geological Survey wrote that a sharp rise in earthquakes in Oklahoma is largely considered the result of that deep pumping of waste fluids. On September 3, 2016, an earthquake near Pawnee, Oklahoma, measured magnitude 5.8, the largest ever recorded in the state. Magnitude is a number that states the size of an earthquake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Coal and oil still cost lives and land" -->
An explosion of gas and coal dust killed 29 miners at the Upper Big Branch mine in Montcoal, West Virginia, on April 5, 2010. It was the worst mine disaster in the United States in 40 years. Performance Coal Company, part of Massey Energy, ran the mine. Federal mine inspectors named Massey's "corporate culture" as the root cause. By that they meant the habits and rules that the company's leaders set. Officials of the Mine Safety and Health Administration issued 369 citations and orders, which are written notices of broken safety rules, and fined the company $10.8 million.

In December 2015 Massey's former chief executive, Don Blankenship, was convicted of conspiring, or planning with others, to break federal mine safety laws. It was a misdemeanor, a less serious crime than a felony. On April 6, 2016, Judge Irene Berger gave him the longest sentence the law allowed. He got one year in prison and a $250,000 fine. After prison came a year of supervised release, when a court officer checks on him. He was not convicted of causing the miners' deaths.

Black lung came back among coal miners in the 2000s. In 2016 researchers at NIOSH, the federal agency that studies workers' health, found 60 cases of the worst form of the disease in 20 months at one small clinic in Kentucky. An NPR investigation found 962 recent cases of advanced black lung. That was more than double the 441 cases NIOSH had recorded in the 40 years before. *Smithsonian Magazine* reported both counts in 2017.

In 2016 the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe opposed the Dakota Access oil pipeline where it crosses under Lake Oahe. The lake is the tribe's water supply, upstream of its reservation. Protest camps began in April 2016. On December 4, 2016, Army Corps of Engineers officials denied the easement for the crossing. An easement is permission to cross land, here federal land under the lake. On January 24, 2017, President Trump ordered the Army Corps to review and approve the easement quickly. Army Corps officials approved it on February 8, 2017, and oil moved through the pipeline later that year. The tribe kept up its fight in court.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gas passes coal, and the sun and wind climb" -->
In 2016 natural gas made 34 percent of American electricity and passed coal for a full year for the first time, according to the EIA. In 2025, by EIA figures, natural gas made about 41 percent of the country's electricity, nuclear power about 18 percent and coal about 17 percent. Wind made about 11 percent, solar about 7 and hydropower about 6.

Wind, solar, flowing water and other sources that do not run out are called renewable energy. In 2025 all renewable sources together made about 24 percent of American electricity. Wind and solar together made about 17 to 18 percent, a record. The two figures differ because of rounding.

In its projections for 2024 to 2034, the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics expects wind turbine service technicians to be the fastest-growing job in the country, up 49.9 percent. Solar panel installers come second, up 42.1 percent. The two jobs together are projected to add fewer than 20,000 jobs.

Americans also began buying cars that run partly or fully on electricity. A hybrid car has both a gasoline engine and an electric motor. A battery electric car runs on electricity alone. According to the EIA, about 20 percent of the new cars, SUVs and pickups sold in 2024 were hybrids or electric. In 2025 the share rose to about 22 percent. But 2025 was also the first year that sales of battery electric cars fell. Federal tax credits for buyers of these cars ended on September 30, 2025. In each of the months left in 2025, battery electric cars made up less than 6 percent of sales.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Texas grid fails, 2021" -->
In February 2021, during a winter storm named Uri, power plants of every kind in Texas stopped working in the cold. Gas, coal, nuclear and wind plants all failed. More than 4.5 million households, about 10 million people, lost power, some of them for days. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the North American Electric Reliability Corporation give these figures in their final report on the failure.

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas, called ERCOT, runs the Texas power grid. A grid is the network of power plants and wires that serves an area. ERCOT's operators cut off power to customers to keep the whole grid from failing. They cut 20,000 megawatts, the largest cutoff of its kind in American history. A megawatt is a million watts. ERCOT officials later said the grid came within 4 minutes and 37 seconds of a total collapse.

In 2022 the Texas Department of State Health Services counted 246 deaths from the storm. Some researchers argue that the official count is much too low. Reporters at BuzzFeed News, for example, studied how many more people died that month than usual. Damage estimates run above $195 billion.

### Cristian Pavón Pineda

> **Who:** An 11-year-old sixth grader who died in his family's mobile home during the Texas blackout.
> **When and where:** Conroe, Texas, February 16, 2021.

Cristian Pavón Pineda was 11 years old when he died in his family's mobile home in Conroe, Texas, on February 16, 2021. The home had been without power since the Sunday before, and the temperature there fell to about 10 degrees. The day before he died, he saw snow for the first time.

The family of five slept in one room to keep warm. Cristian shared a bed with his younger brother. His aunt, Jaliza Yera, told a TV station that he went to bed in "a shirt, a sweater, two pairs of pants, socks on." His stepfather put another blanket over him. In the morning his family found that he would not wake up.

Early reports, and his family's lawsuit, said he died of hypothermia. Hypothermia means the body's temperature has fallen too low to keep a person alive. Doctors did an autopsy on February 18, 2021. An autopsy is an examination of a body to find the cause of death. It found that he died of carbon monoxide poisoning, Conroe police said in May 2021. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. Published reports do not say where the gas in the home came from. By February 21, more than 30 people in Texas had died in the storm, Al Día News reported. Many of them died of carbon monoxide poisoning after using cars or generators to keep warm.

On February 22, 2021, his mother, Maria Elisa Pineda Guzman, sued ERCOT and Entergy Texas, the power company, for more than $100 million. Her lawyer was Tony Buzbee. Officials had called the power cuts temporary rolling outages, meaning planned cuts that move from area to area. The suit says ERCOT and Entergy Texas did not tell families how long the outages would last. "Accurate information might have saved Cristian Pineda's young life," the suit says. ERCOT's statement called his death a "tragedy" and said ERCOT was "confident that our grid operators made the right choice to avoid a state-wide blackout." Published reports do not say how the lawsuit ended.

Two new nuclear reactors at the Vogtle plant in Georgia began running in 2023 and 2024. Unit 3 went into commercial service on July 31, 2023, and Unit 4 on April 29, 2024, according to the EIA. They were the first newly built American reactors in more than 30 years. One other reactor started in between. Watts Bar Unit 2 in Tennessee, owned by the TVA, began running in 2016, but work on it had begun in 1973. Workers stopped building it in 1985 and started again in 2007.

The owners of two closed plants plan to run them again. Holtec International owns the Palisades plant in Michigan, which shut down in May 2022. The company got a $1.52 billion federal loan to restart it. Holtec missed its targets of 2025 and early 2026. As of July 2026 it had not said when the reactor would run, the news site Canary Media reported.

Three Mile Island Unit 1 is the reactor that did not melt down in 1979. It closed in 2019. Its owner, Constellation Energy, renamed it the Christopher M. Crane Clean Energy Center and plans to run it again. Constellation signed a 20-year agreement to sell its power to Microsoft. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission is expected to decide on its operating license in May 2027, the news outlet WITF reported in July 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What energy independence means" -->
In 2019 the United States produced more energy than it used for the first time since 1957, according to the EIA. Americans produced 101.0 quadrillion Btu of energy that year and used 100.2 quadrillion. A Btu, or British thermal unit, is the heat needed to warm one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. A quadrillion is a million billion.

People call this energy independence. It means the country produces more energy in total than it uses. It does not mean the country stopped buying fuel from other countries. In November 2019 the United States still imported 5.8 million barrels of crude oil a day and exported 3.0 million, by EIA figures.

In 2025 the country sold a record 31 quadrillion Btu of energy to other countries and bought 21 quadrillion Btu from them, the EIA reported in May 2026. Oil and products made from it were 63 percent of the energy sold abroad, and natural gas was 29 percent.
