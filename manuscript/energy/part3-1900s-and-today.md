## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 14 minutes**

Electric wires reached almost every American home in these fifty years. About 8 households in 100 had electricity in 1890, and 94 in 100 did by 1950. Emanuela Cardia worked out those figures in a 2008 study from the census, the government's count of the people and their homes, taken every ten years. Most farms got their wires only after 1935.

Coal still heated most homes. The 1940 census found that more than half of American homes were heated mainly with coal, and about a quarter with wood.

A single well in Texas began to pour out an estimated 100,000 barrels of oil a day in 1901. In World War II, most of the oil burned by the Allies, the countries fighting Germany, Italy and Japan, came from the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spindletop, 1901" -->
A well on Spindletop hill, about 3 miles south of Beaumont, Texas, struck oil 1,139 feet down on January 10, 1901. Oil shot more than 100 feet into the air. The well poured out an estimated 100,000 barrels a day until workers capped it nine days later, on January 19.

**Nine days.** All of Texas had produced 836,000 barrels of oil in the whole year of 1900. At the estimated rate, the Spindletop well poured out about 900,000 barrels in its nine days, more than that whole year's total.

**The salt dome.** Anthony F. Lucas led the drilling. According to the Texas State Historical Association, he was the leading American expert on salt domes, large masses of salt buried deep underground, and Spindletop hill stood on one. Other geologists of the time doubted that oil lay in the salt domes along the coast of the Gulf of Mexico. Lucas was sure it did, and the well proved him right.

Several oil companies started at Spindletop or grew large there, among them the Texas Company, later called Texaco, and Gulf Oil.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What coal cost the miners" -->
Coal miners died at work by the thousands in the early 1900s. Officials of the federal Mine Safety and Health Administration, part of the national government, keep a count for every year. In 1900, 1,489 coal miners died in accidents at work. The worst year was 1907, when 3,242 died. The count was still 2,063 in 1930, and it fell to 643 in 1950.

The yearly counts from 1900 through 2025 add up to about 105,000 coal miners who died in accidents. The counts do not include miners who died of disease from their work.

**Monongah.** Explosions in mines No. 6 and No. 8 at Monongah, West Virginia, killed 362 men by the official count on December 6, 1907. It is the worst mine disaster in American history. Later estimates run above 500, because the explosion destroyed the system that kept track of which miners were underground, so no one could make a full count.

Some of the dead were boys as young as eight. Mine companies of the time hired more workers than they listed, and their lists left out many of the children who worked underground. The dead included immigrants from Italy, Poland and Hungary, and Black workers. About 1,000 children lost a father.

**What went wrong.** Experts at the time did not agree on what set off the explosions. According to Clio, a history website run from Marshall University, a loaded coal car broke loose near the top of a trestle, a raised track, and ran back into mine No. 6. Some experts blamed torn electrical wires. Others blamed miners' lamps.

C. W. Watson was the president of the Fairmont Coal Company, which Clio names as the owner of the mines. He said the explosion "could only have been caused by the careless use of a mining lamp." The West Virginia Mine Wars Museum names a different owner, the Consolidation Coal Company. Neither history names anyone charged with a crime. After Monongah, members of Congress created the federal Bureau of Mines.

**Ludlow.** Colorado National Guard soldiers, the state's own force of part-time soldiers, killed striking coal miners and their families at Ludlow, Colorado, in 1914. The coal miners of southern Colorado had gone on strike in 1913, stopping work to demand changes. The coal they mined supplied Colorado's steel mill and its railroads. Most of them worked for the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, whose main owner was John D. Rockefeller Jr.

The miners wanted the company's owners to obey Colorado's mining laws and to accept their union, the United Mine Workers, a group workers form so they can bargain with owners together. The strikers and their families lived in tent camps. Governor Elias M. Ammons sent the Colorado National Guard, and the mine owners agreed to pay for it.

Fighting broke out at the tent camp at Ludlow on April 20, 1914. No one knows who fired the first shot. Guardsmen burned the tents. At least 19 people were killed: one guardsman, five miners, and thirteen women and children. The women and children had hidden in a pit under a tent, and fumes from the burning filled the pit, so they died because they could not breathe.

Guardsmen killed three strike leaders who were under a flag of truce, a white flag that asks for a pause in fighting. They shot the strike leader Louis Tikas, a Greek American, three times in the back.

In the days after, miners attacked mines across the region and killed mine bosses and guards. No one knows how many people died in those days, but more died than at Ludlow. President Woodrow Wilson sent the US Army on April 28. The strike ended in December 1914, and the miners won none of the union's goals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-insull" name="Samuel Insull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Insull

> **Who:** Edison's former secretary, who tied more than 85 electric companies into one chain that collapsed in 1932.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1892 to 1932.

**Tried three times after his companies failed, he was never found guilty.**

Samuel Insull built a chain of electric companies that served millions of people, and the chain collapsed in 1932. He had worked for Thomas Edison as his private secretary. In 1892 Insull took over the Chicago Edison company, and he built it into Commonwealth Edison.

**The chain.** Insull tied together more than 85 companies through holding companies, companies that own shares, or pieces of ownership, of other companies. Insull's companies owned shares in one another, and they had borrowed much of the money behind them. Many small investors bought shares in his companies.

**The fall.** Insull's companies failed in 1932, during the Great Depression, and investors lost millions of dollars. Insull fled to Europe. In October 1932 a grand jury in Chicago, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof for a trial, charged him with crimes.

He moved to Greece, and officials there refused to send him back. In 1934, after US officials pressed them, Greek officials told him to leave. Officials of Turkey arrested him, and US State Department officers brought him back to the United States to stand trial.

He was tried three times. One trial was for fraud, cheating people by lying. Another was for embezzlement, stealing money that others had trusted him to handle. The third was for breaking federal bankruptcy law, the rules for what happens when a person or a company cannot pay its debts. He was found not guilty all three times.

Franklin Roosevelt, running for president, named Insull in a campaign speech in 1932. In 1935 members of Congress passed the Public Utility Holding Company Act, which they wrote to stop anyone from building another chain of electric companies like Insull's.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-insull" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rivers into power, and who lost their homes" -->
Federal officials had large dams built to make electricity from 1933 to 1945. In the Tennessee Valley alone, they made an estimated 82,000 people leave their homes to do it. A hydroelectric dam holds back a river, and water rushing down through the dam turns machines that make electricity.

**The TVA.** President Franklin Roosevelt signed the law that created the Tennessee Valley Authority, or TVA, on May 18, 1933. The TVA was a federal corporation, a company owned by the national government. Its board and staff ran it, and TVA officials had dams built in the Tennessee Valley. Before the TVA, about 3 in 100 families in the valley had electricity. Work on the first TVA dam, Norris Dam, began in October 1933.

**Who had to leave.** TVA officials bought or took about 1.3 million acres from their owners from 1933 to 1945, and they made those 82,000 people leave to make room for 16 hydroelectric dams. For Norris Dam alone, TVA officials made more than 3,000 families leave.

**The price.** TVA officials set the price of each piece of land, and an owner who would not sell at that price lost the land anyway. TVA lawyer John Snyder put it this way: "a price is set and we buy at that price or we condemn." To condemn land is to take it by law without the owner's agreement, at a price the government sets. TVA's own economists estimated that condemning land got it for about 60 percent of what a private buyer would pay.

The TVA's board and staff made these choices together. Wayne Moore's 1995 history of the TVA land-taking does not name one official who made them.

**Hoover Dam.** Hoover Dam stands on the Colorado River, between Arizona and Nevada, and it was one of the big federal dams that made electricity in these years. Its first generator began sending electricity over 266 miles of wire to Los Angeles on October 9, 1936. Some records date the start to October 26, 1936, the day that generator reached full operation. Chapter 12, Landmarks, tells how many men died building the dam.

**Grand Coulee.** Officials of the federal Bureau of Reclamation had Grand Coulee Dam built on the Columbia River, and it was finished in 1941. The dam stands 550 feet high, and its builders gave it no fish ladders, the rising pools that let salmon swim upstream past a dam. Salmon and steelhead, a trout that swims to the sea and back, could no longer reach the whole upper Columbia Basin, and their runs there ended.

The lake behind the dam flooded Kettle Falls, where Native people had fished for salmon for about 9,000 years. The catch there had averaged about 644,500 fish a year. Native people also lost thousands of acres of low, flat land beside the river where they grew food, and they lost their way to the places where they gathered roots and berries.

As the dam neared completion in June 1940, the Colville Confederated Tribes held a three-day Ceremony of Tears to mourn the loss of the fishery at Kettle Falls. Between 8,000 and 10,000 people came, from the Spokane, Yakima, Nez Perce, Coeur d'Alene and other nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Osage oil" -->
Some of the oil Americans burned in the 1920s came from under the Osage Nation's land in Oklahoma. Under the Osage Allotment Act of 1906, the Osage kept the rights to the oil and minerals under their land, owned in common. Each Osage person with a share, called a headright, received part of the oil money. Under a law passed in 1921, local judges put many Osage adults' money under the control of white guardians, people the judges chose to handle it.

**The murders.** From about 1920 to 1925, husbands, guardians and others killed Osage headright holders to inherit their shares of the oil money. A cattleman named William Hale planned to have members of one Osage family killed so that his nephew, Ernest Burkhart, would inherit their headrights. It was the family of Ernest's wife, Mollie. Federal agents later proved that Hale ordered the murders.

**Mollie's family.** Mollie Burkhart's mother was Lizzie Q. Kyle. Her sisters were Anna Brown and Rita Smith, and Rita's husband was Bill Smith. Henry Roan was Mollie's cousin.

**The killings.** Kelsie Morrison shot Anna Brown in May 1921. He later admitted that he killed her at Hale's request. Henry Roan was shot and killed in January 1923. A cowboy named John Ramsey admitted that Hale had hired him to kill Roan, though Ramsey later took back his confession.

A bomb blew up Rita Smith's home on March 10, 1923. It killed Rita, Bill Smith and Nettie Brookshire, who worked for them. Ramsey confessed that he took part in those murders too, and he named Hale as the leader, along with two other men.

**The sentences.** Ernest Burkhart admitted his guilt in June 1926 and got life in prison for the murder of Bill Smith. Morrison got life that November for killing Anna Brown. Burkhart testified against his uncle and Ramsey. Hale got life in January 1929, and Ramsey got life that November, both for the murder of Henry Roan. Chapter 2 tells how Osage leaders brought in the federal agents who solved the case.

**Not known:** whether Mollie's mother, Lizzie Q. Kyle, was poisoned when she died in July 1921, or by whom. Poison was suspected, but the Oklahoma Historical Society's and the FBI's accounts do not say.
**Disputed:** the number of dead. Writers for the Oklahoma Historical Society count about 24 violent or suspicious deaths, and the FBI's history of the case says at least two dozen. The Osage Nation counts more than 60 murders and suspicious deaths.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The night the lights came on" -->
Fewer than 11 farms in 100 had electricity from a central power station in 1935. A Tennessee farmer, whose name was not recorded, had said in 1930 that "the greatest thing on earth is to have the love of God in your heart." The next greatest thing, he said, "is to have electricity in your house." David Nye records the words in his book *Electrifying America*. In 1984 the national association of electric cooperatives named its history of the program *The Next Greatest Thing*.

**The REA.** President Roosevelt created the Rural Electrification Administration, or REA, on May 11, 1935, with Executive Order 7037, a rule a president issues on his own authority, without a vote of Congress. He signed the Rural Electrification Act on May 20, 1936. Under that law, REA officials made long-term, low-cost loans to cooperatives, businesses owned by the people who use them. Farmers' cooperatives used the loans to build power lines.

Louisan Mamer, who worked for the REA teaching farm families to use electricity in their homes, toured farm towns in the 1930s and 1940s with a traveling show called the electric circus. She showed families electric appliances and how to cook with electricity.

**Pedernales.** In the Texas Hill Country, power first ran through the lines of the Pedernales Electric Cooperative on October 27, 1939. The cooperative had 1,800 miles of line and 422 first customers. Families coming home that night saw their farmhouses lit for the first time. About half of American farms had electricity by 1945, about 9 in 10 by 1950, and about 97 in 100 by 1960.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pearl-yates" name="Pearl Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pearl Yates

> **Who:** A country schoolteacher in western North Carolina who remembered when the lights first came on in her home.
> **When and where:** Haywood County, North Carolina, 1939.

**She and her children walked room to room, looking at the lights.**

Pearl Yates taught high school English in the countryside of western North Carolina. When the electric lights came on in her home, she walked from room to room with her small children. "We'd stop and look and they were so pretty and we'd go look at another room and another room," she said. The power reached her part of Haywood County in July 1939.

**Before the lines.** She cooked on a wood stove, and washing clothes took a whole day, in a large pot. Officials of the power company, Carolina Power and Light, would not run lines into the countryside where the Yates family lived, so people there formed a cooperative. Many neighbors did not trust it at first, because outsiders ran it and members had to pay money up front. Organizers went from door to door and signed many of them up.

Bonnie Bishop interviewed Pearl Yates and her husband, Oral Yates, a former North Carolina state lawmaker, on May 29, 1984. The interview was part of a set recorded for the 50th anniversary of the REA, and it is kept in the Southern Oral History Program at the University of North Carolina.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pearl-yates" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil for a world war" -->
Six of every seven barrels of oil the Allies used in World War II came from the United States. Writers at the American Oil & Gas Historical Society count about 6 billion of the 7 billion barrels.

**Pipelines.** Tanker ships carried oil from Texas along the coast, and German submarines were sinking them, so workers built two pipelines on land. The Big Inch, a pipe 24 inches wide, ran from East Texas to Illinois and on to the East, and it was finished in August 1943. Together with a second line, the Little Big Inch, it carried more than 350 million barrels of oil east before the war in Europe ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 12 minutes**

Splitting atoms became a new way to make American electricity after 1951, when a test reactor in Idaho lit four light bulbs. The first full-scale American plant built only for civilian power began sending electricity into a power company's lines in Pennsylvania in 1957. A reactor at Three Mile Island, also in Pennsylvania, partly melted in 1979, and after that American power companies stopped ordering new reactors.

Americans kept burning more oil and natural gas after World War II, according to the US Energy Information Administration. Officials of the Arab members of OPEC, a group of countries that sell oil, cut off oil sales to the United States in October 1973, and drivers waited in long lines for gasoline. Members of Congress then passed new energy laws and set up a federal Department of Energy, which opened in 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How does a nuclear power plant make electricity?" -->
A nuclear power plant makes electricity from the heat of splitting atoms. Inside the reactor, the machine where the atoms are split, atoms of uranium split in two, and each split gives off a large amount of energy as heat. This splitting is called fission.

The fuel is uranium, a heavy metal dug out of the ground as ore, rock that holds a metal, and it sits in the core, the center of the reactor. The heat boils water into steam. The steam spins the blades of a turbine, a wheel with many blades, and the turbine turns a generator, which turns that motion into electric current.

**Four light bulbs.** A test reactor in Idaho called EBR-1 made the first usable electricity ever produced from nuclear energy on December 20, 1951. It lit four 200-watt light bulbs. The town of Arco, Idaho, about 1,200 people, ran on electricity from a nearby reactor called BORAX III for about an hour on July 17, 1955. Arco was the first town lit only by nuclear power.

**Shippingport.** President Eisenhower gave a speech called "Atoms for Peace" to the General Assembly of the United Nations on December 8, 1953. Work on a nuclear power plant at Shippingport, Pennsylvania, began in 1954. The plant sent its first electricity into the lines of Duquesne Light, a power company, on December 18, 1957. Five days later it reached its full power of 60,000 kilowatts, or 60 million watts. That was enough to light 300,000 bulbs like the ones EBR-1 lit.

Shippingport was the first full-scale nuclear power plant in the United States built only to make electricity for civilian use, meaning not for the military. It was not the first to make nuclear electricity. EBR-1 and smaller American reactors came before it, and so did reactors built for more than one purpose. A British plant, Calder Hall, began sending power in 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Garrison Dam and the Three Affiliated Tribes" -->
Members of Congress paid for Garrison Dam on the Missouri River in North Dakota in 1947. The dam was part of the Pick-Sloan Plan of the Army Corps of Engineers, the part of the Army that builds dams and waterways. Under that plan, the dams were to stop floods and make electricity. The Army Corps was in charge of Garrison Dam, and it was finished in 1953.

**Under the lake.** The dam's lake, Lake Sakakawea, flooded the farmland along the river on the Fort Berthold Reservation, the home of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara, called the Three Affiliated Tribes. It covered the river bottomlands, the low, flat lands beside the river where the tribes farmed. Counts of the flooded land run from 153,000 to about 155,000 acres.

Farms, homes, schools, a hospital, businesses, churches, cemeteries, roads and whole towns went under the water, among them Elbowoods, Sanish and Van Hook. By 1954 the Elbowoods school building stood under 50 feet of water.

**Who had to leave.** Under the federal act for taking the tribes' land, about 300 families had to leave their homes before the lake covered them. By one count, that was more than 90 percent of the tribes' people.

**How the land was taken.** Federal planners had chosen the dam's plan without asking the tribes. In May 1948 tribal leaders signed a contract giving up the tribes' land for $5,105,625. Tribal chairman George Gillette was in tears as he signed. He said the contract broke the Treaty of Fort Laramie, a signed agreement the tribes had made with the United States: "The truth is, as everyone knows, our Treaty of Fort Laramie ... and our constitution are being torn to shreds by this contract."

Members of Congress passed a law in 1949 that set out how the tribes would be paid for their land, and the president signed it. In 1950 the tribes' adults voted on whether to accept the terms of that law. Of the 991 adults, 525 voted yes. Tribal members feared that if they refused, they would be paid even less later.

**Not known:** whether a vote against the terms could have stopped the dam.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black lung and the 1969 law" -->
An explosion at the Consol No. 9 coal mine in Farmington, West Virginia, killed 78 miners on November 20, 1968. Members of Congress then passed the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969, which became law on March 30, 1970. Under it, federal inspectors had to check every underground coal mine four times a year and every surface mine twice a year, and they could close a mine where they found a danger to life. Miners with black lung disease could also get federal payments.

**What black lung does.** Black lung is a group of lung diseases caused by breathing coal mine dust. The body's defending cells try to break down the grains of dust and cannot, so the cells burst and release chemicals that damage the lung. Over the years, scar tissue and hard lumps form in the lungs.

Miners cough up dark, inky phlegm, the thick slime that comes up from the lungs. In the worst form, large masses of scar tissue fill the lungs, and the miner gasps for every breath. According to US Department of Labor figures reported in *Smithsonian* magazine in 2017, more than 76,000 miners had died of black lung since 1968.

Officials of the Mine Safety and Health Administration count the coal miners who died in accidents at work each year. They counted 311 in 1968, the year of Farmington, and 260 in 1970, the year the law took effect. By 2000 the count had fallen to 38, and in 2014 it was 16, the lowest ever recorded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1973: the gasoline runs short" -->
Officials of the Arab members of OPEC announced an embargo, an order that stops trade, on oil sales to the United States in October 1973. They acted because US officials were sending military supplies to Israel in the Yom Kippur War, which began on October 6, 1973, when Egypt and Syria attacked Israel's forces. The embargo lasted until March 1974. In about six months, the price of a barrel of oil rose from about $2.90 to $11.65, about four times as much.

**Long lines.** Gas stations ran out of gasoline, and drivers waited in long lines. Officials in many states let drivers buy gas only on certain days, by license plate number: cars with odd numbers on odd-numbered days, and cars with even numbers on even-numbered days. Members of Congress set a national speed limit of 55 miles an hour, which became law in January 1974. A US Department of Energy report gives the average price of gasoline as 39 cents a gallon in 1973 and 53 cents in 1974.

More energy laws followed. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act became law on December 22, 1975. Under it, federal officials set up the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, a store of crude oil, the oil that comes straight out of the ground, kept for emergencies.

The law also contained the first fuel-economy standards for cars, rules for how far a car must go on a gallon of gas. New cars had to average 27.5 miles per gallon by 1985, about twice what they got then. The federal Department of Energy opened on October 1, 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="david-falconer" name="David Falconer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### David Falconer

> **Who:** A government photographer who recorded Oregon's gasoline shortage in pictures and in his own captions.
> **When and where:** Oregon, October 1973 to May 1974.

**In Portland he photographed a man in line with a gas can.**

David Falconer photographed the gasoline shortage in Oregon and wrote down what he saw. He worked for Project DOCUMERICA, which officials of the Environmental Protection Agency started in 1972 to photograph environmental problems around the country. About 120 photographers took part, and the National Archives keeps Falconer's pictures and captions.

**Closed pumps.** At Sutherlin, on Interstate 5, he photographed closed pumps in October 1973. His caption states, "By midday gasoline was becoming unavailable along interstate highways." That month in Lincoln City, gas stations closed early and stayed closed on weekends, and "No Gas Today" signs went up.

**Portland.** In December 1973 he photographed a man who had run out of gas and was standing in line with a gas can. The man's name was not recorded. By February 1974, drivers there with even-numbered license plates lined up for gas on even-numbered days, Monday through Friday. Anyone could try on Saturday if gas was left, and all Portland stations closed on Sundays.

Falconer wrote that Oregon was the first state to use this odd-even plan. In May 1974, after the embargo had ended, he photographed a sign at a downtown Portland station saying its gas for the day was sold out.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-falconer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What went wrong at Three Mile Island?" -->
A stuck valve let cooling water escape from the Unit 2 reactor at the Three Mile Island power plant in Pennsylvania on March 28, 1979, and the reactor's core overheated and partly melted down. It was the worst accident in American history at a nuclear power plant that sold its electricity. In a meltdown, the reactor's fuel gets so hot that it melts.

**The valve.** General Public Utilities ran the reactor. A small fault in the cooling system came first. Then a relief valve, a valve that opens to let out pressure, stuck open. The operators had no gauge that showed the valve was open, and they believed the system held too much water, so they cut back the water flowing into it. The core lost its water and overheated, and at least 45 percent of it melted.

Governor Dick Thornburgh advised pregnant women and preschool children living within 5 miles to leave on March 30, and he told people within 10 miles to stay indoors. Officials gave out messages that conflicted with each other. About 140,000 people left on their own.

A small amount of radiation escaped. Radiation is energy that substances such as a reactor's fuel give off, and it can damage the cells of the body. The average dose to people within 10 miles was 0.08 millisieverts, about the same as one chest X-ray. A millisievert is a unit that measures how much radiation a body takes in. No one got more than 1 millisievert, about a third of the radiation an average American gets from nature in a year.

Scientists at the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the federal agency that oversees nuclear plants, and more than 12 health studies since 1981, found no health effects they could detect. No deaths are linked to the accident.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the uranium came from" -->
Miners dug uranium on Navajo land for the federal government and later for power plants. Federal officials did not warn them about radon, a gas from the uranium that caused lung cancer. Until 1966 the federal Atomic Energy Commission was the only buyer, and after that uranium was also sold to fuel power plants.

Miners dug nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore from Navajo land from 1944 to 1986, according to the Environmental Protection Agency. About 10,000 people worked in uranium, and about a quarter of them were Navajo.

**No warning.** Uranium gives off radon, a radioactive gas, which means it gives off energy that can damage the cells of the body. Navajo miners say that no one taught them about the dangers of uranium mining, and that no one gave them protective gear or fresh air in the mines.

A federal committee, the Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments, reported in 1995 that "at least several hundred" uranium miners had died of lung cancer from breathing radon. Its members found that federal officials did not require the mine owners to pump fresh air into the mines. They also did not warn the miners.

**Church Rock.** A dam holding radioactive waste, owned by the United Nuclear Corporation, broke near Church Rock, New Mexico, on July 16, 1979, four months after Three Mile Island. About 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid and 1,100 tons of solid waste poured down the Rio Puerco, past about 1,700 Navajo people.

A 2013 study estimated that the spill released 46 curies of radioactivity, against 13 curies for Three Mile Island. Curies measure how much radioactive material was let loose. The millisieverts given for Three Mile Island measure something else: how much radiation a person's body took in. The US Geological Survey called the spill "the largest accidental radioactive release in the United States."

No official declared an emergency or ordered anyone to leave. A New Mexico state agency called the effects on people and the environment "quite limited," but its report also warned that water pumped out of the mines into the river might be a greater danger over time. Governor Bruce King turned down the Navajo Nation's request for a federal disaster declaration. Chapter 13, The Elements, tells the spill in full.

**Not known:** how many of the people living along the river were harmed by the spill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Panels on the White House roof" -->
President Carter held a ceremony for 32 new solar panels on the roof of the West Wing of the White House on June 20, 1979. The panels used sunlight to heat water. They were in use for about seven years. In 1986, while President Reagan was in office, workers took the panels down to repair the roof, and no one put them back.

Some of the panels went to Unity College in Maine in 1991, where workers put 16 of them back up to heat water for the cafeteria. One panel went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History in 2009. Others are at the Carter Library and at a solar museum in Dezhou, China.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 11 minutes**

Natural gas took coal's place as the fuel that makes the most American electricity. Drillers got more and more of that gas by fracking, which cracks rock deep underground to free the gas inside. Natural gas made more electricity than coal over a full year for the first time in 2016, according to the US Energy Information Administration, or EIA. Wind and solar power made a record share of the country's electricity in 2025, about 17 to 18 percent, by EIA figures.

Americans produced more energy than they used in 2019, for the first time since 1957. A winter storm shut down power plants across Texas in February 2021, and more than 4.5 million households lost power. A new nuclear reactor began running in Georgia in 2023, the first new American reactor to start since 2016.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What is fracking?" -->
Fracking, short for hydraulic fracturing, is a way of getting gas and oil out of rock deep underground. Drillers pump water, sand and chemicals down a well at high pressure. The pressure cracks the rock, and the sand holds the cracks open so that gas or oil can flow out. Many of these wells turn and run sideways through the rock for thousands of feet, which is called horizontal drilling.

**From Texas shale.** George Mitchell's company, Mitchell Energy, began working the Barnett Shale, a layer of rock made of hardened mud, in Texas in the early 1980s. Mitchell and his company spent about twenty years trying to get gas out of it. In the late 1990s an engineer, Nick Steinsberger, used a cheaper way of fracking called slickwater fracturing, which cut the cost enough that selling the shale gas made money. In the 2000s drillers joined fracking with horizontal drilling.

American wells produced 16 times as much shale gas in 2023 as in 2007, according to the EIA. By 2018 the United States was the world's largest producer of both natural gas and crude oil.

**The argument.** People disagree about fracking. Supporters say it brought cheaper energy and jobs. They add that natural gas burns cleaner than coal, and that power companies have replaced coal-burning plants with gas-burning ones.

Critics worry that fracking fluid can pollute groundwater and that methane, the main gas in natural gas, leaks from wells and pipes. Earthquakes are a third worry.

Drillers pump wastewater from oil and gas wells deep underground. Scientists at the US Geological Survey wrote in 2017 that the sharp rise in Oklahoma's earthquakes is widely seen as the result of that deep pumping of waste fluids. An earthquake near Pawnee, Oklahoma, measured 5.8 in magnitude, the number that states an earthquake's size, on September 3, 2016. It was the largest ever recorded in the state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mine deaths and black lung" -->
An explosion of gas and coal dust killed 29 miners at the Upper Big Branch mine in Montcoal, West Virginia, on April 5, 2010. It was the worst mine disaster in the United States in 40 years. Performance Coal Company, part of Massey Energy, ran the mine.

The federal Mine Safety and Health Administration's report on the explosion identifies Massey's "corporate culture," its habits and ways of working, as the root cause. Officials of the agency issued 369 citations and orders, written notices of broken safety rules, with fines of $10.8 million.

**The trial.** Massey's former chief executive, Don Blankenship, was convicted in December 2015 of conspiring, or planning with others, to break federal mine safety laws. It was a misdemeanor, a less serious kind of crime. Judge Irene Berger gave him the longest sentence the law allowed on April 6, 2016: one year in prison and a $250,000 fine. A year of supervised release followed, when a court officer checks on him. He was not convicted of causing the miners' deaths.

Black lung came back among coal miners in the 2000s. Researchers at NIOSH, the federal agency that studies workers' health, found 60 cases of the worst form of the disease in 20 months at one small clinic in Kentucky in 2016. Reporters at National Public Radio found 962 cases of advanced black lung in the 2010s, more than double the 441 cases NIOSH had recorded in the 40 years before. *Smithsonian Magazine* reported both counts in 2017.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standing Rock and the pipeline" -->
Pumping more oil also meant moving it. A company called Energy Transfer Partners built the Dakota Access pipeline to carry crude oil from the Bakken oil field in North Dakota to Illinois. Members of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe opposed it in 2016 where it crosses under Lake Oahe, upstream of their reservation. The lake is the tribe's water supply, and they feared that a spill would pollute it.

Protest camps began in April 2016. Army Corps of Engineers officials denied the easement for the crossing, the permission to cross federal land under the lake, on December 4, 2016. President Trump ordered the Army Corps to review and approve the easement quickly on January 24, 2017. Army Corps officials approved it on February 8, 2017, and oil moved through the pipeline later that year. The tribe's lawsuits continued.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Natural gas, wind and solar, 2016 to 2025" -->
Natural gas passed coal for a full year for the first time in 2016, when it made 34 percent of American electricity, according to the EIA. By 2025 it made about 41 percent, more than twice coal's share of about 17 percent. Nuclear power made about 18 percent.

**Renewables.** Wind, solar, flowing water and other sources that do not run out are called renewable energy. In 2025 they made about 24 percent of American electricity: wind about 11 percent, solar about 7 and hydropower, from flowing water, about 6.

Wind and solar together made a record share. The EIA's yearly report puts it at about 17 percent, but its rounded shares for wind and solar add up to 18, so the share was about 17 to 18 percent.

Analysts at the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics expect wind turbine service technicians to be the fastest-growing job in the country from 2024 to 2034, up 49.9 percent. Solar panel installers come second, up 42.1 percent. The two jobs together are projected to add fewer than 20,000 jobs.

**Electric cars.** Americans also began buying cars that run partly or fully on electricity: hybrids, which have both a gasoline engine and an electric motor, and battery electric cars, which run on electricity alone. According to the EIA, about 20 percent of the new cars, SUVs and pickups sold in 2024 were hybrids or electric, and in 2025 the share rose to about 22 percent.

But 2025 was also the first year that sales of battery electric cars fell. Federal tax credits for buyers of these cars, money taken off the buyer's tax bill, ended on September 30, 2025. In each of the months left in 2025, battery electric cars made up less than 6 percent of sales.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did the Texas lights go out in 2021?" -->
Texas power plants of every kind broke down in the cold during a winter storm named Uri in February 2021. To keep the state's whole power grid from failing, the people who run it cut off power to millions of customers. More than 4.5 million households, about 10 million people, lost power, some of them for days. Reports on the failure by federal energy regulators and by researchers at the University of Texas give these figures, and so do news reports.

**The grid.** The Electric Reliability Council of Texas, called ERCOT, runs the Texas grid, the network of power plants and wires that serves the state. Some of its gas, coal, nuclear and wind plants stopped working. ERCOT's operators cut 20,000 megawatts, a megawatt being a million watts. That was more than 300 times the 60 megawatts the Shippingport nuclear plant made in 1957, and the largest cutoff of its kind in American history.

ERCOT officials later said the grid came within 4 minutes and 37 seconds of a total collapse. Texas state health officials counted 246 deaths from the storm in 2022, but others have argued that the official count is much too low. Reporters at BuzzFeed News compared the number of Texans who died in the week of the worst blackouts with the number who would normally die. They estimated that about 700 people were killed by the storm in that week alone. Damage estimates run above $195 billion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cristian-pavon-pineda" name="Cristian Pavón Pineda" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Cristian Pavón Pineda

> **Who:** An 11-year-old sixth grader who died in his family's mobile home during the Texas blackout.
> **When and where:** Conroe, Texas, February 16, 2021.

**He had seen snow for the first time the day before.**

Cristian Pavón Pineda was 11 years old when he died in his family's mobile home in Conroe, Texas, on February 16, 2021. The home had been without power since the Sunday before, and the temperature there fell to about 10 degrees. Al Día News reported that he had seen snow for the first time the day before he died.

The family of five slept in one room to keep warm. Cristian shared a bed with his younger brother. His aunt, Jaliza Yera, told a TV station that he went to bed in "a shirt, a sweater, two pairs of pants, socks on." His stepfather put another blanket over him. In the morning his family could not wake him.

**The cause.** Early reports, and his family's lawsuit, said he died of hypothermia, which means the body's temperature has fallen too low to keep a person alive. But an autopsy, an examination of a body to find the cause of death, found on February 18, 2021, that he died of carbon monoxide poisoning, Conroe police said in May 2021.

Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. Across Texas, more than 30 people had died in the storm by February 21, Al Día News reported. Many of them died of carbon monoxide poisoning after using cars or generators to keep warm.

**The lawsuit.** His mother, Maria Elisa Pineda Guzman, sued ERCOT and Entergy Texas, the power company, for more than $100 million on February 22, 2021. Her lawyer was Tony Buzbee. Officials had called the power cuts temporary rolling outages, planned cuts that move from area to area. The suit states that ERCOT and Entergy Texas did not give his family good enough information about how long the outages would last.

"Accurate information might have saved Cristian Pineda's young life," the suit states. In a statement, ERCOT officials called his death a "tragedy" and said they were "confident that our grid operators made the right choice to avoid a state-wide blackout."

**Not known:** where the carbon monoxide in the home came from, and how the lawsuit ended. Published reports do not say.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New and restarted reactors" -->
Two new nuclear reactors at the Vogtle plant in Georgia began selling power in 2023 and 2024: Unit 3 on July 31, 2023, and Unit 4 on April 29, 2024, according to the EIA. Unit 3 was the first new reactor to start in the United States since Watts Bar Unit 2 in Tennessee, owned by the TVA, began running in 2016.

Work on Watts Bar 2 had begun in 1973. Workers stopped building it in 1985 and started again in 2007, so the two Vogtle reactors were the first American reactors begun after the 1970s to start running.

**Palisades.** The owners of two closed plants plan to run them again. Holtec International owns the Palisades plant in Michigan, which shut down in May 2022, and it got a $1.52 billion federal loan to restart it. Its targets of 2025 and early 2026 passed without a restart.

Workers began loading fuel into the reactor in late August 2026. On August 30 a fuel assembly, a tall bundle of fuel rods, tilted while it was being loaded, and the loading was paused. Officials of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the federal agency that oversees nuclear plants, began a review, and their first results showed no radiation had escaped and no workers were harmed. Holtec's officials had given no date for the restart as of late September 2026, a Michigan TV station reported.

**Three Mile Island again.** Three Mile Island Unit 1 is the reactor that did not melt down in 1979, and it closed in 2019. It now carries the name Christopher M. Crane Clean Energy Center. Leaders of its owner, Constellation Energy, signed a 20-year agreement to sell its power to Microsoft. Members of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission are expected to decide in May 2027 whether to give Constellation an operating license, permission to run the reactor, the news outlet WITF reported in July 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What energy independence means" -->
Americans produced more energy than they used in 2019, for the first time since 1957, according to the EIA. They produced less than 1 percent more than they used.

People call this energy independence: Americans produce more energy in total than they use. It does not mean Americans stopped buying fuel from other countries. In November 2019 Americans still bought nearly twice as much crude oil from other countries as they sold to them, by EIA figures.

Americans sold a record amount of energy to other countries in 2025, about one and a half times as much as they bought from them, the EIA reported in May 2026. Oil and products made from it were 63 percent of the energy sold abroad, and natural gas was 29 percent.

**The three questions, answered**
- Did kerosene really save the whales? No. Whaling was shrinking before kerosene came, and in most homes kerosene replaced camphene, after a Civil War tax on alcohol raised camphene's price.
- How did electricity reach almost every American home? Edison's Pearl Street station began selling current in 1882, and alternating current, which a transformer could raise or lower, carried power from Niagara Falls to Buffalo by 1896. After 1935 federal loans paid for farmers' own power lines, and by 1950, 94 households in 100 had electricity.
- Who paid for America's energy with their freedom, their land or their lives? Enslaved people fed the iron furnaces, at least 271 at Catoctin alone, and about 105,000 coal miners died in accidents from 1900 through 2025. Federal officials made 82,000 people in the Tennessee Valley leave their homes for dams, other dams flooded Kettle Falls and the Fort Berthold farmlands, and husbands, guardians and others murdered Osage people for their oil money.

**What changed:** Americans went from fires of wood to coal, oil, natural gas, the atom, the wind and the sun.

**Five dates to keep**
- February 11, 1808: Jesse Fell burns hard coal in his tavern fireplace.
- August 27, 1859: Drake's crew strikes oil in Pennsylvania.
- September 4, 1882: Edison's Pearl Street station starts selling electricity.
- December 6, 1907: The Monongah explosions kill at least 362 miners.
- 2016: Natural gas passes coal in making American electricity.

**Not known:** The surviving records of the early colonial iron furnaces list the owners, not the men who made the charcoal.

**Where to go:** The *Charles W. Morgan*, the last wooden whaling ship left in the world, is kept at the Mystic Seaport Museum in Mystic, Connecticut.
