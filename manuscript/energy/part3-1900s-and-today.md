## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1950, electric wires reached almost every American home. The census is the government's count of the people and their homes, taken every ten years. In 1890 about 8 in 100 households had electricity. By 1950, 94 in 100 did, according to a 2008 study by Emanuela Cardia that used census figures. Most farms got their wires only after 1935.

Coal still heated most homes. The 1940 census found that more than half of American homes were heated mainly with coal, and about a quarter with wood.

In 1901 a single well in Texas began to pour out an estimated 100,000 barrels of oil a day. In World War II, most of the oil burned by the Allies, the countries fighting Germany, Italy and Japan, came from the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spindletop, 1901" -->
On January 10, 1901, a well on Spindletop hill, about 3 miles south of Beaumont, Texas, struck oil 1,139 feet down. Oil shot more than 100 feet into the air. The well poured out an estimated 100,000 barrels a day until workers capped it nine days later, on January 19. In the whole year of 1900, all of Texas had produced 836,000 barrels. At the estimated rate, the Spindletop well poured out about 900,000 barrels in its nine days, more than that whole year's total.

Anthony F. Lucas led the drilling. According to the Texas State Historical Association, he was the leading American expert on salt domes, large masses of salt buried deep underground. The well stood on a piece of land known as the McFaddin-Wiess and Kyle land. Several oil companies started at Spindletop or grew large there. Among them were the Texas Company, later called Texaco, and Gulf Oil.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What coal cost the miners, 1900 to 1950" -->
Coal miners died at work by the thousands in the early 1900s. Officials of the federal Mine Safety and Health Administration keep a count for every year. Federal means belonging to the national government. In 1900, 1,489 coal miners died in accidents at work. The worst year was 1907, when 3,242 died. In 1930, 2,063 died, and in 1950, 643. From 1900 through 2025, the yearly counts add up to about 105,000 coal miners who died in accidents. The counts do not include miners who died of disease from their work.

On December 6, 1907, explosions in mines No. 6 and No. 8 at Monongah, West Virginia, killed 362 men by the official count. It is the worst mine disaster in American history. Later estimates run above 500. The explosion destroyed the system that kept track of which miners were underground, so no one could make a full count. Some of the dead were boys as young as eight. Mine companies of the time hired more workers than they listed, and their lists left out many of the children who worked underground. The dead included immigrants from Italy, Poland and Hungary, and Black workers. About 1,000 children lost a father.

Accounts differ about which company owned the mines. One names the Fairmont Coal Company, and another names the Consolidation Coal Company. The Fairmont company's president, C. W. Watson, said the explosion "could only have been caused by the careless use of a mining lamp." Experts at the time disagreed about the cause. Some blamed torn electrical wires, and others blamed lamps.

Clio, a history website run from Marshall University, states that a loaded coal car broke loose near the top of a trestle, a raised track, and ran back into mine No. 6. The histories of the disaster by Clio and by the West Virginia Mine Wars Museum do not name anyone charged with a crime. After Monongah, members of Congress, the lawmakers who make the nation's laws, created the federal Bureau of Mines.

In 1914 Colorado National Guard soldiers killed striking coal miners and their families at Ludlow, Colorado. The National Guard is a state's own force of part-time soldiers. The strike had begun in 1913. That year the coal miners of southern Colorado went on strike, which means they stopped work to demand changes. The coal they mined supplied Colorado's steel mill and its railroads. Most of them worked for the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, and its main owner was John D. Rockefeller Jr.

The miners wanted the company's owners to obey Colorado's mining laws. They also wanted the owners to accept their union, the United Mine Workers. A union is a group that workers form so they can bargain with owners together. The strikers and their families lived in tent camps. Governor Elias M. Ammons sent the Colorado National Guard, and the mine owners agreed to pay for it.

On April 20, 1914, fighting broke out at the tent camp at Ludlow. No one knows who fired the first shot. Guardsmen burned the tents. At least 19 people were killed: one guardsman, five miners, and thirteen women and children. The women and children had hidden in a pit under a tent. Fumes from the burning filled the pit, and they died because they could not breathe.

Guardsmen killed three strike leaders who were under a flag of truce, a white flag that asks for a pause in fighting. They shot the strike leader Louis Tikas, a Greek American, three times in the back.

In the days after, miners attacked mines across the region and killed mine bosses and guards. No one knows how many people died in those days, but more died than at Ludlow. President Woodrow Wilson sent the US Army on April 28. The strike ended in December 1914, and the miners won none of the union's goals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="samuel-insull" name="Samuel Insull" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Samuel Insull

> **Who:** Thomas Edison's private secretary, who later built a group of more than 85 electric companies that served millions of people. It collapsed in 1932.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1892 to 1932. Died in Paris in 1938.

Samuel Insull built a chain of electric companies that served millions of people, and the chain collapsed in 1932. He had worked for Thomas Edison as his private secretary. In 1892 Insull took over the Chicago Edison company, and he built it into Commonwealth Edison.

Insull tied together more than 85 companies through holding companies. A holding company is a company that owns shares, or pieces of ownership, of other companies. Insull's companies owned shares in one another, and they had borrowed much of the money behind them. Many small investors bought shares in his companies.

In 1932, during the Great Depression, Insull's companies failed, and investors lost millions of dollars. Insull fled to Europe. In October 1932 a grand jury in Chicago charged him with crimes. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof for a trial. He moved to Greece, and officials there refused to send him back. In 1934, after US officials pressed them, Greek officials told him to leave. Officials of Turkey arrested him, and US State Department officers brought him back to the United States to stand trial.

He was tried three times: for fraud, which means cheating people by lying, for embezzlement, and for breaking federal bankruptcy law. Embezzlement means stealing money that other people trusted you to handle. Bankruptcy law sets the rules for what happens when a person or a company cannot pay its debts. He was found not guilty all three times.

In 1932 Franklin Roosevelt, running for president, named Insull in a campaign speech. In 1935 members of Congress passed the Public Utility Holding Company Act. They wrote it to stop anyone from building another chain of electric companies like Insull's.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="samuel-insull" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rivers into power, and who lost their homes" -->
From 1933 to 1945, federal officials had large dams built to make electricity. In the Tennessee Valley alone, they made an estimated 82,000 people leave their homes to do it. A hydroelectric dam holds back a river. Water rushing down through the dam turns machines that make electricity.

On May 18, 1933, President Franklin Roosevelt signed the law that created the Tennessee Valley Authority, or TVA. The TVA was a federal corporation, a company owned by the national government. Its board and staff ran it, and TVA officials had dams built in the Tennessee Valley. Before the TVA, about 3 in 100 families in the valley had electricity. Work on the first TVA dam, Norris Dam, began in October 1933.

From 1933 to 1945, TVA officials bought or took about 1.3 million acres from their owners. They made those 82,000 people leave to make room for 16 hydroelectric dams. For Norris Dam alone, TVA officials made more than 3,000 families leave. TVA lawyer John Snyder described how the land was taken: "a price is set and we buy at that price or we condemn." To condemn land is to take it by law without the owner's agreement, at a price the government sets. TVA's own economists estimated that condemning land got it for about 60 percent of what a private buyer would pay. Wayne Moore's 1995 history of the TVA land-taking does not name one official who made these choices. The TVA's board and staff made them together.

On October 9, 1936, the first generator at Hoover Dam began sending electricity over 266 miles of wire to Los Angeles. Some records date the start to October 26, 1936, the day that generator reached full operation.

Officials of the federal Bureau of Reclamation had Grand Coulee Dam built on the Columbia River, and it was finished in 1941. The dam stands 550 feet high, and its builders gave it no fish ladders. A fish ladder is a set of rising pools that lets salmon swim upstream past a dam. The dam ended the runs of salmon and of steelhead, a trout that swims to the sea and back, in the whole upper Columbia Basin. The lake behind the dam flooded Kettle Falls, where Native people had fished for salmon for about 9,000 years. The catch there had averaged about 644,500 fish a year. Native people also lost thousands of acres of low, flat land beside the river where they grew food. They lost their way to the places where they gathered roots and berries.

In June 1940, as the dam neared completion, the Colville Confederated Tribes held a three-day Ceremony of Tears to mourn the loss of the fishery at Kettle Falls. Between 8,000 and 10,000 people came, from the Spokane, Yakima, Nez Perce, Coeur d'Alene and other nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Osage oil" -->
Some of the oil Americans burned in the 1920s came from under the Osage Nation's land in Oklahoma. Under the Osage Allotment Act of 1906, the Osage kept the rights to the oil and minerals under their land, owned in common. Each Osage person with a share, called a headright, received part of the oil money. Under a law passed in 1921, local judges put many Osage adults' money under the control of white guardians, people the judges chose to handle it.

From about 1920 to 1925, husbands, guardians and others killed Osage headright holders to inherit their shares of the oil money. William Hale, a cattleman, ordered the murders of one family so that his nephew, Ernest Burkhart, could inherit their headrights. Burkhart was married to Mollie Burkhart. Federal agents later proved that Hale ordered the murders.

In May 1921 Kelsie Morrison shot Mollie's sister Anna Brown. He later admitted that he killed her at Hale's request. Their mother, Lizzie Q. Kyle, died in July 1921, and poison was suspected. The accounts of the Oklahoma Historical Society and the FBI do not say whether she was poisoned, or by whom. A cowboy named John Ramsey admitted that Hale hired him to kill their cousin Henry Roan. Roan was shot. Those two accounts do not say who fired the shot. On March 10, 1923, a bomb blew up the home of Mollie's sister Rita Smith. It killed Rita, her husband, Bill Smith, and Nettie Brookshire, who worked for them. Ramsey confessed that he took part in those murders. He named Hale as the leader, along with two other men, Henry Grammar and Asa Kirby.

Burkhart admitted his guilt in June 1926 and got life in prison. Morrison got life in November 1926. Hale got life in January 1929, and Ramsey got life that November. Hale and Ramsey went to the federal prison at Leavenworth.

The counts of the dead differ. Writers for the Oklahoma Historical Society count about 24 violent or suspicious deaths, and the FBI's history of the case says at least two dozen. The Osage Nation counts more than 60 murders and suspicious deaths.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The night the lights came on" -->
In 1935 fewer than 11 farms in 100 had electricity from a central power station. Most farms got their wires after federal officials began lending money to farmers' cooperatives to build power lines.

On May 11, 1935, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 7037, which created the Rural Electrification Administration, or REA. An executive order is a rule that a president issues on his own authority, without a vote of Congress. On May 20, 1936, Roosevelt signed the Rural Electrification Act. Under that law, REA officials made long-term, low-cost loans to cooperatives owned by farmers. A cooperative is a business owned by the people who use it. The farmers' cooperatives used the loans to build power lines. About half of American farms had electricity by 1945, about 9 in 10 by 1950, and about 97 in 100 by 1960.

In 1930 a Tennessee farmer, whose name was not recorded, said: "The greatest thing on earth is to have the love of God in your heart, and the next greatest thing is to have electricity in your house." David Nye records the words in his book *Electrifying America*. In 1984 the national association of electric cooperatives named its history of the program *The Next Greatest Thing*.

In the 1930s and 1940s, Louisan Mamer, who worked for the REA teaching farm families to use electricity in their homes, toured farm towns with a traveling show called the electric circus. She showed families electric appliances and how to cook with electricity.

In the Texas Hill Country, power first ran through the lines of the Pedernales Electric Cooperative on October 27, 1939. The cooperative had 1,800 miles of line and 422 first customers. Families coming home that night saw their farmhouses lit for the first time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pearl-yates" name="Pearl Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Pearl Yates

> **Who:** A high school English teacher in the countryside of western North Carolina. Electricity reached her area in July 1939, and she told about it in an interview in 1984.
> **When and where:** Haywood County, North Carolina, 1939.

Pearl Yates walked from room to room with her small children when the electric lights came on in her home. "We'd stop and look and they were so pretty and we'd go look at another room and another room," she said. The power reached her part of Haywood County, North Carolina, in July 1939.

Before then, she cooked on a wood stove. Washing clothes took a whole day, in a large pot. Officials of the power company, Carolina Power and Light, would not run lines into the countryside where the Yates family lived. So people there formed a cooperative. Many neighbors did not trust it at first, because outsiders ran it and members had to pay money up front. Organizers went from door to door and signed many of them up.

On May 29, 1984, Bonnie Bishop interviewed Pearl Yates and her husband, Oral Yates. Oral Yates had worked as a teacher and for the railroad, and he had served as a state representative and a state senator, a lawmaker for North Carolina. The interview was part of a set recorded for the 50th anniversary of the REA. It is kept in the Southern Oral History Program at the University of North Carolina.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pearl-yates" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil for a world war" -->
The Allies used about 7 billion barrels of oil in World War II, and about 6 billion of those barrels came from the United States. Writers at the American Oil & Gas Historical Society give these figures.

Tanker ships carried oil from Texas along the coast, and German submarines were sinking them. So workers built two pipelines on land. The Big Inch, a pipe 24 inches wide, ran from East Texas to Illinois and on to the East. It was finished in August 1943. Together with a second line, the Little Big Inch, it carried more than 350 million barrels of oil east before the war in Europe ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Nuclear power plants began making electricity in the United States in the 1950s. In 1957 the first full-scale American plant built only for civilian power began sending electricity into a power company's lines in Pennsylvania. In 1979 a reactor at Three Mile Island, also in Pennsylvania, partly melted. After that, American power companies stopped ordering new reactors.

Americans kept burning more oil and natural gas after World War II, according to the US Energy Information Administration. In October 1973 officials of the Arab members of OPEC, a group of countries that sell oil, cut off oil sales to the United States. Drivers waited in long lines for gasoline. Members of Congress then passed new energy laws and set up a federal Department of Energy, which opened in 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Electricity from the atom" -->
A nuclear power plant makes electricity from uranium, a heavy metal dug out of the ground as ore, which is rock that holds a metal. The fuel sits in the core, the center of the plant's reactor. A reactor is the machine where the atoms are split. Inside it, atoms of uranium split in two, and each split gives off a large amount of energy as heat. This splitting is called fission. The heat boils water into steam. The steam spins the blades of a turbine, a wheel with many blades. The turbine turns a generator, a machine that turns motion into electric current.

A test reactor in Idaho called EBR-1 made the first usable electricity ever produced from nuclear energy, on December 20, 1951. It lit four 200-watt light bulbs. On July 17, 1955, the town of Arco, Idaho, about 1,200 people, ran on electricity from a nearby reactor called BORAX III for about an hour. Arco was the first town lit only by nuclear power.

On December 8, 1953, President Eisenhower gave a speech called "Atoms for Peace" to the General Assembly of the United Nations. Work on a nuclear power plant at Shippingport, Pennsylvania, began in 1954. On December 18, 1957, the plant sent its first electricity into the lines of Duquesne Light, a power company. On December 23 it reached its full power of 60,000 kilowatts. A kilowatt is 1,000 watts. At 200 watts a bulb, 60,000 kilowatts could light 300,000 bulbs like the ones EBR-1 lit.

Shippingport was the first full-scale nuclear power plant in the United States built only to make electricity for civilian use, meaning not for the military. It was not the first to make nuclear electricity. EBR-1 and smaller American reactors came before it, and so did reactors built for more than one purpose. A British plant, Calder Hall, began sending power in 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Garrison Dam and the Three Affiliated Tribes" -->
In 1947 members of Congress paid for Garrison Dam on the Missouri River in North Dakota. The dam was part of the Pick-Sloan Plan of the Army Corps of Engineers, the part of the Army that builds dams and waterways. Under that plan, the dams were to stop floods and make electricity. The Army Corps was in charge of Garrison Dam, and it was finished in 1953. Its lake flooded the farmland along the river on the Fort Berthold Reservation. The reservation is the home of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara, called the Three Affiliated Tribes.

The lake behind the dam, Lake Sakakawea, covered the river bottomlands where the tribes farmed. Bottomlands are the low, flat lands beside a river. Counts of the flooded land run from 153,000 to about 155,000 acres. Farms, homes, schools, a hospital, businesses, churches, cemeteries, roads and whole towns went under the water. The drowned towns included Elbowoods, Sanish and Van Hook. By 1954 the Elbowoods school building stood under 50 feet of water.

Under the federal act for taking the tribes' land, about 300 families had to leave their homes before the lake covered them. By one count, that was more than 90 percent of the tribes' people. In 1948, at the signing of the contract for the tribes' land, tribal chairman George Gillette spoke about the Treaty of Fort Laramie. A treaty is a signed agreement between nations, and the tribes had made this one with the United States. Gillette said: "The truth is, as everyone knows, our Treaty of Fort Laramie ... and our constitution are being torn to shreds by this contract." In 1950, 525 of the tribes' 991 adults voted to accept the terms of the government's act for taking the land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black lung and the 1969 law" -->
An explosion at the Consol No. 9 coal mine in Farmington, West Virginia, killed 78 miners on November 20, 1968. Members of Congress then passed the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969, which became law on March 30, 1970. Under it, federal inspectors had to check every underground coal mine four times a year and every surface mine twice a year. Inspectors could close a mine where they found a danger to life. Under the law, miners with black lung disease could also get federal payments.

Black lung is a group of lung diseases caused by breathing coal mine dust. The body's defending cells try to break down the grains of dust and cannot. The cells burst and release chemicals that damage the lung. Over the years, scar tissue and hard lumps form in the lungs. Miners cough up dark, inky phlegm, the thick slime that comes up from the lungs. In the worst form, large masses of scar tissue fill the lungs, and the miner gasps for every breath. According to US Department of Labor figures reported in *Smithsonian* magazine in 2017, more than 76,000 miners had died of black lung since 1968.

Officials of the Mine Safety and Health Administration count the coal miners who died in accidents at work each year. They counted 311 in 1968, 203 in 1969, 260 in 1970, 133 in 1980 and 38 in 2000. In 2014 the count was 16, the lowest ever recorded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1973: the gasoline runs short" -->
In October 1973 officials of the Arab members of OPEC, a group of countries that sell oil, announced an embargo on oil sales to the United States. An embargo is an order that stops trade. They acted because US officials were sending military supplies to Israel in the Yom Kippur War. That war began on October 6, 1973, when Egypt and Syria attacked Israel's forces. The embargo lasted until March 1974. In about six months, the price of a barrel of oil rose from about $2.90 to $11.65, about four times as much.

Gas stations ran out of gasoline, and drivers waited in long lines. Officials in many states let drivers buy gas only on certain days, by license plate number. Cars with odd numbers could buy on odd-numbered days, and cars with even numbers on even-numbered days. Members of Congress set a national speed limit of 55 miles an hour, and it became law in January 1974. A US Department of Energy report gives the average price of gasoline as 39 cents a gallon in 1973 and 53 cents in 1974.

More energy laws followed. On December 22, 1975, the Energy Policy and Conservation Act became law. Under it, federal officials set up the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, a store of crude oil kept for emergencies. Crude oil is oil just as it comes out of the ground. The law also contained the first fuel-economy standards for cars, rules for how far a car must go on a gallon of gas. New cars had to average 27.5 miles per gallon by 1985, about twice what they got then. The federal Department of Energy opened on October 1, 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="david-falconer" name="David Falconer" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### David Falconer

> **Who:** A photographer for the Environmental Protection Agency's DOCUMERICA project. He photographed Oregon's gasoline shortage and wrote captions for his pictures.
> **When and where:** Oregon, October 1973 to May 1974.

David Falconer photographed the gasoline shortage in Oregon and wrote down what he saw. He worked for Project DOCUMERICA. Officials of the Environmental Protection Agency started the project in 1972 to photograph environmental problems around the country. About 120 photographers took part. The National Archives keeps Falconer's pictures and captions.

At Sutherlin, on Interstate 5, he photographed closed pumps in October 1973. His caption states, "By midday gasoline was becoming unavailable along interstate highways." That month in Lincoln City, gas stations closed early and stayed closed on weekends, and "No Gas Today" signs went up.

He took more pictures in Portland. In December 1973 he photographed a man who had run out of gas and was standing in line with a gas can. The man's name was not recorded. By February 1974, drivers there with even-numbered license plates lined up for gas on even-numbered days, Monday through Friday. Anyone could try on Saturday if gas was left, and all Portland stations closed on Sundays. Falconer wrote that Oregon was the first state to use this odd-even plan. In May 1974, after the embargo had ended, he photographed a sign at a downtown Portland station saying its gas for the day was sold out.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-falconer" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three Mile Island, 1979" -->
The Unit 2 reactor at the Three Mile Island power plant in Pennsylvania partly melted down on March 28, 1979. It was the worst accident in American history at a nuclear power plant that sold its electricity. In a meltdown, the reactor's fuel gets so hot that it melts.

General Public Utilities ran the reactor. A small fault in the cooling system came first. Then a relief valve, a valve that opens to let out pressure, stuck open and let cooling water escape. The operators had no gauge that showed the valve was open. They believed the system held too much water, so they cut back the water flowing into it. The core lost its water and overheated. At least 45 percent of the core melted.

On March 30, Governor Dick Thornburgh advised pregnant women and preschool children living within 5 miles to leave. He told people within 10 miles to stay indoors. Officials gave out messages that conflicted with each other. About 140,000 people left on their own.

A small amount of radiation escaped. The average dose to people within 10 miles was 0.08 millisieverts, and no one got more than 1 millisievert. A millisievert is a unit that measures how much radiation a body takes in. Scientists at the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and more than 12 health studies since 1981, found no health effects they could detect. No deaths are linked to the accident.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the uranium came from" -->
Miners dug uranium on Navajo land for the federal government and later for power plants. Federal officials did not warn uranium miners about radon, a gas from the uranium that caused lung cancer. From 1944 to 1986, miners dug nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore from Navajo land, according to the Environmental Protection Agency. About 10,000 people worked in uranium, and about a quarter of them were Navajo. Until 1966 the federal Atomic Energy Commission was the only buyer. After that, uranium was also sold to fuel power plants.

Uranium gives off radon, a radioactive gas. Something radioactive gives off energy that can damage the cells of the body. Navajo miners say that no one taught them about the dangers of uranium mining. They say no one gave them protective gear or fresh air in the mines. In 1995 a federal committee, the Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments, reported that "at least several hundred" uranium miners had died of lung cancer from breathing radon. The committee found that federal officials did not require the mine owners to pump fresh air into the mines. They also did not warn the miners.

On July 16, 1979, four months after Three Mile Island, a dam owned by the United Nuclear Corporation, holding radioactive waste, broke. It stood near Church Rock, New Mexico. About 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid and 1,100 tons of solid waste poured down the Rio Puerco, past about 1,700 Navajo people. A 2013 study estimated that the spill released 46 curies of radiation. It estimated 13 curies for Three Mile Island. A curie is a unit that measures radioactivity. A New Mexico state agency called the effects "quite limited." Governor Bruce King turned down the Navajo Nation's request for a federal disaster declaration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Panels on the White House roof" -->
President Carter held a ceremony for 32 new solar panels on the roof of the West Wing of the White House on June 20, 1979. The panels used sunlight to heat water. They were in use for about seven years. In 1986, while President Reagan was in office, workers took the panels down to repair the roof, and no one put them back.

In 1991 some of the panels went to Unity College in Maine. Workers there put 16 of them back up to heat water for the cafeteria. In 2009 one panel went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. Others are at the Carter Library and at a solar museum in Dezhou, China.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have changed the fuels they burn to make electricity. In 2016, for the first time over a full year, natural gas made more American electricity than coal, according to the US Energy Information Administration, or EIA. Drillers got more and more of that gas by fracking, which cracks rock deep underground to free the gas inside. Wind and solar power made a record share of the country's electricity in 2025, about 17 to 18 percent, by EIA figures.

In 2019 Americans produced more energy than they used, for the first time since 1957. In February 2021 a winter storm shut down power plants across Texas, and more than 4.5 million households lost power. In 2023 a new nuclear reactor began running in Georgia. It was the first new American reactor to start since 2016.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fracking" -->
After drillers began fracking shale, a rock made of hardened mud, American wells produced more natural gas and then more crude oil than the wells of any other country. Fracking is short for hydraulic fracturing. Drillers pump water, sand and chemicals down a well at high pressure. The pressure cracks the rock, and the sand holds the cracks open so that gas or oil can flow out. Many of these wells turn and run sideways through the rock for thousands of feet. That is called horizontal drilling.

In the early 1980s, George Mitchell's company, Mitchell Energy, began working a layer of shale in Texas called the Barnett Shale. Mitchell and his company spent about twenty years trying to get gas out of it. In the late 1990s an engineer, Nick Steinsberger, used a cheaper way of fracking called slickwater fracturing. It cut the cost enough that selling the shale gas made money. In the 2000s drillers joined fracking with horizontal drilling.

American wells produced about 5 billion cubic feet of shale gas a day in 2007 and about 80 billion cubic feet a day in 2023, according to the EIA. In 2011 American wells became the world's largest producers of natural gas. In 2018 they passed Russia's wells to become the world's largest producers of crude oil, the EIA reports. In 2025 American wells pumped a record 13.6 million barrels of crude oil a day, by EIA figures published in 2026.

People disagree about fracking. Supporters say it brought cheaper energy and jobs. They add that natural gas burns cleaner than coal, and power companies have replaced coal-burning plants with gas-burning ones.

Critics worry that fracking fluid can pollute groundwater and that methane, the main gas in natural gas, leaks from wells and pipes. Earthquakes are a third concern. Drillers pump wastewater from oil and gas wells deep underground. In 2017, scientists at the US Geological Survey wrote that the sharp rise in Oklahoma's earthquakes is widely seen as the result of that deep pumping of waste fluids. On September 3, 2016, an earthquake near Pawnee, Oklahoma, measured magnitude 5.8, the largest ever recorded in the state. Magnitude is a number that states the size of an earthquake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mine deaths, black lung and a pipeline" -->
An explosion of gas and coal dust killed 29 miners at the Upper Big Branch mine in Montcoal, West Virginia, on April 5, 2010. It was the worst mine disaster in the United States in 40 years. Performance Coal Company, part of Massey Energy, ran the mine. The report on the explosion by the federal Mine Safety and Health Administration identifies Massey's "corporate culture" as the root cause. A company's culture is the set of habits and ways of working inside it. Officials of the Mine Safety and Health Administration issued 369 citations and orders, which are written notices of broken safety rules, with fines of $10.8 million.

In December 2015 Massey's former chief executive, Don Blankenship, was convicted of conspiring, or planning with others, to break federal mine safety laws. It was a misdemeanor, a less serious crime than a felony. On April 6, 2016, Judge Irene Berger gave him the longest sentence the law allowed. He got one year in prison and a $250,000 fine. After prison came a year of supervised release, when a court officer checks on him. He was not convicted of causing the miners' deaths.

Black lung came back among coal miners in the 2000s. In 2016 researchers at NIOSH, the federal agency that studies workers' health, found 60 cases of the worst form of the disease in 20 months at one small clinic in Kentucky. Reporters at National Public Radio found 962 cases of advanced black lung in the 2010s. That was more than double the 441 cases NIOSH had recorded in the 40 years before. *Smithsonian Magazine* reported both counts in 2017.

In 2016 members of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe opposed the Dakota Access oil pipeline where it crosses under Lake Oahe. The lake is the tribe's water supply, upstream of its reservation. Protest camps began in April 2016. On December 4, 2016, Army Corps of Engineers officials denied the easement for the crossing. An easement is permission to cross land, here federal land under the lake. On January 24, 2017, President Trump ordered the Army Corps to review and approve the easement quickly. Army Corps officials approved it on February 8, 2017, and oil moved through the pipeline later that year. The tribe's lawsuits continued.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Natural gas, wind and solar, 2016 to 2025" -->
Natural gas made 34 percent of American electricity in 2016 and passed coal for a full year for the first time, according to the EIA. In 2025, by EIA figures, natural gas made about 41 percent of the country's electricity, nuclear power about 18 percent and coal about 17 percent. Wind made about 11 percent, solar about 7 and hydropower about 6.

Wind, solar, flowing water and other sources that do not run out are called renewable energy. In 2025 all renewable sources together made about 24 percent of American electricity. Wind and solar together made about 17 to 18 percent, a record. The EIA's yearly report puts wind and solar at about 17 percent. Its rounded shares for wind (11 percent) and solar (7 percent) add up to 18.

Analysts at the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics have projected jobs for 2024 to 2034. They expect wind turbine service technicians to be the fastest-growing job in the country, up 49.9 percent. Solar panel installers come second, up 42.1 percent. The two jobs together are projected to add fewer than 20,000 jobs.

Americans also began buying cars that run partly or fully on electricity. A hybrid car has both a gasoline engine and an electric motor. A battery electric car runs on electricity alone. According to the EIA, about 20 percent of the new cars, SUVs and pickups sold in 2024 were hybrids or electric. In 2025 the share rose to about 22 percent. But 2025 was also the first year that sales of battery electric cars fell. Federal tax credits for buyers of these cars, money taken off the buyer's tax bill, ended on September 30, 2025. In each of the months left in 2025, battery electric cars made up less than 6 percent of sales.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Texas grid fails, 2021" -->
In February 2021, during a winter storm named Uri, Texas power plants of every kind broke down in the cold. Some gas, coal, nuclear and wind plants stopped working. More than 4.5 million households, about 10 million people, lost power, some of them for days. Reports on the failure by federal energy regulators and by researchers at the University of Texas give these figures, and so do news reports.

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas, called ERCOT, runs the Texas power grid. A grid is the network of power plants and wires that serves an area. ERCOT's operators cut off power to customers to keep the whole grid from failing. They cut 20,000 megawatts, the largest cutoff of its kind in American history. A megawatt is a million watts. ERCOT officials later said the grid came within 4 minutes and 37 seconds of a total collapse.

In 2022 Texas state health officials counted 246 deaths from the storm. Some researchers argue that the official count is much too low. Reporters at BuzzFeed News, for example, studied how many more people died that month than usual. Damage estimates run above $195 billion.

### Cristian Pavón Pineda

> **Who:** An 11-year-old sixth grader who died in his family's mobile home during the Texas blackout.
> **When and where:** Conroe, Texas, February 16, 2021.

Cristian Pavón Pineda was 11 years old when he died in his family's mobile home in Conroe, Texas, on February 16, 2021. The home had been without power since the Sunday before, and the temperature there fell to about 10 degrees. Al Día News reported that he had seen snow for the first time the day before he died.

The family of five slept in one room to keep warm. Cristian shared a bed with his younger brother. His aunt, Jaliza Yera, told a TV station that he went to bed in "a shirt, a sweater, two pairs of pants, socks on." His stepfather put another blanket over him. In the morning his family could not wake him.

Early reports, and his family's lawsuit, said he died of hypothermia. Hypothermia means the body's temperature has fallen too low to keep a person alive. An autopsy is an examination of a body to find the cause of death. An autopsy on February 18, 2021, found that he died of carbon monoxide poisoning, Conroe police said in May 2021. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. Published reports do not say where the gas in the home came from.

Across Texas, more than 30 people had died in the storm by February 21, Al Día News reported. Many of them died of carbon monoxide poisoning after using cars or generators to keep warm.

On February 22, 2021, his mother, Maria Elisa Pineda Guzman, sued ERCOT and Entergy Texas, the power company, for more than $100 million. Her lawyer was Tony Buzbee. Officials had called the power cuts temporary rolling outages, meaning planned cuts that move from area to area. The suit states that ERCOT and Entergy Texas did not give his family good enough information about how long the outages would last. "Accurate information might have saved Cristian Pineda's young life," the suit states. In a statement, ERCOT officials called his death a "tragedy" and said they were "confident that our grid operators made the right choice to avoid a state-wide blackout." Published reports do not say how the lawsuit ended.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New and restarted reactors" -->
Two new nuclear reactors at the Vogtle plant in Georgia began running in 2023 and 2024. Unit 3 began selling power on July 31, 2023, and Unit 4 on April 29, 2024, according to the EIA. Unit 3 was the first new reactor to start in the United States since Watts Bar Unit 2 in Tennessee, owned by the TVA, began running in 2016. Work on Watts Bar 2 had begun in 1973. Workers stopped building it in 1985 and started again in 2007. So the two Vogtle reactors were the first American reactors begun after the 1970s to start running.

The owners of two closed plants plan to run them again. Holtec International owns the Palisades plant in Michigan, which shut down in May 2022. The company got a $1.52 billion federal loan to restart it. Holtec missed its targets of 2025 and early 2026. Workers began loading fuel into the reactor in late August 2026. On August 30 a fuel assembly, a tall bundle of fuel rods, tilted while it was being loaded, and the loading was paused. Officials of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the federal agency that oversees nuclear plants, began a review, and their first results showed no radiation had escaped and no workers were harmed. As of late September 2026, Holtec had given no date for the restart, a Michigan TV station reported.

Three Mile Island Unit 1 is the reactor that did not melt down in 1979. It closed in 2019. Its owner, Constellation Energy, renamed it the Christopher M. Crane Clean Energy Center and plans to run it again. Constellation signed a 20-year agreement to sell its power to Microsoft. Members of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission are expected to decide in May 2027 whether to give Constellation an operating license, which is permission to run the reactor. The news outlet WITF reported that date in July 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What energy independence means" -->
Americans produced more energy than they used in 2019, for the first time since 1957, according to the EIA. Americans produced 101.0 quadrillion Btu of energy that year and used 100.2 quadrillion. A Btu, or British thermal unit, is the heat needed to warm one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. A quadrillion is a million billion.

People call this energy independence. It means Americans produce more energy in total than they use. It does not mean Americans stopped buying fuel from other countries. In November 2019 Americans still bought 5.8 million barrels of crude oil a day from other countries and sold 3.0 million barrels a day to them, by EIA figures.

In 2025 Americans sold a record 31 quadrillion Btu of energy to other countries and bought 21 quadrillion Btu from them, the EIA reported in May 2026. Oil and products made from it were 63 percent of the energy sold abroad, and natural gas was 29 percent.
