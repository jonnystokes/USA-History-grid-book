## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the people who lived here did their work with the strength of their own bodies and with fire. Wood was the fuel. People burned it to heat their homes, to cook and to see after dark.

North of Mexico there were no horses, oxen or mules. People carried and dragged loads with their own muscle, and they moved heavy goods by canoe. Dogs were the only animals that hauled loads. On the Plains, dogs dragged a travois, which is two long poles tied together at one end so that they form a narrow V. The load rode on the poles, and their far ends dragged along the ground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire set on purpose" -->
Native people lit fires on purpose, on regular cycles. Today this is called cultural burning. It means lighting small fires and keeping them under control. The fires help the plants and animals that people use for food, clothing and ceremonies. People burned land to clear space for crops and for travel, and they used fire to hunt game.

In the eastern part of the continent, oak and chestnut trees fill many forests. According to the National Park Service, those forests grew that way because people burned them on purpose to get certain nut crops.

In what is now northern California, Karuk and Yurok people burned their hazel shrubs so that the shrubs would grow new stems. Weavers used those stems to make baskets. Karuk and Yurok people still do these burns. In 2021, a team of four researchers published a study of the burns done today, and the US Forest Service lists it among its research papers. Burning every three to five years gave the best stems. In the first season after a fire, the shrubs grew 13 times as many basket stems.

For most of the 1900s, the federal officials in charge of forests tried to put out wildfires. They wanted to protect water supplies, towns and timber for sale. Under the rules those officials made, Native people could do far fewer burns. Writers for The Nature Conservancy reported in 2020 that Native people who burn in the old way still face punishment. Their report does not say who punishes them. In 2013, Yurok people worked with California's state fire department on a small burn on Yurok land. In May 2014, organizers with the Cultural Fire Management Council held the first Yurok training event for this kind of burning.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish explorers and soldiers brought horses here. A horse could drag a travois farther and faster than a dog could, and it could carry more weight. Most people living here still did their work with muscle, fire and wood for the whole century.

In 1598, at the very end of the century, Spanish colonists in New Mexico built water mills. A history of the state's mills, printed in a Santa Fe newspaper, states that they were the first mills powered by water in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses come ashore" -->
In May 1539, Hernando de Soto landed near what is now Tampa Bay, Florida, with 11 ships. He brought about 250 horses, 1,000 soldiers on foot, many dogs and a herd of pigs. In April 1540 he reached Hymahi, a small Native village where two rivers meet. Writers for the National Park Service say the rivers must have been the Congaree and the Wateree, in what is now South Carolina. De Soto took all of the village's food. He burned at least one of the people who lived there to death while questioning that person about a town called Cofitachequi.

In 1540, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado led a large party north from Mexico into the Southwest. He had more than 300 armed Europeans and at least 1,200 Native people who marched on the Spanish side. Many dozens of servants and enslaved people went too. The Spanish brought horses with them. Coronado's party went as far as what is now Kansas and returned in 1542.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first water mills, 1598" -->
In 1598, Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into what is now New Mexico. That year they founded a town called San Juan de los Caballeros on the Rio Grande. The land there belonged to the Tewa, one of the Pueblo nations. Pueblo is a Spanish word that means town or village. The Spanish gave that name to the Native peoples of the Southwest who lived in towns built of stone and of adobe, which is brick made of mud and dried in the sun. Early in 1599 the colonists moved to a new capital, San Gabriel. They built it at a pueblo town near Ohkay Owingeh, a Tewa town. Writers for the National Park Service call that site abandoned. They do not say whether Tewa people had been made to leave it.

According to a history of New Mexico's mills in the *Santa Fe New Mexican* newspaper, Oñate's colonists built their first water mills in 1598. These were small tub mills. In a tub mill, the water wheel lies flat in the stream. The moving water spins the wheel, and the wheel turns the millstone directly. The millstone grinds grain into flour. The colonists hauled their millstones north on the Camino Real, which means the Royal Road. It ran about 1,600 miles north from Mexico City.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists built their first windmills and water mills in the 1600s. For the first time in these colonies, wind turned a mill, and falling water turned mills that ground grain and sawed logs. All other work still took muscle, and colonists burned wood in open fireplaces to heat their homes and cook.

Two other kinds of energy came into use in these years. Native people rode horses and fed them corn. Along the coast, colonists began to hunt whales for oil to burn in their lamps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first wind and water mills" -->
In 1621 George Yeardley, the governor of the Virginia colony, had a windmill built at Flowerdew Hundred, on the James River. It is the first windmill recorded in English America. Members of the colony's council, its group of leaders, wrote to the Virginia Company of London, the company in charge of the colony. Their letter records Yeardley's "good example" of building a windmill. Archaeologists, scientists who dig up the past, later dug at the spot. They found a round pit five feet across with iron hoops and brackets still in place. They took it to be the remains of Yeardley's windmill.

Flowerdew Hundred was land of the Weyanock, a Native nation whose language belonged to the Algonquian family of languages. Yeardley brought 15 men from England in 1619 to grow tobacco there. That same year, the crews of two armed ships, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, attacked a Portuguese ship carrying 350 enslaved Africans. They seized 50 to 60 of the captives. In late August 1619 the *White Lion* brought its share to Point Comfort, Virginia. Yeardley and a merchant named Abraham Peirsey bought them in exchange for food. At least 11 of these Africans were taken to Flowerdew Hundred, Yeardley's land. The archaeologists' account of Flowerdew does not say who moved them there. A count of the colony's people taken in 1624 and 1625 records seven Africans at Flowerdew as laborers. It gives no names for them.

Records from 1621 also list a gristmill near Jamestown, the first recorded in the English colonies. A gristmill is a mill that grinds grain.

In 1634, three English carpenters built a sawmill and a gristmill run by water at Newichawannock, in what is now South Berwick, Maine. These mills were among the first water mills in New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-chadbourne" name="William Chadbourne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Chadbourne

> **Who:** An English carpenter, one of three men that Captain John Mason sent through his Laconia Company to build a sawmill and a gristmill at Newichawannock.
> **When and where:** What is now South Berwick, Maine, from July 1634.

William Chadbourne was one of the carpenters who built some of the first water mills in New England. In July 1634 he, James Wall and John Goddard arrived from England on a ship called the *Pied Cow*. They worked under a contract with the Laconia Company of Captain John Mason. Under the contract, they were to build a sawmill and a gristmill on the Asbenbedick River, also called the Little Newichawannock.

A sawmill uses the power of water to cut logs into boards. In a letter of May 1631, Thomas Eyre wrote that a model of a sawmill had been sent to Newichawannock on the *Pied Cow*. The carpenters sailed on the same ship in 1634. Chadbourne and his partners built the sawmill in a rocky gorge. The spot is below the Great Works bridge on Brattle Street in South Berwick today.

Writers for the Old Berwick Historical Society say the sawmill is "thought to be the first" mill in America with an overshot wheel. On an overshot wheel, water pours onto the top of the wheel. The weight of the water turns it. People in other towns of the region say that their town had the first sawmill.

The river's name, Newichawannock, is an Abenaki word. Abenaki people lived there. Between 1616 and 1618, before the carpenters came, diseases brought by Europeans, such as measles or smallpox, killed seven out of every ten of them. The Old Berwick Historical Society's histories of the mills do not say that Mason's men asked the Abenaki for permission to build. A leader named Rowls survived the diseases. In 1650 he sold his village land, Quamphegan, to a man named Thomas Spencer for five pounds.

Mason died in 1638. After that, the mills fell apart. People stole the mills' materials, and one of the mills later burned. The society's timeline of the mills does not name who stole them. In 1652 James Wall made a sworn statement in a lawsuit about where the 1634 mills had stood. A sworn statement is one a person makes after promising, under the law, to tell the truth.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-chadbourne" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wood for every fire" -->
Wood was the main fuel in colonial homes. People measured firewood in cords. A cord is a stack of wood 4 feet high, 4 feet wide and 8 feet long. Estimates of how much wood one colonial household burned in a year run from about 15 cords to as many as 40. The low figure, 15 cords, comes from one New England estimate of the 1600s. The high figures, 30 to 40 cords, are for a home that heated and cooked with open fireplaces. An open fireplace sent most of its heat up the chimney, so a family had to burn a great deal of wood to stay warm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses in Native hands" -->
Native people had horses earlier than the old account said. For a long time, the usual account said that Native people got their horses after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. In that revolt, in August 1680, Pueblo people led by Po'pay, a Tewa religious leader, rose against the Spanish colonists in New Mexico and drove them out. Perhaps 1,500 or more horses came into Native hands at one time.

In 2023 a team of scientists published a study in the journal *Science* showing that Native people had horses earlier than that. The team included Lakota, Comanche and Pawnee scholars. They studied the bones of horses found in what are now Wyoming, Kansas and New Mexico. The bones showed that Native people were riding horses, putting bridles on them and feeding them corn by the early 1600s. That was decades before the Pueblo Revolt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil from whales" -->
English colonists on Long Island and in New England began killing whales close to shore for their oil in the 1600s. From about 1690, whale oil was one of the main fuels that Americans burned in their lamps, and it stayed one for about 150 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1700 to 1750, colonial iron furnaces burned charcoal, a fuel made from wood. An iron furnace is a tall oven where fire melts iron out of rock. At the Baltimore Iron Works in Maryland, enslaved people and convicts shipped from Britain hauled charcoal to the furnace around the clock. Lamps burned whale oil. Families who could pay the most bought candles made from a waxy oil in the head of the sperm whale. In 1741 Benjamin Franklin designed an iron stove meant to heat a room with less wood.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal for iron" -->
Iron furnaces burned charcoal as their fuel. Charcoal is wood that has been heated with too little air for it to burn away. The heat drives off the water and gases in the wood. What is left is nearly pure carbon, and it burns hotter than wood. The men who made charcoal were called colliers.

In 1731, five planters started the Baltimore Iron Works on the Patapsco River in Maryland. Dr. Charles Carroll, a surgeon, gave 2,200 acres of land and ran the works at first. His cousins Charles Carroll and Daniel Carroll, along with Daniel Dulany and Benjamin Tasker, put in money and enslaved workers. In the 1600s, the land around the Patapsco lay within the hunting grounds of the Susquehannock nation. The Susquehannock had their villages farther north, along the Susquehanna River.

The Baltimore Iron Works opened with 89 workers. Forty-two of them were enslaved. Many of the workers were enslaved people or transported convicts. A transported convict was a person found guilty of a crime in Britain and sent to the colonies to be sold into years of forced work. Judges in English courts gave that sentence. Merchants shipped the convicts across the ocean, and merchants and ship captains sold them in the colonies, some of them to iron makers. Their forced work lasted 7 to 14 years. The workers hauled cartloads of charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore, the rock that iron comes from, to the top of the furnace in 12-hour shifts. The work went on around the clock, six or seven days a week.

The owners did not give the workers enough food. Some workers ran away, and others worked slowly on purpose. In a good year the partners made a profit equal to 55 percent of the money they had put in. By 1785 an advertisement for the Baltimore Iron Works listed "more than 200 Negroes," which means more than 200 enslaved Black people. Negro was a word used then for a Black person.

The surviving records of these early furnaces list the owners. They do not list the men who made the charcoal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Light after dark" -->
The brightest light in the colonies came from spermaceti. Spermaceti is a waxy oil found in the head of the sperm whale. Candle makers turned it into candles that burned brighter and cleaner than any others and cost the most. Writers at the New Bedford Whaling Museum call spermaceti "the most valuable product of the Yankee whaling industry." Yankee meant someone from New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A stove to save wood" -->
In 1741 Benjamin Franklin designed what he called the Pennsylvania fireplace. It was a stove made of cast iron, which is iron poured into a mold while it is melted. Franklin meant it to give more heat and less smoke from less wood than an open fireplace. Few people bought it at first. David Rittenhouse later improved the design, and after that it sold better.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1755, at a copper mine in New Jersey, a steam engine began pumping water out of the mine. It was the first steam engine in America. A fire under a boiler, a closed tank of water, made the steam that ran it.

One iron furnace in Pennsylvania, Hopewell, burned charcoal made from about an acre of forest every day it ran. At Catoctin Furnace in Maryland, enslaved Black workers and some free Black workers cut the wood, made the charcoal and hauled it to the furnace. After dark, spermaceti candles still gave the brightest light.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
In 1748, water flooded the copper mine of John Schuyler at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey, and the mine had to shut down. Schuyler paid an English engine maker, Jonathan Hornblower, 1,000 pounds to send him a "fire engine" and mechanics to set it up. People at the time called this kind of steam engine a fire engine. Josiah Hornblower used that name in a letter of 1768. The parts arrived in New York in September 1753 on a ship called the *Irene*, after twelve weeks at sea. Jonathan's son Josiah came with them. The parts were taken to Newark, New Jersey, and put together there. By early 1755 the engine was pumping water out of the mine.

The engine was a kind invented in England by Thomas Newcomen. The fire under the boiler turned the water into steam. The steam flowed into a cylinder, a tall round tube. Inside the cylinder, the steam filled the space under a piston, a heavy disk that could slide up and down. Then a jet of cold water sprayed into the cylinder. The cold turned the steam back into water, which left almost nothing under the piston. The weight of the air above pushed the piston down.

The piston was joined to one end of a beam that rocked up and down like a seesaw. The other end of the beam lifted long pump rods that hung down the mine shaft, and the pumps raised the water. Then the weight of the pump rods pulled the piston back up, and the engine started again. Engines of this kind were about two stories high. Their power was about ten horsepower. A horsepower is a unit of power based on the work one horse can do.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-hornblower" name="Josiah Hornblower" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Hornblower

> **Who:** An English engine builder who came with the first steam engine in America and later ran it, at the Schuyler copper mine.
> **When and where:** Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey. Engine at work from 1755. Born 1729, died 1809.

Josiah Hornblower was the young English engineer who came with the first steam engine in America, and later he ran it himself. He was born in 1729, the son of Jonathan Hornblower, an English engine maker. In 1753, when he was about 24, he sailed from London to New York with the parts of the engine. By early 1755 the engine had been put together and was pumping water out of John Schuyler's copper mine at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey.

For the next twenty years the engine worked only part of the time. In 1761 Hornblower and a partner, John Stearndall, leased the mine from the Schuyler family. They paid one seventh of the ore they dug as rent.

In March 1762 the building around the engine caught fire and burned to the ground. A report in the *New York Mercury* of March 22 states that the engine works had cost nearly ten thousand pounds. People at the time guessed that a careless workman had left a candle burning. By 1767 the engine was working again. Richard McCormick, a historian who wrote the history of the engine, does not say who repaired it or when.

Late in 1767, Hornblower had to stop the engine because he had run out of fuel. He got more fuel, put the old pumps back in, made all the repairs needed and started it again in April 1768. Soon the vein, the band of ore running through the rock, dipped deep. He tried to follow it with hand pumps, but they could not keep the water out. His partners had also not sent money they owed him. On a Monday in July 1768, he stopped the mine and let all the workers go. At about eight o'clock that same evening, the engine house caught fire. It burned to ruins in a few minutes while a crowd of people tried to save it. Hornblower wrote that a man had gone into the engine house two hours before to light his pipe. The man said he could not find any fire there to light it with. A report in the *Pennsylvania Chronicle* states that the fire was "supposed to be by Design," because no one knew of any fire being used there lately. That means people thought someone set it on purpose.

Mining at Schuyler's stopped in 1773. Hornblower stayed in New Jersey for the rest of his life, and he died there in 1809. Accounts of what became of the engine differ. One says it was broken up around 1800. Another says it was repaired in 1793 and pumped water into the 1800s. Half of its cylinder is now in the Smithsonian Institution.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-hornblower" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal by the acre" -->
Mark Bird built Hopewell Furnace on French Creek, in Berks County, Pennsylvania, in 1771. The land around it had belonged to the Lenni Lenape. After the colony's founder, William Penn, left Pennsylvania in 1701, his agent James Logan handled Penn's land deals. Under Logan, land deals with the Lenni Lenape were often dishonest, and some were frauds, deals built on lies. By about 1750, nearly all Native people had been driven out of the lower Delaware and Susquehanna river valleys. Most moved west to the Allegheny, Monongahela and Ohio river valleys. The history of Hopewell written for the National Park Service does not name the people who drove them out.

When Hopewell's furnace was in blast, which means lit and making iron, it burned up to 800 bushels of charcoal a day. A bushel is a measure for dry goods such as grain or charcoal. To make that much charcoal, workers cut about an acre of forest every day. In a year the furnace used 5,000 to 7,000 cords of wood, from more than 200 acres of woodland. The owners held between 4,000 and 8,000 acres of forest to keep the furnace supplied. Woodcutters, using only axes, cut an average of two cords a day each.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How a collier made charcoal" -->
A collier made charcoal in a pile called a pit, built on flat, cleared ground. He drove a pole 18 feet long into the middle of that ground. The pole was green, which means freshly cut. Around the pole he built a chimney with three corners. Then he stacked about 30 cords of wood around it in three layers and covered the whole pile with leaves and dust.

To light the pit, he shoveled red-hot coals from his cooking fire down the chimney. The wood inside smoldered, burning slowly with very little air. The collier watched it day and night. He walked on top of the pit to find soft spots, where air was getting in, and he filled them. After 10 days to two weeks, the wood had turned into charcoal. One pit made about 1,100 bushels, enough to run the furnace for a day and a half.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who made the charcoal" -->
In 1780, Mark Bird enslaved 18 people: 10 men, 4 women, 3 boys and 1 girl. No one else in Berks County enslaved as many people that year. That same year, Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a law to end slavery in stages. Under it, mainly children born after the law passed could become free. The last record of slavery at Hopewell is from 1821, when Matthew Brooke freed one person he enslaved. Hopewell's owners hired free Black workers only for jobs that took little training. They worked as woodcutters, laborers and teamsters, who drove wagons. According to the National Park Service, no record from Hopewell's 112 years shows a Black worker in a skilled job or as a manager.

Catoctin Furnace, in Maryland, was making iron by 1776. Four brothers named James, Baker, Roger and Thomas Johnson built it. The Johnson family enslaved more than 80 people there. One of them was a skilled man recorded only as Collier Sam. The Catoctin Furnace Historical Society's history gives only his name and his trade. Over the life of the furnace, at least 271 enslaved African Americans worked at Catoctin, along with an unknown number of free African Americans. They mined ore, cut wood, made charcoal, drove wagons and worked the forge, the shop where iron was heated and hammered into shape. Woodcutters cut, hauled and stacked the wood for the colliers, and other workers carried the finished charcoal to the furnace.
