## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, the people who lived here did their work with the strength of their own bodies and with fire. Wood was the fuel. People burned it to heat their homes, to cook and to see after dark.

North of Mexico there were no horses, oxen or mules. People carried loads on their backs and moved heavy goods by canoe. Dogs were the only animals that hauled loads. On the Plains, dogs dragged a travois, which is two long poles tied together at one end so that they form a narrow V. The load sat on the poles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire set on purpose" -->
Native nations set fires on purpose, and they set them on a schedule. Today this is called cultural burning. It means lighting small fires and keeping them under control, so that the plants and animals people use for food, clothing and ceremonies grow well. People burned land to clear space for crops and for travel, and they used fire to hunt game.

In the eastern part of the continent, many forests are full of oak and chestnut trees. According to the National Park Service, those forests grew that way because people burned them again and again to get bigger crops of nuts.

In what is now northern California, Karuk and Yurok people burned their hazel groves every three to five years. A burn made the hazel shrubs grow new stems, and weavers used those stems to make baskets. Karuk and Yurok people still do these burns. In a study of burns done in recent years, the shrubs grew 13 times as many basket stems in the first season after a fire.

In the 1900s, officials of government land agencies put out fires and enforced their own rules for the land. In many places, cultural burning stopped for decades. Over the past few decades, Native nations have brought it back in some areas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish expeditions brought horses here. A horse could carry a rider and drag more weight, farther and faster, than a dog could. Most people living here still did their work with muscle, fire and wood for the whole century.

In 1598, at the very end of the century, Spanish colonists in New Mexico built the first mills powered by water in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses come ashore" -->
In May 1539, Hernando de Soto landed near what is now Tampa Bay, Florida, with 11 ships. He brought about 250 horses, 1,000 soldiers on foot, many dogs and a herd of pigs. At a town called Hymahi, de Soto's men took all of the town's food. De Soto burned at least one of the town's people to death while questioning that person about another town.

In 1540, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado led an expedition north from Mexico into the Southwest. It had more than 300 armed Europeans, at least 1,200 Native allies, and dozens of servants and enslaved people. The Spanish soldiers rode horses and wore armor. The expedition went as far as what is now Kansas and returned in 1542.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first water mills, 1598" -->
In 1598, Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into what is now New Mexico. That year they founded a town called San Juan de los Caballeros on the Rio Grande. The land there belonged to the Tewa, one of the Pueblo nations. Early in 1599 the colonists moved to a new capital, San Gabriel. They built it at an empty pueblo town near Ohkay Owingeh, a Tewa town.

A history of New Mexico's mills in the *Santa Fe New Mexican* newspaper gives 1598 as the year Oñate's colonists built their first water mills. These were small tub mills. In a tub mill, the water wheel lies flat in the stream. The moving water spins the wheel, and the wheel turns the millstone above it directly. The millstone grinds grain into flour. The colonists hauled their grindstones north on the Camino Real, the long road from Mexico.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists built their first windmills and water mills in the 1600s. For the first time in these colonies, wind and falling water turned machines that ground grain and sawed logs. Everything else still ran on muscle and on wood. Colonists burned wood in open fireplaces to heat their homes and cook.

Two other sources of power spread in these years. Native nations raised and rode horses. Along the coast, colonists began to hunt whales for oil to burn in their lamps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first wind and water mills" -->
In 1621 George Yeardley, the governor of the Virginia colony, had a windmill built at Flowerdew Hundred, his land on the James River. It is the first windmill recorded in English America. Members of the colony's council wrote to the Virginia Company in London and praised Yeardley's "good example." Archaeologists later dug up the spot. They found a round pit five feet across, with the iron hoops and brackets that once held the mill in place.

Flowerdew Hundred was land of the Weyanock, a Native nation that spoke an Algonquian language. Yeardley brought 15 men from England in 1619 to grow tobacco there. That same year, at least 11 Africans who had been brought to Virginia were taken to Flowerdew Hundred. A list of the colony's people made in 1624 and 1625 names seven Africans at Flowerdew as laborers. It does not give their names. The English workers and the Africans did the farm work with their own muscle.

Records from 1621 also mention the first gristmill in the English colonies, near Jamestown. A gristmill is a mill that grinds grain.

In 1634, three English carpenters built a sawmill and a gristmill run by water at Newichawannock, in what is now South Berwick, Maine. They were among the first water mills in New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-chadbourne" name="William Chadbourne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Chadbourne

> **Who:** An English carpenter, one of three men that John Mason's Laconia Company sent to build a sawmill and a gristmill at Newichawannock.
> **When and where:** What is now South Berwick, Maine, from July 1634.

William Chadbourne was one of the carpenters who built some of the first water mills in New England. In July 1634 he, James Wall and John Goddard arrived from England on a ship called the *Pied Cow*. They worked under a contract with the Laconia Company of Captain John Mason. The contract told them to build a sawmill and a gristmill on the Asbenbedick River, also called the Little Newichawannock.

A sawmill uses the power of water to cut logs into boards. Mason's company had already sent a model of a sawmill to Newichawannock on the same ship, in 1631. Chadbourne and his partners built the sawmill in a rocky gorge. The spot is below the Great Works bridge on Brattle Street in South Berwick today.

The Old Berwick Historical Society says the sawmill is "thought to be the first" mill in America with an overshot wheel. On an overshot wheel, water pours onto the top of the wheel. The weight of the water turns it. Other towns in the region make their own claims to the first sawmill.

The river's name, Newichawannock, is an Abenaki word. Abenaki people lived there. Between 1616 and 1618, before the carpenters came, diseases brought by Europeans, such as measles or smallpox, killed seven out of every ten of them. No known record shows that Mason's men asked the Abenaki for leave to build. A leader named Rowls survived the diseases. In 1650 he sold his village land, Quamphegan, to an Englishman named Thomas Spencer for five pounds.

Mason died in 1638. After that, the mills fell apart. People carried off their materials, and one of the mills later burned. In 1652 James Wall gave sworn testimony in a lawsuit about where the 1634 mills had stood.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-chadbourne" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wood for every fire" -->
Wood was the fuel for every colonial home. People measured firewood in cords. A cord is a stack of wood 4 feet high, 4 feet wide and 8 feet long. Historians' estimates of how much wood one colonial household burned in a year run from about 15 cords to as many as 40. One estimate from New England in the 1600s gives 15 cords. Other estimates give 30 to 40 cords for a home that heated and cooked with open fireplaces. An open fireplace sent most of its heat up the chimney, so a family had to burn a great deal of wood to stay warm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses in Native hands" -->
Native nations had horses earlier than historians once thought. For a long time, the usual account said that most Native nations got horses after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. In that revolt against the Spanish colonists in New Mexico, perhaps 1,500 or more horses passed into Native hands at one time.

In 2023 a team of scientists published a study in the journal *Science* that moved the date earlier. The team included Lakota, Comanche and Pawnee scholars. They studied the bones of horses found in what are now Wyoming, Kansas and New Mexico. The bones showed that Native people were riding horses, putting bridles on them and feeding them corn by the early 1600s. That was decades before the Pueblo Revolt.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil from whales" -->
English colonists on Long Island and in New England began hunting whales close to shore in the 1600s. The oil came from the whales they killed. From about 1690, whale oil was one of the main fuels that Americans burned in their lamps, and it stayed one for about 150 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1700 to 1750, colonial iron furnaces burned charcoal, a fuel made from wood. Enslaved people and convicts shipped from Britain hauled that fuel to the furnaces around the clock. After dark, families who could pay for it burned whale oil in their lamps or bought candles made from a substance in the head of the sperm whale. In 1741 Benjamin Franklin designed an iron stove meant to heat a room with less wood.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal for iron" -->
Iron furnaces burned charcoal as their fuel. Charcoal is wood that has been heated with too little air for it to burn away. The heat drives off the water and gases in the wood. What is left is nearly pure carbon, and it burns hotter than wood. The men who made charcoal were called colliers. Iron furnaces ran day and night, so they burned charcoal day and night.

In 1731, five planters started the Baltimore Iron Works on the Patapsco River in Maryland. Dr. Charles Carroll, a surgeon, gave 2,200 acres of land and ran the works at first. His cousins Charles Carroll and Daniel Carroll, along with Daniel Dulany and Benjamin Tasker, put in money and enslaved workers. Before English colonists came, the land around the Patapsco lay within the hunting grounds of the Susquehannock nation. The Susquehannock had their villages farther north, along the Susquehanna River.

The Baltimore Iron Works opened with 89 workers. Forty-two of them were enslaved. Many of the other workers were transported convicts. A transported convict was a person found guilty of a crime in Britain and shipped to the colonies to be sold into years of forced work. The workers pulled carts of charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore, the rock that iron comes from, to the top of the furnace in 12-hour shifts. The work went on around the clock, six or seven days a week.

The owners did not give the workers enough food. Some workers ran away, and others worked slowly on purpose. In a good year the partners made a profit equal to 55 percent of the money they had put in. By 1785 the owners advertised that they held "more than 200 Negroes." Negro was a word used then for a Black person.

The records of these early furnaces name the owners. They do not name the men who made the charcoal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Light after dark" -->
Whale oil was one of the main lamp fuels in the colonies from about 1690. The brightest light came from spermaceti. Spermaceti is a waxy oil found in the head of the sperm whale. Candle makers turned it into candles that burned brighter and cleaner than any others, and they cost more than any others. The New Bedford Whaling Museum calls spermaceti "the most valuable product of the Yankee whaling industry."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A stove to save wood" -->
In 1741 Benjamin Franklin designed what he called the Pennsylvania fireplace. It was a stove made of cast iron, which is iron poured into a mold while it is melted. Franklin designed it to give more heat and less smoke from less wood than an open fireplace. Few people bought it at first. David Rittenhouse later improved the design, and after that it sold better.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1755, at a copper mine in New Jersey, a steam engine began pumping water out of the mine. It was the first steam engine in America, and a fire under a boiler made the steam that ran it. Everywhere else, wind, water, wood and muscle still did the work through the Revolution and after.

One iron furnace in Pennsylvania, Hopewell, burned charcoal made from about an acre of forest every day it ran. At Catoctin Furnace in Maryland, enslaved people cut the wood, made the charcoal and hauled it to the furnace. After dark, whale oil and spermaceti candles still gave the best light that money could buy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
In 1748, water flooded the copper mine of John Schuyler at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey, and the mine had to shut down. Schuyler paid an English engine maker, Jonathan Hornblower, 1,000 pounds to send him a "fire engine" and mechanics to set it up. People then called a steam engine a fire engine. The parts arrived in New York in September 1753 on a ship called the *Irene*, after twelve weeks at sea. Jonathan's son Josiah came with them. By early 1755 the engine was put together at the mine and pumping water.

The engine was a kind invented in England by Thomas Newcomen. A fire under a boiler turned water into steam. The steam filled a cylinder under a piston, a heavy disk that could slide up and down inside it. Then a jet of cold water sprayed into the cylinder. The cold turned the steam back into water, which left almost nothing under the piston. The weight of the air above pushed the piston down.

The piston was joined to one end of a beam that rocked up and down like a seesaw. The other end of the beam lifted long pump rods that hung down the mine shaft, and the pumps raised the water. Then the weight of the pump rods pulled the piston back up, and the engine started again. Engines of this kind were about two stories high. Their power was about ten horsepower. A horsepower is a unit of power based on the work one horse can do.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-hornblower" name="Josiah Hornblower" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Hornblower

> **Who:** An English engine builder who put together and ran the first steam engine in America, at the Schuyler copper mine.
> **When and where:** Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey. Engine at work from 1755. Born 1729, died 1809.

Josiah Hornblower put together the first steam engine in America and ran it for years. He was born in 1729, the son of Jonathan Hornblower, an English engine maker. In 1753, when he was about 24, he sailed from London to New York with the parts of the engine. By early 1755 he had it pumping water out of John Schuyler's copper mine at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey.

For the next twenty years the engine worked only part of the time. On March 22, 1762, a New York newspaper reported that the building around the engine had burned to the ground. The newspaper said the engine works had cost nearly ten thousand pounds.

In 1761 Hornblower and a partner, John Stearndall, leased the mine from the Schuyler family. They paid one seventh of the ore they dug as rent. Late in 1767, Hornblower had to stop the engine because he had run out of fuel. He bought more fuel and started it again in April 1768.

By July 1768 the vein of ore had dipped too deep to follow with hand pumps, and his partners had not sent money they owed him. On July 17 he stopped the mine and let all the workers go. That same evening the engine house caught fire. It burned to ruins in a few minutes while a crowd of people tried to save it. A newspaper at the time said the fire was "supposed to be by Design," which means people thought someone set it on purpose.

Mining at Schuyler's stopped in 1773. Hornblower stayed in New Jersey for the rest of his life, and he died there in 1809. The engine was broken up around 1800. Half of its cylinder is now in the Smithsonian Institution.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-hornblower" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal by the acre" -->
Mark Bird built Hopewell Furnace on French Creek, in Berks County, Pennsylvania, in 1771. The land around it had belonged to the Lenni Lenape. After 1701, James Logan handled land for the colony's founder, William Penn, for 50 years. Under Logan, land deals with the Lenni Lenape were often dishonest and sometimes fraudulent, which means based on lies. By about 1750, colonists held nearly all the land in the lower Delaware and Susquehanna river valleys. Nearly all Native people there had been driven out, and most moved west to the Allegheny, Monongahela and Ohio river valleys.

When Hopewell's furnace was in blast, which means lit and making iron, it burned up to 800 bushels of charcoal a day. A bushel is a measure for dry goods such as grain or charcoal. To make that much charcoal, workers cut about an acre of forest every day. In a year the furnace used 5,000 to 7,000 cords of wood, from more than 200 acres of woodland. The owners held between 4,000 and 8,000 acres of forest to keep the furnace supplied. Woodcutters, using only axes, cut an average of two cords a day each.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How a collier made charcoal" -->
A collier made charcoal in a pile called a pit, built on flat, cleared ground. He drove a green pole 18 feet long into the middle of that ground. Around the pole he built a chimney with three corners. Then he stacked about 30 cords of wood around it in three layers and covered the whole pile with leaves and dust.

To light the pit, he shoveled red-hot coals from his cooking fire down the chimney. The wood inside smoldered, burning slowly with very little air. The collier watched it day and night. He walked on top of the pit to find soft spots, where air was getting in, and he filled them. After 10 days to two weeks, the wood had turned into charcoal. One pit made about 1,100 bushels, enough to run the furnace for a day and a half.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who made the charcoal" -->
In 1780, Mark Bird enslaved 18 people: 10 men, 4 women, 3 boys and 1 girl. No one else in Berks County enslaved as many people that year. That same year, Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a law to end slavery in stages. It freed mainly children born after the law. The last record of slavery at Hopewell is from 1821, when Matthew Brooke freed one person he enslaved. Hopewell's owners hired free Black workers only for unskilled and semiskilled jobs. They worked as woodcutters, as laborers and as teamsters, who drove wagons. In Hopewell's 112 years, no record shows a Black worker in a skilled job or as a manager.

Catoctin Furnace, in Maryland, was making iron by 1776. Four brothers named James, Baker, Roger and Thomas Johnson built it. The Johnson family enslaved more than 80 people there. One of them was a skilled man recorded only as Collier Sam. His name and his trade are all that the known records give about him. Over the life of the furnace, at least 271 enslaved African Americans worked at Catoctin, along with an unknown number of free African Americans. They mined ore, cut wood, made charcoal, drove wagons and worked the forge. Woodcutters cut, hauled and stacked the wood for the colliers, and other workers carried the finished charcoal to the furnace.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
