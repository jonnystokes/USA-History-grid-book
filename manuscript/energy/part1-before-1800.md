## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 10 of 37 · Energy · 10 eras · about 75 minutes**

**Americans moved from burning wood to burning coal, oil and natural gas, and getting that energy cost many people their freedom, their land or their lives.**

A tavern keeper named Jesse Fell burned hard coal on an iron grate in his fireplace in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, on February 11, 1808. Most people thought that coal was too difficult to burn at home, but Fell showed that it could warm a house. From the 1820s to the 1840s, people put stoves and furnaces made for that coal into their homes. Miners dug that coal in northeastern Pennsylvania, and on September 6, 1869, a fire in one of their mines, the Avondale mine near Wilkes-Barre, killed 110 people.

This chapter describes the fuels in order, from the fires Native people set on purpose before 1500 to the wind turbines and solar panels of 2025. Watch for two things. One is the main fuel. Wood came first, then coal, which passed wood around 1885, and then oil and natural gas. In 2016 natural gas passed coal as the fuel that made the most American electricity. The other is who did the work and who paid for it: the enslaved people who made charcoal for the iron furnaces, the coal miners killed underground, and the Native nations whose rivers were dammed and whose land held oil and uranium.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Did kerosene really save the whales?
- How did electricity reach almost every American home?
- Who paid for America's energy with their freedom, their land or their lives?

**Five minutes?** Read "The current war" in 1850 to 1900.
**Can skip:** "A stove to save wood", "Oil for a world war", "Panels on the White House roof", "New and restarted reactors". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 2 minutes**

People here did their work with the strength of their own bodies and with fire, and wood was the fuel they burned. They burned it to heat their homes, to cook and to see after dark.

North of Mexico there were no horses, oxen or mules. People carried and dragged loads with their own muscle, and they moved heavy goods by canoe. Dogs were the only animals that hauled loads. On the Plains, dogs dragged a travois, two long poles tied together at one end to form a narrow V. The load rode on the poles, and their far ends dragged along the ground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did Native people set fires on purpose?" -->
Native people lit small fires and kept them under control to help the plants and animals they used for food, clothing and ceremonies. They also burned land to clear space for crops and travel, and they used fire to hunt game. They burned on regular cycles, and the practice is now called cultural burning.

In the eastern part of the continent, oak and chestnut trees fill many forests. According to the National Park Service, those forests grew that way because people burned them on purpose to get certain nut crops. In what is now northern California, Karuk and Yurok people burned their hazel shrubs so that the shrubs would grow new stems for weaving baskets.

**After 1500.** Native people went on burning land long after 1500. For most of the 1900s, though, federal officials in charge of forests tried to put out wildfires, to protect water supplies, towns and timber for sale. Under the rules those officials made, Native people could do far fewer burns.

Yurok people worked with California's state fire department on a small burn on Yurok land in 2013. In May 2014, organizers with the Cultural Fire Management Council held the first Yurok training event for this kind of burning. Writers for The Nature Conservancy reported in 2020 that Native people who burn in the old way still face punishment. Their report does not say who punishes them.

**Basket stems today.** Karuk and Yurok people still burn their hazel shrubs. Four researchers studied those burns and published their findings in 2021, and the study appears among the US Forest Service's research papers. Burning every three to five years gave the best stems. In the first season after a fire, the shrubs grew 13 times as many basket stems.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

Spanish explorers and soldiers brought horses here in the 1500s, and at the very end of the century Spanish colonists built water mills. A horse could drag a travois farther and faster than a dog could, and it could carry more weight. Still, most people living here did their work with muscle, fire and wood for the whole century.

The mills stood in New Mexico. A history of the state's mills, printed in a Santa Fe newspaper, calls them the first mills powered by water in what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses come ashore" -->
Hernando de Soto landed near what is now Tampa Bay, Florida, in May 1539, with 11 ships. He brought about 250 horses, 1,000 soldiers on foot, many dogs and a herd of pigs.

**Hymahi.** De Soto's army reached Hymahi, a small Native village where two rivers meet, in April 1540. Writers for the National Park Service say the rivers must have been the Congaree and the Wateree, in what is now South Carolina. De Soto took all of the village's food. He burned at least one of the people who lived there to death while questioning that person about a town called Cofitachequi.

Francisco Vázquez de Coronado led a large party north from Mexico into the Southwest in 1540, and his men brought horses too. He had more than 300 armed Europeans and at least 1,200 Native people who marched on the Spanish side. Many dozens of servants and enslaved people went with them. Coronado's party went as far as what is now Kansas and returned in 1542.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first water mills, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into what is now New Mexico in 1598. That year they founded a town called San Juan de los Caballeros on the Rio Grande, on land that belonged to the Tewa, one of the Pueblo nations. Pueblo is the Spanish word for town or village. The Spanish used it for the Native peoples of the Southwest who lived in towns built of stone and of adobe, brick made of mud and dried in the sun.

Early in 1599 the colonists moved to a new capital, San Gabriel, which they built at a pueblo town near Ohkay Owingeh, a Tewa town. Writers for the National Park Service call that site abandoned. They do not say whether Tewa people had been made to leave it.

**Tub mills.** Oñate's colonists built their first water mills in 1598. These were small tub mills, with the water wheel lying flat in the stream. The moving water spins the wheel, and the wheel turns the millstone directly, so the stone grinds grain into flour. The colonists hauled their millstones north on the Camino Real, the Royal Road, which ran about 1,600 miles north from Mexico City.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

Wind and falling water began turning mills in the English colonies in the 1600s. The mills ground grain and sawed logs. All other work still took muscle, and colonists burned wood in open fireplaces to heat their homes and cook.

Two other kinds of energy came into use in these years. Native people rode horses and fed them corn, and along the coast colonists began to hunt whales for oil to burn in their lamps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first wind and water mills" -->
George Yeardley, the governor of the Virginia colony, had a windmill built at Flowerdew Hundred, on the James River, in 1621. It is the first windmill recorded in English America. Members of the colony's council, its group of leaders, wrote to the company in London that was in charge of the colony. Their letter records Yeardley's "good example" of building a windmill.

**What the diggers found.** Archaeologists, scientists who dig up the past, later dug at the spot. They found a round pit five feet across with iron hoops and brackets still in place, and they took it to be the remains of Yeardley's windmill.

**Whose land, and whose work.** Flowerdew Hundred was land of the Weyanock, a Native nation whose language belonged to the Algonquian family of languages. Yeardley brought 15 men from England in 1619 to grow tobacco there.

That same year, the crews of two armed ships, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, attacked a Portuguese ship carrying 350 enslaved Africans and seized 50 to 60 of the captives. The *White Lion* brought its share to Point Comfort, Virginia, in late August 1619. Yeardley and a merchant named Abraham Peirsey bought them in exchange for food.

At least 11 of these Africans were taken to Flowerdew Hundred, Yeardley's land. The archaeologists' account of Flowerdew does not say who moved them there. A count of the colony's people taken in 1624 and 1625 records seven Africans at Flowerdew as laborers. It gives no names for them. The records do not say what happened to the other four.

Records from 1621 also list a gristmill, a mill that grinds grain, near Jamestown. It is the first gristmill recorded in the English colonies. Farther north, three English carpenters built a sawmill and a gristmill run by water at Newichawannock, in what is now South Berwick, Maine, in 1634. They were among the first water mills in New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-chadbourne" name="William Chadbourne" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Chadbourne

> **Who:** An English carpenter who helped build one of the first sawmills run by water in New England.
> **When and where:** What is now South Berwick, Maine, from July 1634.

**The mill's model arrived three years before the men who built it.**

William Chadbourne, James Wall and John Goddard arrived from England in July 1634 on a ship called the *Pied Cow*. They worked under a contract with the Laconia Company of Captain John Mason. Under it, the three carpenters were to build a sawmill, to cut logs into boards, and a gristmill on the Little Newichawannock River.

**The model.** Thomas Eyre wrote in a letter of May 1631 that a model of a sawmill had been sent to Newichawannock on the *Pied Cow*, the same ship the carpenters took three years later. Chadbourne and his partners built the sawmill in a rocky gorge. The spot is now below the Great Works bridge on Brattle Street in South Berwick. In 1652, in a lawsuit, James Wall made a sworn statement, a promise under the law to tell the truth, about where the 1634 mills had stood.

**The wheel.** Writers for the Old Berwick Historical Society say the sawmill is "thought to be the first" mill in America with an overshot wheel. On an overshot wheel, water pours onto the top of the wheel, and the weight of the water turns it. People in other towns of the region say that their town had the first sawmill.

**Whose river.** Newichawannock is an Abenaki word, and Abenaki people lived there. Between 1616 and 1618, before the carpenters came, diseases brought by Europeans, such as measles or smallpox, killed seven out of every ten of them. The society's histories of the mills do not say that Mason's men asked the Abenaki for permission to build. A leader named Rowls survived the diseases. In 1650 he sold his village land, Quamphegan, to a man named Thomas Spencer for five pounds.

Mason died in 1638, and after that the mills fell apart. People stole their materials, and the society's timeline of the mills does not name the thieves. Later one of the mills burned.

**Not known:** what became of William Chadbourne after he built the mills. The society's histories of the mills do not say.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-chadbourne" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How much wood did a family burn?" -->
Wood was the main fuel in colonial homes, and one household burned about 15 to 40 cords of it a year. A cord is a stack of wood 4 feet high, 4 feet wide and 8 feet long. Forty cords stacked in one row 4 feet high would run 320 feet, longer than a football field from goal line to goal line.

The low figure, 15 cords, comes from one New England estimate of the 1600s. The high figures, 30 to 40 cords, are for a home that heated and cooked with open fireplaces. An open fireplace sent most of its heat up the chimney, so a family had to burn a great deal of wood to stay warm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Horses in Native hands" -->
Native people were riding horses, putting bridles on them and feeding them corn by the early 1600s. A team of scientists showed this in a study published in the journal *Science* in 2023. The team, which included Lakota, Comanche and Pawnee scholars, studied the bones of horses found in what are now Wyoming, Kansas and New Mexico.

**The old account.** For a long time, the usual account said that Native people got their horses only after the Pueblo Revolt, decades later. In August 1680, Pueblo people led by Po'pay, a Tewa religious leader, rose against the Spanish colonists in New Mexico and drove them out. Perhaps 1,500 or more horses came into Native hands at one time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oil from whales" -->
English colonists on Long Island and in New England began killing whales close to shore for their oil in the 1600s. From about 1690, whale oil was one of the main fuels that Americans burned in their lamps, and it stayed one for about 150 years. By the 1840s it cost too much for most families, and they burned cheaper fuels instead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 3 minutes**

Iron furnaces in the colonies, tall ovens where fire melts iron out of rock, ran on charcoal, a fuel made from wood. Many of the workers who fed the fire at the Baltimore Iron Works, in Maryland, were enslaved people and convicts shipped from Britain. They hauled charcoal to the furnace around the clock.

Lamps burned whale oil, and families who could pay the most bought candles made from a waxy oil in the head of the sperm whale. Benjamin Franklin designed an iron stove in 1741 to heat a room with less wood.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal for iron" -->
Iron furnaces burned charcoal, wood that has been heated with too little air for it to burn away. The heat drives off the water and gases in the wood. What is left is nearly pure carbon, and it burns hotter than wood. The men who made charcoal were called colliers.

**The owners.** Five planters started the Baltimore Iron Works on the Patapsco River in Maryland in 1731. Dr. Charles Carroll, a surgeon, gave 2,200 acres of land and ran the works at first. Two of his cousins, along with Daniel Dulany and Benjamin Tasker, put in money and enslaved workers. In the 1600s, the land around the Patapsco lay within the hunting grounds of the Susquehannock nation, whose villages stood farther north, along the Susquehanna River.

**The workers.** The Baltimore Iron Works opened with 89 workers, and 42 of them were enslaved. Many of its workers were enslaved people or transported convicts, people found guilty of crimes in Britain and sent to the colonies to be sold into 7 to 14 years of forced work. Judges in English courts gave that sentence. Merchants shipped the convicts across the ocean, and merchants and ship captains sold them in the colonies, some of them to iron makers.

The workers hauled cartloads of charcoal, oyster shells and iron ore, the rock that iron comes from, to the top of the furnace in 12-hour shifts. The work went on around the clock, six or seven days a week. The owners did not give the workers enough food. Some workers ran away, and others worked slowly on purpose.

**The profits.** In a good year the partners made back more than half of what they had put in, a profit of 55 percent. By 1785 an advertisement for the Baltimore Iron Works listed "more than 200 Negroes," the word then used for Black people. The people it listed were enslaved.

The surviving records of these early furnaces list the owners. They do not list the men who made the charcoal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What gave the brightest light?" -->
The brightest light in the colonies came from candles of spermaceti, a waxy oil found in the head of the sperm whale. Candle makers turned it into candles that burned brighter and cleaner than any others, and they cost the most. Writers at the New Bedford Whaling Museum call spermaceti "the most valuable product of the Yankee whaling industry," Yankee meaning someone from New England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A stove to save wood" -->
Benjamin Franklin designed what he called the Pennsylvania fireplace in 1741. It was a stove of cast iron, iron poured into a mold while it is melted. Franklin meant it to give more heat and less smoke from less wood than an open fireplace. Few people bought it at first. David Rittenhouse later improved the design, and after that it sold better.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 7 minutes**

The first steam engine in America pumped water out of a copper mine in New Jersey from 1755 on. A fire under a boiler, a closed tank of water, made the steam that ran it.

The iron furnaces still burned charcoal, and they burned it by the acre. Hopewell, in Pennsylvania, used the charcoal from about an acre of forest every day it ran. At Catoctin Furnace in Maryland, enslaved Black workers and some free Black workers cut the wood, made the charcoal and hauled it to the furnace. After dark, spermaceti candles still gave the brightest light.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steam engine in America" -->
Water flooded John Schuyler's copper mine at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey, in 1748, and the mine had to shut down. Schuyler paid an English engine maker, Jonathan Hornblower, 1,000 pounds to send him a "fire engine" and mechanics to set it up. Fire engine was the name people then used for this kind of steam engine.

**The crossing.** The parts arrived in New York in September 1753 on a ship called the *Irene*, after twelve weeks at sea, and Jonathan's son Josiah came with them. The parts were taken to Newark, New Jersey, and put together there. By early 1755 the engine was pumping water out of the mine.

**How it worked.** The engine was a kind invented in England by Thomas Newcomen. The fire under the boiler turned water into steam, and the steam flowed into a cylinder, a tall round tube. Inside the cylinder, the steam filled the space under a piston, a heavy disk that could slide up and down. Then a jet of cold water sprayed into the cylinder. The cold turned the steam back into water, which left almost nothing under the piston, so the weight of the air above pushed the piston down.

The piston was joined to one end of a beam that rocked up and down like a seesaw. The other end of the beam lifted long pump rods that hung down the mine shaft, and the pumps raised the water. Then the weight of the pump rods pulled the piston back up, and the engine started again. Engines of this kind stood about two stories high. Their power was about ten horsepower, a measure based on the work one horse can do.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-hornblower" name="Josiah Hornblower" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Hornblower

> **Who:** The young English engine builder who came over with the first steam engine in America and later ran it.
> **When and where:** The Schuyler copper mine at Second River, now North Arlington, New Jersey, 1753 to 1773.

**The engine house burned the same night he shut the mine.**

For twenty years after it started, the engine worked only part of the time. Josiah Hornblower, who had been about 24 when he came over with it, later ran it himself. In 1761 he and a partner, John Stearndall, leased the mine from the Schuyler family. They paid one seventh of the ore they dug as rent.

**The first fire.** The building around the engine caught fire in March 1762 and burned to the ground. A report in the *New York Mercury* of March 22 states that the engine works had cost nearly ten thousand pounds. People at the time guessed that a careless workman had left a candle burning. The engine was working again by 1767. Richard McCormick, the historian who wrote the engine's history, does not say who repaired it or when.

**Out of fuel.** Hornblower told what happened next in a letter of July 1768, in which he called the engine "the Fire engine." Late in 1767, he had to stop it because he had run out of fuel. He got more, put the old pumps back in, made the repairs and started it again in April 1768.

Soon the vein, the band of ore running through the rock, dipped deep. He tried to follow it with hand pumps, but they could not keep the water out, and his partners had not sent money they owed him.

**The second fire.** On a Monday in July 1768, he stopped the mine and let all the workers go. At about eight o'clock that evening, the engine house caught fire. It burned to ruins in a few minutes while a crowd of people tried to save it. Hornblower wrote that a man had gone into the engine house two hours before to light his pipe, and the man said he could not find any fire there to light it with.

A report in the *Pennsylvania Chronicle* states that the fire was "supposed to be by Design," because no one knew of any fire being used there lately. People thought someone had set it on purpose. Mining at Schuyler's stopped in 1773, and Hornblower stayed in New Jersey for the rest of his life.

**Known:** Half of the engine's cylinder is now in the Smithsonian Institution.
**Not known:** what became of the rest. One account says the engine was broken up around 1800, and another says it was repaired in 1793 and pumped water into the 1800s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-hornblower" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charcoal by the acre" -->
Hopewell Furnace burned the charcoal from about an acre of forest every day it ran. Mark Bird built it on French Creek, in Berks County, Pennsylvania, in 1771. When the furnace was in blast, lit and making iron, it burned up to 800 bushels of charcoal a day, a bushel being a measure for dry goods such as grain.

In a year the furnace used 5,000 to 7,000 cords of wood, from more than 200 acres of woodland. A family heating its home with open fireplaces burned 30 to 40 cords. The owners held between 4,000 and 8,000 acres of forest to keep the furnace supplied. Woodcutters, using only axes, cut an average of two cords a day each.

**Whose land.** The land around Hopewell had belonged to the Lenni Lenape. After the colony's founder, William Penn, left Pennsylvania in 1701, his agent James Logan handled Penn's land deals. Under Logan, the colony's land deals with the Lenni Lenape were often dishonest, and some were frauds, deals built on lies.

By about 1750, nearly all Native people had been driven out of the lower Delaware and Susquehanna river valleys, and most moved west to the Allegheny, Monongahela and Ohio river valleys. The history of Hopewell written for the National Park Service does not name the people who drove them out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How a collier made charcoal" -->
A collier made charcoal in a pile called a pit, built on flat, cleared ground. He drove a green pole, freshly cut and 18 feet long, into the middle of that ground and built a chimney with three corners around it. Then he stacked about 30 cords of wood around the chimney in three layers and covered the whole pile with leaves and dust.

To light the pit, he shoveled red-hot coals from his cooking fire down the chimney. The wood inside smoldered, burning slowly with very little air. The collier watched it day and night, walking on top of the pit to find soft spots where air was getting in and filling them. After 10 days to two weeks, the wood had turned into charcoal. One pit made about 1,100 bushels, enough to run the furnace for a day and a half.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who made the charcoal?" -->
At Catoctin Furnace, in Maryland, enslaved Black workers and free Black workers made it. Four brothers, James, Baker, Roger and Thomas Johnson, built the furnace, and it was making iron by 1776. The Johnson family enslaved more than 80 people there. One of them was a skilled man recorded only as Collier Sam, and the Catoctin Furnace Historical Society's history gives only his name and his trade.

Over the life of the furnace, at least 271 enslaved African Americans worked at Catoctin, along with an unknown number of free African Americans. They mined ore, cut wood, made charcoal, drove wagons and worked the forge, the shop where iron was heated and hammered into shape. Woodcutters cut, hauled and stacked the wood for the colliers, and other workers carried the finished charcoal to the furnace.

**At Hopewell.** Mark Bird enslaved 18 people in 1780: 10 men, 4 women, 3 boys and 1 girl. No one else in Berks County enslaved as many people that year. That same year Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a law to end slavery in stages, but under it mainly children born after the law passed could become free. The last record of slavery at Hopewell is from 1821, when Matthew Brooke freed one person he enslaved.

Hopewell's owners hired free Black workers only for jobs that took little training, as woodcutters, laborers and teamsters, who drove wagons. According to the National Park Service, no record from Hopewell's 112 years shows a Black worker in a skilled job or as a manager.
