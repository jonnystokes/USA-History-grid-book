## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 21 of 37 · Crime, Police, and Justice · 10 eras · about 140 minutes**

**Americans stopped whipping and branding people in public and started locking them in prisons, and from the first colonial courts on, whether a person got a fair trial depended on who that person was.**

Clarence Earl Gideon sat in the Florida state prison at Raiford in 1961, with a five-year sentence for a break-in at a pool hall. He was 50 and had no money for a lawyer. Florida gave free lawyers only to people who could be sentenced to death, so the judge had refused him one. Gideon wrote to the US Supreme Court in pencil. In 1963 all nine justices agreed that a state must give a lawyer to a poor person charged with a serious crime. At his second trial, the jury found him not guilty after an hour.

This chapter follows crime and punishment in order, from the clan laws of the Cherokee to the police killings and trials of the 2020s. In the 1600s judges had people whipped, branded and hanged in front of their neighbors. By 2009 about 1.6 million people were locked inside American prisons. The people who kept order changed too: settlers took turns on a night watch, then cities hired police, then the national government hired agents. Thousands of people got no trial at all. Mobs burned, hanged and shot them, and for most of those killings nobody was ever punished.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did punishment move from the whipping post to the prison cell?
- Who kept order before there were police?
- What happened to the people in mobs who killed without a trial?

**Five minutes?** Read "From street work to the cell, Philadelphia" in 1750 to 1800, then "Francis McIntosh, St. Louis, 1836" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Counterfeiting was a hanging crime", "A cabinet member convicted, 1929", "Leaders in court", "Whose courts: McGirt, 2020". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

When one person killed another, the families of the two people settled it between them, and a life was owed for a life. No nation north of Mexico kept its laws in writing. Among the Cherokee, the dead person's clan had the duty to take a life from the killer's side. In accounts of other nations, the killer's relatives had the duty to give up a life in exchange. Some families took a payment instead: furs, other goods, or wampum, strings or belts of beads made from shells.

**Known:** The rules, which the nations remembered and later wrote down. Some come from the nations' own spoken traditions, and some from accounts by Native and European writers after about 1600.
**Not known:** The name of any person tried or punished before 1500. No written record names one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee law of blood" -->
Among the Cherokee, a killing was settled between two clans. A clan is a large family group. The Cherokee had seven clans, among them the Wolf, the Deer and the Paint, and a child belonged to the mother's clan.

**What the nation's newspaper said.** The editors of the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the Cherokee Nation's own newspaper, printed an article on the old rules in 1829. The article states that the Cherokee "as a nation, had nothing to do with murder." The nation's leaders did not punish a killing. The clans did. The clan of the dead person had the duty to take revenge by killing the killer.

If the killer ran away, his brother or his nearest relative "was liable to suffer in his stead," in the article's words. The clan could kill the brother or the relative in the killer's place. Every killing counted as murder, so a person who killed someone by accident could be killed in return. A man who killed his own brother answered to no law and no clan.

The same article states that about twenty years before 1829, the Cherokee clans met in a council, a large meeting to decide the nation's business, and ended these rules. The Cherokee Nation dates that council to 1810. From then on, murder was a crime against the Cherokee government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Words of comfort instead of revenge" -->
The Haudenosaunee are five nations in what is now New York: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca. Their own tradition says the five nations were once caught in feuds, long runs of killings in which each side took revenge for the last death.

In that tradition, a man named Hiawatha, whose daughters had been murdered, strung shells into wampum. He used the strings to speak words of comfort instead of words of revenge. These words of condolence, comfort for people in grief, became central to the Great Law of Peace, and the five nations joined together under it.

The Haudenosaunee told the Great Law aloud for generations. Two written versions existed by 1910 at Six Nations of the Grand River, a Haudenosaunee community in Ontario. Seth Newhouse, a Mohawk, wrote one, and the chiefs of that community wrote the other. Arthur C. Parker printed them in English in 1916, for the New York State Museum.

**What the written law did to a murderer.** In that version, a chief who committed murder lost his title, and the other chiefs removed him. A war chief then ordered him to leave the land of the five nations and never come back.

An outsider adopted into one of the nations got a warning from a war chief for a first wrong. After a second wrong, the written law says, the person was to be sent out of the land of the five nations for good.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forgiveness at the Green Corn" -->
Once a year, the people of each Muscogee town forgave every crime except murder. The Muscogee, also called the Creek, and other nations of the Southeast did this at the Green Corn Ceremony, held each midsummer when the first corn ripened. Its Muscogee name, the busk, comes from their word for "to fast." People fasted, and they lit the sacred fire again in the town square.

The trader James Adair wrote about the ceremony in the middle 1700s. So did the naturalist William Bartram, who studied plants and animals, and Benjamin Hawkins, a government agent, an official sent to deal with a Native nation. The most detailed descriptions were written in the late 1800s and early 1900s.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 4 minutes**

The only European courts in this land sat at a few Spanish outposts, and there the same officials governed the settlers, commanded the soldiers and judged the cases. Spanish settlers founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565. Juan de Oñate led a Spanish colony into New Mexico in 1598. Everywhere else, the Native nations kept their own laws. The two men who led those colonies, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and Oñate, each decided what happened to hundreds of prisoners.

In a Spanish town, an official called an alcalde acted as the judge. His power covered smaller crimes and quarrels, but in practice he heard most of the cases in his area. A person who lost could appeal, asking a higher judge or the governor himself to change the decision.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Killed without a trial, 1565" -->
The Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés had French Protestants killed after they surrendered to him, in September and October of 1565. Protestants are Christians outside the Catholic Church. The men had no trial.

**The count.** The National Park Service's handbook for Fort Matanzas lists 111 men killed the first time and 134 the second, about 245 in all, with 16 spared each time. Chuck Meide, speaking on the PBS program *Secrets of the Dead*, gives wider ranges for the two killings, from 110 to 200 and from 70 to 150.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Acoma trial, 1599" -->
Juan de Oñate, the leader of the Spanish colony in New Mexico, put about 500 Acoma people on trial in February 1599. He had ordered the attack on their town a month before, and he sat as their judge.

The trial followed a fight at Acoma Pueblo, the Acoma people's town. Spanish soldiers under Juan de Zaldívar came to Acoma in December 1598 and demanded food. Acoma people killed Zaldívar and eleven or twelve of his soldiers. The Acoma people's own account says the soldiers had also raped an Acoma woman, which means they forced her into sex.

Oñate opened an inquiry on December 28, an official hearing to find out what had happened. He asked the Franciscan friars with the colony, Catholic religious brothers, whether a war on Acoma would be just, meaning right and fair. They said yes. In January 1599 Oñate sent Juan de Zaldívar's brother, Vicente de Zaldívar, with about 70 soldiers against Acoma. His soldiers broke into the town, killed Acoma men, women and children, and burned it.

**The count of the dead.** The counts differ. New Mexico's state historian gives about 600. The Pueblo leaders of the All Pueblo Council of Governors give 800, and a National Park Service page gives about 1,500.

The trial of the captives began on February 9, 1599, and lasted three days. Oñate named a defender for the prisoners, and the defender asked for mercy. Acoma witnesses said many of their people had been working in their fields during the first fight. They said that "some wanted to surrender but some refused, and so they fought."

**The sentences.** Oñate gave his sentence on February 12. He sentenced no one to death. Every man over 25 was to have one foot cut off and to be made a slave for twenty years, forced to work for Spanish colonists with no pay and no right to leave. Boys and young men from 12 to 25, and women and girls over 12, were also to be made slaves for twenty years.

Oñate sentenced the old people to be handed over as slaves to the Plains Apache. Two Hopi men, from a different people, had been captured in the fighting. Oñate sentenced each of them to have his right hand cut off and to be sent home to carry word of the punishment to his own people.

**The children.** Oñate declared the Acoma children under 12 innocent. He still ordered them taken from their families and handed to the friars. A National Park Service page states that about 70 girls were sent to convents in Mexico, houses where nuns live, and that in all probability none of them ever came home. The PBS program *POV* states that some scholars believe the children were later sold as slaves. The Pueblo leaders of the All Pueblo Council of Governors give the number of children taken as 60.

**The feet.** The Spanish record states the order, the ages of the prisoners, the twenty-year terms and the taking of the children, and on those points the accounts agree. It states Oñate's order to cut off a foot. It does not say how the cutting was to be done, or whether it was done to each man.

The Acoma people's own history says that 24 men had a foot cut off, and reporters for the Associated Press and NPR give the same number. The historian Marc Simmons first wrote that the feet were cut off, and he later came to doubt it. The historian John L. Kessell doubts it too. He wrote that "the historical record makes no mention of a one-footed Acoma slave."

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 16 minutes**

Judges in the English colonies punished crime on the body and in public. They ordered whippings, brandings and cut-off ears, and they sent people to sit in the stocks where their neighbors could see them. Ordinary settlers in Massachusetts Bay took turns walking a watch to keep order at night. A jail held people waiting for a trial or a punishment, people who had done small wrongs, and people who owed money. Locking people in a prison for years as the punishment itself did not begin until the 1790s.

Colonial judges in Massachusetts Bay and Plymouth also tried Native people who lived near the colonists. Virginia's lawmakers passed a law in 1669 under which a master who beat an enslaved person to death, after the person resisted him, was not charged with a crime. Judges in Massachusetts had 19 people hanged for witchcraft in 1692.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown under martial law, 1610 to 1619" -->
The leaders of the Jamestown colony in Virginia ruled it under martial law, rule by army officers instead of by ordinary courts, from 1610 to 1619. Jamestown stood in Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan people.

Two of the colony's leaders issued the first orders in May and June of 1610, and Sir Thomas Dale added military rules on June 22, 1611. William Strachey printed the whole set in 1612 as the *Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall*. Rule under these laws ended when Sir George Yeardley arrived in Virginia on April 18, 1619.

**The death crimes.** The historian Brent Tarter lists the punishments in the *Encyclopedia Virginia*. The punishment was death for murder, for robbery and for blasphemy, insulting God. It was also death to speak treason, words against the king's rule, to speak against the company that ran the colony, or to trade with Indians without permission.

The printed laws of 1612 also specify death for any man who would "ravish or force any woman, maid or Indian," which means rape. They specify death for "the horrible, and detestable sins of Sodomie" too, and they do not say which acts that word covered.

**The whipping crimes.** The punishment was a whipping for gambling, for missing church, for sex between two unmarried people, for killing farm animals without need, and for stealing crops or farm tools.

The sources disagree about adultery, sex between a married person and someone outside the marriage. The printed laws themselves specify death for it, and Tarter lists it among the whipping crimes.

**What George Percy wrote.** The colonist George Percy wrote an account of the colony's early years in 1625. He wrote about the building of a fort at Henricus in 1611, and about colonists there who ran away to live with the Native people nearby. When they were caught, Dale had them put to death: "Some he apointed to be hanged some burned some to be broken upon wheles others to be Staked and some to be shott to deathe." Percy wrote that Dale did these things "To terrefy the reste," meaning to frighten the other colonists.

Breaking on the wheel was a way of putting a person to death in Europe. The executioner broke the person's arms and legs with a heavy bar or club and tied the body to a large wooden wheel. Percy does not describe how it was done at Jamestown, and he does not explain what "Staked" meant.

Percy also wrote that Dale had men who robbed the colony's storehouse tied to trees and left there until they starved to death. Percy wrote his account partly to defend his own record in Virginia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oñate convicted, 1614" -->
Juan de Oñate was the leader who had sentenced the Acoma people in 1599. Spanish officials later put him on trial in Mexico City for what he had done in New Mexico. They charged him with 30 crimes in 1612 or 1613. They found him guilty of 12 in 1614. Among them were using too much force against the Acoma and hanging two Indians without cause.

The officials banished Oñate from New Mexico for life, ordering him to leave and never return. They also barred him from Mexico City for four or five years and fined him 6,000 ducats, which were Spanish coins. Whether he was later pardoned, officially forgiven and freed from the punishment, is not settled. No record shows that any Acoma person was asked to testify.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The watch and the whipping post in Massachusetts" -->
The court records of the Massachusetts Bay Colony from the early 1630s show who kept order and how the colony's judges punished people. The colonists had settled among the Massachusett people and the Pawtucket, who belonged to a larger group of nations led by Massachusett sachems, or leaders.

From the colony's first year, its judges tried Native people who lived near the colonists. They fined the Massachusett sachem Chickatabut one beaver skin in 1631 for shooting a pig that belonged to Sir Richard Saltonstall. Governor John Winthrop wrote in his journal that Chickatabut paid it right away.

**The watch.** The settlers themselves kept watch at night. On April 12, 1631, the judges ordered a watch of four men every night at Dorchester and four more at Watertown, "to begin att sunsett." Anyone who fired a gun after the watch was set had to pay a fine of 40 shillings, which was English money, or be whipped. In July the judges ordered a watch of six men and an officer every night at Boston.

The watchmen were settlers taking turns, and some of them did not take their turn. The judges fined Daniel Abbott "for refuseing to watch" and Richard Norman for "negligence in watching."

**The whipping post.** Whipping meant being struck on the bare back with a whip, often at a post in a public place. Bartholomew Hill got a whipping for stealing a loaf of bread from John Hoskins, and John Baker got one for shooting at birds on the Sabbath, the day of rest and worship.

John Goulworth confessed to a felony, a serious crime, and the judges had him whipped and then set in the stocks. The stocks were a wooden board with holes that locked a person's ankles while the person sat in a public place. For helping in a crime, John Boggust and John Pickryn had to sit in the stocks at Salem for four hours.

The pillory was a wooden frame on a post, set on a platform in a public place. It locked a person's neck and wrists while the person stood. Britannica's account for students states that it was meant to expose people to "public scorn and ridicule," and to cause "bodily discomfort," meaning the body ached from standing locked in place.

The bilboes were iron rings locked around the ankles and joined to a bar, so the person could not walk away. Edward Seale, punished for drunkenness, sat in the bilboes until the court day ended, "& then to bee severely whiped."

**The branding iron.** Richard Hopkins sold guns, powder and shot to Indians. The court sentenced him in September 1632 to be whipped and "branded with a hott iron on one of his cheekes." Branding meant pressing a red-hot iron into the skin. It burned a mark into the flesh that stayed for life. Robert Scarlett, "a knowen theife," got a whipping in 1635 and the letter T, for thief, branded on his forehead. Then his master, Benjamin Felton, had to send him out of the colony.

Officers of the colony also cut off ears. Three Quakers, members of a Christian group also called the Society of Friends, were held in the Boston prison in September 1658: Christopher Holder, John Copeland and John Rous. Their sentence stated that each man's right ear was to be "cut off by the Hangman."

On September 16 the deputy of the marshal, the colony's officer who carried out the court's orders, came to the prison with a group of men and shut the door. They cut off the right ear of each of the three men. George Bishop, a Quaker, recorded it in his book *New England Judged* in 1661.

**Not known:** How many strokes any of these whippings had, which of the deputy's men cut off the Quakers' ears, and with what tool.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-ratcliffe" name="Philip Ratcliffe" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Ratcliffe

> **Who:** A servant whose ears were cut off for speaking against the Massachusetts government and the church at Salem.
> **When and where:** Boston, June 1631.

**Whipped, ears cut off, fined 40 pounds and banished, for words.**

The judges of the Massachusetts Bay court at Boston sentenced Philip Ratcliffe on June 14, 1631. Their order states that he "shalbe whipped, haue his eares cutt of, fyned 40£, & banished." In today's spelling, he was to be whipped, have his ears cut off, pay a fine of 40 pounds, and leave the colony for good. Governor John Winthrop's journal calls him a servant of a Mr. Cradock.

His crime was "vttering mallitious & scandulous speeches against the govmt & the church of Salem." He had said hateful and shameful things about the colony's government and about the church at Salem. The record says what he said was "proved vpon oath," which means people swore in court that he said it.

Winthrop wrote in his journal that the sentence "was presently executed," which in his day meant it was carried out right away. Neither the court record nor Winthrop says who cut off Ratcliffe's ears, or how. The same judges that day ordered Philip Swaddon whipped for running away from his master, Robert Seely, to go to Virginia.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-ratcliffe" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="When killing an enslaved person was no crime, 1669" -->
The members of Virginia's assembly, the colony's lawmakers, passed "An act about the casuall killing of slaves" in October 1669, while Sir William Berkeley was governor. An enslaved person was a person owned by another person as property and forced to work for no pay.

Under the act, if an enslaved person resisted and died under "correction" by the master, or by someone the master sent, the death "shall not be accompted felony." Correction meant a beating. Under this law, a master who beat an enslaved person to death, after the person resisted him, was not charged with any crime.

The lawmakers gave their reason in the act. They wrote that no one would kill on purpose when the enslaved person was his own "estate," meaning his property. The act is printed in William Waller Hening's collection of Virginia's old laws, the *Statutes at Large*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A murder trial at Plymouth, 1675" -->
The judges of Plymouth Colony tried three Wampanoag men in 1675 for killing another Native man, John Sassamon. Plymouth stood in the country of the Wampanoag. Sassamon, a Christian, had reportedly warned Plymouth's governor that men of the Wampanoag leader Metacom, whom the English called Philip, were planning an attack. Sassamon died at Assawompset Pond on January 29, 1675.

In March the judges ordered one of the three, a man named Tobias, to answer questions about the death. Three men put up their land, worth 100 pounds, as a promise that Tobias would come back to court in June: Tobias himself, a sachem named Tuspaquin, and Tuspaquin's son William.

In June the judges tried Tobias, Tobias's son Wampapaquan and a third man, Mattashunannamo, for Sassamon's murder. The charge stated that they had struck him or twisted his neck until he died, then pushed his body through a hole in the ice of the pond. The three men said they were innocent.

The jury was twelve English colonists. The judges added six Native men to advise the jury. The six had not taken the jurors' oath, but the record states that they "fully concurred," meaning they agreed with the verdict. The jury found all three men guilty, and the judges sentenced them to hang.

Plymouth officials hanged Tobias and Mattashunannamo on June 8, 1675. The judges put off Wampapaquan's hanging for a month. Within that month he was shot to death, and the colony's court record does not say who shot him. Later that June, Metacom's men and the English colonists went to war, starting at Swansea. The English called it King Philip's War.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Salem, 1692" -->
English settlers built Salem on Naumkeag, a home of the Pawtucket people. A plague brought by English traders killed many of the Pawtucket between 1616 and 1619, the City of Salem's tourism office states.

Colonial judges in Massachusetts tried people for witchcraft, using the Devil's power to hurt others, in 1692 and 1693. Accusers named more than 200 people, Smithsonian magazine reports. The judges had 19 of them hanged, and Sheriff George Corwin carried out the hangings. Corwin also had one man pressed to death under stones. At least five more accused people died in jail.

The first accusers were three girls in Salem Village: Betty Parris, who was 9, Abigail Williams, 11, and Ann Putnam Jr., about 12. They said three women were hurting them by witchcraft: Tituba, a woman enslaved in the house of the minister Samuel Parris, and Sarah Good and Sarah Osborn.

The magistrates, or local judges, John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin questioned the three women on March 1, 1692. Tituba denied it. The magistrates kept at her with questions again and again, and then she confessed. Members of the Putnam family later made accusations in dozens of cases.

**The special court.** Governor William Phips set up a special court to try the accused on May 27, 1692. It was called a Court of Oyer and Terminer, words that mean "to hear and to decide," and William Stoughton was its chief judge.

The judges accepted spectral evidence: an accuser's word that the spirit of the accused person had come to her in a dream or a vision and hurt her. Only the accuser could say what she had seen. Stoughton was sure such evidence could be used. The minister Increase Mather wrote: "It were better that ten suspected witches should escape than one innocent person be condemned."

**Pressed to death.** Giles Corey refused to plead guilty or not guilty. Accounts give his age as 71 or 81. Under English law, a court could force a plea by pressing. The prisoner lay on the ground under a wooden board, and officers piled heavier and heavier stones on it until the prisoner answered or died.

Sheriff Corwin made Corey strip naked and lie face up on the ground. Then Corwin placed large rocks on the board one at a time. The pressing went on for two days, and Corey died on the third, September 19, 1692. Because he was never convicted, his children could keep his land under the law. The Massachusetts Historical Society gives that as his reason for refusing to plead.

**A child in the jail.** Sarah Good's daughter Dorothy was a small child when the magistrates had her jailed with her mother in March 1692. The warrant, the written order for her arrest, calls her four years old. Her father later said four or five, and one archive says six.

Her father, William Good, wrote in 1710 that she was kept in prison seven or eight months and "chain'd in the dungeon." He wrote that she was "so hardly used and terrifyed," meaning treated so harshly and frightened so badly, and that she had been a heavy cost to care for ever since. Sarah Good gave birth in jail, and her baby died there. Sarah Good was hanged on July 19, 1692.

Phips shut the special court in October 1692, after accusers named his own wife. The judges of the new court that replaced it would not allow spectral evidence, and they condemned 3 of 56 defendants. Phips had pardoned everyone still held by May 1693. The governor and lawmakers of Massachusetts passed an act in October 1711 that cancelled the convictions of many of the condemned, and they granted 600 pounds to the heirs. The state's lawmakers made a formal apology in 1957.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-nurse" name="Rebecca Nurse" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Nurse

> **Who:** A member of the Salem church whom a jury found not guilty of witchcraft, and who was then convicted and hanged.
> **When and where:** Salem, Massachusetts, March to July 1692.

**A jury found her not guilty, and the judges sent it back.**

Edward and Jonathan Putnam filed a complaint against Rebecca Nurse on March 23, 1692. They said she had hurt Ann Putnam Sr., Ann Putnam Jr. and Abigail Williams by witchcraft. Marshal George Herrick arrested her the next day, on a warrant signed by the magistrates John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin. She had been sick for eight or nine days and could not leave the house.

At her questioning on March 24 she said, "I am as clear as the child unborn." When she moved her hands, the record states, the accusers fell into fits. Thirty-nine of her neighbors signed a statement that they had never had "Any: cause or grounds to suspect her."

Women searched her body for a mark of the Devil. The most experienced of them found nothing that could not come from a natural cause. On June 28 Nurse asked the court in a petition, a written request, for other women to examine her again, two of them midwives, women who help other women give birth. She signed the petition with her mark, because she could not write her name.

At her trial the jury found her not guilty. The judges objected. They pointed to something she had said about two other accused women, Goodwife Hobbs and her daughter: "they used to come among us." Goodwife was the title for a married woman, like "Mrs." today.

The jury went out again. Back in court Nurse did not answer, and one juror, Thomas Fisk, took her silence as evidence against her. The jury then found her guilty. Afterward she explained that she had meant the two women were fellow prisoners. She wrote that she was "something hard of hearing, and full of grief," and that no one had told her how the court took her words.

Governor Phips pardoned her. Several men from Salem then persuaded him to take the pardon back. On July 3 the minister Nicholas Noyes led the members of the Salem church in putting her out of the church, "by unanimous vote," which is called excommunication. Sheriff George Corwin had her hanged on July 19, 1692. Her house in Danvers still stands. Her case file, number 94, is in the University of Virginia's *Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive*.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1700" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Colonial judges punished crime by hurting people where their neighbors could watch: they had people whipped, branded and hanged, and had their ears cut off.
- 1610: Jamestown's leaders put the colony under martial law, with death for murder, robbery and speaking against the company.
- 1669: Virginia's lawmakers wrote that a master who beat a resisting enslaved person to death had committed no felony.
- 1692: Massachusetts judges had 19 people hanged for witchcraft, and the sheriff pressed Giles Corey to death.
**Next:** In the next fifty years the colonies set up separate courts and patrols for enslaved people, and British judges shipped tens of thousands of convicts across the ocean.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 8 minutes**

An enslaved person accused of a crime faced a different kind of trial from a free white person. Under a Virginia law of 1723, a panel of local justices tried an enslaved person accused of a crime that carried death, with no jury. A white defendant in New York could turn away up to twenty jurors without giving a reason, and two enslaved men, Quack and Cuffee, went to trial there in 1741 with no lawyer. White men in South Carolina rode on slave patrols from 1704 to watch and control enslaved people.

Free colonists in Virginia took their quarrels over land and debts to a county court of local justices, which met about once a month. A lender could have a person who could not pay a debt locked in jail. British judges also sent about 52,200 convicts across the ocean to work in the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The county court and the debtors' jail" -->
Local courts sat in the Virginia settlements by 1622, made up of local judges called commissioners of the peace, later called justices of the peace. The justices usually met once a month. The members of Virginia's assembly named these courts county courts in 1642, and by the end of the colonial years Virginia had 61 counties.

The justices gave out licenses to marry and to keep taverns, recorded deeds and wills, and settled quarrels over land and over debts. A deed is a paper showing who owns land. Emily Salmon, writing in the *Encyclopedia Virginia*, calls serving as a justice "the logical stepping stone" to a seat in the House of Burgesses, Virginia's assembly.

**The debtors' jail.** A jail in these years held people waiting for a trial or a punishment, and people who owed money. From the first colonies until the middle 1800s, a lender could have a person who could not pay a debt locked in jail. Under English law, which the colonists brought with them, the prisoner even paid the cost of being kept there.

Robert C. Voigt, writing in the *Encyclopedia of North Carolina*, explains that jailing a debtor gave the lender "a forceful means" of collecting the money. A person in jail could not work to earn it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts shipped from Britain" -->
British judges sent about 52,200 convicts by ship to the American colonies between 1700 and 1775, a punishment called transportation. More than 20,000 of the convicts went to Virginia, and about 80 percent of all of them went to Maryland or Virginia. Emily Jones Salmon gives these numbers in the *Encyclopedia Virginia*.

Members of Parliament, Britain's lawmaking body, passed the Transportation Act in 1718. Under it British officials paid merchants to carry convicts across the ocean, and Jonathan Forward was the first merchant to hold the contract. The convicts were sold as soon as they landed, and Salmon does not name the sellers. Most of them were forced to work for their buyers for seven to fourteen years, mostly in tobacco fields. Others went to tradesmen, shipbuilders and ironworks.

Virginia's lawmakers tried to stop the shipments. They passed a law in 1722 meant to make shipping convicts too expensive. Forward complained, and the members of the Board of Trade, officials in London who watched over the colonies, overturned the law. Benjamin Franklin wrote against the shipments in 1751. The last convict ship reached the James River in Virginia in 1776.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Slave patrols and courts without juries" -->
White South Carolinians set up official slave patrols in 1704, copying the island colony of Barbados. A slave patrol was a group of white men who rode out to watch and control enslaved people. The patrollers were the same men who served in the militia, the colony's part-time citizen army. At first wealthy men, called gentlemen, rode alongside poorer white farmers. The patrollers were paid for the work from 1734 to 1737.

Enslaved people at Stono, in South Carolina, rebelled against the people who enslaved them in 1739. After that, the members of the South Carolina assembly ordered "constant, regular patrols" in the Negro Act of 1740. Negro was an old word for a Black person. In Charleston, the town's night watch patrolled the streets.

**What a patrol did.** A patrol's route ran 10 to 15 miles. In later years a typical patrol was a few men on horseback. They searched the cabins where enslaved people lived, broke up their gatherings and guarded the roads. The *South Carolina Encyclopedia* states that this patrol system lasted until the end of the Civil War.

**Courts with no jury.** A Virginia law of 1723 states how enslaved people were to be tried for crimes that carried death. Justices named for each case tried the accused person at the county courthouse. They could convict on a confession or on the oath of one witness. All of this was done "without the solemnity of a jury," which means with no jury at all. If they found the person guilty, they ordered the execution. The owner of the accused person could come and speak in the person's defense.

The justices could also convict on the word of Black or Native witnesses if it "shall seem convincing" to them. The law calls these witnesses "Negroes, Mulattos, or Indians," old words for Black people, for people with one Black and one white parent or ancestor, and for Native people.

The same law states a punishment for any Black or Native witness found to have lied. The person's ear was to be nailed to the pillory, and the person was to stand there for an hour. Then the ear was to be cut off. An hour later the other ear was to be nailed and cut off the same way.

Then the person was to get 39 lashes of the whip, "well laid on, on his or her bare back, at the common whipping-post." The justices had this warning read aloud to every such witness.

**Not known:** Who was to carry out this punishment, and how often it was done.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New York trials of 1741" -->
New York judges tried about 200 people in 1741 for a plot to burn the city, after a string of fires that began in March. Daniel Horsmanden was one of the three judges. Officials burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 more. They also hanged four white people, two men and two women. John and Sarah Hughson and Peggy Kerry were hanged on June 12.

Burning at the stake meant chaining a person to an upright post with wood piled around it. Then the wood was set on fire, so the person burned to death.

**The witnesses.** The first witness before the grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether a person should be charged with a crime, was Mary Burton. She was a 16-year-old servant of the tavern keeper John Hughson. At first she refused to talk. She began to talk as she was being taken to the dungeon at City Hall. Enslaved witnesses named Sandy and Fortune testified after being promised their lives. The grand jury told Sandy that enslaved people who confessed and named others "were certainly pardoned, and shipped off."

Judge Horsmanden questioned suspects himself before he sent them to the grand jury. White defendants had more protection under New York law: each could turn away up to twenty jurors without giving a reason. Quack and Cuffee, two enslaved men, had no lawyer.

Douglas Linder, who wrote an account of the trials for the Famous Trials project at the University of Missouri-Kansas City, writes of "grave questions about the contours of the suspected conspiracy." He means serious doubts about what the supposed plot really was and who was in it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="quack-and-cuffee" name="Quack and Cuffee" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Quack and Cuffee

> **Who:** Two enslaved men in New York City who were burned at the stake in the trials of 1741.
> **When and where:** New York City, tried and burned in May 1741.

**At the stake, each man was told the other had confessed.**

Quack was enslaved by a painter named Roosevelt, and Cuffee by Adolph Philipse. On May 12 the undersheriff, the sheriff's deputy, put Cuffee in a cell with a white prisoner, Arthur Price. He gave the two men "a tankard of punch now and then," a large cup of a mixed drink, so that Price could draw Cuffee into talking. Price then swore that Cuffee had spoken about the fires.

Attorney General Richard Bradley, the colony's chief lawyer, led the case against the two men. His side called eleven witnesses. Quack and Cuffee had to defend themselves. They called ten witnesses, and most of them said the two men had been somewhere else when the fires started. The men said they were innocent. The jury, Horsmanden wrote, was "soon returned" and found them guilty. Judge Horsmanden sentenced them to be "chained to a stake, and burnt to death" on Saturday, May 30, 1741.

The sheriff brought them to the stake at about three o'clock that afternoon, with piles of wood around them. Horsmanden wrote that the crowd "were very impatient" for the fire. The deputy secretary, a Mr. Moore, and Quack's owner, Mr. Roosevelt, told each man that the other had already confessed. Both men then confessed and named other people.

Moore asked the sheriff to wait while someone asked the governor for a reprieve, an order to put off the burning. The sheriff said he could not take the men back through the crowd without a strong guard. Horsmanden called the governor's answer "conditional and discretionary," meaning it came with conditions and left room for choice. He wrote that "the execution proceeded." He does not name the men who lit the fire.

The names they gave led to more trials. Horsmanden told the story in his own book, *A Journal of the Proceedings*, in 1744.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="crime-justice" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 11 minutes**

Pennsylvania's lawmakers began to replace the whip and the gallows with the prison cell. Before then, a convict in Pennsylvania could be fined, made to pay back what he took, whipped, branded or hanged. From 1786 he could also be put to hard labor in the streets. The lawmakers turned the Walnut Street Jail in Philadelphia into the country's first state prison in 1790. After that, a judge could send a convict there for years, to work and to spend part of the time alone in a cell.

The rights of accused people were written down too. Lawmakers in enough states approved the Bill of Rights, the first ten additions to the Constitution, in 1791, and four of them list rights for accused people. Not everyone got a trial. Armed settlers in Pennsylvania killed twenty Conestoga people in 1763, and no one was ever charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counterfeiting was a hanging crime" -->
Colonial paper money carried the warning "To Counterfeit is Death." Counterfeiting means making fake money. Owen Sullivan was an engraver, a person who cuts designs into metal for printing, and he was born in Ireland. He ran gangs that passed fake bills from New England to New York.

The *Boston Evening Post* printed a report from Providence, Rhode Island, on October 9, 1752. It said that Sullivan "has lately had both his Ears crop'd, and been branded on both his Cheeks with the Letter C." Cropping meant cutting off part of the ear, and the C was for counterfeiting. He had been punished for faking Rhode Island's paper money. The newspaper does not name who cut and branded him.

New York judges later convicted him of faking and passing New York's bills, and he was hanged on May 10, 1756.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Conestoga killings, 1763" -->
Armed white settlers from Paxton Township and nearby places in Pennsylvania killed all twenty people of the Conestoga town in December 1763. No one was ever charged with the killings. The Conestoga were descendants of the Susquehannock people. Their town stood near Lancaster, on land that the Penn family had set aside for them. The land was supposed to be under the Penn family's protection.

Native nations to the west were at war with the British in 1763, in what is called Pontiac's War. The killers claimed that the unarmed Conestoga were hiding spies for the enemy. The historian Michael Goode calls that charge "a fabrication," which means a lie. Earlier in the war, Lieutenant Governor James Hamilton had offered a reward of 25 pounds for each Native scalp. A scalp is the skin and hair cut away from the top of a person's head.

**The first attack.** Benjamin Franklin wrote about the killings in a pamphlet in 1764. He counted twenty people in the town: seven men, five women and eight children. At daybreak on December 14, 1763, fifty-seven men rode in with guns, short swords and hatchets. They found six people at home, three men, two women and a boy. The men shot them, stabbed them and killed them with hatchets.

Shehaes was a very old man who had been at a treaty meeting with William Penn in 1701. He was in his bed, and they cut him to pieces there. The attackers scalped all six. Then they burned the houses.

**The workhouse.** Lancaster's magistrates put the other fourteen Conestoga people in Lancaster's workhouse, a building where poor people were made to work, to keep them safe. On December 27 about fifty of the Paxton men broke open the workhouse door. Franklin wrote that the families knelt, with the children holding on to their parents, and said they were innocent.

The men killed all fourteen with guns and hatchets, among them children about a year old and babies still nursing. Will Soc was shot at the door. A company of British soldiers stationed nearby did nothing to stop the attack.

Franklin named some of the dead. They included Shehaes, his daughter Peggy, and her husband John Smith, a Cayuga, with their child of about three. They also included Betty and her son Peter, and Sally, whose Native name was Wyanjoy.

Governor John Penn offered a reward for the killers' names. In January 1764, as many as 200 of the Paxton men marched toward Philadelphia. They said they were protesting the government's failure to protect them, and they wanted to reach the Native refugees on Province Island, near the city. They stopped at Germantown when they heard that the city's militia had gathered. Franklin worked out a compromise: the marchers agreed to break up, and their leader, Matthew Smith, was allowed into the city to publish their complaints.

No one ever came forward with the killers' names. A modern introduction to Franklin's pamphlet, published by Miami University, states that no one was ever charged and tried.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Three trials in Boston, 1770" -->
Judges in Boston heard three murder trials in 1770. One was of a customs informer who had shot a boy. The other two were of a British captain and of eight British soldiers.

Ebenezer Richardson fired a gun from his house into a crowd of boys on February 22, 1770, and killed Christopher Seider, who was 10 or 11. Richardson was a customs informer, a man who told on smugglers to the customs officials, the officers who collected taxes on goods brought in by ship. A jury found Richardson guilty of murder on April 21. The judges thought he was innocent, and they put off sentencing him.

**The soldiers.** British soldiers fired into a crowd on March 5, 1770, and killed five people. Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray and James Caldwell died that night. Samuel Maverick, 17, died the next morning, and Patrick Carr died nearly two weeks later. Captain Thomas Preston and eight soldiers were arrested and jailed.

No lawyer in Boston would defend them until John Adams agreed, with Josiah Quincy and Robert Auchmuty. Adams later wrote that in a free country, a lawyer "ought to be the very last thing that [an] accused Person should want." In his day, want meant lack: an accused person must never be left without a lawyer.

A jury found Preston not guilty on October 30. The soldiers' trial began on November 27. After two and a half hours, the jury found six soldiers not guilty. It found Hugh Montgomery and Mathew Kilroy guilty of manslaughter, killing someone without planning to, and manslaughter could be punished by death. The two men used an old English rule called benefit of clergy, which let a first-time offender who could read escape a death sentence once.

Instead of being hanged, each man was branded on the hand, at the base of the right thumb. On December 14, 1770, Sheriff Stephen Greenleaf pressed a red-hot iron onto each man's thumb in the courtroom, and it burned a mark into the skin that stayed for life. John Adams remembered in 1822 that when the sheriff came near, the two soldiers "burst into tears." The *Boston Gazette* reported that after the branding they were "discharged," which means let go.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From street work to the cell, Philadelphia" -->
Pennsylvania's lawmakers tried two new punishments in place of hanging between 1786 and 1790. The historian Louis Masur found that in the ten years before 1786, Pennsylvania officials had hanged about 40 people for robbery or burglary.

**The wheelbarrow men.** The lawmakers passed a law in 1786 under which judges sentenced convicts to "continued hard labour publicly and disgracefully imposed." People called the prisoners "wheelbarrow men." Ann Warder wrote in her diary on March 30, 1787, that the convicts cleaned the streets with "an iron collar around their neck and waist to which a long chain is fashioned and at the end a heavy ball."

Their heads were shaved, and a guard went with each gang. Warder wrote that at first the prisoners "preferred death" to being shown in public this way. Thirty wheelbarrow men escaped in one night in October 1788. The lawmakers ended the law by 1790.

**The reformers.** Thirty-seven Philadelphians formed the Philadelphia Society for Alleviating the Miseries of Public Prisons in 1787, a group to ease the suffering in the prisons. Among them were the doctor Benjamin Rush, Benjamin Franklin and Bishop William White. They drew on the ideas of John Howard, an English prison reformer.

In the city's old jail, men, women and children were held together, whatever their crime. Annie Anderson, writing in the *Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia*, states that the sheriff there forced money out of prisoners, sold them liquor from a bar and held back their food.

**The cells.** Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed a new law in April 1790. Under it the Walnut Street Jail became the Walnut Street Prison, a state prison for convicts from all of Pennsylvania. The lawmakers ordered a separate building of cells, called a penitentiary house, for "the more hardened and atrocious offenders," the convicts who had done the worst crimes. The word penitentiary comes from penitence, being sorry for wrongdoing.

Each cell was six feet wide, eight feet long and nine feet high, and thirty-six of them were built. Judges could sentence a prisoner to time alone in one of these cells, which is called solitary confinement.

The law professor David Shapiro studied the prison's records for the *Harvard Law Review* in 2019. He found that judges sent 748 people to the prison between 1795 and 1800, and sentenced 29 of them to time in solitary confinement. Margaret Price, convicted of counterfeiting in 1795, got four years with four months in solitary.

Prisoners worked in a nail factory inside the prison. Keepers locked a prisoner who refused to work in a cell. Caleb Lownes wrote in 1799 that such a prisoner stayed there "some weeks, without labour, bed, or furniture, of any kind, except a vessel to hold his drink, and another his mush, and a blanket."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rights for the accused, 1791" -->
Lawmakers in enough states approved the first ten amendments to the Constitution, called the Bill of Rights, on December 15, 1791. An amendment is an addition or change to the Constitution. Four of them list rights of people accused of crimes:

- The Fourth Amendment requires officers to have a warrant, a judge's written order, to search a home. A judge may give one only when there is good reason to believe a crime took place.
- The Fifth forbids forcing anyone to be a witness against himself. That means no one can be forced to confess, or to say anything that could help prove him guilty.
- The Sixth requires, for an accused person, "a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury," a jury whose members favor neither side, and the help of a lawyer.
- The Eighth forbids "cruel and unusual punishments" and bail set too high. Bail is money paid to the court so an accused person can go free until the trial.

At first these rules applied only to the federal government and its courts. State courts did not have to follow them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Patrick Lyon

> **Who:** A Philadelphia blacksmith held three months in the Walnut Street Prison for a bank robbery he did not commit.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1798 to January 1799.

**He walked 150 miles to clear his name and was locked up.**

Patrick Lyon, a Philadelphia blacksmith born in Scotland, did a rush job for the Bank of Pennsylvania in 1798. He changed the locks on two of its iron vault doors. It was his last job before he left the city. He told the bank's carpenter that the doors were not right for a bank and the locks were not safe. A stranger came to his shop with the carpenter and spent a long time looking at the doors, keyholes and locks.

Then yellow fever spread through Philadelphia, and about 1,300 people died of it. Lyon left the city by boat for Lewes, Delaware, with his 19-year-old apprentice, a young man learning his trade. The apprentice died of the fever there.

On the night of August 31, while Lyon was away, someone took $162,821 from the bank's vault without breaking in. Lyon heard that he was the main suspect, and he walked back to Philadelphia to clear his name. "I had come 150 miles to surrender myself," he wrote.

High Constable John Haines, the chief of the city's constables, wanted the $2,000 reward. He and the bank's officers questioned Lyon. The bank's officers were sure that the man who changed the locks had made an extra key. A magistrate sent Lyon to the Walnut Street Prison and set his bail at $150,000.

Lyon called the prison "cold, damp, unwholesome and solitary." His cell was 12 feet by 4 feet. "I read until I was tired and walked until I was weary," he wrote. He caught yellow fever there and lived.

The robbers were Isaac Davis, a carpenter, and the bank's porter, Thomas Cunningham, who died of the fever. Davis was the stranger who had studied the locks. He confessed and gave back the money, and the governor promised him a pardon. Davis seems never to have spent a day in prison. The bank's officers still said Lyon had helped by making a key. He stayed in prison until January 1799, when a grand jury refused to charge him.

Lyon told what had happened to him in a book, his 1799 *Narrative*. He sued the bank's officers and the constable for keeping him in prison without cause. A jury awarded him $12,000 in 1805. The men he sued appealed, and in 1807 they paid him $9,000 to settle the case. The painter John Neagle later painted him as *Pat Lyon at the Forge*.
