## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1908 the US attorney general put 34 federal agents, agents of the national government, to work. By the 1930s the staff of their Bureau of Investigation kept the nation's fingerprint files and worked in a crime laboratory. Federal agents hunted gangsters such as John Dillinger, and they raided the gangs that sold alcohol during Prohibition, the years when making and selling it was against the law. Mobs still took Black prisoners out of jails and killed them. In Chattanooga in 1906, the sheriff sent his guards away on the night a mob came for Ed Johnson. In the South, some deputies whipped Black suspects until they confessed. Three times in the 1930s, the justices of the US Supreme Court threw out verdicts because of how Black defendants had been treated.

In Tulsa in 1921, white men, many of them made deputies by the police and given a police officer's powers, burned the Black district of Greenwood and killed as many as 300 people. No white person was sent to prison for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ed-johnson" name="Ed Johnson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ed Johnson

> **Who:** A Black man wrongly convicted, or found guilty, of rape and sentenced to death, then lynched after the justices of the US Supreme Court ordered that he be kept safe.
> **When and where:** Chattanooga, Tennessee, January to March 1906.

A jury found Ed Johnson guilty of a rape he did not commit. Rape means forcing a person into sex. The justices of the US Supreme Court stopped his hanging and ordered the sheriff to protect him. A mob then lynched him on a bridge in Chattanooga. To lynch means to kill a person as a mob, with no trial.

On January 23, 1906, a man attacked Nevada Taylor, a young white woman, from behind as she walked home from a streetcar stop. He choked her with a leather strap. On January 24 or 25, Ed Johnson, a Black man who worked as a porter at the Last Chance Saloon, was arrested. The court record does not name the officer who arrested him.

A witness, Will Hixson, may have given the police Johnson's name to collect a $375 reward. At the trial, thirteen witnesses swore that Johnson was at the saloon at the time of the attack. An all-white, all-male jury found him guilty of rape on February 11, and the judge sentenced him to hang. The court had chosen his lawyers, and they dropped his appeal. An appeal is a request for a higher court to look at the case again.

Two Black lawyers, Noah Parden and Styles Hutchins, took up the appeal. They asked a federal court for habeas corpus, an order to review whether a prisoner is held lawfully, and the federal judges turned them down on March 10. Parden went to Washington and met Justice John Marshall Harlan of the US Supreme Court on March 17. The justices issued a stay, an order that stopped the hanging until they could hear the case. They ordered Sheriff Joseph Shipp to keep Johnson safe. The order is dated March 19 in the Court's later opinion, and one account dates Harlan's stay to March 18.

The order appeared in Chattanooga's evening newspapers on March 19. That evening Shipp sent away the usual guards and left only the night jailer, Jeremiah Gibson. Between nine o'clock and midnight, a mob broke into the jail and took Johnson to the county bridge over the Tennessee River. The men put a rope around his neck, threw it over a beam and pulled him up. The rope broke or slipped, and he fell. They pulled him up a second time and shot him. One account says a deputy sheriff fired five more shots into his head. Shipp came to the jail during the break-in and made no effort to stop the mob or to send for help. Johnson's gravestone gives his last words: "God Bless you all. I AM A Innocent Man."

For the first and only time, the justices of the US Supreme Court held a criminal trial themselves. The charge was contempt of court, the crime of defying a court's order. Twenty-five men were tried. In May 1909 the justices found six guilty: Shipp, Luther Williams, Nick Nolan, Jeremiah Gibson, Henry Padgett and William Mayes. Shipp, Williams and Nolan got 90 days in jail, and the other three got 60 days. Shipp was welcomed home as a hero. The justices' ruling in that trial, *United States v. Shipp* (1909), states the facts of the lynching and of the contempt trial.

In February 2000, Judge Doug Meyer of the Hamilton County Criminal Court set aside Johnson's conviction. He ruled that the all-white jury and the trial judge's refusal to move the trial out of Chattanooga had made it unfair. The Death Penalty Information Center, a group that collects facts about the death penalty, reported that a memorial to Johnson was dedicated in Chattanooga on September 19, 2021.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ed-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A federal police force, 1908 to 1934" -->
On July 26, 1908, Attorney General Charles Bonaparte ordered the lawyers of the Department of Justice to send most of their investigations to his chief examiner, Stanley W. Finch. Finch handed each one to one of 34 agents. Nine of the agents came from the Secret Service, and Bonaparte hired 25 himself. The group was later called the Bureau of Investigation. Today it is the Federal Bureau of Investigation, or FBI.

In 1924 Attorney General Harlan Fiske Stone made J. Edgar Hoover, then 29, the Bureau's director. Hoover fired agents he called "political hacks," men who had their jobs as favors from politicians. He required new agents to pass background checks and physical tests, and from January 1928 he trained them. The Bureau had 441 agents in 1924 and 339 five years later.

On July 1, 1924, Hoover set up a division to hold the country's fingerprint files. A fingerprint is the pattern of ridges on a fingertip, and it is different for every person. A print left on a glass or a gun can show who touched it. Police anywhere could send in prints and ask whether they matched a known criminal. Bureau officials began keeping the national crime counts in 1930. The FBI's own history gives November 24, 1932, as the official birthday of its crime laboratory, which agent Charles Appel did most of the work to build.

In June 1933 gunmen killed four lawmen in Kansas City. In July 1934 agents led by Melvin Purvis found the gangster John Dillinger in Chicago and shot him dead as he left a movie theater. The FBI's history pages record these events.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs and the courts, 1908 and 1930" -->
In August 1908, white mobs attacked Black residents of Springfield, Illinois, for nearly three days. They lynched two men, Scott Burton and William Donegan, and destroyed dozens of homes. The mobs drove nearly 2,000 Black residents out of the city for good. Officials called out the state militia, the state's part-time soldiers. About 150 people were arrested, and few of them were convicted, a National Park Service article states.

On August 7, 1930, a white mob of 5,000 to 10,000 people broke into the Grant County Jail in Marion, Indiana. They beat and hanged two Black men, Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith, both 19. A third prisoner, James Cameron, 16, survived. He was later convicted as an accessory, a person who helps with a crime, and served four years. He was pardoned in 1993, which means he was officially forgiven. The NAACP, a group that worked for the rights of Black Americans, named 27 suspects in the mob. All-white juries found the leaders who were tried not guilty. The Equal Justice Initiative, a group in Montgomery, Alabama, founded by the lawyer Bryan Stevenson, records these killings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa, 1921: no one sent to prison" -->
On the night of May 31, 1921, as many as 10,000 white Tulsans attacked Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and the police helped organize them. Greenwood stood on Muscogee (Creek) land. The attack lasted into the afternoon of June 1. The attackers burned 35 city blocks and killed Black residents. Estimates of the dead run from 50 to 300. Lawyers at the US Department of Justice wrote in 2025 that the massacre "killed as many as 300 Tulsans, perhaps even more." Some bodies were thrown in the Arkansas River, and some were buried in unmarked mass graves.

The attack began with an arrest. Tulsa police arrested Dick Rowland, a 19-year-old Black man, after a white man accused him of attacking Sarah Page, the white woman who ran an elevator he used. Police Chief John Gustafson later told a federal agent that Page had no scratches or bruises. She said Rowland had not said anything bad to her, and she would not press charges. A white crowd gathered at the courthouse. Armed Black men from Greenwood also came to the courthouse.

Tulsa police then made hundreds of white men special deputies, men given a police officer's powers for a time. The Justice Department's 2025 review states that many of them had been calling for a lynching and had been drinking. Police officers helped organize these deputies into the forces that attacked Greenwood. At Gustafson's later trial, a witness named John Oliphant said the police "were the chief fellows setting fires." The 2025 review reports that Oliphant named two men as the chief arsonists, the leaders in setting the fires: a police officer named Brown, and a man called "Cowboy Long." Dr. A. C. Jackson, a Black surgeon, walked out of his house with his hands up to surrender. A young white man shot him, and Jackson died of his wounds. No one was ever charged with killing him.

Governor James Robertson declared martial law, rule by the military instead of by ordinary officials, at 11:30 in the morning on June 1. Police, special deputies and National Guard soldiers arrested Black Tulsans whether or not anyone suspected them of a crime. They held between 4,000 and 6,000 people under armed guard. The camps were at Convention Hall, at McNulty Park, a baseball park, and at the fairgrounds. Guard officers arrested some white residents for stealing, but they held no white residents in the camps.

A state grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether to charge people with crimes, heard almost 200 witnesses. Its members charged about 70 men, many of them Greenwood's Black leaders, with starting a "riot." On June 17 the *Tulsa Tribune* ran the headline "Roundup of 64 Indicted Blacks Is On: No Warrants Issued for Whites." Indicted means charged with a crime. The grand jurors' report, printed on June 26, identified the armed Black men who had gone to the courthouse as a "direct cause." Most of the cases were later dismissed, which means thrown out, including the one against Dick Rowland. Some white men were accused of looting, which means stealing during a riot, and none of them went to prison. No white person was ever sent to prison for the killings or the burning.

The grand jurors also charged Police Chief Gustafson with neglect of duty, which means failing to do his job, and with dishonest dealings from before the massacre. He was convicted and removed from his job. Less than a year before the massacre, a mob had taken a white prisoner, Roy Belton, from the same jail and lynched him, and Gustafson had ordered his police not to step in.

In February 2001 the members of a state commission published a report on the massacre that recommended paying the survivors. In January 2025 lawyers in the Civil Rights Division of the US Department of Justice published their own review, *Review and Evaluation: Tulsa Race Massacre*. The review states that police and white residents worked together in the attack. It states that if today's civil rights laws had been in force in 1921, the attackers could have been tried and convicted. The youngest of them would now be more than 115 years old, so no one can be tried.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Osage murders, 1921 to 1929" -->
In the early 1920s, men murdered members of the Osage Nation in Oklahoma to take their oil money. The Osage earned more than $30 million in 1923 from oil under their land, the Osage reservation. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation. Osage people held headrights, shares of that money, and when an owner died, a person who was not Osage could get the share. Under a 1921 law, local judges appointed white guardians to control the money of many Osage adults. The judges handed many of these jobs to their friends. Guardians stole from the Osage people whose money they controlled, and some guardians and husbands killed Osage people to inherit their headrights.

The cattleman William Hale, known as the "King of the Osage Hills," set out to get the headrights of Mollie Burkhart's family. Mollie was married to his nephew, Ernest Burkhart. If Mollie's sister Anna, their mother and two more sisters died in that order, all their headrights would pass to Ernest. Hale could then control them. Federal agents later proved that Hale ordered the murders. Anna Brown was shot on May 22, 1921. Her mother, Lizzie Q. Kyle, died in July 1921, and people suspected poison. Their cousin Henry Roan was shot in January 1923, on Hale's orders, for his insurance money. On March 10, 1923, men blew up the house of Mollie's sister Rita Smith. The bomb killed Rita, her husband Bill and Nettie Brookshire, who worked in their house. Mollie herself was probably being poisoned.

Counts of the Osage people murdered in these years differ. The Oklahoma Historical Society's encyclopedia gives about 24, an FBI history page gives at least two dozen, and the Osage Nation counts more than 60. Someone also threw a lawyer, W. W. Vaughn, from a train. The FBI's history page does not name his killer.

In April 1923 members of the Osage Tribal Council asked federal officials to send detectives, and Bureau of Investigation officials sent agents. Tom White led four agents who worked undercover, in secret and under false jobs, as a cattle buyer, an insurance salesman, an oil prospector and an herb doctor. Ernest Burkhart pleaded guilty in June 1926 and got a life sentence. A jury convicted Hale in January 1929, and he was sent to prison for life. Kelsie Morrison and John Ramsey also got life sentences. Hale, Ramsey and Burkhart were later let out on parole, which means let out early and watched by officials. In 1965 Governor Henry Bellmon pardoned Burkhart, which means he officially forgave him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A cabinet member convicted, 1929" -->
Albert Fall was secretary of the interior, a member of the president's cabinet in charge of federal lands. He leased two oil fields that the government kept for the navy to two oil men. The fields were Teapot Dome in Wyoming and Elk Hills in California. Edward Doheny gave Fall a $100,000 "loan" with no interest, and Harry Sinclair gave him about $300,000 in bonds and cash.

On October 25, 1929, a jury found Fall guilty of taking a bribe, a secret payment for a favor, from Doheny. He was sentenced to one year in prison and a $100,000 fine. He was the first person convicted of a crime he committed as a cabinet member. A jury found Doheny not guilty of paying the bribe. Sinclair was jailed for contempt of Congress, which means refusing to obey Congress, and for trying to influence a jury. His term was six months by one account and nine by another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of convict leasing in Alabama, 1928" -->
Alabama's officials rented county prisoners, more than 95 percent of them Black, to coal companies until 1928. This was called convict leasing. The companies' bosses forced the prisoners to work in the mines and whipped them for talking back, for trying to escape and to make them work harder. On April 8, 1911, an explosion at the Banner mine killed 123 Black county prisoners.

James Knox, a prisoner from Mobile, was killed at the Flat Top mine in 1924. His death was first reported as a suicide by swallowing poison. An autopsy, a doctor's examination of the body after death, showed that his heart had failed and that someone had injected the poison into his body after he died. The City of Birmingham's history of the case states that his fatal injuries came from being whipped with a steel wire and held in hot water. The historian Mary Ellen Curtin, writing in the *Encyclopedia of Alabama*, describes him as a prisoner tortured by being lowered into a vat of boiling water. Neither account names the men who whipped him, put him in the water or injected the poison. On June 30, 1928, Alabama's officials ended convict leasing, the last in any state to do so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliot-ness-al-capone" name="Eliot Ness and Al Capone" movie="The Untouchables (1987)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliot Ness and Al Capone

> **Who:** The Chicago gang boss who sold illegal beer and liquor during Prohibition, and the federal agent whose team raided his breweries.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1925 to 1932.
> **Movie:** *The Untouchables* (1987), a film directed by Brian De Palma. It is a drama, not a documentary, and it changes many of the facts.

Al Capone went to prison for not paying his taxes, after years of running a gang that sold illegal alcohol. From 1920 to 1933, under the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution, making and selling alcohol was against the law. The ban was called Prohibition. Capone became the boss of a Chicago gang in 1925, after its leader, Johnny Torrio, was wounded and quit. His men made and sold beer and liquor.

In 1929 federal officials hired Eliot Ness, who was 26, to run the Prohibition agents in Chicago. Ness's team grew from six agents to about ten. From March 1931 they raided Capone's stills, where liquor was made, and his breweries. Gangsters offered them bribes, and the agents turned them down. A Chicago newspaper writer, Charles Schwarz, called them "untouchables."

Capone was arrested several times. On May 17, 1929, police in Philadelphia arrested him for carrying a hidden gun, and he served nine months. In February 1931 a federal judge sentenced him to six months for contempt of court. Agents of the Treasury Department built the case that sent him away for years. On October 18, 1931, a jury found him guilty of tax evasion, which means not paying taxes that are owed. On November 24 the judge sentenced him to eleven years in federal prison and fined him $50,000. He also had to pay $215,000 in back taxes.

On May 3, 1932, Ness was one of the agents who took Capone to the train for the federal prison in Atlanta. It is the only time the two men are known to have met. Capone later served part of his sentence at Alcatraz, a federal prison. He was released on November 16, 1939, with brain damage caused by syphilis, a disease passed on through sex. Ness died of a heart attack in 1957. His book *The Untouchables* came out months after his death. The FBI's history page on Capone and a *TIME* article on Ness describe their lives.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliot-ness-al-capone" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beaten into confessing: the third degree" -->
The "third degree" meant police using cruelty to force a prisoner to confess. In May 1929 President Herbert Hoover set up a national commission to study crime and law enforcement. The commission's 1931 report on police in 15 cities states that "physical brutality, protracted questioning, threats, prolonged illegal detention" were "widespread." Protracted questioning means questioning that goes on for hours or days. Prolonged illegal detention means holding a person for a long time when the law does not allow it.

In Mississippi on March 30, 1934, a man named Raymond Stewart died, and three Black men were later charged with his murder. That night Deputy Sheriff Dial and a group of white men seized Arthur Ellington, a Black man. They hanged him by a rope from the limb of a tree, let him down, and hanged him again. When he still said he was innocent, they tied him to the tree and whipped him. Days later the deputy arrested him again and whipped him until he agreed to confess.

On April 1 the same deputy, the jailer and other white men made Ed Brown and Henry Shields take off their clothes. They bent the two men over chairs and cut their backs open with a leather strap that had buckles on it. They kept whipping until the men confessed in the words the deputy demanded. The three men were charged with Stewart's murder on April 4, tried the next day and sentenced to death. In court the deputy admitted the whippings. Speaking of Ellington's whipping, he said it was "Not too much for a negro." He added, "not as much as I would have done if it were left to me." Negro was a word used then for a Black person.

In 1936, in *Brown v. Mississippi*, all the justices of the US Supreme Court agreed to throw out the convictions. Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote: "The rack and torture chamber may not be substituted for the witness stand." The rack was an old device for torturing a person by stretching the body.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Scottsboro cases, 1931 to 1937" -->
On March 25, 1931, nine Black teenagers and young men, aged 13 to 20, were riding a freight train in Alabama. When the train stopped near the town of Paint Rock, local police and a mob seized them. Two white women on the train, Victoria Price and Ruby Bates, accused them of rape. The nine were Olen Montgomery, Clarence Norris, Haywood Patterson, Ozie Powell, Willie Roberson, Charlie Weems, Eugene Williams, and the brothers Andy and Roy Wright.

Juries sentenced eight of them to death. At the trial of Roy Wright, who was 13, the jurors could not agree between death and life in prison, and the judge declared a mistrial, which means the trial ended with no verdict. In 1932, in *Powell v. Alabama*, the justices of the US Supreme Court ordered new trials because the young men had not had real lawyers.

Ruby Bates later said that Price had made up the story to avoid being charged with vagrancy, which means having no home or job. Bates then testified for the defense. Judge James Horton set aside a guilty verdict against Patterson. In 1935, in *Norris v. Alabama*, the justices threw out the convictions again, because officials had kept Black people off the county's jury lists. Seven more years of trials followed. The men were let out of prison between 1943 and 1976. In 2013 Governor Robert Bentley of Alabama signed a law that led to pardons for all nine. A pardon officially forgives a person for a crime. Daren Salter describes the cases in the *Encyclopedia of Alabama*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000, the number of people in American prisons grew more than eight times over. State and federal prisons held about 166,000 people with sentences in 1950 and about 1.38 million at the end of 2000. At the end of 2000, about 46 of every 100 sentenced prisoners were Black. Officials of the Bureau of Justice Statistics, part of the US Department of Justice, counted these numbers. From the 1970s on, lawmakers in Congress and the states wrote long prison terms that judges had to give, many of them for drug crimes.

Between 1961 and 1968, the justices of the US Supreme Court set new rules for police and courts. Police could not use evidence from an illegal search. State courts had to give a lawyer to a poor person charged with a serious crime. Police had to warn people of their rights before questioning them. In 1972 the justices struck down the death-penalty laws then in use, and in 1976 they allowed new ones. Some trials ended with no one convicted, among them the 1955 murder trial for the killing of Emmett Till and the 1992 state trial of the officers who beat Rodney King.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="emmett-till" name="Emmett Till" movie="Till (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Emmett Till

> **Who:** A 14-year-old Black boy from Chicago, kidnapped and murdered by two white men whom an all-white jury then found not guilty.
> **When and where:** Money and Sumner, Mississippi, August and September 1955.
> **Movie:** *Till* (2022), a feature film about his mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, and her fight after his death.

Emmett Till was 14 when two white men kidnapped and killed him in Mississippi, and an all-white, all-male jury set them free. In August 1955 Till, who lived in Chicago, was visiting relatives near Money, Mississippi. On August 24 he went into Bryant's Grocery and Meat Market, owned by a white couple, Roy and Carolyn Bryant. As Carolyn Bryant left, he whistled at her. Lawyers at the US Department of Justice tell these facts in their 2021 notice closing the case.

Between 2 and 3:30 in the morning of August 28, Roy Bryant and his half-brother, J. W. Milam, came to the house of Till's great-uncle, Mose Wright, who was 64. Milam carried a pistol and a flashlight. The two men took the boy away by force, with at least one other person.

Three days later his body was found in the Tallahatchie River. His killers had beaten him on his back and hips and shot him above the right ear, and one eye had been forced from its socket. The men had fastened a cotton gin fan around his neck with barbed wire to sink the body. The Department of Justice gives the fan's weight as 75 pounds, and another account gives 70. He was identified by his father's silver ring. His mother, Mamie Till Bradley, later Mamie Till-Mobley, held an open-casket funeral in Chicago so that people could see what had been done to him.

Bryant and Milam were tried for murder in the Tallahatchie County Courthouse in Sumner in September 1955. Women and Black people were kept off the jury. The Justice Department's 2021 notice does not name the officials who kept them off. Mose Wright stood up in court and pointed to Milam as one of the men. On September 23 the all-white, all-male jury found both men not guilty after 67 minutes. One juror said, "If we hadn't stopped to drink pop, it wouldn't have taken that long." In November a grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether to charge people with a crime, refused to charge them with kidnapping, although they had admitted taking the boy.

In January 1956 Bryant and Milam told the writer William Bradford Huie, for *Look* magazine, that they had kidnapped and murdered Till. The magazine paid them between $3,600 and $4,000. They could not be tried again for the murder. Under the rule called double jeopardy, a person who has been found not guilty cannot be tried a second time for the same crime.

FBI agents reopened the case in 2004. In February 2007 a Mississippi grand jury did not charge Carolyn Bryant. Justice Department lawyers reviewed the case again after 2017 and closed it in December 2021. They stated that they could not charge anyone with a federal crime. On July 25, 2023, President Joe Biden made places connected to Emmett Till and his mother a national monument.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="emmett-till" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="clarence-earl-gideon" name="Clarence Earl Gideon" movie="Gideon's Trumpet (1980)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Clarence Earl Gideon

> **Who:** A poor man in Florida who was refused a lawyer, wrote to the US Supreme Court from prison in pencil, and won the right to a lawyer for every person charged with a serious crime.
> **When and where:** Panama City, Florida, 1961 to 1963.
> **Movie:** *Gideon's Trumpet* (1980), a television film with Henry Fonda as Gideon, based on Anthony Lewis's 1964 book.

Clarence Earl Gideon wrote to the US Supreme Court from prison. In his case the justices ruled that a state must give a lawyer to anyone charged with a serious crime who cannot pay for one. On June 3, 1961, someone broke into the Bay Harbor Pool Room in Panama City, Florida. The thief took coins from the jukebox and the cigarette machine, and beer, wine and soda. A 22-year-old man named Henry Cook said he had seen Gideon leave with his pockets full of coins.

Gideon, who was 50, had no money for a lawyer. Under Florida's law, only people who could be sentenced to death got free lawyers, so Judge Robert McCrary Jr. refused to give him one. Gideon defended himself in court. The jury convicted him on August 4, 1961, and the judge sentenced him to five years in prison.

From the state prison at Raiford, Gideon wrote to the US Supreme Court in pencil, asking the justices to hear his case. The justices gave him a lawyer, Abe Fortas. On March 18, 1963, all nine justices agreed, in a ruling written by Justice Hugo Black, that a state must give a lawyer to a person charged with a felony who cannot pay. A felony is a serious crime.

At Gideon's second trial, on August 5, 1963, a local lawyer, W. Fred Turner, showed that Cook had lied at the first trial about his own criminal record. The jury found Gideon not guilty after an hour. He died in 1972.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="clarence-earl-gideon" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rules for the police station, 1961 to 1968" -->
The justices of the Supreme Court wrote several more rules for police and courts in these years.

On May 23, 1957, Cleveland police forced their way into the home of Dollree Mapp. They waved a paper they called a search warrant, a judge's written order allowing a search. No such paper was ever shown in court. They searched the house and found books they called obscene, meaning too sexual for the law to allow. She was sentenced to one to seven years. On June 19, 1961, in *Mapp v. Ohio*, six of the nine justices ruled that evidence police get from an illegal search cannot be used in a state trial.

On March 13, 1963, Phoenix police officers Carroll Cooley and Wilfred Young arrested Ernesto Miranda and questioned him for two hours about the kidnapping and rape of an 18-year-old woman. He confessed. No one had told him he could stay silent or have a lawyer. On June 13, 1966, in *Miranda v. Arizona*, five of the nine justices ruled that police must tell a person they are holding about these rights before questioning. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the ruling. Afterward, officers across the country began reading people their rights aloud from "Miranda warning" cards. Arizona prosecutors, the government lawyers who bring the charges in court, tried Miranda again without the confession, and on March 1, 1967, a jury convicted him again.

In June 1964 the sheriff of Gila County, Arizona, took Gerald Gault, 15, into custody without telling his parents. A neighbor, Ora Cook, had complained that he made an obscene phone call. Judge Robert McGhee sent him to the State Industrial School until he turned 21, about six years. An adult could have been fined for the same act. In 1967 eight of the nine justices ruled that children in juvenile court have the right to hear the charges, to have a lawyer, to question witnesses and to stay silent.

On October 31, 1963, Cleveland detective Martin McFadden stopped John W. Terry, patted the outside of his clothes and found a gun. On June 10, 1968, in *Terry v. Ohio*, eight of the nine justices ruled that police may stop a person they reasonably suspect is armed and about to commit a crime. They may pat the outside of the person's clothes for weapons, even without grounds to arrest. This is called a stop and frisk.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The men who killed civil rights workers, in court" -->
In the 1960s, members of the Ku Klux Klan and some Southern officers killed people who worked for Black Americans' rights. The Ku Klux Klan is a secret white group that used terror against Black people and their supporters. In the cases below, no killer was convicted of murder at the time. Some were convicted later, in federal courts or decades afterward.

On September 15, 1963, a bomb exploded under the steps of the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama. It killed four girls: Addie Mae Collins, 14, Denise McNair, 11, Carole Robertson, 14, and Cynthia Wesley, 14. Klan members had set it. FBI agents named Robert Chambliss as the main suspect. In 1968 FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover closed the case without charging anyone and ordered the evidence sealed. Chambliss was convicted in 1977. Thomas Blanton was convicted in 2001 and Bobby Frank Cherry in 2002, with the help of secret FBI recordings made in 1964. Another suspect, Herman Cash, died before he could be tried.

Byron De La Beckwith waited three decades for a verdict. He was tried twice in 1964 for the 1963 murder of Medgar Evers, a civil rights leader. Both juries could not agree, and a jury convicted him in 1994.

On June 21, 1964, local police in Neshoba County, Mississippi, jailed three civil rights workers for "speeding." They were James Chaney, 21, a Black college student from Mississippi, and Andrew Goodman, 20, and Michael Schwerner, 24, white men from New York. After dark the police let them go "into the hands of the Ku Klux Klan." The three were stopped again, forced into a sheriff's car, driven to a country road and shot dead. On October 20, 1967, a federal jury convicted seven of 18 men charged, among them Deputy Sheriff Cecil Price and Samuel Bowers, a Klan leader. Their sentences ran from three to ten years. Edgar Ray Killen, a Klan leader and former preacher who had organized the killings, went free because one juror would not convict a preacher. On June 21, 2005, a jury in Mississippi convicted Killen of manslaughter, which means killing a person without planning to murder, and he died in prison in 2018.

In February 1965 Alabama State Trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Jimmie Lee Jackson, 26, in the stomach in a cafe in Marion, Alabama, and Jackson died eight days later. Fowler pleaded guilty in 2010 to misdemeanor manslaughter, a lesser charge for killing a person. He was sentenced to six months and served five.

In March 1965 four men in Selma attacked James Reeb, a minister from Boston. One of them struck him in the head with a club, and Reeb died two days later. The prosecutor let a man whose brother was a suspect sit on the jury, and the jury found all three men tried not guilty.

On March 25, 1965, Klan members Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., William Orville Eaton and Eugene Thomas shot and killed Viola Liuzzo, a mother of five from Detroit, as she drove on Highway 80 in Alabama. A paid FBI informer, Gary Thomas Rowe, rode in their car. An informer is a person who secretly passes information to the police or the FBI. State juries did not convict the three men. Federal juries convicted all three of violating her civil rights, and each got 10 years in prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Attica, September 1971" -->
On September 9, 1971, about 1,281 of the roughly 2,200 men held at Attica, a New York state prison, took over the prison. They held 42 guards and other workers as hostages. In July the prisoners had made demands about food, mail, medical care and parole. Parole is early release from prison under rules and supervision. During the takeover someone beat a guard, William Quinn, and he died. In 1975 a jury found a prisoner guilty of his murder. Governor Nelson Rockefeller refused to come to the prison.

At 9:46 in the morning on September 13, officers dropped tear gas into the prison yard. State troopers, deputies and guards fired into the smoke, some with shotguns loaded with buckshot. They killed at least 39 people: 29 prisoners and 10 of the hostages. Counting the whole four days, 43 people died. Rockefeller and other officials first said the prisoners had cut the hostages' throats. The medical examiners, the doctors who examine bodies after a death, found that the hostages had died of gunshot wounds from the officers' guns. Afterward, prison officers tortured prisoners who had survived. Pages of an official report released in 2015 describe torture, burning and sexual abuse.

Prosecutors charged 62 prisoners with 1,289 crimes. They charged one state trooper, for reckless endangerment, which means putting people in danger through carelessness. On April 5, 1975, a jury found the prisoner John Hill guilty of murdering William Quinn, and found another prisoner, Charley Joe Pernasilice, guilty of assault in Quinn's death. In December 1976 Governor Hugh Carey pardoned the prisoners who had pleaded guilty. Hill received clemency, a governor's mercy that cut his punishment short, about a year after his trial. In settlements, New York State officials paid $8 million to prisoners and their families in 2000 and $12 million to the families of the prison workers in 2005.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Executions stop and start again, 1972 to 1999" -->
On June 29, 1972, in *Furman v. Georgia*, five of the nine justices struck down the death-penalty laws then in use. More than 600 people were on death row, waiting to be executed. Justice Potter Stewart wrote that the death sentences were "cruel and unusual in the same way that being struck by lightning is cruel and unusual." Being struck by lightning happens to very few people, by chance. In the next four years, lawmakers in 35 states and in Congress wrote new death-penalty laws. On July 2, 1976, in *Gregg v. Georgia*, the justices allowed them.

Gary Gilmore murdered Max Jensen, 25, a gas station worker, on July 19, 1976, and Bennie Bushnell, 26, a motel manager, the next night, in Utah. He asked to be executed. At 8:07 in the morning on January 17, 1977, a firing squad shot him at the Utah State Prison. A firing squad is a group of shooters who fire rifles at the prisoner's heart. There were five shooters, and prison officials said one of them had a blank. Gilmore was the first person executed after the 1976 ruling.

On December 7, 1982, Texas officials carried out the first execution by lethal injection. Lethal injection means killing a person with drugs put into a vein through a tube in the arm. The Death Penalty Information Center, a group that collects facts about the death penalty, describes the usual three-drug method. The first drug puts the person to sleep, the second stops the muscles from moving, which stops the breathing, and the third stops the heart. Some states later used a single large dose of a drug such as pentobarbital.

New death sentences reached their highest number, 325, in 1986. Executions rose to 98 in 1999, the most in any year since 1976.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wounded Knee, 1973: the marshals and the trial" -->
On February 27, 1973, members of the American Indian Movement took over the village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, and held it for 71 days. US marshals, FBI agents and other federal officers surrounded the village with armored vehicles and automatic weapons.

On the night of March 16, someone opened fire on a group of US marshals. A bullet hit one of them, Lloyd Grimm, and left him paralyzed from the waist down. The Associated Press reported in 2000 that no suspects were ever found. On April 17 a bullet hit Frank Clearwater, an Eastern Cherokee man inside the village, in the head, and he died on April 25. In 2000 the FBI released a report on deaths on the Pine Ridge Reservation, where Wounded Knee lies. It found that Clearwater was killed by gunfire from federal officers, who were firing back after people shot at them. On April 26 a federal sniper shot and killed Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont, an Oglala man. The FBI report gives the date as April 27 and says he died in a gunfight with federal officers at a roadblock. A US attorney, a federal prosecutor, reviewed his killing and charged no one.

Federal prosecutors charged the movement's leaders, Dennis Banks and Russell Means. After their trial had run for eight and a half months, US District Judge Fred Nichol threw out the charges in 1974. He wrote that "the misconduct by the government in this case is so aggravated that a dismissal must be entered in the interests of justice." Aggravated here means serious.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drug laws and long sentences, 1973 to 1994" -->
Lawmakers wrote the long sentences that filled the prisons. Some of those sentences were mandatory minimums. A mandatory minimum is the shortest sentence a law allows for a crime, and a judge cannot give less.

In 1973 New York's lawmakers passed Governor Nelson Rockefeller's drug laws. Selling two ounces or having four ounces of heroin, cocaine or marijuana brought 15 years to life in prison.

On June 19, 1986, the basketball player Len Bias died from cocaine. House Speaker Tip O'Neill had Democratic leaders write a new federal drug law, and the Republicans who then ran the Senate passed their own. President Ronald Reagan signed the law on October 27, 1986. Under it, 5 grams of crack cocaine brought at least five years in federal prison, the same sentence as 500 grams of powder cocaine. Crack is a form of cocaine that is smoked. A person with crack faced the same sentence as a person with 100 times as much powder.

Staff of the US Sentencing Commission reported that in 1992, about 91 of every 100 people sentenced in federal court for crack were Black, and about 3 were white. In 2004, of the people who entered treatment for crack, 53 of every 100 were Black and 38 were white.

In 1994 members of Congress passed the largest crime law in the country's history. Representative Jack Brooks of Texas sponsored it in the House. Senator Joe Biden of Delaware wrote the Senate version, and President Bill Clinton signed it. The law set aside money for 100,000 new police officers and $9.7 billion for prisons. Under it, 60 more federal crimes could be punished by death, and people in prison could no longer get federal college grants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kemba-smith" name="Kemba Smith" movie="Kemba (2024)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kemba Smith

> **Who:** A college student sentenced to 24 and a half years in federal prison for a crack cocaine ring run by a man who beat her, though she never sold or used drugs.
> **When and where:** Virginia, and federal prison, 1994 to 2000.
> **Movie:** *Kemba* (2024), a film about her life, shown on BET+.

Kemba Smith was sentenced to 24 years and 6 months in federal prison, with no parole, although she had never sold or used drugs. She grew up near Richmond, Virginia, and went to Hampton University. There she met Peter Hall, who ran a crack cocaine ring. The NAACP Legal Defense Fund, a group of civil rights lawyers, states that she was a victim of his violence at home.

On September 1, 1994, she turned herself in to federal agents. She pleaded guilty to conspiracy to sell cocaine, to lying to federal agents and to conspiracy to launder money. Conspiracy means agreeing with others to commit a crime. To launder money means to hide where money from crime came from. The NAACP Legal Defense Fund states that she never sold or used drugs, and that federal prosecutors still charged her with conspiracy to sell cocaine. Federal judge Richard B. Kellam gave her the sentence.

She was pregnant during her case. Accounts differ on whether her son was born before or after she was sentenced. She said of her son, "I had two days with him before giving him to my parents."

President Bill Clinton cut her sentence short on December 22, 2000, after about six and a half years in prison. The Clinton Library describes her as "a first time non-violent drug offender." On January 19, 2025, President Joe Biden pardoned her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kemba-smith" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many people were in prison" -->
Officials of the Bureau of Justice Statistics, part of the US Department of Justice, have counted prisoners since 1925. In 1950 state and federal prisons held about 166,000 people with sentences, or 109 for every 100,000 Americans. In 1972 they held about 196,000.

The count began to climb in 1974. It reached about 330,000 in 1980, about 771,000 in 1990 and about 1.38 million in 2000. The rate rose from 292 for every 100,000 Americans in 1990 to 478 in 2000. Counting local jails, about 2.07 million people were locked up at the end of 2000.

That year, almost 10 of every 100 Black men aged 25 to 29 were in prison. The figure for white men that age was about 1 in 100.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Killed for his shoes, 1989" -->
On May 2, 1989, James David Martin, 17, killed Michael Eugene Thomas, 15, to take his basketball shoes. Thomas was a ninth grader at Meade Senior High School in Anne Arundel County, Maryland. He had bought the shoes, Air Jordans, for $115.50. Martin led him into the woods near the school, raped him and strangled him to death. Then Martin took the shoes and ran.

The school stood on federal land, so Martin was tried in federal court. He pleaded guilty to voluntary manslaughter, a charge for killing that is less serious than murder, and he was sentenced to seven years in prison. In May 1990 *Sports Illustrated* ran a cover story by Rick Telander called "Your Sneakers or Your Life."

Martin was released in 1996 and moved to New York City. On February 23, 1998, in the Bronx, he grabbed 14-year-old Marleny Cruz by the throat, beat her, sexually abused her and strangled her to death. He also strangled a woman named Cicela Santiago in Allentown, Pennsylvania. On October 28, 2016, he pleaded guilty to manslaughter in the killing of Marleny Cruz and got 20 more years in prison. The *New York Daily News*, the *New York Times* and the *New York Post* reported these crimes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rodney King, 1991 and 1992" -->
Early on March 3, 1991, after a car chase, Los Angeles police officers Laurence Powell, Timothy Wind and Theodore Briseno and Sergeant Stacey Koon shocked Rodney King with a Taser, a weapon that fires an electric shock. They hit him with their batons while other officers watched. Police Chief Daryl Gates said the officers struck him "between fifty-three and fifty-six times." King had a broken bone in his face, a broken right ankle, and bruises and cuts. George Holliday filmed the beating from his apartment.

The trial was moved to Simi Valley, in Ventura County. The jury included ten white jurors. On April 29, 1992, the jurors found all four officers not guilty of assault, and they could not agree on one charge against Powell. Rioting spread across Los Angeles from April 29 to May 3. One count gives 63 dead and another gives 55. Police or National Guard soldiers shot 10 or 11 of them. More than 12,000 people were arrested.

In 1993 a federal jury convicted Koon and Powell of violating King's civil rights, and each got 30 months in prison. The jury found Wind and Briseno not guilty. King won $3.8 million from the city of Los Angeles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two deaths at police hands in New York, 1983 and 1999" -->
On September 15, 1983, New York City Transit Police officers arrested Michael Stewart, 25, a Black graffiti artist. They arrested him at the First Avenue subway station for writing graffiti. In their custody he was beaten unconscious. He was hogtied, his ankles bound and his hands tied to his feet with an elastic strap. The account of his death does not name which officers struck him. He reached Bellevue Hospital in a coma and died on September 28, after 13 days. The cause of death was listed as cardiac arrest, which means the heart stopped. The city's medical examiner, Elliot Gross, gave three different findings. Among them were an injury to the neck and blows to the body. Doctors hired by Stewart's family found that he had been strangled. Six officers were tried for criminally negligent homicide, which means causing a death through carelessness, and for assault and perjury, which means lying under oath. On November 24, 1985, an all-white jury found them not guilty. A lawsuit against eleven officers and the transit authority ended with a payment of $1.7 million.

On February 4, 1999, four New York City police officers in plain clothes, Sean Carroll, Richard Murphy, Edward McMellon and Kenneth Boss, fired 41 shots at Amadou Diallo outside his building in the Bronx. Diallo, 23, was an immigrant from Guinea. Nineteen of the shots hit him and killed him. He had no gun, and he had taken out his wallet. The officers were charged with second-degree murder, and the trial was moved to Albany. On February 25, 2000, a jury found all four not guilty. City officials paid his family $3 million in 2004.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wrong man: DNA and the Central Park case" -->
DNA is the chemical code inside the cells of a living body, and it differs from person to person. Cells left at a crime can be tested to show whether they came from a certain person.

In 1985 a Maryland jury convicted Kirk Bloodsworth of the 1984 rape and murder of Dawn Hamilton, a nine-year-old girl in Rosedale, Maryland. The jury convicted him on the word of five witnesses, and he was sentenced to death.

In 1993 a DNA test showed that the attacker's cells, saved in a paper bag in the judge's chambers, were not his. He was freed after more than nine years in prison, and Governor William Donald Schaefer pardoned him. He was the first person on death row in the United States cleared by DNA. A year before, in 1992, the lawyers Barry Scheck and Peter Neufeld had founded the Innocence Project at Cardozo Law School in New York, a group that uses DNA tests to free people who were wrongly convicted. In 2003 a DNA database matched the cells to Kimberly Shay Ruffner, who had been held in a cell one floor below Bloodsworth. Ruffner pleaded guilty in 2004.

On April 19, 1989, a man attacked and raped Trisha Meili while she was running in Central Park in New York City. Police questioned five teenagers for at least seven hours before recording their statements on video. They were Antron McCray, 15, Kevin Richardson, 14, Yusef Salaam, 15, Raymond Santana, 14, and Korey Wise, 16. Four of them confessed on video, then took it back within weeks, saying the police had lied to them and pressured them. The DNA found at the scene matched none of the five.

On May 1, 1989, Donald Trump paid for full-page ads in four New York newspapers calling for the death penalty. The ads named no defendant. Juries convicted all five teenagers in 1990.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="crime-justice" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
At the end of 2009 American prisons held about 1.62 million people, more than at any earlier time. The count then began to fall. State and federal prisons held about 1.25 million people at the end of 2023. That is the latest national count that officials of the Bureau of Justice Statistics had published by September 2026. In 2023 Black Americans were in prison at almost five times the rate of white Americans.

From 1989 through 2025, judges and governors cleared 3,767 people of crimes they had been convicted of, by the count of the National Registry of Exonerations. Clearing a convicted person this way is called an exoneration. In 2020, 62 of every 100 local police departments used body cameras. Police killed at least 1,383 people in 2024 and 1,314 in 2025, by the count of one group. Some officers were charged and convicted, and others were never charged, or juries found them not guilty. Murders rose 29 percent in 2020. Violent crime then fell 9.3 percent from 2024 to 2025. FBI officials reported in 2026 that the 2025 murder rate, 4.1 for every 100,000 people, tied 1955 and 1956 for the lowest they have recorded.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fewer prisoners, a gap that shrank but stayed wide" -->
The prison count reached 1,617,970 at the end of 2009, the highest in the Bureau of Justice Statistics count, which goes back to 1925. In 2010 it fell for the first time since 1972.

Lawmakers also cut some of the long drug sentences. In April 2009 New York's lawmakers ended the Rockefeller minimum sentences. Senator Dick Durbin of Illinois wrote the Fair Sentencing Act, and President Barack Obama signed it on August 3, 2010. Under the new law, it took 28 grams of crack, not 5, to bring the five-year minimum, so the gap between crack and powder cocaine sentences went from 100 to 1 down to 18 to 1. On December 21, 2018, President Donald Trump signed the First Step Act, which applied that change to people already in prison. In its first year, more than 2,000 federal prisoners got shorter sentences under the crack change, and more than 3,000 were let out under changed rules for time off for good behavior.

At the end of 2023, about 1,254,000 people were in state and federal prisons. In the middle of 2024, about 657,500 more were in local jails, by the bureau's count. In 2023, for every 100,000 Black Americans, 929 were serving prison sentences. For every 100,000 white Americans, 190 were. Ten years earlier, in 2013, the two rates had been 1,348 and 237.

A conviction for a felony can also take away a person's right to vote. In 2024 about 4 million Americans could not vote because of a felony conviction, including 1 in every 22 Black adults. The Sentencing Project, a group that studies prisons and sentences, counted them in 2024.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="exonerated-five" name="The Exonerated Five" movie="When They See Us (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### The Exonerated Five

> **Who:** Five New York City teenagers convicted in 1990 of a rape in Central Park, cleared in 2002 when another man confessed and his DNA matched.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1989 to 2014.
> **Movie:** *When They See Us* (2019), a Netflix drama series directed by Ava DuVernay. *The Central Park Five* (2012) is a documentary by Ken Burns, Sarah Burns and David McMahon.

Five teenagers spent years locked up for a rape that another man had committed alone. They were Antron McCray, Kevin Richardson, Yusef Salaam, Raymond Santana and Korey Wise, who were 14 to 16 years old in 1989. Juries convicted them in 1990 of the attack on Trisha Meili in Central Park.

Judge Thomas Galligan sentenced the four younger boys to 5 to 10 years in custody for young people. Wise, who was 16, was tried as an adult. His jury found him not guilty of rape and attempted murder and guilty of sexual abuse, assault and riot. He was sentenced to 5 to 15 years in an adult prison. Santana was released in 1995, McCray in 1996, and Salaam and Richardson in 1997.

In 2001 a prisoner named Matias Reyes met Wise at Auburn prison in New York. Reyes was serving a life sentence for other rapes. In 2002 Reyes told officials that he had raped Trisha Meili and that he had acted alone. A DNA test matched him. Wise was released in August 2002, after about 13 years locked up.

Prosecutors in District Attorney Robert Morgenthau's office looked into the case again and asked the court to throw out the convictions. On December 19, 2002, Justice Charles Tejada vacated all five convictions, which means he cancelled them. In January 2003 a panel chosen by Police Commissioner Raymond Kelly accepted the DNA result but said the five had "most likely" also taken part in the attack. Justice Tejada and the district attorney did not accept that view. Reyes was never charged for the attack on Meili, because the five-year time limit for charging the crime had passed.

In 2014 New York City officials paid the five $41 million to settle their lawsuit. Four of them got about $7.1 million each. Wise got $12.2 million, because he had served six more years than the others.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="exonerated-five" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Proving convictions wrong" -->
An exoneration means a judge or a governor has cleared a person of a crime after a conviction, because new evidence shows the person did not do it. The National Registry of Exonerations counted 3,767 exonerations in the United States from 1989 through the end of 2025. The Innocence Project states that DNA tests helped in more than 600 of them.

In 2025 alone, 97 people were exonerated. On average each of them had lost 14.2 years in prison, and 59 of the 97 were Black. In at least 70 of the cases, police, prosecutors or other officials had broken the rules, which the Registry calls official misconduct. Since 1989, payments to people who were cleared have added up to more than $5.5 billion. The National Registry of Exonerations gave these figures in its 2026 report.

In 2014 a judge threw out the conviction of George Stinney Jr., a 14-year-old Black boy who had been executed 70 years before. On March 22, 1944, two white girls, Betty June Binnicker, 11, and Mary Emma Thames, 8, were killed in Alcolu, South Carolina. Stinney was accused. In April 1944 he was tried, convicted and sentenced to death in one day. The all-white jury took less than ten minutes. Governor Olin Johnston refused to spare him, and on June 16, 1944, South Carolina officials put him to death in the electric chair. On December 16, 2014, Judge Carmen Mullen threw out his conviction. She ruled that he had not had a real defense and that his confession had probably been forced. No court has named anyone else as the killer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The death penalty, 2000 to 2026" -->
Executions in the United States fell from 85 in 2000 to 11 in 2021, then rose to 47 in 2025. By September 18, 2026, officials had carried out 27 executions that year. Judges and juries handed down 23 new death sentences in 2025, and 2,024 people were on death row on October 1, 2025. Twenty-three states have no death penalty. The Death Penalty Information Center, a group that collects facts about the death penalty, gave these figures in September 2026.

Since 1977 officials in the United States have carried out 1,681 executions. They killed 1,490 of those people by lethal injection and 163 by electrocution. Electrocution means killing a person with a strong electric current sent through the body. They killed 19 in gas chambers, sealed rooms filled with poison gas, 6 by firing squad and 3 by hanging. Of the victims of the crimes behind these executions, 76 of every 100 were white.

In 2002 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that states may not execute people with intellectual disabilities, which means a lasting limit on a person's ability to learn and reason. In 2005 they ruled that states may not execute people for crimes committed before age 18.

The Death Penalty Information Center's fact sheet counts at least 203 people sentenced to death since 1973 who were later cleared and freed. The table in the same document adds up to 202. The group's staff also list 21 people executed since 1976 who, they say, may have been innocent. Among them are Cameron Todd Willingham, executed in Texas in 2004, and Troy Davis, executed in Georgia in 2011. No court or governor has declared any of the 21 innocent. The group notes that courts do not usually hear a claim of innocence once the person is dead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Danziger Bridge, New Orleans, 2005" -->
On September 4, 2005, in the days after Hurricane Katrina, New Orleans police officers shot six unarmed Black people on the Danziger Bridge. They killed James Brissette, 17, and Ronald Madison, 40, who was shot in the back. Among the officers were Kenneth Bowen, Robert Gisevius, Anthony Villavaso and Robert Faulcon. The officers then worked to cover up what they had done.

Officers were convicted in 2011. In September 2013 federal Judge Kurt Engelhardt threw out those convictions and ordered a new trial. He pointed to what he called "highly unusual, extensive and truly bizarre actions" by the prosecutors. On April 20, 2016, five former officers pleaded guilty, and they were sentenced to 3 to 12 years in prison. In a separate case, Roland Bourgeois Jr., 55, of the Algiers Point neighborhood, was sentenced in 2019 to 10 years in prison. He had shot three Black men who were trying to flee the area.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police killings and what happened in court" -->
No government agency keeps a full count of the people police kill. Campaign Zero, an advocacy group, which means a group that campaigns for a cause, counted at least 1,383 people killed by police in 2024 and 1,314 in 2025. By its count, police killed Black Americans at 2.6 times the rate of white Americans in 2025. In 2020, 62 of every 100 local police departments used body cameras, cameras worn on an officer's uniform, the Bureau of Justice Statistics reported in 2023.

On August 9, 2014, officer Darren Wilson shot and killed Michael Brown, 18, in Ferguson, Missouri. A St. Louis County grand jury did not charge Wilson. Federal prosecutors did not charge him in 2015, and in 2020 the county prosecutor, Wesley Bell, reviewed the case and did not charge him either.

On March 4, 2015, lawyers of the US Department of Justice reported on the Ferguson police. They found a "pattern or practice of unlawful conduct" that broke the First, Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments. Amendments are additions to the Constitution, and these three protect free speech, protect people from unfair searches and arrests, and promise equal treatment under the law. That means police there broke the law again and again, in ways that went against rights the Constitution protects. In 2010 the city's finance director had written to the police chief that "unless ticket writing ramps up significantly," it would be hard to raise more money the next year. Of the city court's 2013 cases in which a judge issued an arrest warrant, an order to arrest someone, 92 of every 100 were against Black people. The report describes a 32-year-old Black man who sat in his parked car in a park in the summer of 2012. An officer blocked in his car and demanded his Social Security number. The officer accused him, with no cause, of being a pedophile, a person who wants to sexually abuse children. When the man spoke of his rights, the officer arrested him at gunpoint on eight city charges. One charge was for giving his name as "Mike" instead of "Michael." Another was for not wearing a seat belt while sitting in a parked car. The man told investigators he lost his job doing work for the federal government because of the charges.

Officer Daniel Pantaleo killed Eric Garner, 43, on Staten Island, New York, on July 17, 2014. Pantaleo held him around the neck in a chokehold, which police rules banned. Garner said "I can't breathe" 11 times. The medical examiner ruled his death a homicide, a killing by another person. A grand jury did not charge Pantaleo. In July 2019 Attorney General William Barr decided against federal charges, and in August 2019 Police Commissioner James O'Neill fired Pantaleo.

Tamir Rice, 12, was holding a toy gun at a park in Cleveland on November 22, 2014. The person who called 911 said twice that the gun was "probably fake" and the person "probably a juvenile," meaning a young person. The dispatcher, the worker who sent the call to the officers, did not pass that on. Officer Timothy Loehmann shot Tamir almost as soon as he arrived and killed him. A grand jury did not charge Loehmann. Loehmann was fired in 2017 for lying on his job application.

On March 13, 2020, officers in Louisville, Kentucky, forced open Breonna Taylor's door with a warrant to search for drugs. Her boyfriend, Kenneth Walker, fired once and hit officer Jonathan Mattingly. Officers fired 32 shots, and Myles Cosgrove fired the shot that killed Taylor, who was 26. A grand jury did not charge Mattingly or Cosgrove. A former detective, Kelly Goodlett, pleaded guilty in 2022 to helping put false facts on the warrant. In November 2024 a federal jury convicted Brett Hankison of using excessive force, which means more force than the law allows. His shots hit no one. In July 2025 he was sentenced to 33 months. Louisville's city officials paid $12 million.

On May 25, 2020, in Minneapolis, officer Derek Chauvin knelt on George Floyd's neck for more than nine minutes and killed him. Floyd was 46. On April 20, 2021, a jury convicted Chauvin of second-degree murder, third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter. These are different levels of charges for causing a death. On June 25, 2021, Judge Peter Cahill sentenced him to 22 and a half years. The state's sentencing rules set 12 and a half years, and the judge gave more after finding "particular cruelty." Chauvin also pleaded guilty to a federal crime. In July 2022 Judge Paul Magnuson sentenced him to 21 years for it, served at the same time as the state sentence. The three other officers got federal sentences: Thomas Lane 2 and a half years, J. Alexander Kueng 3 years and Tou Thao 3 and a half years. Minneapolis city officials paid Floyd's family $27 million.

On January 7, 2023, in Memphis, Tennessee, five police officers pulled Tyre Nichols, 29, from his car. They were Tadarrius Bean, Demetrius Haley, Emmitt Martin III, Desmond Mills Jr. and Justin Smith. They sprayed him with pepper spray and shocked him with a Taser. When they caught him near his mother's house, they punched him, kicked him and beat him with a baton. He died on January 10 of injuries to his head. Mills and Martin pleaded guilty. In October 2024 a federal jury convicted Haley on all counts, and Bean and Smith only of trying to influence witnesses. In May 2025 a state jury found Bean, Haley and Smith not guilty of all state charges, including murder. In August 2025 Judge Sheryl Lipman ordered a new federal trial for the three, because the first judge may have been biased. No new verdict had been reported by September 2026.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was charged after school shootings" -->
After some shootings at schools, prosecutors charged officers who did not act, or a parent who supplied the gun.

On February 14, 2018, Nikolas Cruz, 19, a former student, shot and killed 14 students and three staff members at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Parkland, Florida, and wounded 17 others. The report of a Florida state commission states that Deputy Scot Peterson "fled to a position of personal safety while Cruz shot and killed" students and staff. On June 29, 2023, a jury found Peterson not guilty of felony child neglect and other charges.

On May 24, 2022, a gunman killed 19 children and two staff members at Robb Elementary School in Uvalde, Texas. Officers killed the gunman 77 minutes after the first officers entered the school. A Department of Justice review states that the school district police chief, Pete Arredondo, told officers at several points to wait. A grand jury charged Arredondo and a former school officer, Adrian Gonzales, with child abandonment and endangerment. On January 21, 2026, a jury found Gonzales not guilty of all 29 counts.

On September 4, 2024, at Apalachee High School in Winder, Georgia, a gunman killed two 14-year-old students and two teachers. The gunman, Colt Gray, pleaded guilty to all 55 counts, and on July 27, 2026, he was sentenced to life in prison without parole. His father, Colin Gray, had given him the AR-15-style rifle as a Christmas present. On March 3, 2026, a jury convicted Colin Gray of two counts of second-degree murder and other crimes, and on July 30, 2026, he was sentenced to 15 years.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Crime goes up, then down" -->
Murders in the United States rose from 16,669 in 2019 to 21,570 in 2020, up 29 percent. The Pew Research Center, using FBI data, called it the largest one-year rise in more than a century. The murder rate in 2020 was 7.8 for every 100,000 people, still lower than the rate of 10 in 1991.

From 2024 to 2025 the violent crime rate dropped 9.3 percent, from 362.9 to 327.6 for every 100,000 people. FBI officials called it the largest one-year drop since it began making national estimates in 1936. Murders fell about 18 percent, robberies 18.5 percent and property crimes 12.4 percent. The 2025 murder rate, 4.1 for every 100,000 people, tied 1955 and 1956 as the lowest the FBI has recorded. FBI officials released these figures in August 2026.

On May 30, 2024, a jury in Manhattan convicted former President Donald Trump of 34 felonies. The charges were for falsifying business records to hide a payment of hush money, money paid to keep someone quiet, to Stormy Daniels. He was the first US president convicted of a felony. Agents had found $480,000 in cash and more than $100,000 in gold bars in his home. He resigned in August 2024, and on January 29, 2025, a judge sentenced him to 11 years in prison.

On January 20, 2025, President Trump pardoned everyone convicted over the attack on the US Capitol on January 6, 2021, with one exception. For fourteen members of two groups, the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys, he cut their sentences to the time they had already served.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose courts: McGirt, 2020" -->
On July 9, 2020, in *McGirt v. Oklahoma*, five of the nine Supreme Court justices ruled that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation had never been ended. Members of Congress had set that land aside for the nation in the 1800s. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion. In the 1830s US officials forced the Muscogee (Creek), the Cherokee and three other nations from their homelands in the Southeast to Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma. Those forced removals are called the Trail of Tears. Gorsuch's opinion begins: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise." Under the ruling, Oklahoma's prosecutors could not charge Native people for major crimes there, and federal and tribal courts took those cases.

Jimcy McGirt, a Seminole citizen, had been convicted in a state court of sexually abusing his wife's granddaughter. After the ruling, a federal jury convicted him in November 2020. The judges of Oklahoma's highest criminal court then applied the same reasoning to the lands of other nations. As a result, almost all of eastern Oklahoma, including much of Tulsa, counts as Indian country, the legal name for Native land, in criminal cases. In 2022, in *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta*, the justices ruled that state prosecutors may also charge people who are not Native for crimes against Native victims there.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tulsa's survivors, 2020 to 2026" -->
No white person was ever sent to prison for the 1921 Tulsa massacre. In 2020 survivors of the massacre sued the city, the county and state bodies. In July 2023 Tulsa County Judge Caroline Wall dismissed the case, and in June 2024 the justices of the Oklahoma Supreme Court upheld her ruling.

Viola Ford Fletcher was 7 in 1921. She spoke to Congress on May 19, 2021, and died on November 24, 2025, at 111. Lessie Benningfield Randle, born on November 10, 1914, was the last known living survivor in May 2026.

On June 1, 2025, Tulsa's mayor, Monroe Nichols, announced the Greenwood Trust, a private fund with a goal of $105 million by June 1, 2026. The plan listed $24 million for housing, $60 million for buildings and for clearing blight, which means run-down property, and $21 million for land, scholarships and grants to small businesses. It included no direct payments to the survivors. No total raised had been published by September 2026.
