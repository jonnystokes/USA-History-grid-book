## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 11 minutes**

In the new prisons of New York and Pennsylvania, keepers locked convicts, people found guilty of crimes, alone in cells: at night in New York, and all day and night in Pennsylvania. Both kinds of prison kept prisoners silent. Officials in Boston and New York hired full-time police. Pennsylvania's lawmakers moved hangings inside jail walls in 1834, and Michigan's lawmakers voted to end the death penalty twelve years later.

In the South, white men still policed enslaved people. South Carolina's slave patrols, small groups of white men on horseback, searched the places where enslaved people lived and broke up their gatherings. Virginia's local judges, called justices, tried enslaved people with no jury. White mobs also killed people with no trial at all. A white mob in St. Louis burned Francis McIntosh to death in 1836, and no one was charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee Nation's police, 1808" -->
Under the old Cherokee law of blood, a dead person's clan, not the nation, punished a killing. Leaders of the Cherokee Nation began to change that in 1808, when they set up their own police. Two years later the clans gave up revenge.

The Cherokee lived then in their own country, in parts of what are now Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama and North Carolina. The leaders on the Cherokee national council, who made the nation's laws, passed a written law at Brooms Town on September 11, 1808. Under it they set up "regulating parties," companies of men who made sure people obeyed those laws. The principal chief, or head leader, Black Fox signed it with Path Killer, Toochalar, and Charles Hicks, the council's secretary.

**The regulators.** Each company had six men: a captain, a lieutenant who was second in command, and four privates. The law states the pay: fifty dollars for the captain, forty for the lieutenant and thirty for each private. The regulators went after horse thieves and robbers. They also protected what children got from their dead parents and what widows owned.

A man found guilty of stealing a horse got "one hundred stripes on the bare back," one hundred blows of a whip. Blows like these cut the skin and leave welts, raised stripes of swollen skin, and scars.

If a regulator killed a man who fought back with a weapon, "the blood of him or them shall not be required." That meant the dead man's clan, his mother's large family group, could not kill in return.

**The end of clan revenge.** The seven clans met in council at Oostanallah on April 10, 1810, and passed an "act of oblivion for all lives." Oblivion means forgetting: the clans forgave every life that one clan still owed another. From then on, a person who killed someone "without malice intended," meaning by accident, would not be held guilty. A man who killed his brother in anger would be guilty of murder.

With this act the clans ended the law of blood. The editors of the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the nation's own newspaper, printed both laws on March 13, 1828.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two kinds of prison: Auburn and Eastern State" -->
Prison keepers in New York and Pennsylvania ran two different kinds of prison in the 1820s. In both, they kept prisoners from talking to each other.

**Auburn.** At Auburn Prison in New York, prisoners worked together in groups during the day and slept alone in cells at night. They were not allowed to speak at any time. Elam Lynds became the head keeper in 1821. He had prisoners flogged for breaking even small rules. Flogging means beating a person's bare back with a whip, which cuts the skin and leaves welts and scars.

Lynds and John D. Cray made prisoners march in step with their arms locked together, a way of marching called the lockstep. Lynds dressed them in striped uniforms.

New York's officials used the same plan at Sing Sing, their third state prison. Officials in Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maryland and Washington, D.C., had taken up the Auburn plan by 1829. Officials in most other states chose it later.

**Eastern State.** Pennsylvania's officials opened Eastern State Penitentiary, a prison in Philadelphia, in October 1829. The architect John Haviland laid out seven blocks of cells joined to a center hall. He said he designed it for "watching, convenience, economy and ventilation." By economy he meant saving money, and by ventilation, fresh air.

Each prisoner lived and worked alone in a cell about 8 feet wide and 12 feet long, with its own small exercise yard. A Bible was the only thing he was allowed to keep. Prisoners made shoes and wove cloth in their cells. Whenever a prisoner left his cell, a guard put a hood over his head so that no other prisoner could see his face.

**The iron gag.** Guards punished prisoners who broke the rules. They gave them cold showers, and one account states that guards apparently did this once outdoors in winter. They also used the iron gag. The guards clamped an iron piece over the prisoner's tongue, like the bit in a horse's mouth, and chained it to his hands, which were held behind his back. With his hands chained to it, he could not take it out.

Prison officials put a prisoner named Mathias Maccumsey in the iron gag in 1833 as a punishment for talking, and he died. The prison doctor gave the cause as apoplexy, an old word for a sudden stroke. The historian Jennifer Lawrence Janofsky, writing in the *Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia*, states that the prison's leaders tried to cover up the death.

A committee of Pennsylvania lawmakers investigated charges in 1834 and 1835 that the prison's officials had been cruel to prisoners. The lawmakers did not find the warden, Samuel Wood, guilty of cruel and unusual punishment.

The English writer Charles Dickens visited Eastern State in 1842. He wrote that he believed the system "in its effects, to be cruel and wrong." He also wrote: "I hold the slow and daily tampering with the mysteries of the brain to be immeasurably worse than any torture of the body." He meant that keeping a person alone and silent for years did more harm to the mind than any torture does to the body.

Pennsylvania's prison officials gave up keeping prisoners alone in 1913. Eastern State was built for 250 prisoners, and by the middle 1920s it held about 1,700.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charles Williams

> **Who:** The first prisoner at Eastern State Penitentiary.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, October 1829 to October 1831.

**Prisoner Number 1 got two years alone for stealing a watch.**

Charles Williams was an 18-year-old Black farmer. He had been found guilty of breaking into a house and stealing a silver watch, a gold seal and a gold key, worth $25 in all. A seal was a small stamp, often hung on a watch chain, for pressing a mark into wax. His sentence was two years alone and in silence, with work.

Guards led him into the new prison in October 1829 with a hood over his head. From then on he was Prisoner Number 1, and Jane Brox, who tells his story in the *Public Domain Review*, writes that he was not called Charles again while he was there.

His cell was twelve feet by eight. *Smithsonian* magazine reports that prisoners there spoke to the guards through a small feeding hole and worked alone in their cells. He was let out in October 1831, two years to the day after he went in.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-williams" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="After Nat Turner's revolt, 1831" -->
Nat Turner, an enslaved man, led a revolt in Southampton County, Virginia, in August 1831. Turner and the rebels who joined him killed 55 white men, women and children, by the count of the historian Patrick Breen. A Library of Virginia article gives the number as 57.

White people from Southampton and beyond killed Black people with no trial in the days after the revolt. Breen writes that they killed about three dozen in Southampton County. He also writes that armed units formed "to put down the revolt and in many cases get revenge." A Library of Virginia article states that they killed hundreds, with no exact count.

The county's justices tried the accused rebels in a court of oyer and terminer. In Virginia that meant local justices judged enslaved people with no jury. Breen counts thirty enslaved people and one free Black man sentenced to death. Nineteen were put to death on the justices' orders. Governor John Floyd changed the sentences of the other twelve so they would not die.

Turner hid for two months. He gave himself up on October 30, 1831. The justices tried him on November 5 and ordered him "hanged by the neck until he be dead" on November 11. He was hanged that day, as the justices had ordered. In the same court record, the justices set his value as property at $375. Virginia officials paid that sum to his owner.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hangings move inside the jail" -->
Pennsylvania's lawmakers became the first in any state to ban public executions, hangings in front of crowds, in 1834. The Death Penalty Information Center, a group that collects facts about the death penalty, records this. After that, officials hanged prisoners who had been sentenced to death inside the county prisons.

Michigan's lawmakers voted to end the death penalty on May 18, 1846. An article by the news outlet Michigan Public states that Michigan was the first English-speaking government in the world to do so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Francis McIntosh, St. Louis, 1836" -->
A white mob in St. Louis, Missouri, burned Francis McIntosh alive on April 28, 1836, and no one was ever charged. McIntosh was a free Black man, 26 years old, from Pittsburgh. He worked on the steamboat *Flora* as a cook and as a porter, who carried passengers' bags.

That day Deputy Constable William Mull, a local law officer, arrested McIntosh for getting in the way of officers who were arresting two sailors. Deputy Sheriff George Hammond, a county law officer, helped take him to jail. McIntosh was told he would get five years in prison. He stabbed both officers with a knife. Hammond bled to death. Mull was badly wounded, and accounts differ on whether he died later.

A crowd of several hundred white people gathered, and a city alderman, a member of the city council, was among them. Men from the crowd broke into the jail and took McIntosh out. They chained him to a locust tree near Seventh and Chestnut Streets, then piled wood around his knees and set it on fire. The crowd cheered as he burned, according to a history written at the University of Missouri-St. Louis. He died within about twenty minutes. By one report, he asked to be shot.

**What the judge told the jurors.** Judge Luke Lawless then spoke to the grand jury. He told the jurors to charge any person they could identify. But he said the killing might be the act of a whole crowd moved by one feeling, and if it was, it was beyond the reach of the law. He also blamed the writings of abolitionists, people who worked to end slavery. The jurors, led by their foreman, the head juror, John O'Fallon, charged no one.

Elijah Lovejoy, the editor of the *St. Louis Observer*, wrote against the killing and against the judge. A mob of people who were against ending slavery killed Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois, in November 1837.

A young lawyer named Abraham Lincoln spoke about the McIntosh killing in Springfield, Illinois, in January 1838. He said McIntosh had been "chained to a tree, and actually burned to death." He added that it was "all within a single hour from the time he had been a freeman, attending to his own business, and at peace with the world." Lincoln's words left out the stabbing and Hammond's death.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police take over from the night watch" -->
The people of Boston had kept a town watch, men who walked the streets at night to keep order, since 1631.

**Boston.** A small day police force began work in Boston in 1838, with officers on duty full time beside the night watch. The crime historian Gary Potter calls this the first organized police force paid for with public money and with officers on duty full time, a 2017 *TIME* article reports. Boston's city officials replaced the watch and the day police with one Police Department of 250 officers in 1854.

**New York.** About 320,000 people lived in New York before 1845. A night watch and 182 other officers guarded them: 100 city marshals, 31 constables and 51 city police officers, all kinds of local law officers. New York's state lawmakers passed a Municipal Police Act, a law for a city police force, on May 7, 1844. Under it, city officials were to end the night watch.

The mayor and the city council argued over who would choose the officers. Mayor William Havemeyer and the council finally adopted the law for the city on May 23, 1845. Accounts differ on the size of the force: the website CultureNow states that the 1844 law allowed 1,200 officers, and another count gives 800. After a long argument, the officers first wore a navy blue uniform in 1853.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 21 minutes**

Mobs killed people with no trial in the South, in the West and in New Orleans, and the men in those mobs were almost never punished. After the Civil War, white mobs in the South killed Black people. A killing like this, by a mob, with no trial, is called a lynching. Mobs in the West killed Chinese immigrants, and a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men.

**The count.** The Equal Justice Initiative, a group in Montgomery, Alabama, founded by the lawyer Bryan Stevenson, published a report called *Lynching in America* in 2017. It records 4,075 Black people lynched in twelve Southern states between 1877 and 1950, more than one a week for 73 years. Counting lynchings in other states too, its total is more than 4,400.

The report states that the people who took part "were celebrated and acted with impunity," meaning with no fear of punishment, and that state and federal officials mostly let the killings happen.

Private detectives such as Allan Pinkerton's men worked for railroads and factory owners. Officials in Alabama and other states rented prisoners to mining companies and farm owners, and in Alabama more than 95 percent of the county prisoners and 90 percent of the state prisoners were Black. New York state senators on a committee that met in 1894 and 1895 estimated that New York City's police officers took more than $10 million a year in payments from people breaking the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vigilance committees in San Francisco, 1851 and 1856" -->
A vigilance committee was a group of private citizens who arrested, tried and punished people themselves, outside the courts. About 700 San Francisco men formed one on June 9, 1851. The next day they held their own trial and hanged John Jenkins, a man from Sydney, Australia, for stealing a safe. At that time California law allowed a death sentence for that kind of theft.

That year the committee's members hanged four men. They held and questioned people at their headquarters without due process, the rules a court must follow to give an accused person a fair trial. Of the others they held, they whipped one, forced 28 out of California, handed 15 to the police and courts, and let 41 go.

San Francisco men formed the committee again on May 14, 1856, after James Casey shot the newspaper editor James King of William. William Tell Coleman led it, and its leaders claimed 6,000 members. The committee's members hanged four more men that year, among them Casey and Charles Cora. The boxer James "Yankee" Sullivan killed himself after being held in one of the committee's cells. The members broke up the committee on August 11, 1856. Members of the two committees hanged eight people in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dakota trials, 1862" -->
US Army officers tried 392 Dakota men after the war between the United States and the Dakota in Minnesota in 1862, and hanged 38 of them together at Mankato. The Minnesota River valley was the Dakota homeland.

Colonel Henry Sibley set up a military commission, a court of army officers, to try Dakota prisoners on September 28, 1862. The officers held the trials at the Lower Sioux Agency, also called the Lower Agency. US officials set it up in 1853 to run the new Dakota reservation, and it had a school, shops and homes for the government workers. The war began there in August 1862.

**Five-minute trials.** The trials took place in François LaBathe's summer kitchen, one of the only buildings there still standing. Some trials lasted no longer than five minutes. The officers sentenced 303 men to death and 16 to prison.

The law professor Carol Chomsky of the University of Minnesota wrote that "the evidence was sparse," meaning thin, and that the officers were "biased," meaning they had taken a side before they heard the cases. She wrote that the men on trial had no lawyers, and that the trials were held in a language they did not know.

President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers read all 303 trial records. Lincoln wrote to the Senate that too much mercy might encourage another war, and that too much harshness would be "real cruelty."

He looked first for men found guilty of rape, which means forcing a person into sex, and he found two. Next he added men found to have taken part in "massacres," the killing of many civilians, people who were not soldiers. Men found only to have fought in battles stayed off his list. He approved 39 hangings, and one of those men was later reprieved, which means spared from hanging.

Army officers hanged 38 Dakota men together at Mankato on December 26, 1862. About 4,000 people watched, and 2,000 soldiers stood guard. An article by the Death Penalty Information Center states that it was the largest mass execution in United States history. Two of the 38 were not men Lincoln had approved for hanging: the officers hanged them by mistake. One was Caske, whose Dakota name was Wicaŋȟpi Wastedaŋpi. The other was Wasicuŋ, a white man the Dakota had adopted, whom the officers had found not guilty.

The bodies were cut down and put together in one shallow grave on a sandbar between Mankato's main street and the Minnesota River. Before morning, doctors had dug up most of them to use as cadavers, dead bodies that doctors and medical students cut open to study the human body.

A year later, the army's chief lawyer found that Sibley had never had the power to set up the trials. By then the 38 men had already been hanged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police against the draft riots, 1863" -->
Mobs in New York City attacked the police, burned buildings and killed Black New Yorkers from July 13 to 16, 1863. The rioters were fighting the new Civil War draft, a law that required men to serve in the army when called. The state militia, the state's part-time soldiers, had been sent to Gettysburg, so at first the Metropolitan Police were the only force in the city.

On the first day, rioters beat Police Superintendent John Kennedy, the head of the city's police officers, until he was nearly unconscious and cut his hand with a knife. Police Commissioners Thomas Acton and John Bergen, two of the officials who ran the police, took command. Their officers kept the rioting out of Lower Manhattan, below Union Square. Rioters burned two police station houses. They also burned the Colored Orphan Asylum, a home for Black children, and the more than 200 children inside all got out.

Rioters beat, tortured and killed Black New Yorkers. Village Preservation, a New York history group, records that William Jones, a Black cart driver, had gone out to buy a loaf of bread. A mob chased him on Clarkson Street, beat him, hanged him and set him on fire. Another Black man was attacked by a crowd of 400, hanged from a tree and set on fire. Hundreds of Black New Yorkers left the city, among them the doctor James McCune Smith and his family.

Secretary of War Edwin Stanton sent five regiments, units of soldiers, from Gettysburg to stop the riot, and it ended on July 16. By the official count, 119 or 120 people were killed in the riots. A New-York Historical Society article records the official count of 119 and states that it may be too low.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs that killed Chinese immigrants" -->
White mobs in the West killed Chinese immigrants. After the killings at Los Angeles in 1871 and at Rock Springs in 1885, every man who was charged or arrested went free. The justices of the California Supreme Court had ruled in 1854 that Chinese people could not testify against white people in court.

**Los Angeles, 1871.** A white man named Robert Thompson was killed in Los Angeles on October 24, 1871, during a gunfight between two Chinese groups. A crowd of about 500 people, one tenth of the city, then attacked the Chinese part of town. They killed 18 Chinese men and boys, by the count of the Los Angeles Public Library, or 19 in the list of names the historian Scott Zesch published in 2008. Among the dead was a doctor, Gene Tong.

The mob hanged seven of them from the crossbar of Goller's wagon shop and three from a freight wagon. The next morning seventeen bodies lay in the jail yard.

Grand jurors in Los Angeles brought 25 charges of murder. Only ten men were tried, and seven or eight of them were found guilty of manslaughter. Judges later threw out the verdicts on a technical point of law. District Attorney Cameron Thom chose not to try the men again, and they went free in 1873.

**Rock Springs, 1885.** Rock Springs was a town in Wyoming, which was then a territory, land under the federal government that was not yet a state. Between 100 and 150 armed white miners, railroad workers and townspeople attacked the Chinese miners there on September 2, 1885. They killed 28, wounded 15, and robbed and burned all 79 homes in the town's Chinatown. Federal troops arrived nearly a week after the killings and stayed for 13 years.

Sixteen white miners were arrested and let out on bail, money paid as a promise to come back for trial. The historian Tom Rea, writing for WyoHistory.org, a Wyoming history website, states that "though the killing had been done in daylight, in front of other people, no one could be found who would swear to having seen any crimes." No charges were filed. Chinese diplomats, officials who spoke for China's government, listed nearly $150,000 in losses. Under pressure from the president, members of Congress agreed to pay.

**By 1885:** San Francisco's committees had hanged eight men, army officers had hanged 38 Dakota men at Mankato, and every man charged or arrested for the killings of Chinese immigrants at Los Angeles and Rock Springs had gone free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prisoners rented out: convict leasing" -->
State and county officials in Alabama and other states rented prisoners to private companies after the Civil War. This was called convict leasing. The companies' bosses made the prisoners work in mines, on farms and on railroads. The companies' owners paid the state or the county, not the prisoners.

The historian Mary Ellen Curtin gives the Alabama numbers in the *Encyclopedia of Alabama*. Officials there leased prisoners from 1875 to 1928. More than 95 percent of the county prisoners and 90 percent of the state prisoners there were Black. Most county prisoners had been found guilty of misdemeanors, small crimes such as being drunk in public. They had to work longer to pay off their court costs.

**The whip and the deaths.** The companies' bosses whipped prisoners who tried to escape or talked back, and they whipped others to make them work harder. Each year, four or five of every hundred state prisoners died. Of the 745 county prisoners in the mines in 1896, 181 had died in the two years before. At the Coalburg mine, 90 of every 1,000 prisoners died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="allan-pinkerton" name="Allan Pinkerton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Allan Pinkerton

> **Who:** A Scottish barrel maker who started a private detective agency in Chicago.
> **When and where:** Chicago and across the country, 1850 to 1884.

**His sign was an open eye over the words "We never sleep."**

Allan Pinkerton's detectives were paid by the companies that hired them. They were not police officers. Pinkerton was a cooper, a barrel maker, from Glasgow, Scotland, and in 1843 he settled in Dundee, Illinois. His house there was a hiding place for people escaping slavery. While cutting wood one day he found a gang of counterfeiters, people who made fake money, and told the sheriff, who arrested them.

Pinkerton and the lawyer Edward Rucker started a detective agency in Chicago in 1850, which became the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. The men who ran express companies, which carried money and packages by train, hired his detectives to catch criminals, and so did the men who ran the railroads.

His men kept photographs of known criminals, and some of them joined gangs in disguise. Pinkerton guarded Abraham Lincoln on his way to Washington in February 1861, against what Pinkerton said was a plan to kill him in Baltimore.

Pinkerton's detectives and local helpers raided the Missouri farm of the family of the outlaw Jesse James, a robber on the run from the law, in January 1875. They threw a firebomb into the house. It exploded and killed Jesse's eight-year-old half-brother, Archie. It wounded his mother, Zerelda Samuel. Jesse was not there. Afterward, many people took the side of the James family and blamed the agency for the raid.

Factory owners also hired Pinkerton men after the Civil War as armed guards against workers on strike, workers who had stopped work together to press their employers for changes. Pinkerton died in Chicago in 1884.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="allan-pinkerton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Haymarket trial, 1886" -->
Someone threw a bomb at police at a workers' meeting in Haymarket Square in Chicago on May 4, 1886. Seven officers were killed and sixty were hurt. The bomb thrower was never identified.

Prosecutors charged eight anarchists, people who wanted to do away with all government. They tried the eight as accessories, people who help a crime happen without doing the act. The prosecutors said the men's speeches had led to the bombing. A jury found all eight guilty in three hours. The judge sentenced seven to hang and Oscar Neebe to 15 years in prison.

Four of the men, Albert Parsons, August Spies, George Engel and Adolph Fischer, were hanged on November 11, 1887, as the judge had sentenced them. Louis Lingg had already killed himself in his cell with an explosive someone had smuggled in. Governor Richard Oglesby changed the sentences of two men to life in prison.

Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three men still in prison, Samuel Fielden, Oscar Neebe and Michael Schwab, in June 1893. The pardon freed them and cancelled their punishment. Altgeld wrote that their trial had been unfair.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first electric chair, 1890" -->
William Kemmler became the first person put to death in an electric chair, on August 6, 1890, at Auburn Prison in New York. He had killed Matilda Ziegler in Buffalo in 1889 with a hatchet, or with an axe by one account. Electrocution means killing a person by sending a strong electric current through the body. The current stops the heart and burns the skin where the metal touches it.

A Buffalo dentist, Dr. Albert Southwick, had first suggested it in 1881. He argued that it was kinder than hanging, in which a person sentenced to death could hang for up to 30 minutes before dying. Kemmler's lawyers argued that the electric chair was a cruel and unusual punishment, which the Constitution forbids. The justices of the US Supreme Court turned them down.

Kemmler's chair had two metal plates covered with damp sponges, one on his head and one on his back. Warden Charles Durston, the head of the prison, gave the order, with 17 witnesses in the room. The first shock lasted 17 seconds. It was about 700 volts by one account, or 1,000 by another. A volt is a measure of how strongly electricity pushes through a wire or a body.

The first shock did not kill him. The witnesses smelled burning cloth and burning flesh. A second shock of about 1,030 volts went on for about two minutes, until smoke rose from his head, and Kemmler died. Doctors who examined the body found that the plate on his back had burned through to his spine, the HISTORY.com account states.

The chair ran on alternating current, a kind of electricity that flows back and forth many times a second. Harold Brown, whom New York officials hired to build the chair, used it. *Smithsonian* magazine reports that Thomas Edison, whose company sold the other kind, direct current, was secretly paying Brown to use alternating current. George Westinghouse's company built electric systems that used alternating current. After Kemmler's death Westinghouse called it "a brutal affair" and said: "They would have done better with an axe."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eleven men killed in a New Orleans prison, 1891" -->
A mob in New Orleans broke into the city's prison on March 14, 1891, and killed eleven Italian American men. Five months earlier, the police chief, David Hennessy, had been shot. Nineteen Italian Americans were charged with his murder, among them a 14-year-old boy. Nine were tried. The jury found six not guilty, and the trial of the other three ended in a mistrial, with no verdict. A *Smithsonian* magazine article states that "there was no solid evidence against them."

A newspaper notice stated that citizens should "come prepared for action." A crowd of thousands gathered at the Henry Clay statue. The lawyer William Parkerson told them to "set aside the verdict of that infamous jury" (infamous means known for something bad).

**Who led the mob.** Parkerson and three other city leaders led the killers: the lawyer Walter Denegre, the politician and businessman James Houston, and John Wickliffe, the editor of the *New Delta* newspaper. John M. Parker, who later became governor of Louisiana, and Walter Flower, who later became mayor of New Orleans, were in the mob.

The prison warden, Lemuel Davis, let the 19 prisoners hide. The mob found eleven of them. Men dragged Emmanuele Polizzi outside, hanged him from a lamppost and shot him. He was mentally ill. They hanged Antonio Bagnetto, a peddler who sold fruit, from a tree and shot him. They shot or clubbed to death nine others inside the prison.

The eleven were Antonio Bagnetto, James Caruso, Loreto Comitis, Rocco Geraci, Joseph Macheca, Antonio Marchesi, Pietro Monasterio, Emmanuele Polizzi, Frank Romero, Antonio Scaffidi and Charles Traina. Three of them had been found not guilty, three had had no verdict, and five had never been tried.

**No one charged.** Grand jurors, who met under Judge Robert Marr, a friend of several men in the mob, said they could not identify the killers. They called the killers "several thousands of the first, best, and even the most law-abiding, of the citizens." They charged no one.

Italian officials called home their ambassador, their government's chief representative in the United States. President Benjamin Harrison agreed to pay the families $25,000 to make up for the killings, and federal officials paid $2,211.90 to each family. Mobs lynched at least eight more Italian Americans in Louisiana in the 1890s. No one was charged and tried in court for those killings either.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-smith" name="Henry Smith" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Henry Smith

> **Who:** A Black man burned to death by a white mob in front of thousands of people, without a trial.
> **When and where:** Paris, Texas, February 1, 1893.

**Thousands came by train to watch a man burned to death.**

A white mob in Paris, Texas, tortured Henry Smith with hot irons and burned him to death on February 1, 1893, in front of thousands of people. He never had a trial, and no one was ever arrested for killing him.

**The accusation.** Myrtle Vance, a girl of three or four, was found dead in the woods near Paris in January 1893. Newspaper reports said that she had also been raped, which means forced into sex. The *New York Sun* called Smith her "ravisher," an old word for a man who rapes someone.

Her father, Henry Vance, was a Paris police officer. Several days before, he had arrested Henry Smith for being drunk and disorderly and had beaten him. Smith was accused of killing the girl. The two main accounts of what followed, by the journalist Ida B. Wells and the historian Terry Anne Scott, do not name the person who first accused him. Smith ran away to Arkansas.

Posses of Black and white men went after him. A posse is a group of men gathered to chase someone. They rode the Texas & Pacific and the St. Louis & San Francisco railways for free. Near Hope, Arkansas, a Black constable named Robinson, a local law officer, arrested Smith. Texas Governor James Hogg telegraphed Sheriff D. S. Hammond to hold Smith for a trial. Hogg also ordered that Smith not be taken to Paris. The sheriff and the county attorney answered that they were "helpless" against "an enraged public."

Men brought Smith to Paris by train. The mayor closed the schools. Trains brought people from Dallas, Fort Worth, Texarkana, Fort Smith in Arkansas, and other towns. The *New York Sun* stated that the crowd numbered 10,000, and other accounts state from 5,000 to 15,000. Men paraded Smith through town on a carnival float to a prairie near the railroad depot. There they tied him on a wooden platform ten feet high.

**The torture.** Henry Vance and men of his family burned Smith with red-hot irons for fifty minutes, starting at his feet. The *Sun* identifies them as the girl's father, her brother and two uncles. Another account identifies the father, his 15-year-old son and his brother-in-law. They burned out Smith's eyes and pushed hot irons down his throat.

Then men piled wood and cottonseed hulls, the dry outer shells of cotton seeds, around the platform, poured oil on it and set it on fire. The *New York Sun* reported the torture and the burning on February 2, 1893, and Wells reprinted the report in her book *The Red Record* in 1895. The *Sun* states that people carried off pieces of the burned wood.

Scott, writing on the website BlackPast, adds that Smith rolled out of the fire and fell ten feet to the ground. People in the crowd threw a rope around him and pulled him back into the fire. She writes that men cut his shirt from his body piece by piece and threw the pieces to the crowd.

**What the witnesses said.** The *Sun* stated that Smith had confessed at Hope. People who had seen the girl's body told Wells they saw no sign of the rape the newspapers reported. They said they saw only a small scrape and a bruise, mostly on her neck.

The Reverend King, a Black minister in Paris, said Smith was mentally ill. King said he had tried two years before to get him into an asylum, a hospital for mentally ill people. Men drove King out of Paris for speaking against the lynching. Wells wrote that he was "ridden out of Paris on a rail," carried out of town on a wooden pole.

The day after Smith was killed, Governor Hogg telegraphed the sheriff of Lamar County and asked for the killers to be arrested. A few days later a mob hanged Smith's stepson, William Butler, at Hickory Creek, five miles from Paris, and shot his body. Scott writes that no one was ever punished for killing William Butler either.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police who took payments: New York, 1894" -->
Senator Clarence Lexow and other New York state senators, sitting as a committee, investigated the New York City police in 1894 and 1895. They found that officers took payments from people who broke the law, in return for letting them go on breaking it. Their report ran to 10,576 pages. In it, they estimated that officers extorted more than $10 million a year, forcing people by threats to pay.

The owners of brothels, houses where people paid for sex, paid police captains $30,000 a year. Owners of gambling houses paid $15 to $300 a month to stay open. Officers also paid bribes, secret payments for a favor, to be promoted. Captain Timothy Creedon told the committee he had paid $15,000 to become a captain, although he had scored 97.82 on the promotion test.

An Encyclopedia.com history of the hearings states that the chief of police, Thomas Byrnes, was said to be worth $350,000. The senators also found officers taking part in bribery, frightening voters, cheating in elections and beating people.

**Roosevelt's changes.** Mayor William Strong put Theodore Roosevelt on the city's Board of Police Commissioners in 1895. Roosevelt brought in civil service exams for new officers, tests that a person had to pass to get a government job, and raised the physical standards. He walked the streets at midnight to check that officers were on duty, and he started police patrols on bicycles. He left in 1897 to become assistant secretary of the navy.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A court for children, Chicago, 1899" -->
Illinois lawmakers voted to set up a court for children in Chicago in the spring of 1899. A 2019 article by the Chicago radio station WBEZ states that it was the first juvenile court in the world: a separate court for children accused of crimes, run to help the child instead of only punishing.

Before then, police arrested children, put them in handcuffs and held them overnight in jails and police stations with adult prisoners. In 1882 the Cook County jail in Chicago held more than 250 children aged 14 and under, and at least 20 of them were younger than 11. A 10-year-old boy who stole a pair of shoes waited almost two weeks in the county jail for a grand jury to hear his case.

Julia Lathrop worked at Hull House, a house on Chicago's Near West Side where Jane Addams and other women lived among poor immigrant families and helped them. Lathrop visited Illinois jails and wrote down what she found. Lucy Flower proposed a separate court for children. In 1907 a building for the children's court, with rooms where children were held, opened at Halsted and Ewing Streets.

**If you remember one thing:** White mobs killed Black, Chinese and Italian American people with no trial, and the courts punished almost none of the killers.
In 1850: A death sentence in New York was carried out with a rope, and a child arrested in Chicago could be held in jail with adult prisoners.
In 1900: New York's prisons used the electric chair, and Chicago had a court just for children, which WBEZ calls the first in the world.
**Next:** After 1900 the national government hired its own agents, and mobs went on taking Black prisoners out of jails.
