## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, officials in New York and Pennsylvania built prisons where convicts, people found guilty of crimes, were locked alone in cells. Officials in Boston and New York hired full-time police. Prison keepers in the two states ran two different kinds of prison, and both kept prisoners silent. Pennsylvania's lawmakers moved hangings inside jail walls in 1834, and twelve years later Michigan's lawmakers voted to end the death penalty.

In the South, white men still policed enslaved people. In South Carolina, slave patrols, small groups of white men on horseback, searched the places where enslaved people lived and broke up their gatherings. In Virginia, local judges called justices tried enslaved people with no jury. White mobs also killed people with no trial at all. In St. Louis in 1836, a white mob burned Francis McIntosh to death, and no one was charged.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee Nation's police, 1808" -->
Leaders of the Cherokee Nation set up their own police in 1808. The Cherokee lived then in their own country, in parts of what are now Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama and North Carolina. On September 11, 1808, at Brooms Town, the Cherokee national council, the leaders who made the nation's laws, passed a written law. Under it, the council set up "regulating parties," companies of men who made sure people obeyed those laws. The law was signed by the principal chief, or head leader, Black Fox, with Path Killer, Toochalar, and Charles Hicks, the council's secretary.

Each company of regulators had six men: a captain, a lieutenant who was second in command, and four privates, the ordinary members. The law specified the pay: fifty dollars for the captain, forty for the lieutenant and thirty for each private. The regulators went after horse thieves and robbers. They also protected what children inherited from their parents and what widows owned. A man found guilty of stealing a horse got "one hundred stripes on the bare back," which means one hundred blows of a whip. Blows like these cut the skin and leave welts, raised stripes of swollen skin, and scars. The sources do not state who struck the blows. If a regulator killed a man who fought back with a weapon, "the blood of him or them shall not be required." That meant the dead man's clan could not kill in return. A clan was a large family group, and a Cherokee child belonged to the mother's clan.

On April 10, 1810, at Oostanallah, the clans agreed to an "act of oblivion for all lives." Oblivion means forgetting. Under the act, a person who killed someone "without malice intended," meaning by accident, would no longer be held guilty. With this act, the clans ended the old rule that an accidental killing had to be paid for with a life (*Cherokee Phoenix*, March 13, 1828).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two kinds of prison: Auburn and Eastern State" -->
In the 1820s, prison keepers in New York and Pennsylvania ran two different kinds of prison. In both, they kept prisoners from talking to each other.

At Auburn Prison in New York, prisoners worked together in groups during the day and slept alone in cells at night. They were not allowed to speak at any time. Elam Lynds became the head keeper in 1821. He had prisoners flogged for breaking even small rules. Flogging means beating a person's bare back with a whip. The whip cuts the skin and leaves welts and scars. Accounts of Auburn do not state the number of blows or identify the keepers who swung the whip. Lynds and John D. Cray made prisoners march in step with their arms locked together, a way of marching called the lockstep. Lynds also dressed them in striped uniforms.

New York's officials used the same plan at Sing Sing, their third state prison. By 1829 officials in Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maryland and Washington, D.C., had taken up the Auburn plan. Officials in most other states chose it later.

In Philadelphia, Pennsylvania's officials opened Eastern State Penitentiary, a prison, in October 1829. The architect John Haviland laid out seven blocks of cells joined to a center hall. He said he designed it for "watching, convenience, economy and ventilation." Economy meant saving money, and ventilation meant fresh air. Each prisoner lived and worked alone in a cell about 8 feet wide and 12 feet long, with its own small exercise yard. A Bible was his only possession. Prisoners made shoes and wove cloth in their cells. Whenever a prisoner left his cell, a guard put a hood over his head so that no other prisoner could see his face.

Guards punished prisoners who broke the rules. They gave them cold showers. One account states that guards apparently did this once outdoors in winter. They also used the iron gag. The guards clamped an iron piece over the prisoner's tongue and chained it to his hands, which were held behind his back.

The English writer Charles Dickens visited Eastern State in 1842. He wrote that he believed the system "in its effects, to be cruel and wrong." He also wrote: "I hold the slow and daily tampering with the mysteries of the brain to be immeasurably worse than any torture of the body." He meant that keeping a person alone and silent for years did more harm to the mind than any torture does to the body.

Pennsylvania's prison officials gave up keeping prisoners alone in 1913. Eastern State was built for 250 prisoners, and by the middle 1920s it held about 1,700.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charles Williams

> **Who:** The first prisoner at Eastern State Penitentiary.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, October 1829 to October 1831.

Charles Williams, an 18-year-old Black farmer, was the first prisoner at Eastern State Penitentiary. He had been found guilty of breaking into a house and stealing a silver watch, a gold seal and a gold key, worth $25 in all. His sentence was two years alone and in silence, with work. The sources do not name who convicted and sentenced him.

In October 1829 guards led him into the new prison with a hood over his head. From then on he was Prisoner Number 1, and he was not called Charles again while he was there. His cell was twelve feet by eight. He spoke to the guards through a small hole, the one his food came through, and he worked alone in the cell. He was let out in October 1831, two years to the day after he went in (Jane Brox, *Public Domain Review*, and *Smithsonian* magazine).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-williams" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="After Nat Turner's revolt, 1831" -->
In August 1831 Nat Turner, an enslaved man, led a revolt in Southampton County, Virginia. Turner and the rebels who joined him killed 55 white men, women and children, by the count of the historian Patrick Breen. A Library of Virginia article states that 57 died.

In the days after the revolt, white people from Southampton and beyond killed Black people with no trial. Breen writes that they killed about three dozen in Southampton County. He also writes that armed units formed "to put down the revolt and in many cases get revenge." A Library of Virginia article states that they killed hundreds, with no exact count.

The county's justices tried the accused rebels in a court of oyer and terminer. In Virginia that meant local justices judged enslaved people with no jury. Breen counts thirty enslaved people and one free Black man sentenced to death. Nineteen were put to death on the justices' orders. The sources do not name the men who carried out the executions. Governor John Floyd changed the sentences of the other twelve so they would not die.

Turner hid for two months. He gave himself up on October 30, 1831. The justices tried him on November 5 and ordered him "hanged by the neck until he be dead" on November 11. He was hanged that day, as the justices had ordered. In the same court record, the justices set his value as property at $375. Virginia officials paid that sum to his owner.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hangings move inside the jail" -->
In 1834 Pennsylvania's lawmakers became the first in any state to ban public executions, hangings in front of crowds (Death Penalty Information Center). After that, officials hanged condemned prisoners inside the county prisons.

On May 18, 1846, Michigan's lawmakers voted to end the death penalty. An article by the news outlet Michigan Public states that Michigan was the first English-speaking government in the world to do so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Francis McIntosh, St. Louis, 1836" -->
On April 28, 1836, a white mob in St. Louis, Missouri, burned Francis McIntosh alive, and no one was ever charged. McIntosh was a free Black man, 26 years old, from Pittsburgh. He worked on the steamboat *Flora* as a cook and as a porter, who carried passengers' bags.

That day Deputy Constable William Mull, a local law officer, arrested McIntosh for getting in the way of officers who were arresting two sailors. Deputy Sheriff George Hammond, a county law officer, helped take him to jail. McIntosh was told he would get five years in prison. He stabbed both officers with a knife. Hammond bled to death. Mull was badly wounded, and accounts differ on whether he died later.

A crowd of several hundred white people gathered, and a city alderman, a member of the city council, was among them. Men from the crowd broke into the jail and took McIntosh out. They chained him to a locust tree near Seventh and Chestnut Streets, then piled wood around his knees and set it on fire. The crowd cheered as he burned (University of Missouri-St. Louis). He died within about twenty minutes. By one report, he asked to be shot.

Judge Luke Lawless then spoke to the grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether to charge someone with a crime. He told the jurors to charge any person they could identify. But he said the killing might be the act of a whole crowd moved by one feeling. If it was, he said, it was beyond the reach of the law. He also blamed the writings of abolitionists, people who worked to end slavery. The grand jury, led by its foreman, or head juror, John O'Fallon, charged no one.

Elijah Lovejoy, the editor of the *St. Louis Observer*, wrote against the killing and against the judge. In November 1837 a mob of people who were against ending slavery killed Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois. In January 1838 a young lawyer named Abraham Lincoln spoke about the McIntosh killing in Springfield, Illinois. He said McIntosh had been "chained to a tree, and actually burned to death." Lincoln did not mention the stabbing or Hammond's death.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police take over from the night watch" -->
The people of Boston had kept a town watch since 1631. A watch was a group of men who walked the streets at night to keep order.

In 1838 a small day police began work in Boston, with officers on duty full time beside the night watch. A 2017 *TIME* article, drawing on the crime historian Gary Potter, states that this was the first publicly funded, organized police force with officers on duty full time. In 1854 Boston's city officials replaced the watch and the day police with one Police Department of 250 officers (City of Boston).

Before 1845, New York had about 320,000 people. A night watch and 182 other officers guarded them: 100 city marshals, 31 constables and 51 city police officers. All three were kinds of local law officers. On May 7, 1844, New York's state lawmakers passed a Municipal Police Act, a law for a city police force. Under it, city officials were to end the night watch. The mayor and the city council argued over who would choose the officers. Mayor William Havemeyer and the council finally adopted the law for the city on May 23, 1845. Sources disagree on the size of the force: 1,200 officers by the count of the website CultureNow, or 800 by another count. After a long argument, the officers first wore a navy blue uniform in 1853.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After the Civil War, white mobs in the South killed Black people without any trial, and the killers were almost never punished. A killing like this, by a mob, with no trial, is called a lynching. The report *Lynching in America* (Equal Justice Initiative, 2017) records 4,075 Black people lynched in twelve Southern states between 1877 and 1950, and more than 4,400 in all. It states that state and federal officials mostly tolerated the killings. Mobs in the West also killed Chinese immigrants.

In the same years, private detectives such as Allan Pinkerton's men worked for railroads and factory owners. Officials in Alabama and other states rented prisoners to mining companies and farm owners. In Alabama, nine of every ten of those prisoners or more were Black. In New York, state senators on a committee that met in 1894 and 1895 estimated that police officers took more than $10 million a year in payments from people breaking the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Vigilance committees in San Francisco, 1851 and 1856" -->
A vigilance committee was a group of private citizens who arrested, tried and punished people themselves, outside the courts. About 700 San Francisco men formed one on June 9, 1851. The next day they held their own trial and hanged John Jenkins, a man from Sydney, Australia, for stealing a safe. At that time California law allowed a death sentence for that kind of theft.

In 1851 the committee's members hanged four men and whipped one. They sent fourteen men back to Australia and ordered fourteen others to leave California. They handed fifteen to the police and courts and let forty-one go. The members held and questioned people at their headquarters without due process, the rules a court must follow to give an accused person a fair trial.

On May 14, 1856, after James Casey shot the newspaper editor James King of William, San Francisco men formed the committee again, led by William Tell Coleman. The leaders claimed 6,000 members. Its members hanged four more men that year, among them Casey and Charles Cora. The boxer James "Yankee" Sullivan killed himself after being held in one of the committee's cells. The members broke up the committee on August 11, 1856. Members of the two committees hanged eight people in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dakota trials, 1862" -->
After the war between the United States and the Dakota in Minnesota in 1862, US Army officers tried 392 Dakota men and hanged 38 of them together at Mankato. The Minnesota River valley was the Dakota homeland.

On September 28, 1862, Colonel Henry Sibley set up a military commission, a court of army officers, to try Dakota prisoners. The officers held the trials in François LaBathe's summer kitchen at the Lower Agency. Some trials lasted no longer than five minutes. The law professor Carol Chomsky of the University of Minnesota wrote: "The evidence was sparse, the tribunal was biased, the defendants were unrepresented in unfamiliar proceedings conducted in a foreign language." Sparse means thin, a tribunal is a court, and unrepresented means the prisoners had no lawyers. Biased means the officers had taken a side before they heard the cases. The defendants were the men on trial, and the proceedings were the steps of the trials. The officers sentenced 303 men to death and 16 to prison.

President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers read all 303 trial records. Lincoln first looked for men found guilty of rape, which means forcing a person into sex, and he found two. Next he added men found to have taken part in "massacres," the killing of many civilians, people who were not soldiers. He approved 39 hangings. One of those men was later reprieved, which means spared from hanging.

On December 26, 1862, army officers hanged 38 Dakota men together at Mankato. About 4,000 people watched, and 2,000 soldiers stood guard. An article by the Death Penalty Information Center states that it was the largest mass execution in United States history. Two of the men were hanged by mistake. One was Caske, whose Dakota name was Wicaŋȟpi Wastedaŋpi. The other was Wasicuŋ, a white man the Dakota had adopted, whom the officers had found not guilty.

The bodies were buried together in one shallow grave on a sandbar. Before morning, doctors had dug up most of them to use for medical study (Minnesota Historical Society). A year later, the army's chief lawyer found that Sibley had never had the power to set up the trials.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police against the draft riots, 1863" -->
From July 13 to 16, 1863, mobs in New York City attacked the police, burned buildings and killed Black New Yorkers. The rioters were fighting the new Civil War draft, a law that required men to serve in the army when called. The state militia, the state's part-time soldiers, had been sent to Gettysburg, so at first the Metropolitan Police were the only force in the city.

On the first day, rioters beat Police Superintendent John Kennedy, the head of the city's police officers, until he was nearly unconscious and cut his hand with a knife. Police Commissioners Thomas Acton and John Bergen, two of the officials who ran the police, took command. Their officers kept the rioting out of Lower Manhattan, below Union Square. Rioters burned two police station houses. They also burned the Colored Orphan Asylum, a home for Black children, and the more than 200 children inside all got out.

Rioters beat, tortured and killed Black New Yorkers. William Jones, a Black cart driver, had gone out to buy a loaf of bread. A mob chased him on Clarkson Street, beat him, hanged him and set him on fire (Village Preservation). Another Black man was attacked by a crowd of 400, hanged from a tree and set on fire. Hundreds of Black New Yorkers left the city, among them the doctor James McCune Smith and his family.

Secretary of War Edwin Stanton sent five regiments of soldiers from Gettysburg to stop the riot, and it ended on July 16. By the official count, 119 or 120 people died. A New-York Historical Society article records 119 and states that many historians think more people died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mobs that killed Chinese immigrants" -->
In the West, white mobs killed Chinese immigrants, and judges and juries punished almost no one. In 1854 the justices of the California Supreme Court had ruled that Chinese people could not testify against white people in court.

In Los Angeles on October 24, 1871, a white man named Robert Thompson was killed during a gunfight between two Chinese groups. A crowd of about 500 people, one tenth of the city, then attacked the Chinese part of town. They killed 18 Chinese men and boys, by the count of the Los Angeles Public Library, or 19 by Wikipedia's count. Among the dead was a doctor, Gene Tong. The mob hanged seven of them from the crossbar of Goller's wagon shop and three from a freight wagon. The next morning seventeen bodies lay in the jail yard.

A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether to charge someone with a crime. In Los Angeles, a grand jury made 25 formal charges of murder, called indictments. Only ten men were tried, and seven or eight of them were found guilty of manslaughter, which means killing a person without planning to kill. Judges later threw out the verdicts on a technical point of law. The sources do not name the court. District Attorney Cameron Thom chose not to try the men again, and they went free in 1873.

Rock Springs was a town in Wyoming, which was then a territory, land under the federal government that was not yet a state. On September 2, 1885, between 100 and 150 armed white miners, railroad workers and townspeople attacked the Chinese miners there. They killed 28, wounded 15, and robbed and burned all 79 homes in the town's Chinatown. Federal troops arrived nearly a week after the killings and stayed for 13 years. Sixteen white miners were arrested and let out on bail, which means they paid money as a promise to come back for trial. The historian Tom Rea writes that "though the killing had been done in daylight, in front of other people, no one could be found who would swear to having seen any crimes." No charges were filed (WyoHistory.org). Chinese diplomats, officials who spoke for China's government, listed nearly $150,000 in losses. Under pressure from the president, members of Congress agreed to pay.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prisoners rented out: convict leasing" -->
After the Civil War, state and county officials in Alabama and other states rented prisoners to private companies. This was called convict leasing. The companies' bosses made the prisoners work in mines, on farms and on railroads. The companies' owners paid the state or the county, not the prisoners.

In Alabama, officials leased prisoners from 1875 to 1928. More than 95 percent of the county prisoners and 90 percent of the state prisoners there were Black. Many county prisoners had been convicted of misdemeanors, small crimes such as being drunk in public. They had to work longer to pay off their court costs. The companies' bosses whipped prisoners who tried to escape or talked back, and they whipped others to make them work harder.

Each year, four or five of every hundred state prisoners died. Of the 745 county prisoners in the mines in 1896, 181 had died in the two years before. At the Coalburg mine, 90 of every 1,000 prisoners died (Mary Ellen Curtin, *Encyclopedia of Alabama*).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Allan Pinkerton

> **Who:** A Scottish barrel maker who started a private detective agency in Chicago.
> **When and where:** Chicago and across the country, 1850 to 1884.

Allan Pinkerton ran a private detective agency whose men were paid by the companies that hired them. They were not police officers. He was born in Glasgow, Scotland, in 1819. He was a cooper, a barrel maker, and in 1843 he settled in Dundee, Illinois. His house there was a hiding place for people escaping slavery. While cutting wood one day he found a gang of counterfeiters, people who made fake money, and told the sheriff, who arrested them.

In 1850 Pinkerton and the lawyer Edward Rucker started a detective agency in Chicago. It became the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. Its sign was an open eye with the words "We never sleep." The men who ran express companies, which carried money and packages by train, hired his detectives to catch criminals, and so did the men who ran the railroads. His men kept photographs of known criminals. Some of them joined gangs in disguise. In February 1861 Pinkerton guarded Abraham Lincoln on his way to Washington, against what Pinkerton said was a plan to kill him in Baltimore.

In January 1875 Pinkerton's detectives and local helpers raided the Missouri farm of the family of the outlaw Jesse James, a robber on the run from the law. They threw a firebomb into the house. It exploded and killed Jesse's eight-year-old half-brother, Archie. It wounded his mother, Zerelda Samuel. Jesse was not there. Afterward, many people took the side of the James family and blamed the agency for the raid.

After the Civil War, factory owners also hired Pinkerton men as armed guards against workers on strike, workers who had stopped work together to press their employers for changes. Pinkerton died in Chicago in 1884 (PBS *American Experience*).
<!-- hb-story:end slug="allan-pinkerton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Haymarket trial, 1886" -->
On May 4, 1886, someone threw a bomb at police at a workers' meeting in Haymarket Square in Chicago. Seven officers died and sixty were hurt. The bomb thrower was never identified.

Prosecutors charged eight anarchists, people who wanted to do away with all government. They tried the eight as accessories, people who help a crime happen without doing the act. The prosecutors said the men's speeches had led to the bombing. A jury found all eight guilty in three hours. The judge sentenced seven to hang and Oscar Neebe to 15 years in prison.

On November 11, 1887, Albert Parsons, August Spies, George Engel and Adolph Fischer were hanged. The sources do not name who carried out the hangings. Louis Lingg had already killed himself in his cell with an explosive someone had smuggled in. Governor Richard Oglesby changed the sentences of two men to life in prison. In June 1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three men still in prison, Samuel Fielden, Oscar Neebe and Michael Schwab. The pardon freed them and cancelled their punishment. Altgeld wrote that their trial had been unfair (PBS *American Experience*).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first electric chair, 1890" -->
William Kemmler became the first person put to death in an electric chair, on August 6, 1890, at Auburn Prison in New York. He had killed Matilda Ziegler in Buffalo in 1889 with a hatchet, or with an axe by one account. Electrocution means killing a person by sending a strong electric current through the body. The current stops the heart and burns the skin where the metal touches it.

A Buffalo dentist, Dr. Albert Southwick, had first suggested it in 1881. He argued that it was kinder than hanging, in which a condemned person could hang for up to 30 minutes before dying. Kemmler's lawyers argued that the electric chair was a cruel and unusual punishment, which the Constitution forbids. The justices of the US Supreme Court turned them down.

Kemmler's chair had two metal plates covered with damp sponges, one on his head and one on his back. Warden Charles Durston, the head of the prison, gave the order, with 17 witnesses in the room. The first shock lasted 17 seconds. It was about 700 volts by one account, or 1,000 by another. A volt is a measure of how strongly electricity pushes through a wire or a body. The first shock did not kill him. The witnesses smelled burning cloth and burning flesh. A second shock of about 1,030 volts went on for about two minutes, until smoke rose from his head, and Kemmler died. George Westinghouse's company built electric systems that used alternating current, the kind of current used in the chair. Afterward Westinghouse said: "They would have done better with an axe." Doctors who examined the body found that the plate on his back had burned through to his spine (HISTORY.com).
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eleven men killed in a New Orleans prison, 1891" -->
A mob in New Orleans broke into the city's prison on March 14, 1891, and killed eleven Italian American men. Five months earlier, the police chief, David Hennessy, had been shot. Nineteen Italian Americans were charged with his murder, among them a 14-year-old boy. Nine were tried. The jury found six not guilty, and the trial of the other three ended in a mistrial, with no verdict. A *Smithsonian* magazine article states that "there was no solid evidence against them."

A newspaper notice stated that citizens should "come prepared for action." A crowd of thousands gathered at the Henry Clay statue. The lawyer William Parkerson told them to "set aside the verdict of that infamous jury." Infamous means known for something bad. Parkerson and three other city leaders led the killers. They were the lawyer Walter Denegre, the politician and businessman James Houston, and John Wickliffe, the editor of the *New Delta* newspaper. John M. Parker, who later became governor of Louisiana, and Walter Flower, who later became mayor of New Orleans, were in the mob.

The prison warden, Lemuel Davis, let the 19 prisoners hide. The mob found eleven of them. Men dragged Emmanuele Polizzi outside, hanged him from a lamppost and shot him. He was mentally ill. They hanged Antonio Bagnetto, a peddler who sold fruit, from a tree and shot him. They shot or clubbed to death nine others inside the prison.

The eleven were Antonio Bagnetto, James Caruso, Loreto Comitis, Rocco Geraci, Joseph Macheca, Antonio Marchesi, Pietro Monasterio, Emmanuele Polizzi, Frank Romero, Antonio Scaffidi and Charles Traina. Three of them had been found not guilty, three had had no verdict, and five had never been tried.

A grand jury under Judge Robert Marr, a friend of several men in the mob, said it could not identify the killers. It called them "several thousands of the first, best, and even the most law-abiding, of the citizens." It charged no one. Italian officials called home their ambassador, their government's chief representative in the United States. President Benjamin Harrison agreed to pay the families a $25,000 indemnity, money paid to make up for a harm. Federal officials paid $2,211.90 to each family. In the 1890s mobs lynched at least eight more Italian Americans in Louisiana. No one was prosecuted, which means charged and tried in court, for those killings either.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-smith" name="Henry Smith" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Henry Smith

> **Who:** A Black man burned to death by a white mob in front of thousands of people, without a trial.
> **When and where:** Paris, Texas, February 1, 1893.

On February 1, 1893, a white mob in Paris, Texas, tortured Henry Smith with hot irons and burned him to death in front of thousands of people. He never had a trial, and no one was ever arrested for killing him.

In January 1893 Myrtle Vance, a girl of three or four, was found dead in the woods near Paris. Newspaper reports stated that she had also been sexually attacked. Her father, Henry Vance, was a Paris police officer. Several days before, he had arrested Henry Smith for being drunk and disorderly and had beaten him. Smith was accused of killing the girl. The sources do not name who first accused him. Smith ran away to Arkansas.

Posses of Black and white men went after him. A posse is a group of men gathered to chase someone. They rode the Texas & Pacific and the St. Louis & San Francisco railways for free. Near Hope, Arkansas, a Black constable named Robinson, a local law officer, arrested Smith. Texas Governor James Hogg telegraphed Sheriff D. S. Hammond to hold Smith for a trial. Hogg also ordered that Smith not be taken to Paris. The sheriff and the county attorney answered that they were "helpless" against "an enraged public."

Men brought Smith to Paris by train. The mayor closed the schools. Trains brought people from Dallas, Fort Worth, Texarkana, Fort Smith in Arkansas, and other towns. The *New York Sun* stated that the crowd numbered 10,000, and other accounts state from 5,000 to 15,000. Men paraded Smith through town on a carnival float to a prairie near the railroad depot. There they tied him on a wooden platform ten feet high.

Henry Vance and men of his family burned Smith with red-hot irons for fifty minutes, starting at his feet. The *Sun* identifies them as the girl's father, her brother and two uncles. Another account identifies the father, his 15-year-old son and his brother-in-law. They burned out Smith's eyes and pushed hot irons down his throat. Then men piled wood and cottonseed hulls, the dry outer shells of cotton seeds, around the platform, poured oil on it and set it on fire. Smith rolled out of the fire and fell ten feet to the ground. People in the crowd threw a rope around him and pulled him back into the fire. People took home pieces of his shirt and of the burned wood (*New York Sun*, February 2, 1893, reprinted by Ida B. Wells in *The Red Record*, 1895).

The *Sun* stated that Smith had confessed at Hope. People who had seen the girl's body told Wells they saw no sign of the sexual attack the newspapers reported. The Reverend King, a Black minister in Paris, said Smith was mentally ill. King said he had tried two years before to get him into an asylum, a hospital for mentally ill people. For speaking against the lynching, King was "ridden out of Paris on a rail," which means men carried him out of town on a wooden pole, and he was forced to leave. The sources do not name those men.

The day after Smith was killed, Governor Hogg telegraphed the sheriff of Lamar County and asked for the killers to be arrested. A few days later a mob hanged Smith's stepson, William Butler, at Hickory Creek, five miles from Paris, and shot his body. No one was ever punished for killing William Butler either (Terry Anne Scott, BlackPast).

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Police who took payments: New York, 1894" -->
In 1894 and 1895, Senator Clarence Lexow and other New York state senators, sitting as a committee, investigated the New York City police. The senators found that officers took payments from people who broke the law, in return for letting them go on breaking it. Their report ran to 10,576 pages. In it, they estimated that officers extorted more than $10 million a year. To extort means to force someone to pay money by threats.

The owners of brothels, houses where people paid for sex, paid police captains $30,000 a year. Owners of gambling houses paid $15 to $300 a month to stay open. Officers also paid bribes, secret payments for a favor, to be promoted. Captain Timothy Creedon told the committee he had paid $15,000 to become a captain, although he had scored 97.82 on the promotion test. The chief of police, Thomas Byrnes, was said to be worth $350,000. The senators also found officers taking part in bribery, frightening voters, cheating in elections and beating people.

In 1895 Mayor William Strong put Theodore Roosevelt on the city's Board of Police Commissioners. Roosevelt brought in civil service exams for new officers, tests that a person had to pass to get a government job. He also raised the physical standards. He walked the streets at midnight to check that officers were on duty, and he started police patrols on bicycles. He left in 1897 to become assistant secretary of the navy.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A court for children, Chicago, 1899" -->
In the spring of 1899, Illinois lawmakers voted to set up a court for children in Chicago. A 2019 article by the Chicago radio station WBEZ states that it was the first juvenile court in the world. A juvenile court is a separate court for children accused of crimes, run to help the child instead of only punishing.

Before then, police arrested children, put them in handcuffs and held them overnight in jails and police stations with adult prisoners. In 1882 the Cook County jail in Chicago held more than 250 children aged 14 and under, and at least 20 of them were younger than 11. A 10-year-old boy who stole a pair of shoes waited almost two weeks in the county jail for a grand jury to hear his case.

Julia Lathrop, who worked at Hull House, visited Illinois jails and wrote down what she found. Lucy Flower proposed a separate court for children. In 1907 a court and detention building for children opened at Halsted and Ewing Streets.
