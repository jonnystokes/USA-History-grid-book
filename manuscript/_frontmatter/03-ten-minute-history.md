# The History in Ten Paragraphs

The ten paragraphs below, one for each era, state the main line of the country's history. Every fact here is told in full in one of the 37 chapters.

## Before 1500

People were living in North America at least 21,000 years ago. The oldest sign found so far is a set of human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico. Some scientists questioned their age in 2021, and two other kinds of tests gave the same age in 2023. Estimates of how many people lived here before 1492 run from about 1 million to about 18 million. Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, was the largest city north of Mexico by about 1050. Norse sailors, the people often called Vikings, camped in Newfoundland around the year 1000, the first known visit from Europe. Christopher Columbus reached the Bahamas in 1492, but he never set foot in what is now the United States.

## The 1500s

Spanish soldiers, and the diseases that traveled ahead of them, reached what is now the United States. Juan Ponce de León led the first European expedition to Florida that anyone wrote down, in 1513. Hernando de Soto's expedition crossed the Southeast from 1539 to 1543. Smallpox and other diseases from Europe spread along Native trade routes ahead of the newcomers and killed large shares of whole communities. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine, Florida, in 1565. In January 1599 Juan de Oñate's soldiers stormed and burned Acoma Pueblo, in New Mexico. Oñate sentenced the survivors over age 12 to twenty years of forced work, and he had one foot cut off each man over 25.

## The 1600s

English colonists settled the Atlantic coast, and by the end of the century their farms ran on slavery. Colonists built Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607, and they made the colony pay by growing tobacco. The crew of an English ship, the *White Lion*, landed about 20 captive Africans at Point Comfort, Virginia, in late August 1619. The *Mayflower*'s passengers founded Plymouth in 1620. Judges in Virginia sentenced John Punch, an African who had run away, to serve for life in 1640. Far to the west, Pueblo people rose up together in 1680 and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. By 1700 enslaved Africans outnumbered white indentured servants, people bound to work for years to pay for their crossing, on Virginia's tobacco farms.

## 1700 to 1750

The colonies grew on the work of people who were not free, and those people fought back. Between half and two-thirds of the white people who came from the 1630s to the Revolution came as indentured servants. About 20 enslaved men led by a man called Jemmy seized weapons at the Stono River in South Carolina in September 1739 and marched toward Spanish Florida. The rebels killed about 25 white colonists. The militia, armed colonists called up to fight, killed 35 to 50 of the rebels, and colonists set the rebels' heads on stakes along the roads. Philadelphia, the largest town, had about 13,000 people by 1750.

## 1750 to 1800

A new country came out of these years, and its founders wrote slavery into its constitution. British forces beat the French in a war for the continent that ended in 1763. Members of the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776. After the war for independence, American and British diplomats, the officials who spoke for their countries, agreed in 1783 that the new country's western border would be the Mississippi River. They invited no Native nation to the talks. Delegates in Philadelphia wrote the Constitution in 1787. Under it, each enslaved person counted as three fifths of a free person in deciding how many seats each state got in Congress. Census takers counted 697,681 enslaved people in 1790, in the first census, the government's count of everyone in the country.

## 1800 to 1850

The country grew west by purchase, by treaty and by force, and slavery grew with it. American diplomats agreed in 1803 to pay France $15 million for Louisiana, a huge stretch of land west of the Mississippi River. President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act in 1830, and under it soldiers drove more than 16,000 Cherokee people west in 1838 and 1839. More than 4,000 of them died of cold, hunger and disease by the usual count, and one scientist who studies the size of populations puts the Cherokee losses at 8,000 or more. American armies won a war with Mexico that ended in 1848, and Mexico's leaders gave up more than 525,000 square miles, California among them. The same year, women met at Seneca Falls, New York, and signed a declaration of women's rights. Nearly 4 million people were enslaved by 1860.

## 1850 to 1900

Slavery ended in a war, and then the country spread west across Native land. Confederate soldiers, who fought for the Southern slave states whose leaders had broken away from the country, fired on Fort Sumter in April 1861. Settlers took up 270 million acres under the Homestead Act of 1862, much of it land that US officials had taken from Native nations. About 620,000 soldiers died in the Civil War by the usual count. Slavery ended with the 13th Amendment, a change to the Constitution, in December 1865, except as punishment for a crime. Soldiers of the 7th Cavalry killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people at Wounded Knee in December 1890. In 1896 the Supreme Court justices ruled in *Plessy v. Ferguson* that separating people by race was lawful.

## 1900 to 1950

Americans fought in two world wars in these fifty years and lived through hard times between them. Members of Congress declared war on Germany in April 1917. Under the 19th Amendment, from 1920, no state may deny a citizen the vote because of sex. Stock prices crashed in October 1929, and in the Great Depression of the 1930s about one worker in four had no job. Japanese pilots attacked Pearl Harbor, in Hawaii, on December 7, 1941, and more than 2,400 people were killed or missing. Army officers then removed about 120,000 people with Japanese family roots from the West Coast to camps. More than 400,000 Americans died in the war. A US bomber crew dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima, in Japan, on August 6, 1945, and between 90,000 and 166,000 people there died from it within four months.

## 1950 to 2000

Black Americans forced changes in the law, and American astronauts flew to the Moon. In 1954 the Supreme Court justices ruled that separate schools by race were unlawful. After police in Montgomery, Alabama, arrested Rosa Parks in December 1955 for refusing to give up her bus seat, most of the city's Black people stayed off its buses for a year, until federal judges ended the rule that kept Black riders apart from white riders. The Soviet Union, a large country ruled from Moscow, was the main rival of the United States for most of these years, and its engineers launched the first satellite in 1957. President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act in 1964 and the Voting Rights Act in 1965. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon on July 20, 1969. The Soviet Union broke apart in December 1991.

## 2000 to today

These years began with an attack and two long wars, and then came a pandemic, a disease that spread around the world. Hijackers flew airliners into the World Trade Center and the Pentagon on September 11, 2001, and killed nearly 3,000 people. American forces fought in Afghanistan for twenty years. They invaded Iraq in 2003, after American leaders said Iraq had nuclear, chemical or biological weapons, and none were found. In 2015 the Supreme Court justices ruled that same-sex couples could marry in every state. A Minneapolis police officer murdered George Floyd in May 2020. A crowd attacked the Capitol on January 6, 2021, while members of Congress counted the official votes from each state for president. By September 2026, COVID-19 had killed more than 1.2 million people in the United States.
