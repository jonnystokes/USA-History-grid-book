# A Ten-Minute History

The ten paragraphs below, one for each era, state the main line of the country's history. Every fact here is told in full, with its source, in one of the 37 chapters.

## Before 1500

People were living in North America at least 21,000 years ago. The oldest sign found so far is a set of footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, and some researchers question that date. Estimates of how many people lived here before 1492 run from about 1 million to about 18 million. By about 1050, Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, was the largest city north of Mexico. In what is now New York, five nations joined in the Haudenosaunee league. Norse sailors from Greenland camped in Newfoundland around the year 1000 and left.

## The 1500s

On October 12, 1492, Columbus's ships reached Guanahani, in the Bahamas, where Lucayan Taino people lived. Hernando de Soto's expedition crossed the Southeast from 1539 to 1543. The diseases Europeans carried, smallpox among them, spread along Native trade routes ahead of them and killed large shares of whole communities. In 1565 Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine, Florida. In January 1599 Juan de Oñate's soldiers stormed and burned Acoma Pueblo in New Mexico. Oñate sentenced the survivors over age 12 to twenty years of forced labor and had one foot cut off each man over 25.

## The 1600s

English colonists built Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607, and tobacco made the colony pay. In late August 1619 an English ship, the *White Lion*, landed "20. and odd" captive Africans at Point Comfort, Virginia. The *Mayflower* reached Cape Cod in November 1620, and its passengers founded Plymouth. In 1640 a Virginia court sentenced John Punch, an African who had run away, to serve for life. In 1680 the Pueblo nations rose together and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. By 1700 enslaved Africans outnumbered white indentured servants on Virginia's tobacco farms.

## 1700 to 1750

Between half and two-thirds of the white people who came from the 1630s to the Revolution came as indentured servants, bound to work for years to pay for the crossing. In September 1739 about 20 enslaved men led by a man called Jemmy seized weapons at the Stono River in South Carolina and marched toward Spanish Florida. About 25 white colonists were killed. The militia killed 35 to 50 of the rebels, and colonists set the rebels' heads on stakes along the roads. By 1750 Philadelphia, the largest town, had about 13,000 people.

## 1750 to 1800

British and French armies fought for the continent from 1754 to 1763. The British won, and in 1763 the king drew a line along the Appalachian mountains that colonists were not to cross. On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. The British army surrendered at Yorktown in 1781, and the Treaty of Paris of 1783 set the new country's western border at the Mississippi. No Native nation was invited to Paris. In 1787 delegates in Philadelphia wrote the Constitution. It counted each enslaved person as three fifths of a free person for seats in Congress. The first census, in 1790, counted 3,929,214 people, 697,681 of them enslaved.

## 1800 to 1850

In 1803 American diplomats in Paris agreed to pay France $15 million for 828,000 square miles west of the Mississippi. President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830. Under it, soldiers drove more than 16,000 Cherokee people west in 1838 and 1839. Over 4,000 died by the usual count, and one demographer puts the loss at 8,000 or more. In the treaty of February 2, 1848, Mexico's leaders gave up more than 525,000 square miles, including California. The same year, women met at Seneca Falls, New York, and signed a declaration of women's rights. By 1860 nearly 4 million people were enslaved.

## 1850 to 1900

Confederate soldiers fired on Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861. Under the Homestead Act of 1862, settlers took up 270 million acres, much of it land taken from Native nations. About 620,000 soldiers died in the Civil War by the usual count. Slavery ended with the 13th Amendment, in force from December 6, 1865, except as punishment for a crime. From 1882, under the Chinese Exclusion Act, Chinese laborers could not enter the country. On December 29, 1890, the 7th Cavalry killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people at Wounded Knee. In 1896, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, the Supreme Court justices ruled that separating people by race was lawful.

## 1900 to 1950

The United States entered the First World War on April 6, 1917. The 19th Amendment, in 1920, states that no state may deny the vote on account of sex. Stock prices crashed on October 29, 1929, and by 1933 about one worker in four had no job. Japanese pilots attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and 2,403 people were killed or missing. In 1942 Army officers removed about 120,000 people of Japanese descent from the West Coast to camps. In the Second World War 16,112,566 Americans served and 405,399 died. On August 6, 1945, a US plane dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima.

## 1950 to 2000

From about 1910 to 1970, about 6 million Black Americans left the South. In 1954 the Supreme Court justices ruled that separate schools by race were unlawful. Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, stopped riding the city buses on December 5, 1955. President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act on June 29, 1956, and highway planners routed the new roads mostly through poor neighborhoods, most of them Black. President Lyndon Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964, and the Voting Rights Act on August 6, 1965. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon on July 20, 1969. The Soviet Union dissolved on December 26, 1991.

## 2000 to today

On September 11, 2001, hijackers flew airliners into the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, and 2,977 people were killed. US troops fought in Afghanistan from October 2001 until August 2021, and invaded Iraq on March 20, 2003, on the stated grounds that Iraq had weapons of mass destruction. None were found. On June 26, 2015, the Supreme Court justices ruled that same-sex couples could marry in every state. A Minneapolis police officer murdered George Floyd on May 25, 2020. On January 6, 2021, a crowd attacked the Capitol while Congress counted the electoral votes. From January 2020 through September 2026, COVID-19 killed 1,245,791 people in the United States.
