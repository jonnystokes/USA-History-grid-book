## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, every Native nation in this land had its own songs. People made most music for a purpose, such as a ceremony, a healing, a dance, a game or a burial. Nobody wrote music down in these centuries, so no singer's name and no song's name survive from them. Some of the instruments survive. Archaeologists have dug up wooden flutes and metal-covered panpipes that are more than a thousand years old. The nations kept their songs by teaching them to each new generation, and many of those songs are still sung.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Flutes and panpipes that survive" -->
The oldest known wooden flutes in North America were found in a cave in northeastern Arizona. In 1931 the archaeologist Earl H. Morris explored 15 cliff caves in the Prayer Rock district, which is now part of the Navajo Nation. In the largest cave he found four wooden flutes. He named the place Broken Flute Cave.

The flutes were made between the years 620 and 670. The Arizona State Museum in Tucson holds them today. The museum lists them as Ancestral Pueblo flutes, which means the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples made them. Each flute is a tube of box elder wood between about 68 and 74 centimeters long, a little over two feet. The finger holes are almost the same on each flute, so the maker planned which notes the flute would play. Red, blue and black feathers were tied to one end. Two of the flutes lay broken in trash above a grave. The other two lay whole under a dirt floor, tied together.

Farther east, people of the Hopewell culture built earthworks in the woodlands of eastern North America between the years 1 and 400. Archaeologists have found 105 of their panpipes at 55 sites, from Wisconsin and Ontario down to the Gulf Coast. A Hopewell panpipe is three or four tubes of cane, reed or bone, held together by a jacket of hammered metal. The metal of 102 jackets is known. Of those, 81 are copper, and the rest are silver, copper covered with silver, or iron. Almost all of the panpipes were found in graves or burial mounds, not in houses.

Nobody knows what tunes anyone played on these flutes and panpipes, because no one wrote music down here before 1500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums, rattles, and songs that belong to someone" -->
The Onondaga Nation of what is now New York is one of the Haudenosaunee nations. Onondaga singers play a water drum and a horn rattle. The drum is made of cedar, with deer hide stretched over the top. The horn rattle is a dried, hollowed animal horn with seeds inside. A rattle made of turtle shell is used only in a few ceremonies.

The Onondaga keep two kinds of songs. Anyone may sing social songs anywhere. Ceremonial songs are sung only in the longhouse, the building where ceremonies are held, and outsiders may not watch them.

Among the Tlingit of what is now southeast Alaska, a song can belong to a clan. A clan is a large family group that traces itself back to shared ancestors. A clan owns its songs, its stories, its crests and its ceremonial clothing. The Tlingit word for these owned things is *at.óow*, which means "something owned or purchased." Some songs and stories tell of a clan's losses in a certain place. In the Tlingit view, those songs are the clan's deed to that place, the proof that the place is theirs.

These rules come from the nations' own accounts today. No written record from before 1500 describes them, because no one here wrote in those centuries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### R. Carlos Nakai

> **Who:** A flute player of Navajo (Diné) and Ute descent who plays a traditional cedar flute.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1946, in Flagstaff, Arizona. Still performing in 2026.

R. Carlos Nakai plays a traditional Native cedar flute. Flutes survive from before 1500, but no flute player's name does. Nakai has recorded more than forty albums with Canyon Records in Phoenix, Arizona. The first was *Changes*, in 1983. His concert calendar for 2026 lists shows in Utah and Colorado in September and more in October and November.

Nakai was born in Flagstaff, Arizona, on April 16, 1946. He is of Navajo and Ute descent. His father, Raymond Nakai, was chairman of the Navajo Nation from 1963 to 1970.

He first studied the trumpet. A car accident ruined his embouchure, the way a trumpet player shapes the lips against the mouthpiece, and he could no longer play it. Someone gave him a cedar flute as a gift and challenged him to master it.

His albums *Canyon Trilogy* and *Earth Spirit* each sold 500,000 copies. They were the first recordings of the traditional Native flute to sell that many. In 2014 *Canyon Trilogy* passed one million copies. Between 1994 and 2013 he was nominated for ten Grammy awards. Nakai calls himself "an iconoclastic traditionalist." He means that he treats his heritage as something that changes and grows, not something frozen in the past.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s nearly all the music made in this land was still Native music. European music was heard only where European ships landed, and it was church singing. Spanish priests sang Latin hymns. French and English Protestants sang psalms, which are songs from the Bible. The Europeans sang when they came ashore to claim land and when they held services. Native people watched and listened, and a few of their reactions were written down by the Europeans. No record from the 1500s names a mission choir or a Native singer trained in church music. The first ones on record come after 1600.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns sung while claiming land, Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
In June 1564 French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline on the St. Johns River, near today's Jacksonville, Florida. The land belonged to the Timucua people. The Huguenots' leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on June 30 he ordered his men to sing aloud on the beach to thank God for their safe arrival. In 1865 the historian Francis Parkman wrote that on the morning they began the fort, the men sang a psalm before they started work.

On September 8, 1565, the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at Seloy, a Timucua town, and founded St. Augustine. His chaplain, Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, wrote an account of that day. A chaplain is a priest who serves a group of soldiers or travelers. Mendoza wrote that Menéndez came ashore "to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery." Mendoza walked to meet him carrying a cross and singing the *Te Deum*, a Latin hymn of praise to God. "A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done," he wrote.

On September 20, 1565, Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline and killed most of the Frenchmen there. In a letter of October 15, Menéndez wrote that 132 of them had "their throats cut." He gave his reason in the same letter: "they spread the Lutheran sect," meaning the Protestant faith. When Menéndez came back to St. Augustine, Mendoza and his companions walked ahead of him "in a procession, singing the Te Deum laudamus." In the weeks after, Menéndez had about 245 more French prisoners killed at Matanzas Inlet.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalms at Drake's landing, California, 1579" -->
In June 1579 the English captain Francis Drake stopped on the coast north of San Francisco Bay to repair his ship. The land belonged to the Coast Miwok people. Drake's men stayed for about five weeks. While they were there, they held prayers, sang psalms and read from the Bible, and Miwok people came to watch.

A book about the voyage, *The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake*, came out in London in 1628. Most of it came from the notes of Drake's chaplain, Francis Fletcher. It says the Miwok "tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes" that each time they came, "their first request was Gnaah." That word meant they wanted the Englishmen to sing. In 1947 the anthropologist Robert Heizer found that *gnaah* is a Coast Miwok word. An anthropologist is a scientist who studies people and the ways they live.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
In 1598 Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north across the Rio Grande to start a colony in New Mexico. The land belonged to the Pueblo peoples. The music historian John Koegel writes that Pueblo people began to hear Spanish military music, and to sing Catholic chant and songs in parts, "during the Oñate expedition and its aftermath." The first music teachers there whose names are on record came in the 1600s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonists' music that was written down in the 1600s was church music. In New England, congregations sang psalms from a book printed in Massachusetts in 1640. For most of the century that book printed only the words, with no musical notes. In New Mexico, Spanish friars built organs and trained Pueblo singers to sing Latin church music. The same friars, and the Spanish governors, banned Pueblo religious ceremonies and punished the Pueblo leaders who kept them. From 1619 Africans were brought to the English colonies by force, and they brought their own music. Nobody who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bay Psalm Book, 1640, and lining out" -->
In 1640 Stephen Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre* at Cambridge, Massachusetts. People today call it the *Bay Psalm Book*. It was the first book printed in British North America. Richard Mather and other New England ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into rhyming verse, so that a congregation could sing them. Eleven copies of that first printing survive.

The 1640 book had no printed music. For each psalm it named a tune that people already knew from English psalm books. The ninth edition, printed in 1698, was the first to include music. It printed 13 tunes taken from a music book by John Playford, an Englishman.

Most people in a congregation had no book, and some could not read. In 1644 an assembly of English and Scottish ministers at Westminster, in London, wrote a guide for church services. It said that where many people could not read, the minister or a person he chose should "read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof." This way of singing is called lining out. A leader read or sang one line, and the congregation sang it back. Colonists from England and Scotland brought lining out to America. Old Regular Baptist churches in the United States still sing this way today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" name="Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones

> **Who:** A Spanish Franciscan friar who built the church at San Felipe Pueblo, put an organ in it, and trained Pueblo singers.
> **When and where:** San Felipe Pueblo, a Keres-speaking town in New Mexico. Died in 1609.

Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones put an organ in the church at San Felipe Pueblo and trained singers there to sing church music. Fray is the title of a friar, a man who has joined a Catholic religious order and lives by its rules. Quiñones belonged to the Franciscan order.

San Felipe is a Pueblo town in New Mexico whose people speak the Keres language. Quiñones learned Keres and baptized many people there. He was the custos, the head of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico. At San Felipe he built the church, a house for the friars, a pharmacy and a place to care for the sick.

A Franciscan writer named Agustín de Vetancurt later described his work. Vetancurt wrote that Quiñones "installed an organ to enrich the Divine Service and encouraged music-making by the skilled singers" he had trained. Quiñones died in 1609. The music historian John Koegel calls him "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music" to live in what is now the US Southwest. Neither writer records where Quiñones was born, or what the singers of San Felipe thought of the music they were taught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church music and banned ceremonies in New Mexico" -->
Other friars taught music in more Pueblo towns. Fray Bernardo de Marta came to New Mexico in about 1605. He taught Pueblo people in many towns to sing and to play European instruments, and he died in 1635. By 1634 Fray Roque de Figueredo was teaching Zuni boys to sing in parts.

Spanish church leaders and governors also punished Pueblo people for keeping their own religion. From 1656 to 1665 the Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered friars to go into the kivas, the rooms where Pueblo people held their ceremonies. There the friars burned the masks and took or destroyed sacred objects.

In 1675 the governor, Juan Francisco Treviño, had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested and charged with sorcery, which means using magic to do harm. Four were sentenced to be hanged. Three were hanged, and one killed himself. The rest were whipped in public. In 1680 Pueblo people rose up and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. They killed about 400 Spanish settlers, 21 of them Franciscan friars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
In late August 1619 an English ship called the *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes," in the words of the colonist John Rolfe, at Point Comfort, Virginia. The ship's crew had taken these Africans from a Portuguese slave ship. They came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa.

Enslaved Africans brought their own songs, and their own ways of making instruments. They made music in the English colonies through the 1600s. No one who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives. The first written description of a banjo-like instrument anywhere in the Americas comes from the island of Jamaica. There, in 1687, an English doctor named Hans Sloane wrote about an instrument he called a "strum strump."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, New England ministers began teaching their congregations to sing from written notes. They started singing schools and printed the first books of music lessons in the colonies. From 1731 people in Boston could buy a ticket to a concert. In the plantation colonies, enslaved Black musicians played the fiddle. For many of those musicians, the only written record is a newspaper notice their enslaver printed when they ran away. In 1739 enslaved men rose up near the Stono River in South Carolina, with drums beating. The next year, the men who made South Carolina's laws made it a crime for enslaved people to use drums and horns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Singing by note and the first singing schools" -->
By about 1700 most New England congregations sang by lining out, and they sang by ear. In 1720 the Reverend Thomas Symmes of Bradford, Massachusetts, published a booklet called *The Reasonableness of Regular Singing, or Singing by Note*. Regular singing meant singing from written notes. Symmes wrote that church singing had got worse because "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn."

The Reverend John Tufts of Newbury, Massachusetts, published *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes*. It was the first book of music lessons printed in the English colonies. No copy of the first edition is known, so its date is disputed. The historian Henry Wilder Foote dated it to about 1712. Other sources date it to about 1721. Tufts traveled to give talks about singing and to set up singing schools, where a teacher taught a class to read and sing music.

In 1721 the Reverend Thomas Walter, who was 25, published *The Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained*, with 24 psalm tunes. James Franklin printed it in Boston. It was the first music in the colonies printed with bar lines, the lines that divide music into equal parts of time. New England singing schools kept using it for more than forty years. Singing schools began in New England in the 1720s. The records that survive do not say where the first one met or what a pupil paid.

Young people took up the new singing first. On March 20, 1721, the Boston judge Samuel Sewall wrote in his diary that the minister Cotton Mather had preached that night "in the school house to the young musicians." Sewall wrote that the house was full, "and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston."

Other church members fought the change. Symmes reported that in his town, "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." In 1728 the Reverend Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut wrote answers to the objections people raised. One objection was that singing by note "will bring in organs quickly." Another was that the new way must be wrong "because the young people fall in with it."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concerts for a ticket, and a fiddle for a prize" -->
The *Boston News-Letter* of December 1731 advertised a "Concert of Music" on several instruments. It was held on December 30, 1731, in Mr. Pelham's great room in Boston, and a ticket cost five shillings. It is the earliest advertised concert in Boston that the music historian Oscar Sonneck found. After a concert at Faneuil Hall in 1744, the town's selectmen received £205 for the poor of Boston. The selectmen were the men elected to run the town.

In Virginia, the *Virginia Gazette* of November 26, 1736, announced prizes for a holiday gathering at Page's Warehouse in Hanover County on November 30. One prize was "A fine Cremona Fiddle to be plaid for, by any number of Country Fiddlers." A Cremona fiddle was a violin made in the town of Cremona, in Italy. In November 1737 the races at John Pinkerton's Old Field in Virginia included a fiddling contest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and the enslaved musicians who played them" -->
The banjo came from West Africa, and enslaved Africans built it again in the Americas. Scholars have found more than 60 plucked instruments in West Africa that may be its ancestors. The first known mention of a banjo in the mainland colonies is in John Peter Zenger's newspaper, the *New-York Weekly Journal*, in 1736.

In Virginia, enslavers printed notices in the *Virginia Gazette* when an enslaved person ran away. Each notice described the person's body, clothes and skills, so that strangers could recognize and catch the person. Playing the fiddle was one of the skills these notices listed. On December 5, 1745, Robert Whitfield of Isle of Wight County offered four pistoles for a young man named Tom, who "plays very well on the Violin." A pistole was a Spanish gold coin used in the colonies. In April 1746 John Thornton of Fredericksburg advertised for another man named Tom, about 46 years old, who "plays on the Violin, and is a Sawyer." A sawyer is a man who saws logs into boards.

For many enslaved musicians, these notices are the only records that survive. They survive because the men ran away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harry-fiddler-music" name="Harry, a fiddler who ran to his wife, 1746" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harry, a fiddler who ran to his wife, 1746

> **Who:** An enslaved man who played the fiddle. The man who held him printed a notice for his capture.
> **When and where:** Near Jamestown, Virginia, March 1746.

Harry was an enslaved fiddler who ran away in early March 1746 from the man who held him, near Jamestown, Virginia. Everything known about him comes from one notice in the *Virginia Gazette*, printed between March 20 and 27, 1746. The notice gives him no last name.

The notice says that Harry was about 35 years old and about 5 feet 7 inches tall. It says he had small eyes and "a very large Beard," and that he "plays upon the Fiddle." He had once belonged to Colonel Grymes of Richmond County, Virginia.

The man who printed the notice was William Newgent. A later reprint of the notice spells his first name James. Newgent guessed that Harry had gone to Richmond County, "where he has a Wife." He offered one pistole for Harry's return. At the end he added: "As he ran away without any Cause, I desire he may be punish'd by Whipping, as the Law directs." Newgent was asking whoever caught Harry to have him beaten with a whip. The record does not say whether Harry was caught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harry-fiddler-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums at Stono, and the 1740 law against drums and horns" -->
On September 9, 1739, about 20 enslaved men seized guns near the Stono River, south of Charleston, South Carolina. A report written that year called them "Angola Negroes" and named their leader as a man called Jemmy. They marched south toward Spanish Florida. The report says more men joined them, "calling out Liberty," and they "marched on with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating." Colours here means flags. When there were sixty to a hundred of them, they stopped in a field and "set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them."

The same report says the rebels killed white men, women and children as they marched. The militia, a force of armed planters, attacked them in the field. The planters shot many of the men they captured on the spot, and they shot men who had run back to their plantations. The report counts about 40 Black people and 20 white people killed. Later counts give about 25 white colonists and 35 to 50 rebels killed.

On May 10, 1740, the men of the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. In section 36 they wrote that it was "absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province" to stop enslaved people from "using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments, which may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes." They meant that drums and horns could be used to call enslaved people together to rise up. Under the law, a master or overseer who let enslaved people beat drums, blow horns or gather in public paid a fine of ten pounds each time. Any master, overseer or other person could seize an enslaved person found off the plantation without a written pass and without a white person. The law named Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays in particular. An enslaved person found carrying a weapon could be disarmed and whipped.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, people in the colonies began to write their own music and print it. The best known of them was William Billings, a Boston tanner, who published his first book of church songs in 1770. During the Revolution, drums told soldiers when to wake, march and fight, and both sides sang new words to old tunes. Enslaved and free Black musicians played fiddles, horns, drums and banjos in the port towns and on the plantations. One of them, Newport Gardner, had been brought from Africa as a boy. He became a music teacher in Newport, Rhode Island, and bought his freedom in 1791. That same year, on the Pacific coast, a Spanish expedition wrote down Tlingit songs in musical notes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="William Billings and the New England singing masters" -->
In 1770 William Billings, a 24-year-old Boston tanner, published *The New-England Psalm-Singer*. A tanner turns animal skins into leather. The Library of Congress calls the book "the first publication consisting of sacred music composed entirely by a native-born American." Sacred music is music for worship. The silversmith Paul Revere engraved the picture at the front of the book.

Billings also taught singing schools. He opened one in Boston with John Barry in 1769, and he taught another in Stoughton, Massachusetts, in 1774. The historian Henry Wilder Foote counted about forty American books of church music published before 1800. Most of them were printed in Boston.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-billings" name="William Billings" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Billings

> **Who:** A tanner and singing teacher who wrote about 340 songs for choirs. His 1770 book was the first book of church music written entirely by an American-born composer.
> **When and where:** Boston. Born October 7, 1746. Died September 26, 1800.

William Billings wrote about 340 songs for choirs and published them in six books. He did it while working as a tanner and a singing teacher, and he died poor.

He was born in Boston on October 7, 1746. His father died when Billings was 14, and he went to work learning to tan leather. He taught himself music, mostly from books. In 1853 a music historian named Nathaniel Gould described him as having "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered."

His first book, *The New-England Psalm-Singer* of 1770, included his song "Chester." It opens with the words "Let tyrants shake their iron rod." The singers say that they do not fear tyrants or the chains of slavery, because they trust in God. In 1778 Billings printed a longer "Chester" in his second book, *The Singing Master's Assistant*, with more verses against the British. According to the writer David Stowe, soldiers "learned the words by heart" and carried Billings's songs wherever the army sent them.

American copyright law was weak then. Copyright is the legal right to decide who may copy and sell a piece of writing or music. Other printers copied Billings's tunes without paying him. The men of the Massachusetts legislature once passed a bill to protect his work, but the governor refused to sign it.

His wife, Lucy, died on March 26, 1795, and left him with six children under 18. His friends paid to print his last book, *The Continental Harmony*, in 1794. In the 1790s he worked as a street cleaner. Billings died poor in Boston on September 26, 1800. He was buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-billings" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs and signals of the Revolution" -->
"Yankee Doodle" began as a British song that made fun of the colonists. A British army surgeon, Richard Shuckburgh, is said to have written the words between about 1755 and 1758, during the French and Indian War. Historians dispute that date. In 1768 British soldiers arrived to occupy Boston, and their musicians played the song in the streets to mock the colonists. Early in the war, British and Loyalist troops played it to make fun of the Continental soldiers. Loyalists were colonists who stayed on the British side. The Americans then made up their own verses, and by 1781 "Yankee Doodle" was a favorite American song.

In July 1768 the *Boston Gazette* printed "The Liberty Song." John Dickinson of Pennsylvania wrote its words to the tune of a British navy song, "Heart of Oak." He sent it to his friend James Otis with a note that "indifferent songs are very powerful on certain occasions." By indifferent he meant songs that were not very good.

In the Continental Army, drums gave orders. The drum call called "The Reveille" woke the soldiers in the morning. "The General" told them to take down their tents and get ready to march. "The Assembly" told them to line up at the flags. "To Arms" told them to pick up their weapons and get ready to fight. "The Tattoo," beaten at 9 or 10 at night, told tavern keepers to stop serving the soldiers drinks. According to the American Battlefield Trust, boys under 14 were recommended as drummers, and some fifers were 9 or 10 years old. A fifer plays the fife, a small, high-pitched flute.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black musicians, enslaved and free" -->
Virginia enslavers kept printing notices that named enslaved musicians. On May 4, 1769, William Gregory advertised for Peter, about 44, who had taken with him "a fiddle, which he is much delighted in." On September 8, 1775, David Walker of Dinwiddie County advertised for Jemmy. He wrote that Jemmy "is very fond of singing hymns and preaching," and that he was living near Williamsburg under the name James Williams.

In August 1780 William Allason offered forty pounds for four men who had run away together from near Fauquier Court House, Virginia. One of them was Mark, about 25. He was born on the island of St. Jago, in the Cape Verde Islands off West Africa, and he spoke Portuguese. The notice says Mark "blows the French horn, can play on the fiddle, whistles many tunes well." It says he loved marches and Catholic church music. Allason guessed that the four men planned to get aboard a ship.

In 1774 Philip Vickers Fithian was a tutor, a live-in teacher, to the children of Robert Carter III at Nomini Hall, a plantation in Virginia. His journal records that on a January evening, enslaved people gathered in the schoolroom "& began to play the Fiddle, & dance." Two of Carter's sons, Ben and Harry, joined them, and Fithian sent everyone away. A few days later, he wrote, "several Negroes & Ben, & Harry are playing on a Banjo & dancing."

In Charleston, South Carolina, a newspaper of December 12, 1766, described "a croud of negroes assembled at the lower end of Elliott-street, with a banjer playing," on the evening of December 2. A Charleston town rule of 1750 banned beating drums in town without an officer's permission.

A watercolor painting called *The Old Plantation*, made between about 1785 and 1795, shows enslaved Black men and women dancing. One man plays an early banjo made from a gourd. Another man plays a drum, and two women hold rattles. It is the earliest known painting of a banjo. In 2010 the librarian Susan Shames identified the painter as John Rose, a South Carolina planter who held people in slavery. The Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia, owns it.

Thomas Jefferson held people in slavery at Monticello, his plantation in Virginia. In his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, in the 1787 edition, he wrote about Black musicians. He wrote that "In music they are more generally gifted than the whites." Then he doubted that they could write longer, more complicated music: that, he wrote, "is yet to be proved." In a note he called the "Banjar" the instrument "which they brought hither from Africa." In the same passage he wrote that Black people had "no poetry," and he dismissed the published poems of Phillis Wheatley, a Black woman.

In New England, Black communities held their own election days, when they chose a "king" or a "governor." Their parades had "tambourines, banjos, fiddles and drums."

### Newport Gardner

> **Who:** Born Occramer Marycoo in West Africa, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, where he became a music teacher and composer.
> **When and where:** Born about 1746. Newport, from about 1760. Died in Liberia in 1826.

Newport Gardner was taken from West Africa as a boy, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, and became a music teacher there. His name at birth was Occramer Marycoo. He was about 14 when slave traders brought him to Newport.

Caleb Gardner, a merchant and ship captain, bought him and renamed him Newport Gardner. The music writer Sophie Lowe writes that Caleb Gardner "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings" through the slave trade. The enslaved boy learned English and music in Newport. Many writers name the composer Andrew Law as his teacher. But Law is first known to have been in Rhode Island in 1783, when Gardner was already in his late thirties. Lowe concludes that he "probably had a variety of musical teachers."

In late December 1791 Gardner and some friends won a lottery. He used his share to buy freedom for himself and his family. He opened a school in Newport where he taught music. He was a deacon, a church member who helps lead it, at Newport's First Congregational Church. Later he helped found a Black church, which the historian John Millar dates to 1824.

The writer Eileen Southern credited to him a tune called "Crooked Shanks," printed in 1803. Lowe reports newer research that found the same tune in print in London in 1768, so the credit is uncertain. The words of his anthem, a church song, survive. They begin "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race."

He led a group of Black New Englanders to Liberia, in West Africa. Millar counts about 30 of them, and another historian counts about three dozen. The sources also differ on when they sailed: late December 1825, or January 4, 1826. Gardner died of disease in Liberia within months, in 1826.

In 1791 two Spanish ships anchored in Yakutat Bay, in what is now Alaska. Alejandro Malaspina and José de Bustamante led the expedition. The bay was Tlingit land. Several times, Tlingit people sang "to and with" the Spanish sailors.

Tadeo Haenke, a botanist on the ships who had trained in music, wrote the songs down in musical notes on four sheets of paper. The sheets stayed in the Museo de América, a museum in Madrid, Spain, for more than 200 years. The Tlingit ethnomusicologist Maria Shaa Tláa Williams studied them for the Sealaska Heritage Institute. An ethnomusicologist studies the music of a people and its place in their lives. Williams found that the singers used harmony, two or more notes sung together. The Tlingit scholar Judy Daxootsu Ramos said of the old songs, "we're still practicing that today."
