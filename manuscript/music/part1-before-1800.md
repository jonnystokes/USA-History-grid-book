## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 33 of 37 · Music · 10 eras · about 135 minutes**

**For most of American history, the people who made the music were often not the people who got paid for it.**

Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton, a blues singer from Alabama, recorded "Hound Dog" on August 13, 1952. Her record was No. 1 on the rhythm and blues chart for seven weeks. Don Robey, who owned her record company, put his own name on the copyright form as one of the song's writers. Four years later Elvis Presley recorded the same song and sold about 10 million copies. "I got one check for $500 and never saw another," Thornton said. She died poor in a Los Angeles boarding house in 1984.

This chapter follows American music from wooden flutes made in the 600s to songs streamed over the internet. In every era, look first at who made the music: Native singers, enslaved fiddlers, New England singing teachers, blues singers in Mississippi, a teenage DJ in the Bronx. Then look at who was paid. Again and again the money and the credit went to someone else, such as the white minstrel who copied a Black song, the publisher who put his own name on it, or the company that paid a set fee and kept the rest. Watch, too, for the people who tried to stop music, from lawmakers who banned drums to officials who banned Native dances.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Where did the banjo and the blues come from?
- Why did so many musicians make hit songs and still die poor?
- Who tried to stop people from playing their own music, and why?

**Five minutes?** Read "What the singers said the songs meant" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Jenny Lind's tour, 1850 to 1852", "Concerts for a ticket, and a fiddle for a prize", "The Kennedy Center, 2025 and 2026". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 6 minutes**

Native nations in this land made music for nearly every part of life, and they passed their songs on by teaching them. Their songs were for ceremonies, healing, work, games, courting a sweetheart, storytelling, hunting, farming, war and social dances. None of the nations north of Mexico had a way of writing tunes down, so no singer's name and no song's name from these centuries survives in writing.

Some of the instruments survive. Archaeologists, scientists who dig up and study what people left behind, have found wooden flutes and metal-covered panpipes more than a thousand years old. Onondaga and Tlingit singers today still follow rules about where a song may be sung and who owns it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How old are the oldest flutes?" -->
The oldest known wooden flutes in North America were made between the years 620 and 670. Ancestral Pueblo people, the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples, made them. The archaeologist Earl H. Morris found them in 1931, when he explored 15 caves in the Prayer Rock district of northeastern Arizona, in what is now the Navajo Nation. In the largest cave he found four wooden flutes. That cave is now called Broken Flute Cave.

**The flutes.** Each one is a tube of box elder wood between about 68 and 74 centimeters long, a little over two feet. The finger holes are almost the same on each flute, so the maker planned which notes the flute would play. Red, blue and black feathers were tied to one end. Two of the flutes lay broken in trash above a grave. The other two lay whole under a dirt floor, tied together. The Arizona State Museum in Tucson holds them today.

**The panpipes.** Farther east, people of the Hopewell culture lived in the woodlands of eastern North America between the years 1 and 400. "Hopewell" is a name archaeologists gave them, not the name of a nation. In southern Ohio they built earthworks, mounds and long banks of piled earth. A Hopewell panpipe is a row of three or four tubes of cane, reed or bone, held together by a metal jacket. A player blows across the tubes, and each tube makes a different note.

Archaeologists have found 105 of these panpipes at 55 sites, from Wisconsin and Ontario down to the Gulf Coast. The metal of 102 jackets is known. Of those, 81 are copper. The others are silver, copper covered with silver, iron, or iron and copper together. Almost all of the panpipes were buried with the dead. Very few came from places where people lived.

**Not known:** Nobody knows what tunes anyone played on these flutes and panpipes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums, rattles, and songs that belong to someone" -->
Onondaga singers today play a water drum and a horn rattle. The Onondaga Nation, in what is now central New York, is one of the Haudenosaunee nations. The water drum is usually made of cedar, with deer hide stretched over the top. The horn rattle is a dried, hollowed animal horn with seeds inside. A rattle made of turtle shell is used only in a few ceremonies.

The Onondaga keep two kinds of songs. Social songs can be sung anywhere. Songs for ceremonies are sung only in the longhouse, the building where ceremonies are held, and the public may not watch them.

**Songs a clan owns.** Among the Tlingit of what is now southeast Alaska, a song can belong to a clan, a large family group that traces itself back to shared ancestors. A clan owns its songs, its stories, its crests and the clothing its members wear in ceremonies. The Tlingit word for these owned things is *at.óow*, "something owned or purchased."

Some songs and stories tell of a clan's losses in a certain place. In the Tlingit view, those songs are the clan's deed to that place, the proof that the place is theirs.

**Known:** These rules come from the nations' own accounts today.
**Not known:** No written record from before 1500 describes them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="r-carlos-nakai-music" name="R. Carlos Nakai" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### R. Carlos Nakai

> **Who:** A flute player from a Navajo (Diné) and Ute family, whose records were the first of the traditional Native flute to sell 500,000 copies.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1946, in Flagstaff, Arizona. Still performing in 2026.

**A car crash ruined his trumpet playing, and someone gave him a flute.**

R. Carlos Nakai first studied the trumpet. A car accident ruined the way he shaped his lips against the trumpet's mouthpiece. Someone gave him a cedar flute as a gift and challenged him to master it. His family is Navajo and Ute, and his father, Raymond Nakai, was chairman of the Navajo Nation from 1963 to 1970.

**The records.** Nakai has recorded more than forty albums with Canyon Records in Phoenix, Arizona, starting with *Changes* in 1983. His albums *Canyon Trilogy* and *Earth Spirit* each sold 500,000 copies. They were the first recordings of the traditional Native flute to sell that many. In 2014 the RIAA, the music business group that gives sales awards, named *Canyon Trilogy* a platinum album, its award for one million copies.

He was nominated for ten Grammy awards between 1994 and 2013. Nakai calls himself "an iconoclastic traditionalist," someone who breaks with old ways and keeps them at the same time. His concert calendar for 2026 lists shows in Utah and Colorado in September and more in October and November.

**Why this story is here:** Flutes survive from before 1500, but no flute player's name does, so this era's story belongs to a living player of the traditional cedar flute.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 4 minutes**

The first European music on record in what is now the United States is church singing and soldiers' trumpets, and Native people watched and listened. It comes from three places: the coast of Florida, the coast of California, and New Mexico from 1598. Spanish priests sang Latin hymns, songs that praise God, when they came ashore and when they held services. French and English Protestants, Christians who had broken away from the Catholic Church, sang psalms, songs from the Bible.

Europeans wrote down a few of the Native listeners' reactions. No record from the 1500s names a mission choir or a Native singer trained in church music. The first ones on record come after 1598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns sung at the landings, Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
French Protestants called Huguenots built Fort Caroline in 1564, near the mouth of the St. Johns River and today's Jacksonville, Florida. They built it on the land of the Timucua people. Their leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on June 30 he ordered his men to sing aloud on the beach to thank God for their safe arrival. The historian Francis Parkman wrote in 1865 that on the morning they began the fort, the men sang a psalm before they started work.

**St. Augustine.** The Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at Seloy, a Timucua town, on September 8, 1565, and founded St. Augustine. His chaplain, the priest who served his soldiers, was Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, and Mendoza wrote an account of that day. Menéndez came ashore "to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery," he wrote, meaning cannons fired as a greeting.

Mendoza walked to meet him carrying a cross and singing the *Te Deum*, a Latin hymn of praise to God. "A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done," he wrote.

**The killings.** Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline on September 20, 1565, and killed most of the Frenchmen there. In a letter of October 15, Menéndez wrote that 132 of them had "their throats cut." He gave his reason in the same letter: "they spread the Lutheran sect," meaning the Protestant faith. When Menéndez came back to St. Augustine, Mendoza and his companions walked ahead of him in a line, "singing the Te Deum laudamus."

In the weeks after, shipwrecked Frenchmen surrendered to Menéndez at a place called Matanzas. His soldiers killed 111 of them. On October 12 they killed 134 more who had surrendered with their leader, Jean Ribault.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalms at Drake's landing, California, 1579" -->
The English captain Francis Drake stopped on the California coast in June 1579 to repair his ship, probably at the place now called Drake's Bay. Coast Miwok people lived along that shore. Drake's men stayed for about five weeks. They held prayers, sang psalms and read from the Bible, and Miwok people came to watch.

**"Gnaah."** A book about the voyage, *The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake*, states that the Miwok "tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes" that each time they came, "their first request was Gnaah." That word meant they wanted the Englishmen to sing. The anthropologist Robert Heizer, a scientist who studied people and the ways they live, wrote in 1947 that *gnaah* seems to be a Coast Miwok word.

**Who wrote the book.** It came out in London in 1628. Drake's nephew, also named Francis Drake, put it together. Its title page states that it was collected "out of the Notes of Master Francis Fletcher," the preacher on the voyage, and checked against other notes made on the same voyage. Heizer wrote that who wrote it is uncertain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into New Mexico in 1598 to start a colony on the lands of the Pueblo peoples. Pueblo people then began to hear Spanish military music and to sing Catholic church songs, some of them in parts. The music historian John Koegel dates this to "the Oñate expedition and its aftermath," meaning the journey and the years after it.

The earliest music teachers there that Koegel names are two friars, Catholic men who lived by the rules of a religious order and put the title Fray in front of their names. Fray Bernardo de Marta came in about 1605. Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones died in 1609.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

Colonists in the 1600s brought their church singing with them, and in New Mexico Spanish friars and officials punished Pueblo people for keeping their own ceremonies. In New England, congregations, the people who worship together at a church, sang psalms from a book printed in Massachusetts in 1640. For most of the century each printing of that book held only the words, with no musical notes.

In New Mexico, the friar Cristóbal de Quiñones put an organ in the church at San Felipe Pueblo and trained Pueblo singers. Later friars banned Pueblo religious ceremonies, and a Spanish governor had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested in 1675. Three were hanged. In Virginia, the crew of an English ship landed about twenty captured Africans in 1619. Enslaved Africans made music there through the century, but nobody who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Singing without notes: the Bay Psalm Book and lining out" -->
Stephen Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre* at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1640. People today call it the *Bay Psalm Book*, and it was the first book printed in British North America. Richard Mather and other New England ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into English verse with a steady beat, so that a congregation could sing them. Eleven copies of that first printing survive.

**No notes.** The 1640 book had no printed music. It lists tunes by name, tunes already printed in English psalm books. The ninth edition, printed in 1698, was the first to include music: 13 tunes taken from a music book by an Englishman, John Playford.

**Lining out.** A gathering of ministers at Westminster, in London, wrote a guide for church services in 1644. Where many people in a congregation could not read, the guide says, the minister or a person he chose should "read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof."

This way of singing is called lining out. A leader read or sang one line, and the congregation sang it back. Colonists from England and Scotland brought lining out to America, and members of Old Regular Baptist churches in the United States still sing this way today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" name="Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones

> **Who:** A Franciscan friar who built the church at San Felipe Pueblo, put an organ in it, and trained Pueblo singers.
> **When and where:** San Felipe Pueblo, a Keres-speaking town in New Mexico. Died in 1609.

**He put an organ in a Pueblo church and trained its singers.**

Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones belonged to the Franciscans, a Catholic religious order. He was the leader of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico. San Felipe is a Pueblo town whose people speak the Keres language. Quiñones learned Keres and baptized many Native people in the New Mexico missions, making them members of the Christian church in a ceremony with water. At San Felipe he built the church, a house for the friars, a pharmacy and a place to care for the sick.

**What one writer recorded.** Agustín de Vetancurt, a Franciscan writer in Mexico, described his work in a book of the lives of Franciscan friars, printed in the 1690s. Vetancurt wrote that Quiñones "installed an organ to enrich the Divine Service," meaning the church service, "and encouraged music-making by the skilled singers" he had trained.

Quiñones died in 1609. The music historian John Koegel calls him "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music" to live in what is now the US Southwest. Koegel gives neither his birthplace nor the year he came to New Mexico.

**Not known:** Vetancurt tells only the friar's side, and his account does not record what the singers of San Felipe thought of the music they were taught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church music and banned ceremonies in New Mexico" -->
Other friars taught music in more Pueblo towns. Fray Bernardo de Marta came to New Mexico in about 1605, taught Pueblo people in many towns to sing and to play European instruments, and died in 1635. By 1634 Fray Roque de Figueredo was teaching Zuni boys to sing in parts.

**The ban.** A Spanish church leader and a Spanish governor punished Pueblo people for keeping their own religion. The Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies from 1656 to 1665. He ordered friars to invade the sacred kivas, the rooms where Pueblo people held their ceremonies. There the friars burned the masks and took or destroyed sacred objects.

**The arrests.** The governor, Juan Francisco Treviño, had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested in 1675 and charged with sorcery, using magic to do harm. Four were sentenced to be hanged. Three were hanged, and one killed himself. The rest were shamed and whipped in public, then put in prison. The records do not name the men who passed the sentences or carried out the hangings and the whippings. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be freed, and Spanish officials let them go.

**Po'pay.** One of the whipped men was Po'pay, a leader from Ohkay Owingeh. On August 10, 1680, Po'pay led the Pueblo nations as they rose up together against the Spanish. The Pueblo fighters killed about 400 Spanish settlers, 21 of them Franciscan friars, and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for 12 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
The crew of an English ship called the *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes" at Point Comfort, Virginia, in late August 1619. Those are the words of the colonist John Rolfe, and "Negroes" was a word of that time for Black people. The crew had taken these Africans from a Portuguese slave ship. They came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa.

Enslaved Africans made music in the English colonies through the 1600s, but no one who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives. The first clear description of an early banjo comes from the island of Jamaica. An English doctor there, Hans Sloane, wrote in 1687 about an instrument he called a "strum strump."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 8 minutes**

Two fights over music ran through these fifty years. In New England, church members argued about singing from written notes, and in South Carolina, lawmakers took drums away from enslaved people. New England ministers began teaching their congregations to read notes around 1720. They started singing schools and printed the first books of music lessons in the colonies.

Boston had a concert for a paying audience by 1731, and a ticket cost five shillings. In Virginia, enslaved Black musicians played the fiddle. Three of them appear in notices their enslavers printed when the men ran away in 1745 and 1746. Enslaved men rose up near the Stono River in South Carolina in 1739, with drums beating. The next year South Carolina's lawmakers banned enslaved people from using drums and horns, and fined any master who let them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Singing by note and the first singing schools" -->
New England churches still sang by lining out at the start of the 1700s. "By the end of the seventeenth century singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies," the historian Henry Wilder Foote wrote. In the first 25 years of the 1700s, ministers in New England turned against lining out. They called for "regular singing" instead, which meant singing from written notes.

The Reverend Thomas Symmes of Bradford, Massachusetts, made the case in a booklet of 1720, *The Reasonableness of Regular Singing, or Singing by Note*. He wrote that church singing had got worse because "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn." New England had had singing schools in its early days, Symmes wrote, but people had stopped holding them.

**The first music books.** The Reverend John Tufts of Newbury, Massachusetts, published *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes*, the first book of music lessons printed in the English colonies.

Neither its exact title nor the date of its first edition is known, so its date is disputed. Foote dated it to about 1712. The Sacred Harp Publishing Company, which still prints a book of church songs, dates it to 1721. Tufts traveled to give talks about singing and to set up singing schools, where a teacher taught a class to read and sing music.

Thomas Walter, a 25-year-old nephew of the Boston minister Cotton Mather, published *The Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained* in 1721, with 24 psalm tunes. James Franklin printed it in Boston. It was the first music in the colonies printed with bar lines, the lines that divide music into equal parts of time. It stayed a favorite for more than forty years and went through six editions.

New singing schools spread in the 1720s. The records that survive do not say where the first one met or what a pupil paid.

**The young singers.** Young people took up the new singing first. The Boston judge Samuel Sewall wrote in his diary on March 20, 1721, that "Dr. Mather" had preached that night "in the school house to the young musicians." The house was full, Sewall wrote, "and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston."

**The fight.** Other church members fought the change. In Symmes's town, he reported, "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." He meant that people had been angry and arguing about it for almost six months.

The Reverend Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut wrote answers in 1728 to the objections people raised. One objection was that singing by note "leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly." The Church of England was the official church back in England. Another was that the new way must be wrong "because the young people fall in with it."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concerts for a ticket, and a fiddle for a prize" -->
A "Concert of Music" on several instruments was held in Mr. Pelham's great room in Boston on December 30, 1731. An advertisement in the *Boston News-Letter* that month listed it, and a ticket cost five shillings, a quarter of a British pound. It is the earliest advertised concert in Boston that the music historian Oscar Sonneck found. After a concert at Faneuil Hall in 1744, the town's selectmen, the men elected to run the town, received 205 pounds for the poor of Boston.

**A fiddle for a prize.** A notice in the *Virginia Gazette* of November 26, 1736, listed prizes for a holiday gathering at Page's Warehouse in Hanover County, Virginia, on November 30. One prize was "A fine Cremona Fiddle to be plaid for, by any number of Country Fiddlers." A Cremona fiddle was a violin made in the town of Cremona, in Italy. The races at John Pinkerton's Old Field in Virginia in November 1737 included a fiddling contest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and the enslaved musicians who played them" -->
The banjo has West African roots. A 2014 article on the Oxford University Press blog counts more than 60 plucked instruments in West Africa that are likely forerunners of it. The first known mention of a banjo in the mainland colonies is in John Peter Zenger's newspaper, the *New-York Weekly Journal*, in 1736.

**Runaway notices.** Virginia enslavers put notices in the *Virginia Gazette* when an enslaved person ran away. Each notice described the person's body, clothes and skills, so that strangers could recognize and catch the person. Playing the fiddle was one of the skills these notices listed. The University of Virginia's collection of Virginia runaway notices holds three from before 1751 that name a musician: two men named Tom, and a fiddler named Harry.

Robert Whitfield of Isle of Wight County offered four pistoles, Spanish gold coins used in the colonies, for a young man named Tom, who "plays very well on the Violin." His notice ran in the issue of November 28 to December 5, 1745. In April 1746 John Thornton of Fredericksburg advertised for another man named Tom, about 46 years old, who "plays on the Violin, and is a Sawyer," a man who saws logs into boards.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harry-fiddler-music" name="Harry, a fiddler who ran away, perhaps to his wife, 1746" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harry, a fiddler who ran away, perhaps to his wife, 1746

> **Who:** An enslaved man who played the fiddle. The man who held him put a notice in the newspaper for his capture.
> **When and where:** Near Jamestown, Virginia, March 1746.

**He wore a blue greatcoat. His enslaver wanted him whipped.**

Harry was an enslaved fiddler who ran away in early March 1746 from the man who held him, near Jamestown, Virginia. Everything known about him comes from one notice in the *Virginia Gazette*, printed between March 20 and 27, 1746. The notice gives him no last name.

Harry was about 35 years old and about 5 feet 7 inches tall, with small eyes and "a very large Beard," and he "plays upon the Fiddle." He left wearing a double-breasted dark cloth coat, two dark cotton jackets, a blue greatcoat and buckskin breeches, which are pants that end at the knee. He had once belonged to Colonel Grymes of Richmond County, Virginia.

**The notice.** William Newgent signed it, though a later reprint spells his first name James. Newgent guessed that Harry had gone to Richmond County, "where he has a Wife." He offered one pistole for Harry's return. At the end he added: "As he ran away without any Cause, I desire he may be punish'd by Whipping, as the Law directs." Newgent was asking whoever caught Harry to have him beaten with a whip.

**Not known:** The record does not say whether Harry was caught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harry-fiddler-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums at Stono, and the 1740 law against drums and horns" -->
About 20 enslaved men seized weapons near the Stono River, south of Charleston, South Carolina, on September 9, 1739. A report written that year called them "Angola Negroes" and named their leader as a man called Jemmy. They marched south toward Spanish Florida.

More men joined them, "calling out Liberty," the report says, and they "marched on with Colours displayed," meaning flags, "and two Drums beating." When there were sixty to a hundred of them, they stopped in a field and "set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them."

**The killings.** The same report says the rebels killed white men, women and children as they marched. Lieutenant Governor William Bull raised the militia, a force of armed planters, and the militiamen attacked the rebels in the field the same day. The report says the men captured in the field were questioned and then "shot on the Spot," and men who had run back to their plantations were caught there and shot. It does not name who fired.

The report counts about 40 Black people and 20 white people killed. Later counts give about 25 white colonists and 35 to 50 rebels killed. Colonists cut off the heads of rebels and set them on stakes along the main roads as a warning. They put most of the captured rebels to death and sold the survivors to the West Indies.

**The law.** The men of the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act on May 10, 1740. In section 36 they wrote that it was "absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province," meaning the colony, to stop enslaved people from "using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments."

They gave the reason in the same sentence: such instruments "may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes." They meant that drums and horns could be used to call enslaved people together to rise up.

**What the law allowed.** A master or an overseer, the man who ran the work on a plantation, paid a fine of ten pounds each time he let enslaved people beat drums, blow horns or gather in public. Any master, overseer or other person could seize an enslaved person found off the plantation without a written pass and without a white person.

The law named Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays in particular. If an enslaved man found off the plantation carried a weapon, any master, overseer or other person could take the weapon away, seize him and whip him, even if he carried a pass.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 10 minutes**

Americans began writing and printing their own music in these years. The names of the Black musicians who played beside them survive mainly in the notices enslavers printed when those musicians ran away. A Boston tanner named William Billings published a book of church songs he had written himself in 1770. By 1800 Americans had published about forty books of church music. During the Revolution, officers used drum beats to tell soldiers when to wake, march and fight, and American writers put new words to old tunes.

Black musicians played fiddles, horns, drums and banjos in Virginia, in Charleston and in New England. Slave traders took one of them, Newport Gardner, from Africa as a boy. He became a music teacher in Newport, Rhode Island, and bought his freedom in 1791. That same year, on the Pacific coast, a man sailing with a Spanish expedition wrote down Tlingit songs in musical notes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="William Billings and the New England singing masters" -->
William Billings, a Boston tanner who turned animal skins into leather for a living, published *The New-England Psalm-Singer* in 1770. A Library of Congress research guide calls it "the first publication consisting of sacred music," meaning music for worship, "composed entirely by a native-born American." Paul Revere is credited with engraving the picture at the front of the book, cutting it into a metal plate so it could be printed.

Billings also taught singing schools. He opened one in Boston with John Barry in 1769, and he taught another in Stoughton, Massachusetts, in 1774. The historian Henry Wilder Foote counted about forty American books of church music published before 1800, most of them printed in Boston.

### William Billings

> **Who:** A tanner and singing teacher who wrote about 340 songs for choirs. His 1770 book was the first book of church music written entirely by an American-born composer.
> **When and where:** Boston. Born October 7, 1746. Died September 26, 1800.

**A tanner with one blind eye wrote 340 songs and died poor.**

William Billings wrote about 340 songs for choirs and published them in six books. He did it while working as a tanner and a singing teacher. His father died when Billings was 14, and he went to work learning to tan leather. He was mostly self-taught in music, though he may have had some lessons from John Barry of Boston's New South Church. A writer named Nathaniel Gould described him in 1853 as having "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered."

**"Chester."** His first book, *The New-England Psalm-Singer* of 1770, included his song "Chester." It opens with the words "Let tyrants shake their iron rod." The singers say that they do not fear tyrants or the chains of slavery, because they trust in God. Billings printed a new version with five verses in *The Singing Master's Assistant* in 1778. Soldiers "learned the words by heart," according to the writer David Stowe, and carried Billings's songs wherever the army sent them.

**No copyright.** Other printers copied Billings's tunes without paying him. Before the Revolution, the men who made Massachusetts's laws voted for a bill, a proposed law, to give Billings copyright over his first book: the legal right to decide who may copy and sell it. The governor, a Tory loyal to the British king, refused to sign it. Stowe writes that the governor may have refused because Billings had ties to patriots such as Samuel Adams.

**Poor at the end.** His friends paid to print his book *The Continental Harmony* in 1794. His wife, Lucy, died on March 26, 1795, and left him with six children under 18. In the 1790s he worked as a street cleaner. Billings died poor in Boston on September 26, 1800, and was buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs and signals of the Revolution" -->
British officers first sang "Yankee Doodle" to make fun of the colonists. The man most often named as the writer of the words is Richard Shuckburgh, a British army surgeon, in about 1755 or 1758, during the French and Indian War. The date is disputed.

British soldiers arrived to occupy Boston in 1768, and their musicians played the song in the streets to mock the colonists. Early in the war, British troops and Loyalists, colonists who stayed on the British side, played it to make fun of the Continental soldiers. The Americans then made up their own verses, and by 1781 "Yankee Doodle" was a favorite American song.

**"The Liberty Song."** John Dickinson of Pennsylvania wrote its words to the tune of "Heart of Oak," the anthem, or official song, of the British Royal Navy. The *Boston Gazette* printed it in July 1768. Dickinson sent it to his Massachusetts friend James Otis with a note that "indifferent songs are very powerful on certain occasions." By indifferent he meant songs that were not very good.

**Orders by drum.** In the Continental Army, officers gave orders by drum beat. Under the army rules of Baron von Steuben, each beat had its own name and meaning. "The Reveille" woke the soldiers in the morning. "The General" meant take down the tents and get ready to march, "The Assembly" meant line up at the flags, and "To Arms" meant pick up weapons and get ready to fight.

Drummers beat "The Tattoo" at 9 or 10 at night, and at that signal tavern keepers stopped serving the soldiers drinks. Boys under 14 were recommended as drummers, according to the American Battlefield Trust, and some boys who played the fife, a small, high-pitched flute, were 9 or 10 years old.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black musicians, enslaved and free" -->
Virginia enslavers kept naming enslaved musicians in their runaway notices. William Gregory advertised on May 4, 1769, for Peter, about 44, who had taken with him "a fiddle, which he is much delighted in when he gets any strong drink." David Walker of Dinwiddie County advertised on September 8, 1775, for Jemmy. He wrote that Jemmy "is very fond of singing hymns and preaching," and that he was living near Williamsburg under the name James Williams.

**Mark.** William Allason offered forty pounds in August 1780 for four men who had run away together from near Fauquier Court House, Virginia. One of them was Mark, about 25, born on the island of St. Jago, in the Cape Verde Islands off West Africa. He spoke Portuguese. The notice says Mark "blows the French horn, can play on the fiddle, whistles many tunes well," and that he loved marches and Catholic church music. Allason guessed that the four men planned to get aboard a ship.

**At Nomini Hall.** Philip Vickers Fithian was a tutor, a live-in teacher, to the children of Robert Carter III at Nomini Hall, a Virginia plantation, in 1774. His journal records that on a January evening, enslaved people gathered in the schoolroom "& began to play the Fiddle, & dance." Two of Carter's sons, Ben and Harry, joined them, and Fithian sent everyone away. A few days later, he wrote, "several Negroes & Ben, & Harry are playing on a Banjo & dancing."

**In Charleston.** A gentleman lost his pocketbook on the evening of December 2, 1766, as he passed "a croud of negroes assembled at the lower end of Elliott-street, with a banjer playing." A notice in the *South Carolina Gazette and General Advertiser* of December 12 reported the loss. A Charleston town rule of 1750 banned beating drums in town without an officer's permission.

**The earliest banjo painting.** A painting on paper called *The Old Plantation*, made between about 1785 and 1795, shows enslaved Black men and women dancing. One man plays an early banjo made from a gourd, a large fruit with a hard shell that can be dried and hollowed out. Another man plays a drum, and two women hold rattles. It is the earliest known painting of a banjo.

The librarian Susan Shames identified the painter in 2010 as John Rose, a South Carolina planter who held people in slavery. The Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia, owns it.

**Jefferson.** Thomas Jefferson held people in slavery at Monticello, his plantation in Virginia. In the 1787 edition of his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, he wrote about Black musicians: "In music they are more generally gifted than the whites." Then he doubted that they could write longer, more complicated music: that, he wrote, "is yet to be proved."

In a note he called the "Banjar" the instrument "which they brought hither," meaning here, "from Africa." In the same passage he wrote that Black people had "no poetry," and he dismissed the published poems of Phillis Wheatley, a Black woman.

**Election days.** Black communities in New England held their own election days from about 1750. In New Hampshire and Massachusetts they elected a "king," and in Rhode Island and Connecticut a "governor." The enslavers granted the day off and paid for the feast. The parades had "tambourines, banjos, fiddles and drums," and the feast had rum, gingerbread and "thick, fruit-studded election cakes."

### Newport Gardner

> **Who:** Born Occramer Marycoo in West Africa, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, where he became a music teacher and composer.
> **When and where:** Born about 1746. Newport, from about 1760. Died in Liberia in 1826.

**He won a lottery in 1791 and bought his family's freedom.**

Newport Gardner was about 14 when slave traders took him from West Africa to Newport, Rhode Island. His name at birth was Occramer Marycoo. Caleb Gardner, a merchant and ship captain, bought him and renamed him Newport Gardner. The music writer Sophie Lowe writes that Caleb Gardner "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings" through the slave trade.

**His teachers.** The enslaved boy learned English and music in Newport. The best-known account names Andrew Law as his teacher, Lowe writes. But Law is first known to have been in Rhode Island in 1783, when Gardner was already in his late thirties. Lowe concludes that he "probably had a variety of musical teachers."

**Freedom.** Gardner and some friends won a lottery in late December 1791. With his share he bought freedom for himself and his family, then opened a school in Newport where he taught music. At Newport's First Congregational Church he was a deacon, a church member who helps lead it. In the 1820s he helped found a Black church, according to the historian Edward Andrews, and the historian John Millar writes that Gardner joined the new Colored Union Church in 1824.

**His music.** The writer Eileen Southern credited to him a tune called "Crooked Shanks," printed in 1803. Lowe reports newer research that found the same tune in print in London in 1768, so the credit is uncertain. The words of his anthem, a church song, survive. They begin "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race."

**Liberia.** He led a group of Black New Englanders to Liberia, in West Africa. Millar counts about 30 of them, and Andrews counts about three dozen. Accounts also differ on when they sailed: late December 1825, or January 4, 1826. Gardner died of disease in Liberia within months, in 1826.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tlingit songs written down, 1791" -->
Two Spanish ships led by Malaspina and Bustamante anchored in Yakutat Bay, in what is now Alaska, in 1791. The bay was Tlingit land. Several times, Tlingit people sang "to and with" the Spanish sailors.

Tadeo Haenke was a botanist on the ships, a scientist who studies plants, and he had trained in music. He wrote the songs down in musical notes on four sheets of paper. The sheets stayed in the Museo de América, a museum in Madrid, Spain, for more than 200 years.

**Hearing them again.** The scholar Steve Langdon got pictures of the sheets and sent them to Maria Shaa Tláa Williams, a Tlingit ethnomusicologist, a scholar who studies the music of a people and its place in their lives. Williams found that the singers used harmony, two or more notes sung together, in part of their singing. She found one song, "Canta de la Paz," that was used to settle a quarrel over a stolen piece of clothing.

She then programmed a synthesizer, a machine that makes musical sounds electronically, to play the notes, so people could hear how the songs sounded. Judy Daxootsu Ramos, a Tlingit from Yakutat, said of the old songs, "we're still practicing that today."
