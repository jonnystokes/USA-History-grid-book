## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Long before 1500, Native nations in this land made their own music. The music scholar J. Bryan Burton lists what the songs were for in traditional Native life: ceremonies, healing, work, games, courting, storytelling, hunting, farming, war, and social dances. Nobody wrote music down in these centuries, so no singer's name and no song's name survive from them. Some of the instruments survive. Archaeologists, scientists who dig up and study things people left behind, have found wooden flutes and metal-covered panpipes more than a thousand years old. A panpipe is a row of tubes that a player blows across, each tube making a different note. Singers passed songs on by teaching them, and Onondaga and Tlingit singers still follow rules about where a song may be sung and who owns it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Flutes and panpipes that survive" -->
The oldest known wooden flutes in North America were found in a cave in northeastern Arizona. In 1931 the archaeologist Earl H. Morris explored 15 caves in the Prayer Rock district, which is now part of the Navajo Nation. In the largest cave he found four wooden flutes. That cave is now called Broken Flute Cave.

The flutes were made between the years 620 and 670. The Arizona State Museum in Tucson holds them today. The museum's exhibit identifies them as Ancestral Pueblo flutes, which means the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples made them. Each flute is a tube of box elder wood between about 68 and 74 centimeters long, a little over two feet. The finger holes are almost the same on each flute, so the maker planned which notes the flute would play. Red, blue and black feathers were tied to one end. Two of the flutes lay broken in trash above a grave. The other two lay whole under a dirt floor, tied together.

Farther east, people of the Hopewell culture lived in the woodlands of eastern North America between the years 1 and 400. "Hopewell" is a name archaeologists gave them, not the name of a nation. In southern Ohio they built earthworks, which are mounds and long banks of piled earth. Archaeologists have found 105 of their panpipes at 55 sites, from Wisconsin and Ontario down to the Gulf Coast. A Hopewell panpipe is three or four tubes of cane, reed or bone, held together by a metal jacket. The metal of 102 jackets is known. Of those, 81 are copper. The others are silver, copper covered with silver, iron, or iron and copper together. Almost all of the panpipes were found buried with the dead. Very few came from places where people lived.

Nobody knows what tunes anyone played on these flutes and panpipes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums, rattles, and songs that belong to someone" -->
The Onondaga Nation of what is now central New York is one of the Haudenosaunee nations. Onondaga singers today play a water drum and a horn rattle. The drum is made of cedar, with deer hide stretched over the top. The horn rattle is a dried, hollowed animal horn with seeds inside. A rattle made of turtle shell is used only in a few ceremonies.

The Onondaga keep two kinds of songs. Social songs can be sung anywhere. Ceremonial songs are sung only in the longhouse, the building where ceremonies are held, and the public may not watch them.

Among the Tlingit of what is now southeast Alaska, a song can belong to a clan. A clan is a large family group that traces itself back to shared ancestors. A clan owns its songs, its stories, its crests and its ceremonial clothing. The Tlingit word for these owned things is *at.óow*, which means "something owned or purchased." Some songs and stories tell of a clan's losses in a certain place. In the Tlingit view, those songs are the clan's deed to that place, the proof that the place is theirs.

These rules come from the nations' own accounts today. No written record from before 1500 describes them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### R. Carlos Nakai

> **Who:** A flute player of Navajo (Diné) and Ute descent who plays a traditional cedar flute.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1946, in Flagstaff, Arizona. Still performing in 2026.

R. Carlos Nakai plays a traditional Native cedar flute. Flutes survive from before 1500, but no flute player's name does. Nakai has recorded more than forty albums with Canyon Records in Phoenix, Arizona. The first was *Changes*, in 1983. His concert calendar for 2026 lists shows in Utah and Colorado in September and more in October and November.

Nakai was born in Flagstaff, Arizona, on April 16, 1946. He is of Navajo and Ute descent. His father, Raymond Nakai, was chairman of the Navajo Nation from 1963 to 1970.

He first studied the trumpet. A car accident ruined the way he shaped his lips against the trumpet's mouthpiece. Someone gave him a cedar flute as a gift and challenged him to master it.

His albums *Canyon Trilogy* and *Earth Spirit* each sold 500,000 copies. They were the first recordings of the traditional Native flute to sell that many. In 2014 *Canyon Trilogy* was named a platinum album, the award for one million copies. Between 1994 and 2013 he was nominated for ten Grammy awards. Nakai calls himself "an iconoclastic traditionalist." An iconoclast is a person who breaks with old ways. A traditionalist is a person who keeps them.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s European music was heard only in the few places where European ships landed. Everywhere else, the only music was Native music. Most of the European music on record is church singing, along with soldiers' trumpets. Spanish priests sang Latin hymns, which are songs that praise God. French and English Protestants, Christians who had broken away from the Catholic Church, sang psalms, which are songs from the Bible. The Europeans sang when they came ashore and when they held services. Native people watched and listened, and Europeans wrote down a few of their reactions. No record from the 1500s names a mission choir or a Native singer trained in church music. The first ones on record come after 1598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hymns sung at the landings, Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
In 1564 French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline near the mouth of the St. Johns River, near today's Jacksonville, Florida. They built it on the land of the Timucua people. The Huguenots' leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on June 30 he ordered his men to sing aloud on the beach to thank God for their safe arrival. In 1865 the historian Francis Parkman wrote that on the morning they began the fort, the men sang a psalm before they started work.

On September 8, 1565, the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at Seloy, a Timucua town, and founded St. Augustine. His chaplain, Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, wrote an account of that day. A chaplain is a priest who serves a group of soldiers or travelers. Mendoza wrote that Menéndez came ashore "to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery," which means cannons fired as a greeting. Mendoza walked to meet him carrying a cross and singing the *Te Deum*, a Latin hymn of praise to God. "A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done," he wrote.

On September 20, 1565, Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline and killed most of the Frenchmen there. In a letter of October 15, Menéndez wrote that 132 of them had "their throats cut." He gave his reason in the same letter: "they spread the Lutheran sect," meaning the Protestant faith. When Menéndez came back to St. Augustine, Mendoza and his companions walked ahead of him in a line, "singing the Te Deum laudamus." In the weeks after, shipwrecked Frenchmen surrendered to Menéndez at Matanzas Inlet. His soldiers killed 111 of them. On October 12 they killed 134 more who had surrendered with their leader, Jean Ribault.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Psalms at Drake's landing, California, 1579" -->
In June 1579 the English captain Francis Drake stopped on the California coast to repair his ship, probably at the place now called Drake's Bay. Coast Miwok people lived along that shore. Drake's men stayed for about five weeks. While they were there, they held prayers, sang psalms and read from the Bible, and Miwok people came to watch.

A book about the voyage, *The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake*, came out in London in 1628. Drake's nephew, also named Francis Drake, put it together. Its title page states that it was collected "out of the Notes of Master Francis Fletcher," the preacher on the voyage, and other men's notes. The scholar Robert Heizer wrote that who wrote it is uncertain. The book states that the Miwok "tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes" that each time they came, "their first request was Gnaah." That word meant they wanted the Englishmen to sing. In 1947 Heizer, an anthropologist, wrote that *gnaah* seems to be a Coast Miwok word. An anthropologist is a scientist who studies people and the ways they live.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Mexico, 1598" -->
In 1598 Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into New Mexico to start a colony on the lands of the Pueblo peoples. The music historian John Koegel writes that Pueblo people began to hear Spanish military music, and to sing Catholic chant and songs in parts, "during the Oñate expedition and its aftermath," meaning the journey and the years after it. The first music teachers there whose names are on record are two friars, Catholic men who lived by the rules of a religious order. Fray Bernardo de Marta came in about 1605. Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones died in 1609.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The colonists' music on record from the 1600s is church music. In New England, congregations, the people who worship together at a church, sang psalms from a book printed in Massachusetts in 1640. For most of the century that book printed only the words, with no musical notes. In New Mexico, Spanish friars put an organ in the church at San Felipe Pueblo and trained Pueblo singers to sing Latin church music. Later friars banned Pueblo religious ceremonies. In 1675 a Spanish governor had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested, and three were later hanged. In 1619 the crew of an English ship landed about twenty captured Africans in Virginia. Enslaved Africans made music there through the 1600s, but nobody who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bay Psalm Book, 1640, and lining out" -->
In 1640 Stephen Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre* at Cambridge, Massachusetts. People today call it the *Bay Psalm Book*. It was the first book printed in British North America. Richard Mather and other New England ministers turned the Psalms of the Bible into English verse with a steady beat, so that a congregation could sing them. Eleven copies of that first printing survive.

The 1640 book had no printed music. It lists tunes by name, tunes already printed in English psalm books. The ninth edition, printed in 1698, was the first to include music. It printed 13 tunes taken from a music book by John Playford, an Englishman.

Many people in a congregation could not read. In 1644 an assembly of ministers at Westminster, in London, wrote a guide for church services. The guide states that where many people could not read, the minister or a person he chose should "read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof." This way of singing is called lining out. A leader read or sang one line, and the congregation sang it back. Colonists from England and Scotland brought lining out to America. Old Regular Baptist churches in the United States still sing this way today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" name="Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones

> **Who:** A Franciscan friar who built the church at San Felipe Pueblo, put an organ in it, and trained Pueblo singers.
> **When and where:** San Felipe Pueblo, a Keres-speaking town in New Mexico. Died in 1609.

Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones put an organ in the church at San Felipe Pueblo and trained singers there to sing church music. Fray is the title of a friar, a man who has joined a Catholic religious order and lives by its rules. Quiñones belonged to the Franciscan order.

San Felipe is a Pueblo town in New Mexico whose people speak the Keres language. Quiñones learned Keres and baptized many Native people in the New Mexico missions. He was the leader of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico. At San Felipe he built the church, a house for the friars, a pharmacy and a place to care for the sick.

A writer named Agustín de Vetancurt later described his work in a book of the lives of Franciscan friars. Vetancurt wrote that Quiñones "installed an organ to enrich the Divine Service and encouraged music-making by the skilled singers" he had trained. Quiñones died in 1609. The music historian John Koegel calls him "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music" to live in what is now the US Southwest. No surviving record says where Quiñones was born, or what the singers of San Felipe thought of the music they were taught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="fray-cristobal-de-quinones-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church music and banned ceremonies in New Mexico" -->
Other friars taught music in more Pueblo towns. Fray Bernardo de Marta came to New Mexico in about 1605. He taught Pueblo people in many towns to sing and to play European instruments, and he died in 1635. By 1634 Fray Roque de Figueredo was teaching Zuni boys to sing in parts.

A Spanish church leader and a Spanish governor also punished Pueblo people for keeping their own religion. From 1656 to 1665 the Franciscan leader Alonso de Posada forbade Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered friars to invade the sacred kivas, the rooms where Pueblo people held their ceremonies. There the friars burned the masks and took or destroyed sacred objects.

In 1675 the governor, Juan Francisco Treviño, had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested and charged with sorcery, which means using magic to do harm. Four were sentenced to be hanged. Three were hanged, and one killed himself. The rest were shamed and whipped in public, then put in prison. The records do not name the men who passed the sentences or carried out the hangings and the whippings. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be freed, and the Spanish let them go. One of the whipped men was Po'pay, a leader from Ohkay Owingeh. On August 10, 1680, Po'pay led the Pueblo nations in a rising against the Spanish. The Pueblo fighters killed about 400 Spanish settlers, 21 of them Franciscan friars, and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for 12 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What arrived with enslaved Africans" -->
In late August 1619 the crew of an English ship called the *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes," in the words of the colonist John Rolfe, at Point Comfort, Virginia. The crew had taken these Africans from a Portuguese slave ship. They came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa.

Enslaved Africans made music in the English colonies through the 1600s, but no one who wrote in those years left a description of it that survives. The first clear description of an early banjo comes from the island of Jamaica. There, in 1687, an English doctor named Hans Sloane wrote about an instrument he called a "strum strump."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1600s" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
New England ministers began teaching their congregations to sing from written notes around 1720. They started singing schools and printed the first books of music lessons in the colonies. The earliest Boston concert with a newspaper advertisement took place in 1731, and a ticket cost five shillings. In Virginia, enslaved Black musicians played the fiddle. Three of them appear in newspaper notices that their enslavers printed when they ran away in 1745 and 1746. In 1739 enslaved men rose up near the Stono River in South Carolina, with drums beating. The next year, the men who made South Carolina's laws passed a law to stop enslaved people from using drums and horns, and fined any master who let them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Singing by note and the first singing schools" -->
"By the end of the seventeenth century singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies," the historian Henry Wilder Foote wrote. New England churches still sang by lining out. In the first 25 years of the 1700s, ministers in New England turned against lining out and called for "regular singing" instead. In 1720 the Reverend Thomas Symmes of Bradford, Massachusetts, published a booklet called *The Reasonableness of Regular Singing, or Singing by Note*. Regular singing meant singing from written notes. Symmes wrote that church singing had got worse because "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn."

The Reverend John Tufts of Newbury, Massachusetts, published *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes*. It was the first book of music lessons printed in the English colonies. Neither the title nor the exact date of the first edition is known, so its date is disputed. Foote dated it to about 1712. Other sources date it to about 1721. Tufts traveled to give talks about singing and to set up singing schools, where a teacher taught a class to read and sing music.

In 1721 the Reverend Thomas Walter, who was 25, published *The Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained*, with 24 psalm tunes. James Franklin printed it in Boston. It was the first music in the colonies printed with bar lines, the lines that divide music into equal parts of time. It stayed a favorite for more than forty years and was printed in six editions. Symmes wrote that New England had had singing schools in the colony's early days, but people had stopped holding them. New singing schools spread in the 1720s. The records that survive do not say where the first one met or what a pupil paid.

Young people took up the new singing first. On March 20, 1721, the Boston judge Samuel Sewall wrote in his diary that "Dr. Mather" had preached that night "in the school house to the young musicians." Sewall wrote that the house was full, "and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston."

Other church members fought the change. Symmes reported that in his town, "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." He meant that people had been angry and arguing about it for almost six months. In 1728 the Reverend Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut wrote answers to the objections people raised. One objection was that singing by note "leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly." The Church of England was the official church back in England. Another was that the new way must be wrong "because the young people fall in with it."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Concerts for a ticket, and a fiddle for a prize" -->
An advertisement in the *Boston News-Letter* of December 1731 listed a "Concert of Music" on several instruments. It was held on December 30, 1731, in Mr. Pelham's great room in Boston, and a ticket cost five shillings. It is the earliest advertised concert in Boston that the music historian Oscar Sonneck found. After a concert at Faneuil Hall in 1744, the town's selectmen received £205 for the poor of Boston. The selectmen were the men elected to run the town.

In Virginia, a notice in the *Virginia Gazette* of November 26, 1736, listed prizes for a holiday gathering at Page's Warehouse in Hanover County on November 30. One prize was "A fine Cremona Fiddle to be plaid for, by any number of Country Fiddlers." A Cremona fiddle was a violin made in the town of Cremona, in Italy. In November 1737 the races at John Pinkerton's Old Field in Virginia included a fiddling contest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fiddles, banjos, and the enslaved musicians who played them" -->
The banjo has West African roots. Scholars have found more than 60 plucked instruments in West Africa that are likely forerunners of it. The first known mention of a banjo in the mainland colonies is in John Peter Zenger's newspaper, the *New-York Weekly Journal*, in 1736.

In Virginia, enslavers printed notices in the *Virginia Gazette* when an enslaved person ran away. Each notice described the person's body, clothes and skills, so that strangers could recognize and catch the person. Playing the fiddle was one of the skills these notices listed. In the issue of November 28 to December 5, 1745, Robert Whitfield of Isle of Wight County offered four pistoles for a young man named Tom, who "plays very well on the Violin." A pistole was a Spanish gold coin used in the colonies. In April 1746 John Thornton of Fredericksburg advertised for another man named Tom, about 46 years old, who "plays on the Violin, and is a Sawyer." A sawyer is a man who saws logs into boards. The University of Virginia's collection of Virginia runaway notices holds three from before 1751 that name a musician: these two men named Tom, and a fiddler named Harry.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harry-fiddler-music" name="Harry, a fiddler who ran away, perhaps to his wife, 1746" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harry, a fiddler who ran away, perhaps to his wife, 1746

> **Who:** An enslaved man who played the fiddle. The man who held him printed a notice for his capture.
> **When and where:** Near Jamestown, Virginia, March 1746.

Harry was an enslaved fiddler who ran away in early March 1746 from the man who held him, near Jamestown, Virginia. Everything known about him comes from one notice in the *Virginia Gazette*, printed between March 20 and 27, 1746. The notice gives him no last name.

The notice says that Harry was about 35 years old and about 5 feet 7 inches tall. It says he had small eyes and "a very large Beard," and that he "plays upon the Fiddle." He left in a double-breasted dark cloth coat, two dark cotton jackets, buckskin breeches and a blue greatcoat. Breeches are pants that end at the knee. He had once belonged to Colonel Grymes of Richmond County, Virginia.

The man who printed the notice was William Newgent. A later reprint of the notice spells his first name James. Newgent guessed that Harry had gone to Richmond County, "where he has a Wife." He offered one pistole for Harry's return. At the end he added: "As he ran away without any Cause, I desire he may be punish'd by Whipping, as the Law directs." Newgent was asking whoever caught Harry to have him beaten with a whip. The record does not say whether Harry was caught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harry-fiddler-music" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drums at Stono, and the 1740 law against drums and horns" -->
On September 9, 1739, about 20 enslaved men seized weapons near the Stono River, south of Charleston, South Carolina. A report written that year called them "Angola Negroes" and named their leader as a man called Jemmy. They marched south toward Spanish Florida. The report says more men joined them, "calling out Liberty," and they "marched on with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating." Colours here means flags. When there were sixty to a hundred of them, they stopped in a field and "set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them."

The same report says the rebels killed white men, women and children as they marched. Lieutenant Governor William Bull raised the militia, a force of armed planters, and it attacked them in the field the same day. The report says the men captured in the field were questioned and then "shot on the Spot," and men who had run back to their plantations were caught there and shot. It does not name who fired. The report counts about 40 Black people and 20 white people killed. Later counts give about 25 white colonists and 35 to 50 rebels killed. Colonists cut off the heads of rebels and set them on stakes along the main roads as a warning. They put most of the captured rebels to death and sold the survivors to the West Indies.

On May 10, 1740, the men of the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. In section 36 they wrote that it was "absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province" to stop enslaved people from "using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments." The Province was the colony. They gave the reason in the same sentence: such instruments "may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes." They meant that drums and horns could be used to call enslaved people together to rise up. An overseer was the man who ran the work on a plantation and gave orders to the enslaved people there. Under the law, a master or overseer who let enslaved people beat drums, blow horns or gather in public paid a fine of ten pounds each time. Any master, overseer or other person could seize an enslaved person found off the plantation without a written pass and without a white person. The law named Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays in particular. An enslaved person found carrying a weapon could be disarmed and whipped.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1770 a Boston tanner named William Billings published a book of church songs that he had written himself. By 1800 Americans had published about forty books of church music. During the Revolution, drums told soldiers when to wake, march and fight, and American writers put new words to old tunes. Black musicians played fiddles, horns, drums and banjos in Virginia, in Charleston and in New England, and most of those named in the records were enslaved. Slave traders took one of them, Newport Gardner, from Africa as a boy. He became a music teacher in Newport, Rhode Island, and bought his freedom in 1791. That same year, on the Pacific coast, Tadeo Haenke, a man sailing with a Spanish expedition, wrote down Tlingit songs in musical notes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="William Billings and the New England singing masters" -->
In 1770 William Billings, a Boston tanner born in 1746, published *The New-England Psalm-Singer*. A tanner turns animal skins into leather. A Library of Congress research guide describes the book as "the first publication consisting of sacred music composed entirely by a native-born American." Sacred music is music for worship. Paul Revere is credited with engraving the picture at the front of the book.

Billings also taught singing schools. He opened one in Boston with John Barry in 1769, and he taught another in Stoughton, Massachusetts, in 1774. The historian Henry Wilder Foote counted about forty American books of church music published before 1800. Most of them were printed in Boston.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-billings" name="William Billings" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Billings

> **Who:** A tanner and singing teacher who wrote about 340 songs for choirs. His 1770 book was the first book of church music written entirely by an American-born composer.
> **When and where:** Boston. Born October 7, 1746. Died September 26, 1800.

William Billings wrote about 340 songs for choirs and published them in six books. He did it while working as a tanner and a singing teacher, and he died poor.

He was born in Boston on October 7, 1746. His father died when Billings was 14, and he went to work learning to tan leather. He was mostly self-taught in music, though he may have had some lessons from John Barry of Boston's New South Church. In 1853 a music historian named Nathaniel Gould described him as having "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered."

His first book, *The New-England Psalm-Singer* of 1770, included his song "Chester." It opens with the words "Let tyrants shake their iron rod." The singers say that they do not fear tyrants or the chains of slavery, because they trust in God. In 1778 Billings printed a longer "Chester," with five verses, in *The Singing Master's Assistant*. According to the writer David Stowe, soldiers "learned the words by heart" and carried Billings's songs wherever the army sent them.

American copyright law was weak then. Copyright is the legal right to decide who may copy and sell a piece of writing or music. Other printers copied Billings's tunes without paying him. Before the Revolution, the men who made Massachusetts's laws passed a bill to protect his first book. The governor, a Tory loyal to the British king, refused to sign it. Stowe writes that the governor may have refused because Billings had ties to patriots such as Samuel Adams.

In 1794 his friends paid to print his book *The Continental Harmony*. His wife, Lucy, died on March 26, 1795, and left him with six children under 18. In the 1790s he worked as a street cleaner. Billings died poor in Boston on September 26, 1800. He was buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-billings" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs and signals of the Revolution" -->
British officers first sang "Yankee Doodle" to make fun of the colonists. A British army surgeon, Richard Shuckburgh, is said to have written the words in about 1755 or 1758, during the French and Indian War. Historians dispute that date. In 1768 British soldiers arrived to occupy Boston, and their musicians played the song in the streets to mock the colonists. Early in the war, British and Loyalist troops played it to make fun of the Continental soldiers. Loyalists were colonists who stayed on the British side. The Americans then made up their own verses, and by 1781 "Yankee Doodle" was a favorite American song.

In July 1768 the *Boston Gazette* printed "The Liberty Song." John Dickinson of Pennsylvania wrote its words to the tune of "Heart of Oak," the anthem of the British Royal Navy. He sent it to his Massachusetts friend James Otis with a note that "indifferent songs are very powerful on certain occasions." By indifferent he meant songs that were not very good.

In the Continental Army, drums gave orders. Under the army rules of Baron von Steuben, the drum beat named "The Reveille" woke the soldiers in the morning. "The General" told them to take down their tents and get ready to march. "The Assembly" told them to line up at the flags. "To Arms" told them to pick up their weapons and get ready to fight. "The Tattoo," beaten at 9 or 10 at night, told tavern keepers to stop serving the soldiers drinks. According to the American Battlefield Trust, boys under 14 were recommended as drummers, and some fifers were 9 or 10 years old. A fifer plays the fife, a small, high-pitched flute.

Virginia enslavers kept printing notices that named enslaved musicians. On May 4, 1769, William Gregory advertised for Peter, about 44, who had taken with him "a fiddle, which he is much delighted in when he gets any strong drink." On September 8, 1775, David Walker of Dinwiddie County advertised for Jemmy. He wrote that Jemmy "is very fond of singing hymns and preaching," and that he was living near Williamsburg under the name James Williams.

In August 1780 William Allason offered forty pounds for four men who had run away together from near Fauquier Court House, Virginia. One of them was Mark, about 25. He was born on the island of St. Jago, in the Cape Verde Islands off West Africa, and he spoke Portuguese. The notice says Mark "blows the French horn, can play on the fiddle, whistles many tunes well." It says he loved marches and Catholic church music. Allason guessed that the four men planned to get aboard a ship.

In 1774 Philip Vickers Fithian was a tutor, a live-in teacher, to the children of Robert Carter III at Nomini Hall, a plantation in Virginia. His journal records that on a January evening, enslaved people gathered in the schoolroom "& began to play the Fiddle, & dance." Two of Carter's sons, Ben and Harry, joined them, and Fithian sent everyone away. A few days later, he wrote, "several Negroes & Ben, & Harry are playing on a Banjo & dancing."

In Charleston, South Carolina, a notice in the *South Carolina Gazette and General Advertiser* of December 12, 1766, reported a stolen pocketbook. A gentleman lost it on the evening of December 2, as he passed "a croud of negroes assembled at the lower end of Elliott-street, with a banjer playing." A Charleston town rule of 1750 banned beating drums in town without an officer's permission.

A painting on paper called *The Old Plantation*, made between about 1785 and 1795, shows enslaved Black men and women dancing. One man plays an early banjo made from a gourd. Another man plays a drum, and two women hold rattles. It is the earliest known painting of a banjo. In 2010 the librarian Susan Shames identified the painter as John Rose, a South Carolina planter who held people in slavery. The Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia, owns it.

Thomas Jefferson held people in slavery at Monticello, his plantation in Virginia. In his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, in the 1787 edition, he wrote about Black musicians. He wrote that "In music they are more generally gifted than the whites." Then he doubted that they could write longer, more complicated music: that, he wrote, "is yet to be proved." In a note he called the "Banjar" the instrument "which they brought hither from Africa." In the same passage he wrote that Black people had "no poetry," and he dismissed the published poems of Phillis Wheatley, a Black woman.

In New England, from about 1750, Black communities held their own election days. In New Hampshire and Massachusetts they elected a "king," and in Rhode Island and Connecticut a "governor." The enslavers granted the day off and paid for the feast. The parades had "tambourines, banjos, fiddles and drums," and the feast had rum, gingerbread and "thick, fruit-studded election cakes."

### Newport Gardner

> **Who:** Born Occramer Marycoo in West Africa, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, where he became a music teacher and composer.
> **When and where:** Born about 1746. Newport, from about 1760. Died in Liberia in 1826.

Newport Gardner was taken from West Africa as a boy, enslaved in Newport, Rhode Island, and became a music teacher there. His name at birth was Occramer Marycoo. He was about 14 when slave traders brought him to Newport.

Caleb Gardner, a merchant and ship captain, bought him and renamed him Newport Gardner. The music writer Sophie Lowe writes that Caleb Gardner "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings" through the slave trade. The enslaved boy learned English and music in Newport. Many writers name the composer Andrew Law as his teacher. But Law is first known to have been in Rhode Island in 1783, when Gardner was already in his late thirties. Lowe concludes that he "probably had a variety of musical teachers."

In late December 1791 Gardner and some friends won a lottery. He used his share to buy freedom for himself and his family. He opened a school in Newport where he taught music. He was a deacon, a church member who helps lead it, at Newport's First Congregational Church. Later he helped found a Black church, which the historian John Millar dates to 1824.

The writer Eileen Southern credited to him a tune called "Crooked Shanks," printed in 1803. Lowe reports newer research that found the same tune in print in London in 1768, so the credit is uncertain. The words of his anthem, a church song, survive. They begin "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race."

He led a group of Black New Englanders to Liberia, in West Africa. Millar counts about 30 of them, and the historian Edward Andrews counts about three dozen. Accounts also differ on when they sailed: late December 1825, or January 4, 1826. Gardner died of disease in Liberia within months, in 1826.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tlingit songs written down, 1791" -->
In 1791 two Spanish ships anchored in Yakutat Bay, in what is now Alaska. Alejandro Malaspina and José de Bustamante led the expedition. The bay was Tlingit land. Several times, Tlingit people sang "to and with" the Spanish sailors.

Tadeo Haenke, a botanist on the ships who had trained in music, wrote the songs down in musical notes on four sheets of paper. A botanist is a scientist who studies plants. The sheets stayed in the Museo de América, a museum in Madrid, Spain, for more than 200 years. The scholar Steve Langdon got pictures of the sheets and sent them to Maria Shaa Tláa Williams, a Tlingit ethnomusicologist. An ethnomusicologist studies the music of a people and its place in their lives. Williams found that the singers used harmony, two or more notes sung together, in part of their singing. She found one song, "Canta de la Paz," that was used to settle a quarrel over a stolen piece of clothing. She then programmed a synthesizer, a machine that makes musical sounds electronically, to play the notes, so people could hear how the songs sounded. Judy Daxootsu Ramos, a Tlingit from Yakutat, said of the old songs, "we're still practicing that today."
