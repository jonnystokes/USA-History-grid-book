## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Enslaved Black people in the South between 1800 and 1850 made songs at work, at worship and on the days off their enslavers allowed, such as Christmas. Some of them later wrote down what those songs meant. In the same years, white performers darkened their faces with burnt cork and sang and danced as Black characters who were lazy, stupid, grinning fools. This blackface minstrel show became one of the most popular kinds of entertainment in the country. The money went to white performers and publishers. Stephen Foster, a white songwriter from Pittsburgh, wrote many of its best-known songs. In Philadelphia a free Black bandleader, Francis Johnson, published more than 200 pieces of music. In the South, singers learned church music from books printed in shape notes, where the shape of each note tells the singer which sound to sing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the singers said the songs meant" -->
Frederick Douglass was enslaved on farms in Talbot County, Maryland. He escaped from slavery in 1838. In 1845 he published the story of his life. In it he wrote that enslaved people sang as they walked to the main farm to collect their monthly food, making up the words as they went. Some people in the North took such singing as a sign that enslaved people were happy. Douglass wrote that this was a mistake: "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy." He added, "I have often sung to drown my sorrow, but seldom to express my happiness."

In a second book, in 1855, Douglass wrote that enslavers wanted to hear singing. "A silent slave is not liked by masters or overseers," he wrote. When the workers went quiet, the words they heard were "Make a noise." An overseer was the man who drove enslaved people at their work. Teamsters, the men who drove the wagons, sang so that the overseer would know where they were and that the work was going on.

Some songs had a second meaning. In 1836 Douglass and five men planned to escape together. He named them in his book: Henry Harris, John Harris, Sandy Jenkins, Charles Roberts and Henry Bailey. As they planned, they kept singing a hymn, a church song, about being "bound for the land of Canaan." In the Bible, Canaan is the land God promised to the people of Israel after they escaped from slavery in Egypt. Douglass wrote, "the north was our Canaan." Someone betrayed the plan, and it failed. Douglass never found out who.

At Christmas, enslaved people on the Maryland farms played fiddles and beat out rhythms on their own bodies with their hands. This was called "juba" beating. The singers made up the words of the juba songs as they went. Douglass wrote down one verse that begins "We raise de wheat, Dey gib us de corn." It goes on to list the work the enslaved people did and the scraps they were given for it.

Harriet Tubman escaped from slavery in Maryland in 1849. Later she went back to lead others north. She told the writer Sarah Bradford how she used hymns as signals. People hiding in the woods came out only after she walked past singing a certain hymn twice. If she sang "Moses go down in Egypt" instead, they stayed hidden, "for dere's danger in de way." Bradford printed Tubman's account in 1869.

Douglass and Tubman describe songs with hidden meanings and songs used as signals. A different claim is that the song "Follow the Drinking Gourd" was a map that led people from Alabama north to the Ohio River. That claim comes from a single article printed in 1928 by H. B. Parks, a Texas scientist who studied insects and collected songs. The best-known words of the song were written in 1947 by a folk singer, Lee Hays. The researcher Joel Bresler found that no one ever collected the song in Alabama. He also found no older use of "drinking gourd" to mean the Big Dipper, the group of stars that points to the North Star. Bresler and the scholar James Kelley doubt that the song was ever a map.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Congo Square, New Orleans" -->
In 1817 the mayor of New Orleans issued a city law that limited the gatherings of enslaved people to one place at the back of town, across Rampart Street, on Sunday afternoons. The space was later called Congo Square. There enslaved people sold goods, sang, danced and played music. In 1819 the architect Benjamin Latrobe wrote in his journal that he saw 500 to 600 enslaved people dancing there. He called it "savage."

City officials stopped the Sunday music and dancing in 1835. It started again, and officials stopped it again in 1851. The records that survive do not give the names of the mayor who limited the gatherings or of the officials who stopped them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blackface minstrelsy, from about 1830" -->
Blackface minstrelsy was a stage show in which white men darkened their faces with burnt cork and acted as Black people. David Pilgrim of the Jim Crow Museum at Ferris State University writes that white audiences liked seeing Black people shown as "singing, dancing, grinning fools." He writes that these shows helped spread the belief that Black people were lazy, stupid and "inherently less human," which means less human by birth.

Thomas Dartmouth Rice was a white actor, born in New York in 1808. He sang and danced a song called "Jump Jim Crow" in blackface. Pilgrim dates his first performance as Jim Crow to 1828. Other accounts say only that it had become his main act by 1832, when a Philadelphia newspaper notice announced that "Mr. Rice will appear and sing Jim Crow." Rice said he copied a disabled Black man who cared for horses and sang as he worked, and that he bought the man's clothes to wear on stage. The time, the place and the truth of this story are disputed. No record names that man, and no record shows that he or any other Black musician was paid for what the minstrels copied.

Rice played to sold-out theaters from about 1832 to 1844, and he took the act to London in 1836. By 1838 people were using "Jim Crow" as an insult for Black people. By the end of the 1800s, "Jim Crow" was the name for the laws and customs that white officials and lawmakers used against Black people. Under them, Black people were kept out of white schools, railroad cars and waiting rooms.

On what was probably January 31, 1843, four white men in New York performed for a paying audience as the Virginia Minstrels. They were Dan Emmett, Billy Whitlock, Dick Pelham and Frank Brower. Earlier blackface acts had one singer or dancer. The Virginia Minstrels performed as a group, with fiddles, banjos, tambourines and the bones, a rhythm instrument. From 1845 a group led by Edwin P. Christy, the Christy Minstrels, started many of the features of the minstrel show. The players sat in a half circle. Two comic players called Mr. Tambo and Mr. Bones sat at the ends, and a leader called Mr. Interlocutor sat in the middle.

The money went to the white performers and to the music publishers who printed their songs. Rice made a fortune, although he died poor in 1860.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The banjo goes on stage" -->
The banjo came from West African instruments, and enslaved Black people played it in America for about a hundred years before any white man played it on a stage. Joel Walker Sweeney was a white man born in 1810 in Appomattox County, Virginia. He learned to play the banjo from enslaved people there. In April 1839 he played the banjo on a stage in New York. He is the earliest known person to have done so. Four years later, two of the Virginia Minstrels played banjos in their show.

A story often told says that Sweeney added the banjo's short fifth string. No proof of that exists. Older banjos, and pictures made before his time, already show the short fifth string.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Stephen Foster

> **Who:** A white songwriter who wrote songs for blackface minstrel shows and for singing at home, and one of the first Americans to earn a living only by writing songs.
> **When and where:** Born July 4, 1826, in Lawrenceville, Pennsylvania, now part of Pittsburgh. Worked in Pittsburgh, Cincinnati and New York. Died January 13, 1864, in New York.

Stephen Foster wrote many of the best-known songs of the minstrel shows. He was one of the first Americans to earn a living only by writing songs. He was born on July 4, 1826, in Lawrenceville, which is now part of Pittsburgh.

Foster's family held a Black girl named Kitty as a bound servant. A bound servant had to work for a family, without pay, until a set number of years ran out. The University of Pittsburgh library's biography of Foster calls this arrangement "enslavement in all but name." In 1834 Foster's father wrote that a woman named Mrs. Collins had given the family the girl as "a present." The girl had more than three years left to serve. He wrote that it saved them the cost of hiring a girl.

At nine, Foster sang blackface songs such as "Jim Crow" in a boys' theater set up in a carriage house, and he was paid a small sum each week. He learned the style mostly from songs that white performers wrote to mock Black people. In 1846 and 1847 he kept the money records for his brother's shipping company in Cincinnati. The company made money from cotton picked by enslaved people in the South.

His song "Oh! Susanna" was first advertised in Pittsburgh in September 1847. Within a year it was published as one of the songs of the Sable Harmonists, a blackface group. At the time, no American song had sold more than 5,000 copies of its sheet music, the printed music people bought to play at home. "Oh! Susanna" sold more than 100,000. Its original second verse used a racist word for Black people and told, as comedy, of an explosion that killed 500 of them.

From 1850 the minstrel leader Edwin P. Christy paid Foster $10 a song for the right to sing each new song first. For "Old Folks at Home" in 1851, Christy paid $15, and the sheet music named Christy as the writer. In 1852 Foster asked to have his own name put back. Christy refused.

That year Foster wrote to Christy about the words of his songs. He said he had made them suitable for "refined people," instead of the "trashy and really offensive words" of other minstrel songs. The University of Pittsburgh writers say he most likely did this to sell more sheet music. His only openly political songs before the Civil War supported the Democrats who opposed ending slavery. One of them, "The Abolition Show" of 1856, attacked the people working to end slavery. Frederick Douglass, however, said in 1855 that songs such as "Old Kentucky Home" and "Uncle Ned" could make listeners feel for enslaved people.

In about 1857 Foster sold his rights to future payments on his songs, in return for money right away. He moved to New York in 1860 and kept writing, including more minstrel songs. In January 1864 he fell in his hotel room and was badly cut on his neck and face. He died at Bellevue Hospital three days later, at 37. His wallet held 37 cents in paper money and three pennies.

Other people later changed the words that are sung today. In 1986 two Kentucky lawmakers, Representative Carl Hines and Senator Georgia Davis Powers, won votes to sing "people" in place of a racist word in "My Old Kentucky Home," the state song since 1928.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="stephen-foster" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Church songs: Richard Allen's hymn book and shape notes" -->
In 1801 Richard Allen, a Philadelphia minister who had been born enslaved, put together two hymn books, one of 54 hymns and one of 64. The books contain the words and no music. In 1816 Allen formally organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church, a church run by Black Methodists.

Also in 1801, William Little and William Smith printed a music book in Philadelphia called *The Easy Instructor*. Its music is written in shape notes. In shape notes, each note has one of four shapes, and each shape stands for one sound to sing. The book was made for singing schools.

In 1844 two Georgia men, Benjamin Franklin White and Elisha J. King, published *The Sacred Harp*, a shape-note book of more than 250 songs. Some of its oldest songs are by William Billings, a Boston singing teacher who wrote church songs in the late 1700s. By 1800 his tunes had mostly dropped out of use in Boston, but singers in the rural South kept them. Sacred Harp singers sit in a hollow square and sing in four parts with no instruments. People still sing from the book, and a new edition was printed in 2025.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Francis Johnson

> **Who:** A free Black bandleader, bugle player and composer in Philadelphia who published more than 200 pieces of music.
> **When and where:** Born June 16, 1792, in Philadelphia. Died there April 6, 1844.

Francis Johnson led the band at Philadelphia's dances and balls, and he published more than 200 pieces of music. He was a free Black man. A church record in Philadelphia gives his birth date as June 16, 1792. Some books say he was born on the island of Martinique, but they give no evidence.

He grew up in a free Black neighborhood of Philadelphia. By 15 he could play the flute, the violin, the bugle and the piano. He played in taverns, then as the fiddler at the Exchange Coffee House.

A Philadelphia publisher, George Willig, printed his dance music. The writer Richard Grant, in *Smithsonian* magazine, dates the first piece to 1810 and calls it the first music published by a Black American. Other sources date his first known collection to 1818. That year a book about Philadelphia called him the "leader of the band at all balls." He played the keyed bugle, a brass horn with keys. He wrote dances, marches and songs.

In November 1837 Johnson took his four-man band to England. Grant writes that no Black American musician had taken a band to Europe before. Some historians say Johnson played for Queen Victoria. Others say that has not been proven.

In 1842 and 1843, Johnson and nine Black musicians toured eight states. In St. Louis in December 1842, the band members were served with warrants, papers from a court that order an arrest. The charge was being "Free Negros within the State of Missouri without a license." Missouri law required free Black people to hold a license to be in the state. The records do not name the officials who served the warrants. A firemen's club that had hired the band paid a lawyer, and the court ended without a ruling, so the band left. In Allegheny City, Pennsylvania, a mob gathered outside the hall where the band played. The mob attacked the musicians in the street, threw stones, bricks and rotten eggs, and shouted racist insults. Several musicians were badly hurt. The band played the next night anyway. No record names anyone in the mob.

Johnson died on April 6, 1844, when a blood vessel near his heart burst. At his funeral his band played a funeral march he had written. One of the largest funeral crowds in Philadelphia's history came to it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="francis-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hutchinson Family sing against slavery" -->
The Hutchinson Family Singers were brothers from Milford, New Hampshire: John, Asa, Jesse and Judson Hutchinson. They gave their first concert in Milford on November 6, 1840. Their sister Abby joined them when she was about 12. In 1842 they earned up to $130 a concert.

From 1843 they sang songs against slavery. In 1844 they sang "Get Off the Track!" to the tune of a minstrel song, "Old Dan Tucker." In 1845 the Hutchinsons toured Britain with Frederick Douglass.

In the spring of 1847 the Hutchinsons sang in Philadelphia to audiences of Black and white people together. The city's mayor, John Swift, said more concerts would cause a riot. He ruled that no antislavery speech could be given at the hall and that no Black person could sit in the audience. The Hutchinsons refused to sing for a white audience only.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1867 three white Northerners printed the first book of spirituals, the religious songs that enslaved Black people had made. After the Civil War, students at Fisk University, a school for freed Black people, sang those songs on tour to keep their school open. In the war itself, army bands played for soldiers on both sides, and bandsmen carried the wounded. At a government boarding school for Native children in Pennsylvania, the officer in charge gave the boys brass instruments to stop their drumming. As the band grew, the boys stopped singing their own songs. From 1890 singers recorded songs on wax cylinders that machines could play back. In 1899 Scott Joplin, a Black pianist in Missouri, published the "Maple Leaf Rag," a piece of ragtime, piano music with a bouncing rhythm. In the South, Black farm workers were making the music later called the blues.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jenny Lind's tour, 1850 to 1852" -->
In January 1850 the showman P. T. Barnum agreed to pay the Swedish singer Jenny Lind $1,000 a concert to tour the United States. She arrived in New York in September 1850, and Barnum sold some of the tickets by auction. She sang 93 concerts for Barnum and earned about $350,000. Barnum made at least $500,000. Lind gave her share to charities, most of it to free schools in Sweden.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War songs and army bands, 1859 to 1865" -->
"Dixie" was first sung on April 4, 1859, in New York by Bryant's Minstrels, a white blackface troupe. It is credited to Dan Emmett, one of the Virginia Minstrels. Since the 1910s or 1920s, people in Mount Vernon, Ohio, have said that Emmett took the song from the Snowdens, a Black family of musicians in Knox County, Ohio. Some historians think the Snowden story may be true, and others reject it. In December 1860, in Charleston, South Carolina, a band played "Dixie" after each vote to leave the Union. In February 1861 a band played it when Jefferson Davis became president of the Confederacy.

In 1861 Union soldiers from Massachusetts made up the song "John Brown's Body," about the leader of an 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry, Virginia. They sang it to the tune of a hymn from camp meetings, big outdoor church gatherings. Julia Ward Howe heard soldiers singing it near Washington, D.C. In November 1861 she wrote new words to the same tune. *The Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine, paid her $4 and printed her "Battle Hymn of the Republic" on its front page in February 1862.

Under an army order of August 3, 1861, each Union regiment, one unit of soldiers, could have a band of 24 musicians. In July 1862 members of Congress voted to send the regimental bands home. They kept one band for each brigade, a larger group of soldiers made up of several regiments. Bandsmen also carried the wounded. Albert Mann, a bandsman from Massachusetts, wrote that the army surgeon taught the band to use "stretchers, bandages and tourniquets." A tourniquet is a band tied tight around an arm or leg to stop heavy bleeding. The bandsmen were to go into battle with the regiment, "rescue the wounded, if possible, and carry them to the field hospital."

At Gettysburg, in July 1863, the bands of the 26th and 11th North Carolina regiments played while "heavy firing" went on, the 26th's band leader recalled. A British visitor heard a Confederate band play polkas and waltzes while cannon fired.

In January 1862 the Hutchinson Family sang a song against slavery to Union soldiers in Virginia. Its words included "Let Slavery die!" Some of the soldiers booed. General George McClellan, who opposed using the war to end slavery, then banned the Hutchinsons from singing in the army camps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spirituals written down" -->
In 1862 Thomas Wentworth Higginson, a white abolitionist, commanded the 1st South Carolina Volunteers. It was a Union regiment of men who had been enslaved. An abolitionist was a person who worked to end slavery. A drummer boy in the regiment told Higginson about a hymn with the words "We'll soon be free." He said that enslaved people in Georgetown, South Carolina, had been whipped for singing it when Abraham Lincoln was elected in 1860. The boy said the enslavers thought "de Lord" in the song meant the Yankees, the Northern soldiers. In a later version of the story, Higginson wrote that the singers had been put in jail. Neither account names the drummer boy, the singers, or the people who punished them.

In 1867 three white Northerners, William Francis Allen, Lucy McKim Garrison and Charles Pickard Ware, published *Slave Songs of the United States*. It contains 136 songs and was the first book of spirituals ever printed. A spiritual is a religious song made by enslaved Black people. Most of the songs in the book came from Gullah Geechee people, Black people of the South Carolina islands. They lived on St. Helena Island and had been freed during the war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Fisk Jubilee Singers, from 1871" -->
Fisk University opened in Nashville, Tennessee, after the Civil War to teach freed Black people. By 1871 it was running out of money, and its classes met in old buildings of a Union army hospital. George White, a white Northern missionary, kept the school's money and led its music. On October 6, 1871, he set out with nine students on a singing tour to raise money. They were Isaac Dickerson, Maggie Porter, Minnie Tate, Jennie Jackson, Benjamin Holmes, Thomas Rutling, Eliza Walker, Green Evans and Ella Sheppard.

At first they sang spirituals only as extra songs at the end of a concert. Audiences asked for more. White named the group the Jubilee Singers, after the "year of jubilee" in the Bible, when slaves were set free.

After one early concert in Ohio, the singers gave their profit to the victims of the Chicago fire of October 1871. "We had thirty dollars and sent every penny to Chicago and didn't have anything for ourselves," Maggie Porter said. She also said, "There were many times, when we didn't have place to sleep or anything to eat." Ella Sheppard wrote of "the ill treatment at hotels, on railroads, poorly attended concerts, and ridicule." George White and another man walked through sleet, snow and rain from hotel to hotel, looking for one that would take the singers in. The records do not name the hotels that turned them away.

In March 1872 the singers sang for President Ulysses S. Grant at the White House. In 1873 they sang for Queen Victoria in England. They had raised $20,000 by the spring of 1872, about $40,000 from their first American tours, and nearly $50,000 by May 1874. The money they had raised by 1874 paid for Jubilee Hall, Fisk's first permanent building, which opened on January 1, 1876. It became a National Historic Landmark in 1975. Over seven years of touring, the singers raised about $150,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ella-sheppard" name="Ella Sheppard" movie="Jubilee Singers: Sacrifice and Glory (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ella Sheppard

> **Who:** Born into slavery, she became the pianist, arranger and assistant director of the Fisk Jubilee Singers.
> **When and where:** Born February 4, 1851, at the Hermitage, Andrew Jackson's plantation near Nashville, Tennessee. Toured the United States and Europe. Died in Nashville in 1914 or 1915 (the records differ).
> **Movie:** *Jubilee Singers: Sacrifice and Glory* (2000), a documentary about the group in the PBS series *American Experience*.

Ella Sheppard arranged most of the spirituals that the Fisk Jubilee Singers sang. To arrange a song is to write out the parts for each voice. She was born into slavery on February 4, 1851, at the Hermitage, Andrew Jackson's plantation near Nashville.

Her father, Simon Sheppard, paid $1,800 to buy his own freedom, and then he bought Ella's. The woman who held Ella's mother, Sarah, refused to sell her. She said, "She is mine and she shall die mine. Let Simon get another wife." When Ella was three, her mother was sold to a plantation in Mississippi. Ella did not see her again until she was 15. The records do not name the woman who held Sarah or the man who bought her.

In 1856, after a riot in Nashville, white officials there made stricter rules for free Black people. Simon fell into debt and was afraid that his family would be seized and sold. He took Ella to Cincinnati, Ohio. There a German woman taught her piano. A white teacher taught her in secret, at night, letting her in through the back door.

Her father died of cholera in 1866. Cholera is an infection of the gut that people catch from dirty food or water, and it drains so much water from the body that a person can die. Ella taught music in Gallatin, Tennessee, and saved about $6 in five months. In 1868 she entered Fisk. The money lasted three weeks, and she stayed by giving music lessons and washing dishes.

She played piano, organ and guitar. She arranged the spirituals for the group to sing in four parts, and she led the singers from her place among them on stage. She collected more than a hundred songs. In the summer of 1875 she wrote down the music of eight spirituals for a printed book of the group's songs. The book did not name her. She later wrote that the old songs "were sacred to our parents, who used them in their religious worship," and that at first the students did not want to sing them for strangers.

Her mother once told her, "My back was never struck, but my heart is like a checkerboard with its stripes of sorrow." In 1890 Sheppard trained a new group of Jubilee Singers at Fisk.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ella-sheppard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native songs, drums and the first recordings" -->
In 1879 the army officer Richard Henry Pratt opened the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, a boarding school for Native children. Students came from 54 or more nations. Early in the school's history, a rule was made that students could not speak their own languages or practice "Indian customs." The school's paper does not say who made the rule. In 1896 an article in that paper, *The Red Man*, stated that the school's aim was "the complete transformation of the Indians." It called any "display of savagery and barbarism, even in song and language," out of place in the school. Those were the paper's words for the students' own songs and languages. The article states that in the first year, the only music heard from the boys' rooms was the drum.

Pratt told a Boston donor, Mrs. Walter Baker, that he wanted to stop the drumming. As the paper quotes him, he said, "If you will give me a set of brass band instruments I will give them to the 'tom tom' boys ... and this will stop the 'tom tom.'" The school band began in 1881. The article states that no one ever ordered the students to stop singing their own songs. It also states that as the band grew, the singers of those songs fell silent. Dennison Wheelock, an Oneida student who entered Carlisle in 1885, later led the school band.

On March 30, 1883, Hiram Price, the federal Commissioner of Indian Affairs, issued rules that made the Sun Dance and other ceremonial dances "Indian offenses." The rules named dances and feasts, not songs, but the songs belonged to the dances. The punishment was the loss of rations, the food the government handed out to Native people on reservations. For a first offense, officials could hold back a person's rations for up to ten days. For a later offense, they could hold back rations for 15 to 30 days. They could instead put the person for up to 30 days in the agency prison, the jail at the government office on the reservation. Officials could enforce these rules until 1978.

On December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee, soldiers of the 7th Cavalry, an army unit on horseback, killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children. The soldiers had been taking the guns of a Lakota band led by Spotted Elk. His people had taken up a religious movement called the Ghost Dance, whose followers danced in a circle.

From March 15 to 18, 1890, in Calais, Maine, the scientist Jesse Walter Fewkes recorded Passamaquoddy songs and stories on 36 wax cylinders. The Passamaquoddy are a Native nation whose homeland lies on both sides of the border between Maine and New Brunswick, in Canada. The singers were Peter Selmore, Newell Josephs and Peter Lacoute. In a Library of Congress essay, Kelly Revak writes that these are "largely credited" as the first recordings of Native American music. Since the 1980s Passamaquoddy speakers have used the recordings to bring old songs back. In 2009 Wayne Newell said people were working on them at home "so we can sort of resurrect older songs."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-philip-sousa" name="John Philip Sousa" movie="Stars and Stripes Forever (1952)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Philip Sousa

> **Who:** Leader of the United States Marine Band and writer of more than 100 marches.
> **When and where:** Born November 6, 1854, in Washington, D.C. Led the Marine Band from 1880 to 1892, then his own band. Died March 6, 1932, in Reading, Pennsylvania.
> **Movie:** *Stars and Stripes Forever* (1952), a Hollywood film loosely based on his memoir.

John Philip Sousa wrote more than 100 marches, including "The Stars and Stripes Forever," the national march of the United States. He was born in Washington, D.C., on November 6, 1854.

His father played in the Marine Band. When Sousa was 13, his father signed him into the Marine Corps as an apprentice musician, a boy learning the work. He did it to keep his son from joining a circus band. Sousa served seven years.

On October 1, 1880, Sousa became the 17th leader of the Marine Band. He led it for 12 years under five presidents, from Rutherford B. Hayes to Benjamin Harrison. In 1892 he formed a band of his own.

On Christmas Day 1896 Sousa was on a ship crossing the Atlantic. He had just learned that his band's manager, David Blakely, had died. On that voyage he began "The Stars and Stripes Forever," and he finished it in April 1897. In 1987 members of Congress voted to make it the national march. Sousa also wrote 15 operettas, short comic operas, and he helped develop the sousaphone, a large brass horn made for marching bands.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-philip-sousa" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Recording begins" -->
In December 1877 Thomas Edison's workers built his first phonograph, a machine that recorded sound and played it back. Machines good enough to sell came on the market in 1888.

In 1890 two phonograph companies paid George W. Johnson 20 cents for each two-minute song he recorded on a wax cylinder. Johnson was a Black street whistler, born in Virginia in 1846. The cylinders played in coin-operated machines. No way yet existed to copy a cylinder in large numbers, so Johnson sang the same songs over and over, one cylinder at a time. Between 1890 and 1895 he made an estimated 25,000 to 50,000 cylinders.

His two best sellers were "The Laughing Song," which he wrote, and a whistling song by a white writer that mocked Black people. The title of that song used a racist insult for a Black person. In 1891 "The Laughing Song" was the best-selling record in the country for ten weeks, and Johnson is counted as the first Black recording star. In 1899 Johnson was put on trial for the murder of Roskin Stuart, the woman he lived with as his wife, who had been found beaten in their apartment. The jury found him not guilty. By 1905 the companies could press many copies from one recording, and they no longer needed him. His friend Len Spencer hired him as a doorman for his office. Johnson died in 1914 and was buried in a grave without a marker.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ragtime, and songs that mocked Black people" -->
Ragtime is music with a "ragged" rhythm. The tune lands between the beats while a steady beat keeps time underneath. On the piano, the left hand keeps the steady beat and the right hand plays the tune. Ragtime began among African Americans in the late 1800s. Historians disagree about the first printed ragtime piece, and they date it to 1895 or 1897.

In 1895 and 1896 Ernest Hogan, a Black performer from Bowling Green, Kentucky, who had started out in minstrel shows, wrote a hit song whose title used the same racist insult. The title said that all Black people look alike. Many other writers copied it. People at the time named these copies after the insult. They were songs that used racist words and pictures to mock Black people.

### Scott Joplin

> **Who:** A Black composer and pianist who wrote the "Maple Leaf Rag" and an opera, *Treemonisha*.
> **When and where:** Born in northeastern Texas between mid-1867 and early 1868 (the date is not known for certain). Lived in Texarkana, Sedalia and St. Louis, Missouri, and New York. Died April 1, 1917, in New York.

Scott Joplin wrote the "Maple Leaf Rag," and in his last years he paid to print his own opera. He was born in northeastern Texas. The exact date is not known. The historian Edward A. Berlin places it between June 1867 and the middle of January 1868.

His father, Giles, had been enslaved in North Carolina. His mother, Florence, was born free in Kentucky. She cleaned houses, and Scott learned to play on her employer's piano. A teacher, Julius Weiss, gave him lessons.

Churches and brothels were among the few places where a Black pianist could find steady work. A brothel is a house where men paid women for sex. In 1891 he was a member of the Texarkana Minstrels, a group raising money for a monument to Jefferson Davis, the former president of the Confederacy. In 1894 he moved to Sedalia, Missouri. There he played cornet in a town band and studied music at George R. Smith College.

On August 10, 1899, Joplin signed a contract with John Stark, a Sedalia music store owner and publisher, to publish the "Maple Leaf Rag." Joplin was to get one cent for each copy sold. His first biographer said it sold 75,000 copies in six months. Berlin later found that the first 400 copies took a year to sell, which paid Joplin about $1. Later sales gave him a small, steady income. By 1909 they would have paid him about $600 a year, Berlin found.

He moved to St. Louis in 1901 and to New York in 1907. He wrote an opera, *Treemonisha*. It is set on a Texas plantation after the Civil War, and it tells of a young, educated Black woman who helps free her people from ignorance and superstition. No one would pay to stage it. In 1911 he printed it with his own money. In 1915 he played it in a hall in Harlem, with only a piano.

By 1916 Joplin had syphilis in its late stage. Syphilis is a disease passed from person to person during sex. Without treatment it can spread to the brain, where it causes confusion, changes in personality and dementia, the loss of memory and of the ability to think. On February 2, 1917, he was admitted to Manhattan State Hospital, a hospital for mental illness. He died there of the disease on April 1, 1917. No record describes how he was treated there. He was buried in a grave with no marker.

*Treemonisha* was first fully staged in January 1972, in Atlanta. In 1974, 57 years after his burial, his grave was given a marker. In 1976 Joplin was given a Pulitzer Prize, a top American award.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The blues begins, and nobody wrote it down" -->
The blues is a kind of Black music from the South. It grew out of the songs and calls that enslaved people sang as they worked in the fields, and out of the songs of Black workers on farms, docks and river boats after slavery ended. No one knows who first played the blues, or when. Historians place its beginning between 1870 and 1900. In those years freed Black families in the South moved from slavery into sharecropping, which means farming another man's land in return for a share of the crop. No one wrote down a description of the blues before 1900. The first written accounts come from just after 1900.
