## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Record companies began to record the blues, jazz and the music later called country in the first half of the 1900s. Black musicians in the South made the blues and jazz. White and Black singers and fiddlers in the Southern mountains made country music. Most singers got a set fee for each side of a record and nothing more, and the companies kept the rest of the money. In 1921 Americans spent $106 million on records. From the 1920s, radio brought music into homes for free. Owners and managers of clubs, hotels and concert halls kept Black people out, even Black stars. In 1939 Billie Holiday recorded "Strange Fruit," a song about Black people murdered by white mobs. That same year Marian Anderson sang outdoors in Washington to more than 75,000 people, after the men and women in charge of two indoor halls refused her.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first written accounts of the blues, 1901 to 1903" -->
The first written account of the kind of songs later called the blues comes from 1901 and 1902, in Mississippi. That May and June, Charles Peabody, an archaeologist from Harvard University, dug into a Native mound in Coahoma County. He hired nine to fifteen Black men from the town of Clarksdale to do the digging. The men sang while they dug. Peabody wrote in 1903 that bosses sometimes hired a strong singer just to keep workers going. The men's songs were about hard luck, love and pay day. In one line the singer said he had been arrested for murder though he had never harmed anyone. Peabody did not use the word blues.

In about 1903 the Black bandleader W. C. Handy was waiting for a train at Tutwiler, Mississippi. A man beside him played a guitar by sliding a knife along the strings, and sang. No one wrote down the man's name. Handy later turned the song into "Yellow Dog Blues."

In 1912 Handy sold "The Memphis Blues," with all rights to it, for $100. In 1914 he published "Saint Louis Blues." With Harry Pace, a Black banker, he ran a company called Pace and Handy that published music. Handy called himself the "Father of the Blues." The pianist Jelly Roll Morton said that Handy had not started the music.

The singer Gertrude "Ma" Rainey said that in 1902 a girl in Missouri sang her a sad song about a man who had left a woman. Rainey began singing it in her traveling tent shows. Paramount Records' advertisements later called her the "Mother of the Blues." Between 1923 and 1928 she made more than 100 records.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Records for Black buyers, and who was paid, 1920 to 1938" -->
On August 10, 1920, Mamie Smith, a Black singer, recorded "Crazy Blues" for the Okeh record company in New York. The Black songwriter Perry Bradford wrote it. Within two months, buyers had paid a dollar each for 75,000 copies. It was the first blues record to become a big hit.

Record company managers then went looking for Black singers to sell records to Black buyers. By 1922 managers at Okeh were calling these records "race records." In 1921 the Black businessman Harry Pace started Black Swan Records. Black Swan paid its singers fair wages and let them record under their own names. Paramount took over Black Swan in 1924.

Most singers were paid a set fee for each side of a record, and no royalty. A royalty is a small payment for every copy sold, paid for as long as the record sells. With a set fee, a singer earned the same whether the record sold a hundred copies or a million.

Robert Johnson, a Black guitarist from Mississippi, recorded 29 songs in 1936 and 1937, in a hotel room in San Antonio and in Dallas, both in Texas. His best seller, "Terraplane Blues," sold about 10,000 copies. The records do not say what he was paid. He died near Greenwood, Mississippi, on August 16, 1938, at 27. No record shows what killed him. In 1998 a Mississippi court ruled that his son, Claud Johnson, should get the royalties from his songs, which by then came to more than $1 million.

In June 1949 the music magazine *Billboard* renamed its "race records" sales chart "rhythm and blues." A writer named Jerry Wexler suggested the new name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bessie-smith" name="Bessie Smith" movie="Bessie (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bessie Smith

> **Who:** A Black blues singer, the best-selling blues singer of the 1920s.
> **When and where:** Born in Chattanooga, Tennessee, on April 15, 1894 (the 1900 census gives July 1892). Recorded in New York. Died near Clarksdale, Mississippi, on September 26, 1937.
> **Movie:** *Bessie* (2015), an HBO drama film, with Queen Latifah as Smith.

Bessie Smith's first record sold 780,000 copies in six months, and she was paid $125 for each side, with no royalty. She was born in Chattanooga, Tennessee. Her family kept April 15, 1894, as her birthday. The 1900 census gives July 1892.

Both her parents died when she was a child. She and her brother Andrew sang and danced for coins on the streets of Chattanooga. In 1912 she joined a traveling show as a dancer. Ma Rainey sang in the same show.

In 1923 Frank Walker of Columbia Records signed her. Her first record, "Down Hearted Blues," was the one that sold 780,000 copies in six months. Her contract paid her $125 for each side that could be used. Later contracts paid $150 and then $200 a side, and none of them paid a royalty. Record companies paid white singers more, and they gave white singers royalties.

She recorded between 150 and 200 songs. The Library of Congress gives both numbers. On tour she earned up to $2,000 a week, and she traveled in her own railroad car.

On September 26, 1937, the car she rode in hit the back of a slow truck on Route 61 near Clarksdale, Mississippi. Her partner, Richard Morgan, was driving. Her right arm was almost torn off at the elbow. A white doctor from Memphis, Hugh Smith, stopped to help. She was taken to the G. T. Thomas Afro-American Hospital in Clarksdale. Doctors there amputated her arm, which means they cut it off, and she died that morning.

In 1937 the record producer John Hammond wrote that she died because a white hospital refused to treat her. Dr. Smith said the ambulance would never have taken her to a white hospital. Her biographer, Chris Albertson, found no evidence for Hammond's story.

More than 10,000 people came to her funeral in Philadelphia. Her grave had no stone until 1970, when the singer Janis Joplin helped pay for one. Later the son of her husband sued Columbia for her royalties. The judges ruled against him because he had waited too long to sue. They did not decide whether she had been cheated.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bessie-smith" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jazz on record, 1917 to 1931" -->
Jazz began among Black musicians in New Orleans in the early 1900s. The first jazz record, though, was made by white musicians. On February 26, 1917, five white musicians from New Orleans, calling themselves the Original Dixieland Jass Band, recorded two songs in New York for the Victor company. Victor's managers put the record on sale in May 1917. By some counts it sold more than a million copies. The band's leader, Nick LaRocca, later claimed that he had invented jazz. A story says that Victor had asked a Black New Orleans cornet player, Freddie Keppard, to record first, in 1915, and that he said no. The historian John Edward Hasse calls that story a legend.

The Cotton Club in Harlem, New York, was run by Owney Madden, a gangster who sold illegal liquor. Madden's managers hired Black musicians and dancers, and they let in only white customers, apart from a few Black stars. The club's dancers had to be light-skinned, at least 5 feet 6 inches tall and under 21. Duke Ellington's band played there from December 4, 1927, to June 30, 1931. When Ellington asked, the owners let in a few more Black customers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="louis-armstrong" name="Louis Armstrong" movie="Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Louis Armstrong

> **Who:** A Black trumpet player and singer from New Orleans who changed how jazz was played and sung.
> **When and where:** Born in New Orleans. Most sources give August 4, 1901. He always said July 4, 1900. Lived in Chicago and New York. Died in New York on July 6, 1971.
> **Movie:** *Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues* (2022), a documentary about him.

Louis Armstrong learned to play the cornet in a home for Black boys in New Orleans, where a judge had sent him at 11. On New Year's Eve 1912 he fired his stepfather's gun into the air. It was loaded with a blank. Police arrested him, and the next day a judge sent him to the Colored Waif's Home, a reform home for Black boys. There a teacher, Peter Davis, taught him the cornet in the home's band.

In 1922 the bandleader Joe "King" Oliver invited him to Chicago to play second cornet in his band. From November 1925 Armstrong made records for Okeh with his own groups, the Hot Five and the Hot Seven.

On September 17, 1957, in Grand Forks, North Dakota, a reporter asked Armstrong about the fight over nine Black students entering Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. Armstrong said: "The way they are treating my people in the South, the government can go to hell." He called President Dwight Eisenhower "two faced" and said he had "no guts."

Staff at a radio station in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, threw out his records. Officials at the University of Arkansas cancelled his invitation to play a dance. The Black congressman Adam Clayton Powell said Armstrong "didn't know what he was talking about." On September 24 Eisenhower sent soldiers to protect the students. Armstrong then sent him a telegram: "If you decide to walk into the schools with the little colored kids, take me along Daddy. God bless you."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="louis-armstrong" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music on record: Bristol, 1927" -->
From July 25 to August 5, 1927, Ralph Peer of the Victor record company recorded 76 songs by 19 singers and groups in Bristol, Tennessee. He set up on the third floor of a hat and glove company's building. Each performer got $50 in cash for each side, plus 2½ cents for each record sold. The Carter Family recorded on August 1 and 2, and a singer named Jimmie Rodgers on August 4.

Peer took no salary from Victor. Instead he kept the copyright of the songs he recorded, including very old songs that no one had written down. A copyright is the legal right to be paid when a song is printed, recorded or played. In 1928 Peer started a company, Southern Music Publishing, to collect that money. He offered to publish the songs that A. P. Carter of the Carter Family had gathered, if Carter would claim that he wrote them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maybelle-carter" name="Maybelle Carter" movie="The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maybelle Carter

> **Who:** Guitarist and singer in the Carter Family, who changed how the guitar is played in country music.
> **When and where:** Born May 10, 1909, in Nickelsville, Virginia. Recorded at Bristol, Tennessee, in 1927, at 18. Died October 23, 1978, in Hendersonville, Tennessee.
> **Movie:** *The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken* (2005), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about the family.

Maybelle Carter found a way to play the guitar that made it a lead instrument in country music. She picked out the tune with her thumb on the low strings and brushed the chords with her finger on the high strings. People call it the "Carter scratch." She first recorded "Wildwood Flower," played this way, in 1928.

She sang and played with Sara Carter and A. P. Carter as the Carter Family. At Bristol in August 1927 she was 18. Each of the three was paid Victor's rate of $50 a side and 2½ cents a record.

From December 1928 A. P. Carter went looking for old songs in the mountains of Virginia and Tennessee with Lesley Riddle. Riddle was a Black guitarist from North Carolina. In 1927 he had lost his right leg at the knee after an accident at a cement plant. On their trips, Carter wrote down the words while Riddle remembered the tunes. Riddle then taught the tunes to Maybelle and Sara. He lived with the Carters for a time. Maybelle said she learned guitar runs from him, including the ones she played in "Cannonball Blues."

No record shows that Riddle was paid, or named as a writer, for any of the songs he helped gather.

Later Maybelle led a group with her daughters, called Mother Maybelle and the Carter Sisters. From 1968 she toured with her son-in-law, the singer Johnny Cash.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maybelle-carter" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Grand Ole Opry, from 1925" -->
On November 28, 1925, George D. Hay started a Saturday night country music show on WSM, a Nashville radio station owned by the National Life and Accident Insurance Company. He called it the *WSM Barn Dance*. The first player was a 77-year-old fiddler, Uncle Jimmy Thompson.

On December 10, 1927, the *Barn Dance* came on the air after a program of opera music. The Black harmonica player DeFord Bailey played his "Pan American Blues." Hay then told listeners, "from now on, we will present 'The Grand Ole Opry.'" From 1939 to 1956 one hour of the show went out across the country on the NBC radio network.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="deford-bailey" name="DeFord Bailey" movie="DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost (2002)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### DeFord Bailey

> **Who:** A Black harmonica player and the first star of the Grand Ole Opry.
> **When and where:** Born December 14, 1899, in Smith County, Tennessee. Played on Nashville radio from 1926 to 1941. Died July 2, 1982.
> **Movie:** *DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost* (2002), a Nashville Public Television documentary about him.

DeFord Bailey was a Black harmonica player whose music gave the Grand Ole Opry its name. He was born in Smith County, Tennessee, on December 14, 1899. His family played what he called "Black hillbilly music."

Polio struck him when he was three. Polio is a disease caused by a virus that can damage the nerves that move the muscles. It stunted Bailey's growth and left his back bent.

He played on the *WSM Barn Dance* from June 1926. In December 1927, after his "Pan American Blues," George D. Hay gave the show the name Grand Ole Opry. Sources disagree about his pay. One says he got a fixed $5 a show at first, less than the white players he toured with. The Country Music Hall of Fame says he was one of the best-paid stars on the show.

He toured the South with white stars such as Roy Acuff, Bill Monroe and Uncle Dave Macon. Hotels and restaurants would not serve him because he was Black. The other players brought him food, or got him into hotels by the fire escape.

In 1941 most radio stations stopped playing songs licensed by ASCAP, a group that collected money for songwriters, because ASCAP had raised its fees. Managers at WSM told Bailey not to play his ASCAP songs, which were some of his most popular ones. They fired him at the end of 1941. In 1945 Hay wrote a book about the show. In it he called Bailey "lazy" and wrote that he "refused to learn new tunes." Bailey later said that Hay had treated him kindly and had written those words to please his bosses.

After 1941 Bailey ran a shoeshine stand in Nashville and rented out rooms. He almost never played in public again until 1974. In 2005 he was named to the Country Music Hall of Fame.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="deford-bailey" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio, the Depression and two fights over pay, 1906 to 1944" -->
In 1906 John Philip Sousa wrote that records and player pianos were becoming a "substitute for human skill, intelligence and soul." He called records "canned music." In 1909 members of Congress passed a copyright law that let anyone record a song without asking, as long as they paid the song's owner a set fee.

Americans spent $106 million on records in 1921. They did not spend that much again until 1945. In the early 1930s record sales fell to about one tenth of what they had been in the 1920s. People who had lost their jobs in the Great Depression stopped buying records, and music on the radio was free.

Radio stations paid fees to play songs. The fees went to ASCAP, a group that collected money for songwriters. In 1939 ASCAP announced higher fees, and radio station owners started their own song-licensing group, called BMI. In 1941 almost every station, and all three national networks, stopped playing ASCAP songs. ASCAP had turned away most writers of blues, gospel, country and Latin music. BMI signed them, and radio played more of their songs.

On August 1, 1942, James Petrillo, the head of the musicians' union, told its members to stop making records. He wanted musicians to be paid when radio stations and jukeboxes played records instead of hiring live bands. The owners of Decca agreed in September 1943 to pay a fee on every record into a union fund. The heads of Victor and Columbia agreed on November 11, 1944. Singers were allowed to keep recording with voices alone, so for two years singers made records without bands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gospel, shape notes and prison recordings" -->
Gospel is a kind of Black church music. Thomas A. Dorsey had played piano for Ma Rainey under the name "Georgia Tom." In August 1932 his wife, Nettie, and their newborn son died, and he wrote "Take My Hand, Precious Lord." That year he started the first Black-owned company to publish gospel music. In 1933 he started a national convention of gospel choirs. He led the music at Pilgrim Baptist Church in Chicago for fifty years. "I'm not ashamed of my blues," he said. "It's all the same talent."

Mahalia Jackson came to Chicago from New Orleans in 1927, and Dorsey trained her. She would sing only gospel. Managers at Decca offered to keep recording her if she sang blues, and she refused. Her "Move On Up a Little Higher" of 1947 sold about two million copies.

In 1934 Judge Jackson, a Black singing teacher in Ozark, Alabama, printed *The Colored Sacred Harp*. Judge was his first name. It was a book of 77 shape-note hymns by Black singers, and 18 of them were his own. He and Bishop J. D. Walker paid to print 1,000 copies, and Jackson sold them door to door.

From 1933 John Lomax and his son Alan recorded Black singers in Southern prisons for the Library of Congress. John Lomax said he wanted music "not yet influenced by jazz and the radio." At Angola, the Louisiana state prison, they recorded Huddie Ledbetter, called Lead Belly. He was serving time for stabbing a man in a fight. He was let out in 1934. A prison official later wrote that his singing had nothing to do with his release.

John Lomax then managed Lead Belly and took a share of his pay. The two men split over money in 1935. In 1936 Lomax published a book that printed Lead Belly's song "Goodnight, Irene" with both men named as its writers. Lomax had recorded the song. He had not written it. In 1950, the year after Lead Belly died, a group called the Weavers sold 2 million copies of "Goodnight, Irene."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native songs, Hawaiian guitar and a new songwriter" -->
On March 28, 1900, the Carlisle Indian School band played the "Aboriginal Suite" at Carnegie Hall in New York. Its composer and conductor was Dennison Wheelock, the Oneida student who had come to lead the Carlisle band. He was its first Native bandmaster. He later became a lawyer and argued cases for Native nations before the Supreme Court.

Scientists recorded thousands of Native songs on wax cylinders in these years. By the 1980s the Library of Congress held nearly 8,000 such cylinders. Many held sacred songs meant only for ceremonies. One collector, Francis La Flesche, took singers from Oklahoma to Washington, D.C., so that neighbors who objected could not stop him from recording ceremonial songs. No record shows that the singers were paid. From 1907 Frances Densmore recorded the songs of the Ojibwe, Sioux, Mandan, Hidatsa, Tohono O'odham, Seminole and other nations for the Smithsonian Institution.

In 1889 Joseph Kekuku, a Hawaiian student at the Kamehameha School for Boys, found that he could change a guitar's pitch by sliding a steel bar along the strings. Congress annexed Hawaii, making it American land, in 1898. From 1904 Kekuku played this steel guitar in theaters across the mainland United States.

Irving Berlin, born Israel Beilin, came to New York from the Russian Empire in 1893, at age five. He could not read music, and he could play the piano in only one key. His "Alexander's Ragtime Band" was a hit in 1911. He owned his own songs through his own publishing company. He gave all the money from "God Bless America," which Kate Smith first sang in 1938, to the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="woody-guthrie" name="Woody Guthrie" movie="Bound for Glory (1976)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Woody Guthrie

> **Who:** A folk singer and songwriter who wrote about poor farm families, workers and migrants in the 1930s and 1940s.
> **When and where:** Born July 14, 1912, in Okemah, Oklahoma. Lived in Texas, California and New York. Died October 3, 1967, in New York.
> **Movie:** *Bound for Glory* (1976), a Hollywood film based loosely on his own book about his life.

Woody Guthrie wrote "This Land Is Your Land" and many other songs about poor farm families and workers. He was born in Okemah, Oklahoma, on July 14, 1912.

His mother, Nora, had Huntington's disease and was put in a hospital. Huntington's is a disease passed down in families. It destroys parts of the brain. A person with it makes movements they cannot control, has changes in mood, and loses the ability to think.

In the 1930s Guthrie joined the farm families who left the Great Plains for California after years of drought, the time called the Dust Bowl. He sang on a Los Angeles radio station, KFVD. In 1940 he made an album called *Dust Bowl Ballads* for Victor.

On February 23, 1940, in a New York hotel, he wrote "This Land Is Your Land." He wrote it as an answer to Irving Berlin's "God Bless America," which he kept hearing on the radio. His first title was "God Blessed America for Me." His handwritten copy had two verses that were often left out later. One is about a sign that says "No Trespassing." In the other, hungry people stand in line at a relief office, a place that gave out food or money to the poor.

In May 1941 the Bonneville Power Administration, a federal agency that sold electric power from dams on the Columbia River, hired him for one month for $266. His boss, Stephen Kahn, wanted three pages of songs a day for a film about the dams. Guthrie wrote 26 songs in that month. One of them was "Roll On, Columbia." Kahn later said the government had never gotten "a better investment on its money."

On his guitar Guthrie wrote "This machine kills fascists." Fascists were the followers of the dictators who ruled Italy and Germany. In the Second World War he served in the Merchant Marine, the ships that carried supplies. In 1952 doctors found that he had Huntington's disease. He spent his last years in hospitals in New Jersey and New York, and he died at Creedmoor hospital in New York in 1967, at 55.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woody-guthrie" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Strange Fruit, 1939" -->
"Strange Fruit" is a song about the lynching of Black Americans. Lynching means a mob killing a person without a trial, often by hanging. The song describes the bodies of Black people hanging from trees in the South. It describes their bulging eyes and twisted mouths, and the smell of burning flesh.

Abel Meeropol, a white English teacher in the Bronx, New York, wrote the song under the name Lewis Allan. He wrote it after he saw a photograph of the lynching of Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith in Marion, Indiana.

On August 7, 1930, a white mob of between 5,000 and 10,000 people broke into the jail in Marion. The mob wanted three young Black men held there. The three had been accused of killing a white man and sexually assaulting a white woman. Men in the mob beat Shipp and Smith, who were both 19, and hanged them. People in the crowd took parts of their bodies home as souvenirs. The third prisoner, James Cameron, who was 16, was beaten but lived, after someone in the crowd said he was innocent. The NAACP, a group that fights for the rights of Black Americans, named 27 men it suspected. All-white juries found the leaders not guilty.

Billie Holiday first sang "Strange Fruit" at Café Society, a club in New York, in 1939. She sang it last. The waiters stopped serving, and the room went dark except for a light on her face. Managers at Columbia would not record it. John Hammond, her producer there, said record stores and radio stations in the South would object. Milt Gabler of the small Commodore label recorded it on April 20, 1939. It became her best-selling record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billie-holiday" name="Billie Holiday" movie="Billie (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Billie Holiday

> **Who:** A Black jazz singer who recorded "Strange Fruit," a song about lynching, in 1939.
> **When and where:** Born Eleanora Fagan on April 7, 1915, in Philadelphia. Grew up in Baltimore. Sang in New York. Died July 17, 1959, in New York.
> **Movie:** *Billie* (2019), a documentary about her.

Billie Holiday sang "Strange Fruit," and federal drug agents arrested her three times, the last time in her hospital bed as she was dying. She was born Eleanora Fagan in Philadelphia on April 7, 1915, and grew up in Baltimore.

At nine she was sent to the House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school for girls in Baltimore, for missing school. As a young girl she ran errands in a brothel and scrubbed floors there.

In 1938 she sang with Artie Shaw's band, whose other members were white. At the Lincoln Hotel in New York, the managers made her use the service elevator because white guests complained. She left the band.

Harry Anslinger ran the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, the government's drug police, from 1930 to 1962. On May 16, 1947, federal agents arrested Holiday for having narcotics, illegal drugs, in her New York apartment. She pleaded guilty, and a federal judge sent her to the Alderson Federal Prison Camp in West Virginia. She said later: "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt."

Because of the conviction, New York City officials took away her cabaret card, the license a performer needed to work in any club that sold alcohol. After that she could sing in New York only in concert halls and theaters. In January 1949 a federal agent, George White, arrested her again, in a San Francisco hotel.

In 2015 the writer Johann Hari wrote that Anslinger's agents went after her from 1939 because she sang "Strange Fruit." The jazz writer Lewis Porter points out that no earlier source says this.

In 1959 she was in Metropolitan Hospital in New York with liver and heart disease. On May 31, on Anslinger's orders, federal narcotics agents arrested her in her hospital bed and handcuffed her to it. Police guarded her room until a judge ordered the handcuffs taken off. She died there on July 17, 1959, at 44, with 70 cents in the bank. Her liver was scarred, a disease called cirrhosis, and her heart failed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="billie-holiday" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marian Anderson at the Lincoln Memorial, 1939" -->
In January 1939 Howard University in Washington, D.C., asked to rent Constitution Hall for an Easter concert by the Black singer Marian Anderson. The hall belonged to the Daughters of the American Revolution, or DAR, a women's club. Its leaders refused. Since 1932 the club had let only white performers use the hall. Frank Ballou, the head of Washington's public schools, also refused to let her use the hall of Central High School, a white school. The school board backed him.

First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt quit the DAR on February 26, 1939. She wrote that its leaders had "an opportunity to lead in an enlightened way" and had "failed." Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, then set up a free concert on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson sang there to more than 75,000 people, and millions more heard her on the radio. She began with "My Country, 'Tis of Thee."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marian-anderson" name="Marian Anderson" movie="Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert (1939)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Marian Anderson

> **Who:** A Black contralto, a singer with a low woman's voice, who sang at the Lincoln Memorial in 1939 and was the first Black singer at the Metropolitan Opera.
> **When and where:** Born February 27, 1897, in Philadelphia. Died April 8, 1993, in Portland, Oregon.
> **Movie:** *Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert* (1939), a short film of the concert.

Marian Anderson was a contralto, a singer with a low woman's voice. She was the first Black singer to perform at the Metropolitan Opera in New York. She was born in Philadelphia on February 27, 1897.

As a young woman she applied to a music school in Philadelphia. The school's officials turned her away because they took only white students. She became famous singing in Europe. In Salzburg, Austria, in 1935, the conductor Arturo Toscanini told her she had "a voice heard once in a hundred years."

In 1939 the DAR's president, Sarah Corbin Robert, and its other leaders kept her out of Constitution Hall. She sang outdoors at the Lincoln Memorial instead, on April 9, 1939.

On January 7, 1955, at 57, she sang at the Metropolitan Opera. She sang the part of Ulrica, a fortune teller, in Verdi's opera *Un ballo in maschera*. She sang at the March on Washington in August 1963. That year she was given the Presidential Medal of Freedom, an award given by the president.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the early 1950s Black musicians made rock and roll out of rhythm and blues and gospel. White singers then sold more copies of some of those songs than the Black singers who recorded them first. Record company owners, publishers and managers kept most of the money from many of these songs. Big Mama Thornton said she got one check for $500 for "Hound Dog." In Detroit, Berry Gordy built Motown, which became the largest Black-owned business on one magazine's list. Civil rights marchers sang old church songs with new words, in jails and at mass meetings. In 1973 a teenage DJ and his sister held a party in the Bronx, in New York City, that people now name as the start of hip-hop. In 1985 senators held a hearing on the words of songs, and the heads of record companies agreed to put warning labels on some albums.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rock and roll, and who got paid for it" -->
Sister Rosetta Tharpe, a Black gospel singer from Arkansas, played electric guitar in church music from the late 1930s. Her "Strange Things Happening Every Day," from 1944, was the first gospel record to reach the rhythm and blues sales chart. Some scholars call it the first rock and roll record. She was an influence on Elvis Presley, Little Richard, Chuck Berry and Johnny Cash.

Fats Domino's "The Fat Man," from 1949, had sold a million copies by 1951. Chuck Berry's "Maybellene," from 1955, sold more than a million. When Berry first saw his record, the label named the disc jockey Alan Freed and another man as its co-writers. A disc jockey is a person who plays records on the radio. As co-writers, the two men took part of the money due to the song's writer. Berry later won back full credit.

White singers made cover versions of songs by Black singers. A cover version is a new recording of a song that someone else has already recorded. In 1955 Georgia Gibbs, a white singer, copied LaVern Baker's "Tweedle Dee" note for note. Baker's record reached No. 14 on the national pop chart, and Gibbs's reached No. 1. Baker tried to sue Gibbs for $250,000 and failed. She asked Congress to make copying another singer's arrangement a violation of copyright. Representative Charles Diggs led an inquiry into what he called "song theft."

Pat Boone, a white singer, covered Fats Domino's "Ain't That a Shame" in 1955. It was Boone's first No. 1 record. Domino and his co-writer, Dave Bartholomew, still earned writers' royalties from it. Boone also covered Little Richard's "Tutti Frutti," and Boone's record ranked higher on the national pop chart than Little Richard's own. Little Richard later said that his record company, Specialty Records, had cut his royalty rate without telling him. In 1984 he sued the company and its owner, Art Rupe, for $112 million in royalties he said he was owed. They settled out of court in 1986.

Payola is paying a disc jockey or a radio station in secret to play a record. In 1959 and 1960 a House committee led by Representative Oren Harris held hearings on payola. Alan Freed refused to sign a statement that he had taken no bribes, and the radio station WABC fired him in November 1959. In 1962 he pleaded guilty to taking bribes and paid a $300 fine. In 1960 members of Congress made payola a crime, punished by up to a year in jail. The older song publishers in ASCAP pushed for the hearings, partly because they opposed rock and roll.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hound Dog: Big Mama Thornton and Elvis Presley" -->
Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller, two white songwriters, wrote "Hound Dog." Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton, a Black blues singer from Alabama, recorded it on August 13, 1952. Don Robey's Peacock Records put it out in 1953, and it was No. 1 on the rhythm and blues chart for seven weeks. Robey guessed that it sold 500,000 to 750,000 copies.

Thornton said: "That song sold over two million records. I got one check for $500 and never saw another." Robey put his own name on the copyright form as one of the song's writers.

Elvis Presley recorded "Hound Dog" for RCA on July 2, 1956. His record was No. 1 on the pop chart for 11 weeks and sold about 10 million copies. He had copied the version of a white Las Vegas band, Freddie Bell and the Bellboys, more than Thornton's. Leiber and Stoller earned large royalties only after Presley's record. Thornton died poor in a Los Angeles boarding house in 1984, of heart and liver disease. She was buried in a grave shared with two other people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elvis-presley" name="Elvis Presley" movie="Elvis (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Elvis Presley

> **Who:** A white rock and roll singer from Mississippi and Tennessee whose records sold an estimated 500 million copies.
> **When and where:** Born January 8, 1935, in Tupelo, Mississippi. Moved to Memphis, Tennessee, in 1948. Died August 16, 1977, at his Memphis home, Graceland, at 42.
> **Movie:** *Elvis* (2022), a Hollywood drama film about him and his manager.

Elvis Presley's first record was a blues song by a Black singer, and his record of "Hound Dog" copied a song that had been a hit for a Black singer first. His manager took up to half of some of his income. He was born in Tupelo, Mississippi, on January 8, 1935, and his family moved to Memphis in 1948.

He first heard music at his family's church, an Assembly of God church. In Memphis he listened to Black blues and rhythm and blues singers on the radio.

Sam Phillips owned Sun Records in Memphis. He recorded Black blues musicians such as Howlin' Wolf and B. B. King. His office manager, Marion Keisker, said he often told her: "If I could find a white man who had the Negro sound and the Negro feel, I could make a billion dollars." On the night of July 5, 1954, at Sun, Presley sang "That's All Right," a blues song by the Black singer Arthur Crudup. Phillips recorded it.

Crudup had recorded the song for RCA in 1946. He said later: "I realised I was making everybody rich, and here I was poor." In 1968 a publisher backed out of a $60,000 settlement with him at the last moment. In 1971 a songwriters' group got him more than $10,000 in back pay. He died in 1974.

In November 1955 Phillips and Presley's manager, Colonel Tom Parker, sold Presley's contract to RCA for $40,000. Parker took 25 percent of Presley's pay. In 1967 Presley agreed to raise Parker's share to 50 percent of some of his income. In 1973 Parker sold RCA the rights to all of Presley's old records for $5.4 million. Because of that sale, Presley's family got no royalties on those records after he died. In 1981 Blanchard Tual, a lawyer appointed by a court to check Parker's work, called the 50 percent share "extortionate," which means unfairly high.

Presley served in the Army from 1958 to 1960. His record sales are estimated at about 500 million copies around the world. He died at Graceland on August 16, 1977, at 42.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elvis-presley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Motown, soul and gospel" -->
On January 12, 1959, Berry Gordy Jr., a Black songwriter in Detroit, started a record company with an $800 loan from his family's savings fund. It was later called Motown. From 1960 to 1969, 79 Motown songs reached the top ten of the national pop chart. In 1973 *Black Enterprise* magazine began ranking the largest Black-owned businesses. Motown was first on that list, with $40 million in sales, and it stayed first until 1983. Gordy sold Motown in 1988 for $61 million.

A group of Detroit studio musicians, later called the Funk Brothers, played on most Motown hits from 1959 to 1972. No Motown record listed their names until Marvin Gaye's album *What's Going On* in 1971. Gordy fined any of them he caught recording for other companies.

Ray Charles had been blind since he was about seven. In "I Got a Woman," from 1954, he mixed gospel with the blues, and some church people objected. In 1961 he refused to play a dance in Augusta, Georgia, when he learned that the audience would be split by race. The promoter sued him, and a Georgia court fined him $757. His 1959 contract with ABC-Paramount gave him ownership of his own master recordings, the original tapes that every copy is made from.

Sam Cooke, a gospel singer from Mississippi, started his own record company and his own company to publish his songs. From 1963 his own company owned his master recordings. He was among the first Black artists to control his own business. On December 11, 1964, a Los Angeles motel manager, Bertha Franklin, shot and killed him. A coroner's jury called it justifiable homicide, a killing the law excuses. His family has disputed that account.

James Brown paid for the recording of his 1963 album *Live at the Apollo* himself. On April 5, 1968, the day after Martin Luther King Jr. was killed, Brown gave a free concert in Boston that was shown on television. His song "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud" came out that August.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="aretha-franklin" name="Aretha Franklin" movie="Amazing Grace (2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aretha Franklin

> **Who:** A Black singer and pianist who recorded "Respect" in 1967.
> **When and where:** Born March 25, 1942, in Memphis, Tennessee. Grew up in Detroit, Michigan. Died there August 16, 2018.
> **Movie:** *Amazing Grace* (2018), a documentary film of her 1972 gospel concert in a Los Angeles church.

Aretha Franklin took Otis Redding's song "Respect" and turned it into a woman's demand, and it went to No. 1 in 1967. She was born in Memphis, Tennessee, on March 25, 1942. Her father, the Reverend C. L. Franklin, led New Bethel Baptist Church in Detroit, and she sang there as a child. At 14 she recorded a gospel album, *Songs of Faith*. She had her first child at 12 and her second at 15.

She recorded for Columbia from 1960 to 1966 and had few hits. The producer Jerry Wexler then brought her to Atlantic Records. In January 1967 she recorded for one day at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, before a fight in the studio ended the session.

Redding had written and recorded "Respect" in 1965. In his song a man asks for respect when he comes home. Franklin recorded it on February 14, 1967, in New York. She and her sister Carolyn made it a woman's demand. They spelled out the word "R-E-S-P-E-C-T" and added new lines. It was No. 1 on the pop chart for two weeks and on the rhythm and blues chart for eight. Women and civil rights marchers took it as their own song. Redding called it the song "that a girl took away from me."

In 1970 the Black activist Angela Davis was jailed. Franklin said, "Angela Davis must go free." In January 1972 Franklin recorded the gospel album *Amazing Grace* at New Temple Missionary Baptist Church in Los Angeles. It sold more than two million copies. The film made at the same time was not shown until 2018, because the sound and the pictures could not be matched. In 1987 she became the first woman named to the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. She died in Detroit on August 16, 2018.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aretha-franklin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Songs of the civil rights movement" -->
"We Shall Overcome" started as a church hymn by Charles Albert Tindley, printed in 1901. In 1945 and 1946 Black tobacco workers went on strike at a cigar factory in Charleston, South Carolina. One of them, Lucille Simmons, led the strikers in singing "We Will Overcome." Zilphia Horton of the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee learned it from her. The folk singer Pete Seeger changed "will" to "shall." From 1959 Guy Carawan taught the song to young civil rights workers.

On March 7, 1965, Alabama state troopers and other officers, led by Major John Cloud, attacked about 600 marchers in Selma, Alabama, with nightsticks and tear gas. At least 50 marchers went to the hospital. On March 15 President Lyndon Johnson spoke to Congress and used the song's words: "And we shall overcome." In January 2018, after a court case, the company that held the song's copyright gave it up. Since then anyone may use it freely.

On August 28, 1963, at the March on Washington, Mahalia Jackson, Marian Anderson, Joan Baez, Bob Dylan, Odetta, and Peter, Paul and Mary sang. During Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech, Jackson called out to him, "Tell them about the dream, Martin!"
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" name="Bernice Johnson Reagon" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bernice Johnson Reagon

> **Who:** A Black student in Albany, Georgia, who led singing at civil rights meetings and sang with the SNCC Freedom Singers.
> **When and where:** Born October 4, 1942, in Albany, Georgia. Died July 16, 2024.

Bernice Johnson Reagon led the singing at civil rights meetings in Albany, Georgia, and changed the words of old spirituals to fit the protests. She was born in Albany on October 4, 1942.

She joined the NAACP Youth Council in Albany before workers from SNCC, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, arrived there in the fall of 1961. Officials at Albany State College suspended her for her part in the protests. At mass meetings she took old spirituals and put new words in them. Of one song she said: "So instead I put in freedom and by the second line everyone was singing."

In 1962 she joined the SNCC Freedom Singers, with Cordell Reagon, Rutha Mae Harris and Chuck Neblett. She married Cordell Reagon. The group drove 50,000 miles in nine months to sing about the movement, and its first tour raised nearly $50,000 for SNCC. She said of the group, "There weren't soloists, there were song leaders." She called it "a singing newspaper."

In 1973 she started Sweet Honey in the Rock, a group of Black women who sing without instruments. She later worked for the Smithsonian Institution, recording and studying the songs and stories of Black Americans. She died on July 16, 2024.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bob-dylan" name="Bob Dylan" movie="No Direction Home: Bob Dylan (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bob Dylan

> **Who:** A songwriter of the folk music revival of the early 1960s and after, and winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature.
> **When and where:** Born Robert Allen Zimmerman on May 24, 1941, in Duluth, Minnesota. Grew up in Hibbing, Minnesota. Moved to New York in 1961.
> **Movie:** *No Direction Home: Bob Dylan* (2005), a documentary by Martin Scorsese.

In 2016 Bob Dylan won the Nobel Prize in Literature, a world prize for writers, for his songs. The prize was given to him "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition." He was born Robert Allen Zimmerman in Duluth, Minnesota, on May 24, 1941.

He moved to New York in January 1961, partly to meet Woody Guthrie. Guthrie was then in a mental hospital in New Jersey with Huntington's disease. Dylan's 1963 album *The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan* made his name as a writer of protest songs, songs that speak out against something wrong. At the March on Washington that August he sang "Only a Pawn in Their Game."

At the Newport Folk Festival in 1965 he played an electric guitar with a band. Leading people in folk music reacted with anger.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bob-dylan" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music from Nashville and Bakersfield" -->
Hank Williams learned guitar as a boy in Alabama from a Black street musician, Rufus "Tee-Tot" Payne, who taught him in return for money or meals. Williams had 12 No. 1 country records. He was born with a defect of the spine that caused him pain all his life, and he drank and took painkillers. Grand Ole Opry managers dropped him in August 1952 for drunkenness and missed shows. On January 1, 1953, his heart failed in the back seat of a car while he was being driven to a show. He was 29.

Patsy Cline recorded "Crazy," a song by Willie Nelson, in 1961. She died in a plane crash near Camden, Tennessee, on March 5, 1963, at 30. In 1973 she became the first woman in the Country Music Hall of Fame.

In the late 1950s Buck Owens, Merle Haggard and other musicians in Bakersfield, California, played a loud, plain country music on electric guitars. Nashville records of those years added orchestras, and the Bakersfield musicians broke from that sound. Many of their listeners were the families of people who had come to California in the Dust Bowl years.

Johnny Cash recorded two concerts for prisoners at Folsom State Prison in California on January 13, 1968. The album ends with "Greystone Chapel," a song by a prisoner, Glen Sherley. It sold 500,000 copies by October 1968. Cash himself never served a prison sentence.

Loretta Lynn's "The Pill," from 1975, is about a wife who is tired of being pregnant and starts taking birth-control pills. Managers at some country radio stations refused to play it. It still reached No. 5 on the country chart.

In 1967 Charley Pride became the first Black performer on the Grand Ole Opry since DeFord Bailey last played there in 1941. Pride was the son of Black sharecroppers from Sledge, Mississippi. His first records went to radio stations without his photograph. He had 30 No. 1 country records, and in 1971 the Country Music Association named him Entertainer of the Year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hip-hop from the Bronx, 1973 onward" -->
On August 11, 1973, Clive Campbell, a teenage DJ called Kool Herc, played records at a party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx, New York. A DJ is a person who plays records for dancers or listeners. His sister Cindy Campbell planned the party to earn money for back-to-school clothes. In 2007 city officials named the building the birthplace of hip-hop.

In the same years, fires burned much of the South Bronx. Most were arson, fires set on purpose. Landlords paid people who lived nearby to set fires in the landlords' own buildings, so the landlords could collect insurance money. One landlord collected $125,000 from 21 fires. After a study by the RAND Corporation, city officials closed 12 fire companies in the South Bronx between 1974 and 1976, when the city was nearly out of money. Between 1970 and 1980, seven census tracts, small areas the census counts separately, lost more than 97 percent of their buildings to fire and abandonment. About 250,000 people lost their homes.

"Rapper's Delight," by the Sugarhill Gang, came out on September 16, 1979, from Sylvia Robinson's company, Sugar Hill Records. It reached No. 36 on the national pop chart. Many writers call it the first rap record that sold widely. It used the music of Chic's song "Good Times." Chic's writers, Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards, got credit only after they threatened to sue. One of its rappers used rhymes written by Grandmaster Caz and did not credit him.

"The Message," from 1982, by Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five, described poverty in the inner city. In 2002 it became the first hip-hop record added to the National Recording Registry, the Library of Congress's list of recordings chosen to be kept for the future.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dj-kool-herc" name="DJ Kool Herc" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DJ Kool Herc

> **Who:** Born Clive Campbell in Jamaica, the DJ at the 1973 Bronx party that people name as the start of hip-hop.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1955, in Kingston, Jamaica. Moved to the Bronx, New York, in November 1967, at 13.

DJ Kool Herc played records at the 1973 party that people name as the start of hip-hop. He found a way to make the drum part of a record last as long as the dancers wanted. He was born Clive Campbell in Kingston, Jamaica, on April 16, 1955. In November 1967, at 13, he moved to the Bronx.

At parties he played funk records on two turntables. A funk record has a "break," a short part with only the drums playing. Herc used two copies of the same record. When the break ended on one, he started it again on the other. He called this the "Merry-Go-Round." He called the dancers who danced to the breaks "b-boys" and "b-girls."

His sister Cindy planned the party of August 11, 1973, at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue. Later Herc was stabbed at a club called the Executive Playhouse while trying to stop a fight, and he pulled back from playing. By 1980 he had stopped working as a DJ. The records do not show what he earned from hip-hop.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dj-kool-herc" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Woodstock, music television, compact discs and the lyrics hearing" -->
The Woodstock music festival took place on Max Yasgur's dairy farm in Bethel, New York, from August 15 to 18, 1969. The organizers expected about 50,000 people. Estimates of the crowd run from 400,000 to more than 460,000, and no record names who counted. The fences were not finished, so the festival became free. Three people died. Two died from taking too much of a drug, and a 17-year-old was run over by a tractor while he slept.

The MTV television channel began on August 1, 1981, and played music videos. Its managers said it played rock, and they played few Black artists. In 1983 Walter Yetnikoff, president of CBS Records, threatened to pull all of CBS's videos unless MTV played Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean." MTV began playing it many times a day in March 1983. Compact disc players went on sale in Japan on October 1, 1982, and in the United States in March 1983.

In 1985 Tipper Gore, Susan Baker and two other women in Washington started a group called the Parents Music Resource Center. They wanted to warn parents about sex, violence and drugs in songs. On September 19, 1985, the Senate Commerce Committee held a hearing. The musicians Frank Zappa, Dee Snider and John Denver spoke against labels. On November 1, 1985, the heads of the record companies agreed to put warning labels on some albums. In 1990 they began using a standard "Parental Advisory" sticker. Managers at Walmart and some other stores would not sell albums with the sticker.

In 1978 members of Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act. In it they wrote that Native "ceremonies have been intruded upon, interfered with, and in a few instances banned." The 1883 rules against Native dances could be used until that year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="selena-quintanilla" name="Selena Quintanilla" movie="Selena (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Selena Quintanilla

> **Who:** A Mexican American singer of Tejano music, the Spanish-language music of Texas.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1971, in Lake Jackson, Texas. Killed March 31, 1995, in Corpus Christi, Texas, at 23.
> **Movie:** *Selena* (1997), a Hollywood drama film about her, with Jennifer Lopez.

Selena Quintanilla was a Mexican American singer of Tejano music, the Spanish-language music of Texas. She was killed at 23 by the woman who ran her fan club. She was born in Lake Jackson, Texas, on April 16, 1971.

She sang with her brother and sister in their father Abraham Quintanilla Jr.'s band, Selena y Los Dinos. She learned the Spanish words of her songs by their sound, with her father's help. She won the Tejano Music Award for Female Vocalist of the Year in 1987 and in each of the next nine years. In 1994 she won a Grammy Award for her album *Live!*

Yolanda Saldívar ran Selena's fan club and managed her clothing stores. Saldívar had secretly taken money from the business, more than $30,000 or more than $60,000 by different counts. Selena meant to fire her.

On March 31, 1995, at a Days Inn motel in Corpus Christi, Texas, Saldívar shot Selena with a revolver as Selena tried to leave the room. The bullet hit her lower right shoulder. Selena ran to the lobby and named Saldívar. She died at the hospital that afternoon from loss of blood. Saldívar gave herself up to the police more than nine hours later. She was convicted of murder and sentenced to life in prison. On March 27, 2025, members of the Texas parole board refused to let her out, and they set her next review for March 2030.

Between 30,000 and 40,000 people came to see Selena's coffin. Her album *Dreaming of You* came out after her death. It made her the first Latin artist whose album entered the national album chart at No. 1.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 2000 most Americans stopped buying music and began paying to stream it. Sales of recorded music in the United States peaked in 1999, when most people bought CDs. Then a free program called Napster let people copy songs from each other's computers. By the record companies' own count, sales fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015. By 2025, streaming brought in 82 cents of every dollar the record companies took in. A streaming service pays a fraction of a cent each time a song is played, and it pays the owner of the recording, not the singer. In a 2018 survey, most working musicians said they earned money from playing live. Dolly Parton, a songwriter from the mountains of Tennessee, died on August 25, 2026, at 80. In September 2026 record companies and musicians were in court against companies whose computer programs make songs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Napster, the iPod and streaming, 1999 to 2025" -->
Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker started Napster in June 1999. It let people copy music files from each other's computers for free. In December 1999 record companies sued. A federal judge ordered Napster to block copyrighted songs, and Napster could not do it. It shut down in July 2001. At its peak, in February 2001, it had about 26.4 million users.

On October 23, 2001, Apple's boss, Steve Jobs, showed the iPod, a pocket music player. He said it held "1,000 songs in your pocket." Apple's iTunes Store opened on April 28, 2003, and sold most songs for 99 cents each. By 2014 it had sold 35 billion songs.

Spotify, a company from Sweden, began streaming music in the United States in July 2011. Streaming means playing a song over the internet without buying a copy of it. By March 2026, the company said, Spotify had 300 million paying subscribers around the world.

The Recording Industry Association of America, or RIAA, is the record companies' trade group. It counts what the companies take in. Using its figures, the American Enterprise Institute reported in 2017 that sales peaked in 1999 at $20.9 billion, counted in 2016 dollars so that the rise in prices is taken out. Sales then fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015. In 2016 they were $7.57 billion. That year, for the first time, Americans paid more for streaming than for CDs and downloads. The RIAA reported in March 2026 that in 2025 the companies took in a record $11.5 billion at wholesale, the price the companies themselves are paid. Streaming brought in $9.5 billion of it. Americans also spent $1.04 billion on vinyl records in 2025.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a stream pays" -->
Spotify pays about 70 percent of the money it takes in to the owners of the music, by its own account. About 58.5 percent goes to whoever owns the recording, which is usually a record company. About 12 percent goes to whoever owns the song itself, the publisher and the songwriter. A singer who does not own the recording gets a share from the record company, under the singer's contract.

Spotify does not pay a set price for each play. It pays each owner by that owner's share of all the plays. In February 2025 a company that sends music to streaming services, iMusician, put the average at $0.003 to $0.005 a play, and about $0.004 for a listener in the United States. That is less than half a cent.

From April 1, 2024, Spotify stopped paying anything for a recording played fewer than 1,000 times in a year. Spotify wrote in November 2023 that tens of millions of such recordings had each earned about 3 cents a month. In March 2026 Spotify reported that it paid the music business more than $11 billion in 2025. By its count, more than 1,500 artists earned over $1 million from it that year. The artist ranked 100,000th earned about $7,300.

In March 2021 a musicians' group, the United Musicians and Allied Workers, held protests in 31 cities and asked Spotify to pay one cent a play. In 2018 a group called the Music Industry Research Association asked 1,227 American musicians about their work. Most earned money from playing live, and most could not live on music alone. Of the women who answered, 72 percent said they had been treated unfairly because they were women. The musicians who answered did not name the people who did it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zoe-keating" name="Zoë Keating" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Zoë Keating

> **Who:** A cellist and composer who sells her own recordings and publishes what streaming pays her.
> **When and where:** Born February 2, 1972, in Guelph, Ontario, Canada, to an English mother and an American father. Worked in San Francisco, California, and later Vermont.

Zoë Keating is a cellist who has published what streaming services paid her, so that other musicians could see real numbers. In September 2019 she posted: "206,011 Spotify streams. $753." That came to about a third of a cent for each play. The company that sent her music to Spotify took no cut.

She was born in Guelph, Ontario, in Canada, on February 2, 1972. She played cello in the band Rasputina from 2002 to 2006. Then she began recording alone. She uses a computer to record and repeat her own cello parts as she plays, so that one player sounds like many.

She has released her albums herself, without a record company. Her album *One Cello x 16: Natoma* reached No. 1 on the iTunes classical chart four times. In August 2012 she began publishing her income from sales and streaming.

In May 2014 doctors found that her husband, Jeff Rusch, had stage 4 cancer, cancer that has spread through the body. Their insurance company, Anthem, first refused to pay for his hospital stay. It changed its decision after local news reported the refusal. He died on February 19, 2015.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zoe-keating" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owns a recording: masters and songs" -->
Every recorded song has two copyrights. One covers the song itself, its words and tune. The other covers the master, the first recording, which every copy is made from. Whoever owns the master is paid for every copy and every stream of that recording. Record companies owned most masters. Ray Charles and Sam Cooke were among the few singers of the 1950s and 1960s who owned their own.

In December 2020 Bob Dylan sold the rights to all of his songs to Universal Music Publishing Group. The New York Times estimated the price at more than $300 million, and other reports said about $400 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="taylor-swift" name="Taylor Swift" movie="Miss Americana (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Taylor Swift

> **Who:** A songwriter and singer who recorded four of her first six albums again after a music manager bought the master recordings.
> **When and where:** Born December 13, 1989, in West Reading, Pennsylvania. Moved to Hendersonville, Tennessee, at 14.
> **Movie:** *Miss Americana* (2020), a documentary about her.

Taylor Swift recorded four of her albums a second time, so that she would own the new master recordings. She did it after a music manager bought the masters of her first six albums. She was born in West Reading, Pennsylvania, on December 13, 1989.

At 14 her family moved to Hendersonville, Tennessee. That year a Nashville music publisher signed her as a songwriter, the youngest it had ever signed. After a show at Nashville's Bluebird Cafe on November 3, 2004, she signed with Scott Borchetta's new company, Big Machine Records. Big Machine owned the masters of her first six albums.

On June 30, 2019, the music manager Scooter Braun bought Big Machine for about $330 million, and with it her masters. Swift said she had tried for years to buy them. She said she was offered them only if she signed up to make six more albums for the company. In October 2020 Braun sold the masters to Shamrock Holdings, a company owned by the family of Roy E. Disney, for a reported $405 million.

Swift recorded four of the albums again, and each came out as "Taylor's Version." *Fearless* came out on April 9, 2021, and *Red* on November 12, 2021. *Speak Now* followed on July 7, 2023, and *1989* on October 27, 2023.

Her Eras Tour ran from March 17, 2023, to December 8, 2024, with 149 shows. It took in $2.07 billion, more than any concert tour before it. On May 30, 2025, she said she had bought all six of the original masters from Shamrock. The price was not made public.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="taylor-swift" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Music Modernization Act, 2018" -->
In 2018 members of Congress passed the Music Modernization Act. The House passed it on April 25, the Senate passed it with no votes against on September 19, and President Donald Trump signed it on October 11. The law set up a nonprofit group, the Mechanical Licensing Collective, to keep a list of who owns each song and to pay songwriters for streams. In 2021 the group began paying out money that had been held back because no one knew whose songs had been played.

Recordings made before February 15, 1972, had no federal copyright, so internet radio services did not have to pay for playing them. Many rhythm and blues records of the 1950s and 1960s were made before that date. Under the 2018 law, the singers and musicians on those older records are paid when digital radio plays them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hip-hop, Latin music and country radio" -->
In 2017 hip-hop and R&B made up 24.5 percent of all the music Americans bought or streamed, more than any other kind. Rock came second, with just under 21 percent. Nielsen Music, a company that tracks sales and streams, made the count, and CNBC reported it in January 2018.

In 2025 Latin music brought in $1 billion, 8.8 percent of what the record companies took in across the United States. The RIAA reported in 2026 that it was Latin music's tenth record year in a row.

Jada Watson, a researcher, counts the songs played on country radio. She found that songs by women got 11 percent of the plays by 2022. Songs by Black women and other women of color got almost no plays in the daytime, she reported in 2023.

Beyoncé, a Black singer from Texas, sang her country song "Daddy Lessons" at the Country Music Association Awards on November 2, 2016. Some country fans said she did not belong there, and the association's staff deleted its online posts about her performance. Her country album *Cowboy Carter*, from 2024, made her the first Black woman with a No. 1 album on the country album chart. The Country Music Association gave it no nominations. On February 2, 2025, it won the Grammy Awards for Album of the Year and Best Country Album.

### Kendrick Lamar

> **Who:** A Black rapper and songwriter, the first musician outside classical music and jazz to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music.
> **When and where:** Born June 17, 1987, in Compton, California.

In 2018 Kendrick Lamar became the first musician outside classical music and jazz to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music. He was born in Compton, California, on June 17, 1987. His parents had moved there from the South Side of Chicago in 1984. When he was five, he saw a teenage drug dealer shot and killed outside his apartment. In seventh grade an English teacher, Regis Inge, taught him poetry.

He signed with a small Los Angeles label, Top Dawg Entertainment, in 2005. His 2015 album *To Pimp a Butterfly* used jazz, funk and soul. Protesters in the Black Lives Matter movement used his songs as anthems.

On April 16, 2018, his album *DAMN.* won the Pulitzer Prize for Music. The Pulitzer board called it "a virtuosic song collection unified by its vernacular authenticity and rhythmic dynamism." In plain words, the board praised his skill and the everyday speech and strong rhythm of his songs. The prize was $15,000.

On February 9, 2025, he performed at the Super Bowl halftime show. About 133.5 million people watched, more than had watched any halftime show before. By 2026 he had won 27 Grammy Awards, more than any other rapper.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaths at music festivals, 2017 and 2021" -->
On October 1, 2017, more than 22,000 people were at the last night of the Route 91 Harvest country music festival in Las Vegas, Nevada. At 10:05 p.m., while the country singer Jason Aldean was playing, Stephen Paddock, who was 64, began shooting into the crowd. He fired from his rooms on the 32nd floor of the Mandalay Bay hotel. In ten minutes he fired more than 1,000 rounds. He killed 58 people that night and wounded at least 413 with gunfire, and about 867 people were hurt in all. Two wounded women died later, in 2019 and 2020, so the official count is 60 dead. Paddock shot himself. Investigators never found out why he did it.

On November 5, 2021, about 50,000 people came to the Astroworld Festival in Houston, Texas. When the rapper Travis Scott, who had started the festival, came on stage, the crowd pushed toward him. Ten people died of compressive asphyxiation, which means they were squeezed so hard they could not breathe. Thousands were hurt. The youngest to die was a boy of 9 or 10. The reports differ. In June 2023 a Harris County grand jury decided not to charge Scott or five others, among them Brent Silberstein, a festival manager for the concert company Live Nation. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether someone should be charged with a crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Machines that make music" -->
Auto-Tune is a computer program that moves a singer's off-key notes onto the right pitch. The engineer Andy Hildebrand invented it, and his company put it on sale on September 19, 1997. In 1998 the singer Cher used it to make her voice sound like a machine. The rapper and singer T-Pain used it that way on his 2005 album *Rappa Ternt Sanga* and after. Kanye West used it on his 2008 album *808s & Heartbreak*.

On June 1, 2008, a worker at Universal Studios Hollywood used a blowtorch to heat roof shingles and left before they cooled. The fire that followed burned a video vault. In 2019 Jody Rosen, a writer for the New York Times Magazine, reported that the same fire had destroyed 118,000 to 175,000 of Universal Music Group's master tapes. He wrote that they might include records by Chuck Berry, Muddy Waters and John Coltrane. Universal's managers said the report held "numerous inaccuracies." They confirmed that the masters of one Soundgarden album had burned.

Companies such as Suno and Udio sell computer programs that make whole songs from a typed request. In June 2024 the largest record companies sued both of them. They said the companies had trained their programs on the record companies' recordings without permission. Universal settled with Udio in October 2025, and Warner settled with Suno in November 2025. Udio and Suno agreed to build new programs trained only on music they had permission to use. In September 2026 Universal and Sony were still suing Suno. In court papers, Suno admitted that it had downloaded audio from YouTube. On September 1, 2026, the songwriter Jason Isbell and three other musicians sued Suno on behalf of themselves and other artists.

In March 2025 a federal judge, Eumi Lee, turned down a request from music publishers. They had asked her to stop the company Anthropic from using song lyrics to train its computer program while their case went on. At the end of September 2026, no American court had given a final ruling on whether a company may train a song-making program on recordings without permission.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Kennedy Center, 2025 and 2026" -->
The Kennedy Center is a building for music, theater and dance in Washington, D.C., named for President John F. Kennedy. The National Symphony Orchestra plays there. In February 2025 its board chose President Donald Trump as its chairman. On December 18, 2025, the board voted to add his name to the building, CBS News reported.

Representative Joyce Beatty, a member of the board, went to court against the name change. A federal judge, Christopher Cooper, blocked the name change. Many performers canceled their shows at the center, saying it had become political, and ticket sales fell. On September 15, 2026, minutes after Judge Cooper again blocked the name change, the board voted to close the building, NPR reported. Trump wrote that repairs would not begin unless a higher court allowed his name. In a court filing, the center's executive director, Matt Floca, pointed to falling plaster and decay under the roof. He kept the building closed at least until September 30, 2026, the Washington radio station WTOP reported.

### Dolly Parton

> **Who:** A singer and songwriter from the Great Smoky Mountains of Tennessee who wrote more than 3,000 songs, owned the rights to them, and mailed free books to children.
> **When and where:** Born January 19, 1946, in a one-room cabin in Pittman Center, Sevier County, Tennessee. Moved to Nashville in 1964. Died August 25, 2026, in Nashville, at 80.
> **Movie:** *Dolly Parton: Here I Am* (2019), a documentary about her.

Dolly Parton wrote more than 3,000 songs and kept the rights to them all her life. She started a program that mails free books to children, one a month from birth to age five. She died of cancer in Nashville on August 25, 2026, at 80.

She was born on January 19, 1946, in a one-room cabin in Pittman Center, in Sevier County, Tennessee. She was the fourth of 12 children. Her father, Robert Lee Parton, farmed tobacco and could not read or write. He paid the doctor who delivered her with a sack of cornmeal. Her mother, Avie Lee Parton, sang old ballads to the children. From age six Dolly sang in her grandfather's church.

By ten she sang on a radio and television show in Knoxville. At 13 she made her first record and sang at the Grand Ole Opry. The day after she finished high school in 1964, she moved to Nashville.

In 1967 the country singer Porter Wagoner hired her for his weekly television show. Their duets sold well for six years. From 1969 Wagoner owned nearly half of the company that published her songs. She wrote her first No. 1 song, "Joshua," by herself, and it reached the top of the country chart in February 1971. That same year she recorded "Coat of Many Colors," about her family's poverty. In 1973 she wrote "Jolene" and "I Will Always Love You" within a few days of each other. She wrote "I Will Always Love You" as a goodbye to Wagoner, and she left his show in 1974.

That year Elvis Presley wanted to record "I Will Always Love You." His manager, Colonel Tom Parker, told her that the songwriter had to give Presley's side half of the publishing rights, the rights to the song itself. She said no. In 1992 Whitney Houston recorded the song, and her record sold more than 24 million copies around the world. Parton said later, "I made enough money to buy Graceland," Presley's home.

She wrote "9 to 5" for a 1980 film about three women office workers. It reached No. 1 on the country and pop charts in February 1981. She sold more than 100 million records, had at least 25 No. 1 country songs, and won 11 Grammy Awards. She turned down the Presidential Medal of Freedom three times.

In 1986 she became a partner in a theme park in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, and it was renamed Dollywood. It has about 4,000 to 4,500 workers, more than any other employer in Sevier County. From 2022 Dollywood paid college costs for workers who chose to go.

In 1995 she started the Imagination Library in Sevier County. The Imagination Library says she started it because her father could not read or write. The Imagination Library mails one free book a month to each child who signs up, from birth to age five. Her foundation and local partners pay for the books, and families pay nothing. By 2026 it had mailed more than 300 million books in five countries.

In the spring of 2020 she gave $1 million to Vanderbilt University Medical Center in Nashville for research on the coronavirus. She gave it in honor of her friend Dr. Naji Abumrad. A research paper on Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine listed her fund among its supporters. The US government put about $2.5 billion into that vaccine. After wildfires burned homes near Gatlinburg, Tennessee, in November 2016, her My People Fund paid $1,000 a month for six months to more than 900 families whose homes had burned, and $5,000 in the last month. After Hurricane Helene in 2024, she and her businesses gave $2 million.

Parton owned the rights to her songs all her life, the magazine Billboard reported in August 2026. Sony Music owns her recordings from her years with RCA, about 23 albums, and probably her recordings with Columbia. From 1995 she owned most of her new recordings, through her own record label.

Her husband, Carl Thomas Dean, whom she married in 1966, died on March 3, 2025. On August 21, 2026, she went into the Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center in Nashville. She died four days later. Her family did not say what kind of cancer she had. Her nephew Bryan Seaver, her head of security for more than 20 years, announced her death, as she had asked him to. That day Representative Tim Burchett of Tennessee said, "Today, Tennessee lost one of our greatest ambassadors in Dolly Parton." President Trump ordered flags at federal buildings flown at half-staff for a week. Her family held a private funeral on August 28, and she was buried beside her husband in Nashville. Her family asked people who wanted to honor her to support the Imagination Library.
