## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 29 minutes**

Record companies began to record and sell the blues, jazz and country music in these years, and the companies and the men who held the copyrights kept much of the money. Black musicians in the South made the blues and jazz. Country music came from Southern singers and players, white ones like the Carter Family and Black ones like DeFord Bailey and Lesley Riddle. Bessie Smith's first record sold 780,000 copies, and Columbia paid her a set fee and no share of the sales.

Americans spent $106 million on records in 1921. From the 1920s, anyone with a radio could hear music at home for free. Owners and managers of clubs, hotels and concert halls kept Black people out, even Black stars. Billie Holiday recorded "Strange Fruit" in 1939, a song about Black people murdered by white mobs. That same year Marian Anderson sang outdoors in Washington to more than 75,000 people, after the leaders of two indoor halls in the city refused her.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first written accounts of the blues, 1901 to 1903" -->
The first written account of the kind of songs later called the blues comes from Mississippi in 1901 and 1902. Charles Peabody, an archaeologist, dug into a Native mound in Coahoma County in May and June of both years for Harvard University's Peabody Museum. He hired nine to fifteen Black men from the town of Clarksdale to do the digging, and the men sang while they dug. Peabody wrote in 1903 that bosses sometimes hired a strong singer just to keep workers going.

The men's songs were about hard luck, love and pay day. In one line the singer said he had been arrested for murder though he had never harmed anyone. Peabody did not use the word blues.

**Tutwiler.** The bandleader W. C. Handy was waiting for a train at Tutwiler, Mississippi, in about 1903. A man beside him played a guitar by sliding a knife along the strings, and sang. No record names the man. Handy later published the song as "Yellow Dog Blues."

**"Father of the Blues."** Handy sold "The Memphis Blues" for $100 in 1912, and in 1914 he published "Saint Louis Blues." With Harry Pace, a Black banker, he ran a company called Pace and Handy that published music. Handy called himself the "Father of the Blues." Jelly Roll Morton disagreed. In a letter to the magazine *Down Beat*, he wrote that Handy had not started the music.

**"Mother of the Blues."** The singer Gertrude "Ma" Rainey said that in 1902 a girl in Missouri sang her a song about a man who had left a woman. Rainey began singing it in her shows. Paramount Records later advertised her as the "Mother of the Blues." Between 1923 and 1928 she made more than 100 records.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Records for Black buyers, and who was paid, 1920 to 1938" -->
Mamie Smith, a Black singer, recorded "Crazy Blues" for the Okeh record company on August 10, 1920. The songwriter Perry Bradford wrote it. Managers at Victor had turned Smith down after a try-out, so Bradford went to Fred Hager, who ran recording at Okeh. He told Hager that Smith could "do more with those songs than a monkey can do with a peanut."

Some white people had already warned Hager not to record Black musicians, and he recorded her anyway. Within two months, buyers had paid a dollar each for 75,000 copies. It was the first blues record to become a big hit.

**"Race records."** Record company managers then went looking for Black singers to sell records to Black buyers. By 1922 managers at Okeh were calling these records "race records." The Black businessman Harry Pace started Black Swan Records in 1921. Black Swan paid its singers fair wages and let them record under their own names. Paramount took over Black Swan in 1924.

**A set fee.** Columbia paid Bessie Smith a set fee for each side she recorded. A side was one song on one face of a record. She got no royalty, the small payment for every copy sold that lasts as long as the record sells. Columbia paid white singers more, and it paid them royalties. With a set fee, a singer earned the same whether the record sold a hundred copies or a million.

**Twenty-nine songs.** Robert Johnson, a Black guitarist from Mississippi, recorded 29 songs in 1936 and 1937, in a hotel room in San Antonio and in Dallas, both in Texas. His best seller, "Terraplane Blues," sold about 10,000 copies. His pay for the sessions is not on record. He died near Greenwood, Mississippi, on August 16, 1938, at 27. The cause of his death is not known. One story says he was poisoned, but no one has proved it.

Mississippi judges ruled in 1998 that his son, Claud Johnson, was his only heir, the one person with the right to what Johnson left. That right was worth more than $1 million in royalties.

The editors of *Billboard*, a music magazine, renamed its "race records" sales chart "rhythm and blues" in June 1949. A writer named Jerry Wexler suggested the new name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bessie-smith" name="Bessie Smith" movie="Bessie (2015)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bessie Smith

> **Who:** A Black blues singer whose first record sold 780,000 copies in six months.
> **When and where:** Born in Chattanooga, Tennessee, on April 15, 1894 (the 1900 government count of the people gives July 1892). Recorded for Columbia Records from 1923. Died near Clarksdale, Mississippi, on September 26, 1937.
> **Movie:** *Bessie* (2015), an HBO drama film, with Queen Latifah as Smith.

**Her first record sold 780,000 copies. She got $125 a side.**

Bessie Smith was born in Chattanooga, Tennessee. Her family kept April 15, 1894, as her birthday, but the 1900 census, the government's count of everyone in the country, gives July 1892. Both her parents died when she was a child. She and her brother Andrew sang and danced for coins on the streets of Chattanooga. In 1912 she joined a traveling show as a dancer, and Ma Rainey sang in the same show.

**The pay.** Frank Walker of Columbia Records signed her in 1923. Her first record, "Down Hearted Blues," sold 780,000 copies in six months. Her contract paid her $125 for each side that could be used. Later contracts paid $150 and then $200 a side, and none of them paid a royalty.

She recorded between 150 and 200 songs. Cary O'Dell's essay for the Library of Congress states 150 in one place and close to 200 in another. On tour she earned up to $2,000 a week, and she traveled in her own railroad car.

**The crash.** The car she rode in hit the back of a slow truck on Route 61 near Clarksdale, Mississippi, on September 26, 1937. Her partner, Richard Morgan, was driving. Her right arm was almost torn off at the elbow. A white doctor from Memphis, Hugh Smith, stopped to help. An ambulance took her to the G. T. Thomas Afro-American Hospital in Clarksdale. Doctors there amputated her arm, which means they cut it off, and she died that morning.

**A story that was not true.** The record producer John Hammond wrote in 1937 that she died because a white hospital refused to treat her. Dr. Smith said the ambulance would never have taken her to a white hospital. Her biographer, Chris Albertson, showed that Hammond's story was a myth.

More than 10,000 people came to her funeral in Philadelphia. Her grave had no stone for more than 30 years, until the singer Janis Joplin helped pay for one. Later Jack Gee Jr., the son of her husband, sued Columbia. The judges ruled against him because he had waited too long to sue. They did not decide whether she had been cheated.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bessie-smith" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jazz on record, 1917 to 1931" -->
Jazz began in the Black communities of New Orleans in the late 1800s and early 1900s, but white musicians made the first jazz record. Five white musicians from New Orleans recorded two songs in New York for the Victor company on February 26, 1917. They called themselves the Original Dixieland Jass Band. Victor's managers put the record on sale in May 1917, and by some counts it sold more than a million copies. The band's leader, Nick LaRocca, later claimed that he had invented jazz.

**A legend.** One story says that in 1915 Victor had asked a Black New Orleans player of the cornet, a brass horn like a small trumpet, to record first. The player was Freddie Keppard, and the story says he said no. The historian John Edward Hasse calls that story a legend.

**The Cotton Club.** Owney Madden, a gangster who sold illegal liquor, ran the Cotton Club in Harlem, New York. Madden's managers hired Black musicians and dancers, and they let in only white customers, apart from a few Black stars. The club's dancers had to be light-skinned, at least 5 feet 6 inches tall and under 21. Duke Ellington's band played there from December 4, 1927, to June 30, 1931. When Ellington asked, the people running the club let in a few more Black customers.

**By 1927:** Blues records by Black singers sold in the hundreds of thousands, and the first jazz record, made by white musicians, had sold a million copies by some counts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="louis-armstrong" name="Louis Armstrong" movie="Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Louis Armstrong

> **Who:** A Black trumpet player and singer from New Orleans who made jazz records with his Hot Five and Hot Seven.
> **When and where:** Born in New Orleans. Most sources give August 4, 1901. He often said July 4, 1900. Lived in Chicago and New York. Died in New York on July 6, 1971.
> **Movie:** *Louis Armstrong's Black & Blues* (2022), a documentary about him.

**"The government can go to hell," he told a reporter in 1957.**

Louis Armstrong learned to play the cornet in a home for Black boys in New Orleans, where a judge sent him after an arrest. On New Year's Eve 1912 he fired his stepfather's gun into the air. It was loaded with a blank, a shell with gunpowder but no bullet. Police arrested him, and the next day a judge sent him to the Colored Waif's Home, a reform home for Black boys. There a teacher, Peter Davis, taught him the cornet in the home's band.

**Hot Five, Hot Seven.** The bandleader King Oliver invited him to Chicago in 1922 to play second cornet in his band. From November 1925 Armstrong made records for Okeh with his own groups, the Hot Five and the Hot Seven.

**Little Rock.** A reporter in Grand Forks, North Dakota, asked Armstrong on September 17, 1957, about the fight over nine Black students entering Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. Armstrong said: "The way they are treating my people in the South, the government can go to hell." He called President Dwight Eisenhower "two faced" and said he had "no guts."

Staff at WBKJ, a radio station in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, threw out his records. Officials at the University of Arkansas cancelled his invitation to play a dance. The congressman Adam Clayton Powell said Armstrong "didn't know what he was talking about." On September 24 Eisenhower sent soldiers to protect the students. Armstrong then sent him a telegram: "If you decide to walk into the schools with the little colored kids, take me along Daddy. God bless you."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="louis-armstrong" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music on record: Bristol, 1927" -->
Ralph Peer of the Victor record company recorded 76 songs by 19 singers and groups in Bristol, Tennessee, from July 25 to August 5, 1927. He set up on the third floor of a hat and glove company's building. Each performer got $50 in cash for each side, plus 2½ cents for each record sold. The Carter Family recorded on August 1 and 2, and a singer named Jimmie Rodgers on August 4.

**What Peer kept.** Peer took no salary from Victor. Instead he took a royalty of one cent on each record side, which he split with the singer. He also kept the copyright of the songs he recorded, including old songs passed down from singer to singer, and with it the right to be paid whenever those songs were printed, recorded or played.

Peer started a company in 1928, Southern Music Publishing, to collect that money. He offered to publish the songs that A. P. Carter of the Carter Family had gathered, if Carter would claim that he wrote them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maybelle-carter" name="Maybelle Carter" movie="The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maybelle Carter

> **Who:** Guitarist and singer in the Carter Family, who changed how the guitar is played in country music.
> **When and where:** Born May 10, 1909, in Nickelsville, Virginia. Recorded at Bristol, Tennessee, in 1927, at 18. Died October 23, 1978, in Hendersonville, Tennessee.
> **Movie:** *The Carter Family: Will the Circle Be Unbroken* (2005), a PBS *American Experience* documentary about the family.

**Her thumb played the tune, and one finger kept the rhythm.**

Maybelle Carter found a way to play the guitar that made it a lead instrument in country music. She picked out the tune with her thumb on the three low strings and strummed the three high strings with her index finger to keep the rhythm. It is called the thumb-lead style. She first recorded "Wildwood Flower," played this way, in 1928.

She sang and played with Sara Carter and A. P. Carter as the Carter Family. At Bristol in August 1927 she was 18. The Carter Family was paid Victor's rate of $50 a side and 2½ cents for each record sold.

**Lesley Riddle.** A. P. Carter went looking for old songs from December 1928 with Lesley Riddle, a Black guitarist from Burnsville, North Carolina. Riddle had lost his right leg at the knee in 1927 after an accident at a cement plant. On their trips, Carter wrote down the words while Riddle memorized the tunes. Riddle lived with the Carters for a time and taught Maybelle his finger-picking blues guitar style. Maybelle said she learned guitar runs from him, including the ones she played in "Cannonball Blues."

No record shows that Riddle was paid, or named as a writer, for any of the songs he helped gather.

Later Maybelle led a group with her daughters, called Mother Maybelle and the Carter Sisters. From 1968 she toured with her son-in-law, the singer Johnny Cash.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maybelle-carter" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did the Grand Ole Opry get its name?" -->
George D. Hay named the Grand Ole Opry on the air on December 10, 1927, right after a Black harmonica player, DeFord Bailey, played his "Pan American Blues." Hay had started the show on November 28, 1925, on WSM, a Nashville radio station owned by the National Life and Accident Insurance Company. He first called it the *WSM Barn Dance*. The first player was a 77-year-old fiddler, Uncle Jimmy Thompson.

That December night the *Barn Dance* came on the air after a program of opera music. After Bailey played, Hay told listeners, "from now on, we will present 'The Grand Ole Opry.'" One hour of the show went out across the country on the NBC radio network from 1939 to 1956.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="deford-bailey" name="DeFord Bailey" movie="DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost (2002)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### DeFord Bailey

> **Who:** A Black harmonica player, for fifteen years one of the best-loved stars of the Grand Ole Opry.
> **When and where:** Born December 14, 1899, in Smith County, Tennessee. Played on Nashville radio from 1926 to 1941. Died July 2, 1982.
> **Movie:** *DeFord Bailey: A Legend Lost* (2002), a Nashville Public Television documentary about him.

**White stars got him into hotels by the fire escape.**

DeFord Bailey's family in Smith County, Tennessee, played what he called "Black hillbilly music." He caught polio at three. The virus that causes polio can damage the nerves that move the muscles, and it stunted Bailey's growth and left his back bent.

**The pay.** He played on the *WSM Barn Dance* from June 1926. Accounts of his pay differ. One says he got a fixed $5 at first, less than the white players he toured with. The Country Music Hall of Fame says he was one of the best-paid stars on the show.

**On tour.** He toured the South with white stars such as Roy Acuff, Bill Monroe and Uncle Dave Macon. Hotel and restaurant owners would not serve him because he was Black. The other players brought him food, or got him into hotels by the fire escape.

**Fired.** The managers of most radio stations stopped playing songs licensed by ASCAP, a group that collected money for songwriters, during a fight over fees in 1941. Managers at WSM told Bailey not to play his ASCAP songs, which were some of his most popular ones. They fired him at the end of 1941.

**"Lazy."** Hay wrote a book about the show in 1945. In it he wrote, "Like some members of his race and other races, DeFord was lazy." Hay added that Bailey knew about a dozen tunes and "refused to learn more."

The historian Charles Wolfe reported in 1999 that Bailey remembered it the other way around. Bailey said he wanted to play new tunes, but Hay and the listeners kept asking for the same ones. Bailey also said that Hay had treated him kindly and had written those words to please his bosses.

Bailey ran a shoeshine stand and rented out rooms after 1941. He almost never played in public again until 1974. In 2005 he was named to the Country Music Hall of Fame.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="deford-bailey" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Radio, the Depression and two fights over pay, 1906 to 1944" -->
John Philip Sousa wrote in 1906 that records and player pianos were becoming a "substitute for human skill, intelligence and soul." He called records "canned music." Members of Congress passed a copyright law in 1909 that let anyone record a song without asking, as long as they paid the song's owner a set fee.

**Sales up and down.** Americans spent $106 million on records in 1921. A history of records from the University of Michigan Press says they did not spend that much again until 1945. IASA, a group of sound archivists, says sales did not reach their old peak until the 1950s. During the Great Depression, in the early 1930s, record sales fell to about one tenth of what they had been in the 1920s.

**ASCAP and BMI.** Radio station owners paid ASCAP a fee for the right to play songs by its songwriters, and ASCAP passed the money on to the writers. When ASCAP's leaders announced a big rise in fees in 1939, radio station owners started a group of their own, BMI, so they would have songs to play without paying ASCAP.

Managers at almost every station, and at all three national networks, stopped playing ASCAP songs in 1941. ASCAP's leaders had not wanted writers of blues, gospel, country, folk and Latin music, and BMI became the first such group to sign them.

**The record ban.** James Petrillo, the head of the musicians' union, told its members to stop making records on August 1, 1942. He wanted musicians to be paid when radio stations and jukeboxes played records instead of hiring live bands. Decca's managers signed an agreement in September 1943 to pay a fee on every record into a union fund. Victor and Columbia signed on November 11, 1944. Union rules still let singers record with voices alone, so for two years singers made records without bands.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gospel, shape notes and prison recordings" -->
Gospel is a kind of Black church music. Thomas A. Dorsey had played piano for Ma Rainey under the name "Georgia Tom." His wife, Nettie, and their newborn son died in August 1932, and he wrote "Take My Hand, Precious Lord." That year he started the first Black-owned company to publish gospel music, and in 1933 a national convention of gospel choirs. He led the music at Pilgrim Baptist Church in Chicago for fifty years. "I'm not ashamed of my blues," he said. "It's all the same talent."

**Mahalia Jackson.** She came to Chicago from New Orleans in 1927, and Dorsey trained her. She refused to sing the blues. Managers at Decca offered to keep recording her if she sang blues, and she refused. Her "Move On Up a Little Higher" of 1947 sold about two million copies.

**The Colored Sacred Harp.** Judge Jackson, a shape-note singer from Alabama, printed *The Colored Sacred Harp* in 1934. Judge was his first name, and "Colored" in the title was a word of that time for Black people. It was a book of 77 church songs printed in shape notes, notes whose shapes tell the singer which sound to sing. Eighteen of the songs were his own. He and Bishop J. D. Walker paid to print 1,000 copies, and Jackson sold them door to door.

**Lead Belly.** John Lomax and his son Alan recorded singers in Southern prisons from June 1933. John Lomax said he wanted music "not yet influenced by jazz and the radio." From 1934 he ran the Library of Congress's folk song collection, for a salary of one dollar. At Angola, the Louisiana state prison, they recorded Huddie Ledbetter, called Lead Belly. He was serving time for stabbing a man in a fight.

Years before, Lead Belly had been convicted in Texas of killing a relative, Will Stafford. He was in prison there from 1918 until the governor of Texas, Pat Neff, pardoned him in 1925, after Lead Belly sang him a song asking to be freed. He left Angola on August 1, 1934. A prison official later wrote that his singing had nothing to do with that release.

**"Goodnight, Irene."** John Lomax then managed Lead Belly and took a share of his pay. The two men split over money in 1935. Lomax published a book in 1936 that printed Lead Belly's song "Goodnight, Irene" with both men named as its writers. Lomax had recorded the song. He had not written it. A group called the Weavers sold 2 million copies of "Goodnight, Irene" in 1950, the year after Lead Belly died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Carlisle band and Native songs on wax" -->
The band of the Carlisle Indian School, a boarding school for Native children in Pennsylvania, played the "Aboriginal Suite" at Carnegie Hall in New York on March 28, 1900. Its composer and conductor was Dennison Wheelock, an Oneida graduate of the school. Richard Henry Pratt had made him the Carlisle bandmaster in 1892, the first Native bandmaster there. Wheelock later became a lawyer and argued cases for Native nations before the Supreme Court.

**Songs on wax.** Scientists recorded thousands of Native songs in these years on wax cylinders, tubes of wax that each held two or three minutes of sound. The Library of Congress holds about 10,000 cylinders, and nearly 8,000 of them hold Native songs and speech. Many held sacred songs meant only for ceremonies.

One collector, Francis La Flesche, took singers from Oklahoma to Washington, D.C., so that neighbors who objected could not stop him from recording ceremonial songs. No record shows that the singers were paid. Frances Densmore recorded the songs of the Ojibwe, Sioux, Mandan, Hidatsa, Tohono O'odham, Seminole and other nations for the Smithsonian Institution from 1907.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hawaiian steel guitar" -->
Joseph Kekuku, a Hawaiian student at the Kamehameha School for Boys, found in 1889 that he could change a guitar's pitch by sliding a steel bar along the strings. From 1904 he played his steel guitar in theaters across the mainland United States.

By then Hawaii belonged to the United States. A group of mostly American businessmen had overthrown Hawaii's queen, Liliʻuokalani, in 1893. Before they moved, John L. Stevens, the top US official in Hawaii, had 162 American sailors and Marines brought ashore. Native Hawaiians put 21,269 signatures on petitions, written requests to the government, against joining the United States in 1897. Members of Congress voted to annex Hawaii anyway in 1898, making it part of the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Irving Berlin, who owned his songs" -->
Irving Berlin, born Israel Beilin, came to New York from the Russian Empire in 1893, at age five. He could not read music and could play the piano in only one key, and he wrote "Alexander's Ragtime Band" in 1911. Through his own publishing company he owned his own songs. He gave all the money from "God Bless America," which Kate Smith first sang in 1938, to the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts.

**By 1939:** Anyone with a radio could hear music at home for free, and record sales were still below their 1921 peak. That year one hour of the Grand Ole Opry began going out across the country on NBC.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="woody-guthrie" name="Woody Guthrie" movie="Bound for Glory (1976)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Woody Guthrie

> **Who:** A folk singer and songwriter who wrote about poor farm families, workers and migrants in the 1930s and 1940s.
> **When and where:** Born July 14, 1912, in Okemah, Oklahoma. Lived in Oklahoma, California and New York. Died October 3, 1967, in New York.
> **Movie:** *Bound for Glory* (1976), a Hollywood film based loosely on his own book about his life.

**On his guitar he wrote "This machine kills fascists."**

Woody Guthrie wrote "This Land Is Your Land" and many other songs about poor farm families and workers. His mother, Nora, had Huntington's disease and was put in a hospital. No account names who sent her there. Huntington's is a disease passed down in families that destroys parts of the brain. A person with it makes movements they cannot control, has changes in mood, and loses the ability to think.

**The Dust Bowl.** In the 1930s Guthrie joined the farm families who left the Great Plains for California after years of drought, the time called the Dust Bowl. He sang on a Los Angeles radio station, KFVD. In 1940 he made an album called *Dust Bowl Ballads* for Victor.

**An answer to Berlin.** He wrote "This Land Is Your Land" in a New York hotel on February 23, 1940, as an answer to Irving Berlin's "God Bless America," which Kate Smith sang on the radio. His first title was "God Blessed America for Me." His handwritten copy had two verses that were often left out later. One is about a sign that says "No Trespassing." The other is about hungry people at a relief office, a place that gave out food or money to the poor.

**Twenty-six songs in a month.** Paul Raver, the head of a federal agency called the Bonneville Power Administration, hired him for one month in May 1941 for $266. Guthrie's boss there, Stephen Kahn, wanted three pages of songs a day for a film about the agency's dams on the Columbia River. Guthrie wrote 26 songs in that month. One of them was "Roll On, Columbia." Kahn later said the government had never gotten "a better investment on its money."

**His last years.** The fascists of his guitar's slogan were the followers of the dictators who ruled Italy and Germany. In the Second World War he served in the Merchant Marine, the ships that carried supplies. Doctors found in 1952 that he had Huntington's disease. He spent his last years in hospitals in New Jersey and New York, and he died at Creedmoor hospital in New York in 1967, at 55.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="woody-guthrie" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Strange Fruit, 1939" -->
"Strange Fruit" is a song about the lynching of Black Americans. Lynching means a mob killing a person without a trial, often by hanging. The song describes the bodies of Black people hanging from trees in the South: their bulging eyes and twisted mouths, and the smell of burning flesh.

Abel Meeropol, an English teacher in the Bronx, New York, wrote the song under the name Lewis Allan. He wrote it after he saw a photograph of the lynching of Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith in Marion, Indiana.

**Marion, Indiana.** A white mob of between 5,000 and 10,000 people broke into the jail in Marion on August 7, 1930. The mob wanted three young Black men held there for the murder of a white man, Claude Deeter, and the rape of a woman, Mary Ball. Rape means forcing a person to have sex. Men in the mob beat Shipp and Smith, who were both 19, and hanged them. People in the crowd took parts of their bodies as souvenirs.

**James Cameron.** The third prisoner, James Cameron, who was 16, was beaten but lived, after someone in the crowd said he was innocent. Cameron waited about a year in jail for his trial, and accounts of what Mary Ball said there differ. The historian James H. Madison writes that she said she had been raped but that the night was too dark to tell whether Cameron was one of the men. Fran Kaplan of America's Black Holocaust Museum writes that Ball testified she had not been raped at all.

Cameron was convicted on a lesser charge, of helping before the crime, and a judge sentenced him to 2 to 21 years. He served four years in prison. The governor of Indiana pardoned him in 1993.

**The trials.** Leaders of the NAACP, a group that fights for the rights of Black Americans, named 27 people they suspected of the lynching. All-white juries found the mob's leaders not guilty.

**Café Society.** Billie Holiday first sang "Strange Fruit" at Café Society, a club in New York, in 1939, and she closed her show with it. The waiters stopped serving, the room went dark except for a light on her face, and she sang no more songs after it. Managers at Columbia would not record it. John Hammond of Columbia said record stores and radio stations in the South would object. Milt Gabler of Commodore Records recorded it on April 20, 1939. It became her best-selling record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billie-holiday" name="Billie Holiday" movie="Billie (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Billie Holiday

> **Who:** A Black jazz singer who recorded "Strange Fruit," a song about lynching, in 1939.
> **When and where:** Born Eleanora Fagan on April 7, 1915, in Philadelphia. Grew up in Baltimore. Sang in New York. Died July 17, 1959, in New York.
> **Movie:** *Billie* (2019), a documentary about her.

**Federal agents handcuffed her to her hospital bed as she was dying.**

Billie Holiday was a Black jazz singer whom federal drug agents arrested three times, the last time in her hospital bed. She was born Eleanora Fagan in Philadelphia and grew up in Baltimore.

At nine she was brought before a juvenile court, a court for children, for missing school. The judge sent her to the House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school for girls, where courts sent children who were in trouble. As a young girl she ran errands in a brothel, a house where men paid women for sex, and she scrubbed steps and floors in the homes of her neighborhood.

**The service elevator.** She sang with Artie Shaw's band in 1938, and its other members were white. At the Lincoln Hotel in New York, the managers made her use the service elevator because white guests complained. She left the band soon after.

**Anslinger's agents.** Harry Anslinger ran the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, the government's drug police, from 1930 to 1962. Federal agents arrested Holiday on May 16, 1947, for having narcotics, illegal drugs, in her New York apartment. She pleaded guilty, and a judge sent her to the Alderson Federal Prison Camp in West Virginia for a year. She said later: "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt."

Because of the conviction, she lost her New York City cabaret card, the police license a performer needed to work in any club that sold alcohol. After that she sang in concert halls and theaters. A federal agent named George White, a different man from the Fisk Jubilee Singers' leader, arrested her again in a San Francisco hotel in January 1949.

**Disputed:** No source before 2015 says that Anslinger's agents went after her because she sang "Strange Fruit," the jazz writer Lewis Porter points out. The writer Johann Hari wrote in 2015 that they did, from 1939.

**Her last arrest.** She was in Metropolitan Hospital in New York with liver and heart disease in 1959. Federal narcotics agents arrested her in her hospital bed on May 31 and handcuffed her to it. Writing in *TIME* in 2019, John Halpern and David Blistein said that Anslinger ordered the arrest. Police guarded her room until a judge ordered the handcuffs taken off. She died there on July 17, 1959, at 44, with 70 cents in the bank. Her liver was scarred, a disease called cirrhosis, and her heart failed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="billie-holiday" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Marian Anderson at the Lincoln Memorial, 1939" -->
Officials of Howard University in Washington, D.C., asked in January 1939 to rent Constitution Hall for an Easter concert by the Black singer Marian Anderson. The hall belonged to the Daughters of the American Revolution, or DAR, a women's group. Its leaders refused. DAR leaders had let only white performers use the hall since 1932. Frank Ballou, the head of Washington's public schools, also refused to let her use the hall of Central High School, a white school, and the members of the school board backed him.

**The Lincoln Memorial.** First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt quit the DAR on February 26, 1939. She wrote that its leaders had "an opportunity to lead in an enlightened way" and had "failed." Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, then set up a concert on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. Anderson sang there on Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, to more than 75,000 people, and millions more heard her on the radio. She began with "My Country, 'Tis of Thee."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marian-anderson" name="Marian Anderson" movie="Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert (1939)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Marian Anderson

> **Who:** A Black contralto, a singer with a low woman's voice, who sang at the Lincoln Memorial in 1939 and was the first Black American singer at the Metropolitan Opera.
> **When and where:** Born February 27, 1897, in Philadelphia. Died April 8, 1993, in Portland, Oregon.
> **Movie:** *Marian Anderson: The Lincoln Memorial Concert* (1939), a short film of the concert.

**Toscanini told her she had "a voice heard once in a hundred years."**

As a young woman, Marian Anderson applied to a music school in Philadelphia. The school's officials turned her away because they took only white students. In Salzburg, Austria, in 1935, the conductor Arturo Toscanini told her she had "a voice heard once in a hundred years."

**Constitution Hall.** The DAR's president in 1939, when its leaders kept Anderson out of the hall, was Sarah Corbin Robert.

**The Met.** She sang at the Metropolitan Opera on January 7, 1955, at 57, in the part of Ulrica, a fortune teller, in Verdi's opera *Un ballo in maschera*. She sang at the March on Washington in August 1963. That year she was given the Presidential Medal of Freedom, an award given by the president.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 22 minutes**

Black musicians made rock and roll, soul and hip-hop. White singers then sold more copies of some of those songs than the Black singers who recorded them first. Record company owners, managers and publishers kept much of the money from songs such as "Hound Dog" and "That's All Right."

In Detroit, Berry Gordy built Motown, which became the largest Black-owned business on one magazine's list. Civil rights marchers sang old church songs with new words at their mass meetings.

A teenage DJ and his sister held a party in the Bronx, in New York City, in 1973 that people now name as the start of hip-hop. Senators held a hearing on the words of songs in 1985, and leaders of the RIAA, the record companies' own group, agreed to put warning labels on some albums.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rock and roll, and who got paid for it" -->
Sister Rosetta Tharpe, a Black gospel singer from Arkansas, played electric guitar in church music from the late 1930s. Her "Strange Things Happening Every Day," from 1944, was the first gospel record to reach the rhythm and blues sales chart. Some scholars call it the first rock and roll record. She was an influence on Elvis Presley, Little Richard, Chuck Berry and Johnny Cash.

**Credit taken.** Fats Domino's "The Fat Man," from 1949, had sold a million copies by 1951. Chuck Berry's "Maybellene," from 1955, sold more than a million. When Berry first saw his record, it listed Alan Freed, a disc jockey who played records on the radio, and another man as its co-writers. That gave them a right to part of the money the song earned its writers. Berry later won back full credit.

**Cover versions.** White singers made cover versions of songs by Black singers, new recordings of songs that someone else had already recorded. Georgia Gibbs, a white singer, copied LaVern Baker's "Tweedle Dee" note for note in 1955. Baker's record reached No. 14 on the national pop chart, and Gibbs's copy reached No. 1.

Baker tried to sue Gibbs for $250,000. She asked Congress to treat such copies as a breach of copyright. Representative Charles Diggs led an investigation by Congress into what he called "song theft." No law came of it.

Pat Boone, a white singer, covered Fats Domino's "Ain't That a Shame" in 1955. It was Boone's first No. 1 record. Domino and his co-writer, Dave Bartholomew, still earned writers' royalties from it. Boone also covered Little Richard's "Tutti Frutti," and Boone's record ranked higher on the national pop chart than Little Richard's own.

**Little Richard's lawsuit.** Little Richard later said that the people running his record company, Specialty Records, had cut his royalty rate without telling him. He sued the company and its owner, Art Rupe, in 1984 for $112 million in royalties he said he was owed. They settled out of court in 1986, which means they reached an agreement and the case never went to trial.

**Payola.** Members of a House committee, led by Representative Oren Harris, held hearings in 1959 and 1960 on payola, secret payments to a disc jockey or a radio station to play a record. Alan Freed refused to sign a statement that he had taken no bribes, and the managers of the radio station WABC fired him in November 1959.

Members of Congress made payola a crime in 1960, punished by up to a year in jail. Freed told a court in 1962 that he was guilty of taking bribes and paid a $300 fine. The older song publishers in ASCAP pushed for the hearings, partly because they opposed rock and roll.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hound Dog: Big Mama Thornton and Elvis Presley" -->
The songwriters Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller wrote "Hound Dog." The chapter opening tells the start of this story: Big Mama Thornton's 1952 record, Don Robey's name on the copyright form, and her one check for $500. Robey's Peacock Records put her record out in 1953, and it stayed at No. 1 on the rhythm and blues chart for seven weeks. Robey guessed that it sold 500,000 to 750,000 copies. Thornton said, "That song sold over two million records."

**Presley's record.** Elvis Presley recorded "Hound Dog" for RCA on July 2, 1956. His record was No. 1 on the pop chart for 11 weeks. He followed a reworked version by Freddie Bell and the Bellboys, a band he had heard in Las Vegas that year. Leiber and Stoller earned large royalties only after Presley's record. Thornton died of heart and liver disease in 1984. She was buried in a grave shared with two other people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="elvis-presley" name="Elvis Presley" movie="Elvis (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Elvis Presley

> **Who:** A white rock and roll singer from Mississippi and Tennessee whose records sold an estimated 500 million copies.
> **When and where:** Born January 8, 1935, in Tupelo, Mississippi. Moved to Memphis, Tennessee, in 1948. Died August 16, 1977, at his Memphis home, Graceland, at 42.
> **Movie:** *Elvis* (2022), a Hollywood drama film about him and his manager.

**His manager took 25 percent of his pay, and later 50.**

Elvis Presley recorded a Black singer's blues song at Sun Records in 1954, and his "Hound Dog" followed a song that had been a hit for a Black singer first. He first heard music at his family's church, an Assembly of God church. In Memphis he listened to Black blues and rhythm and blues singers on the radio.

**Sun Records.** Sam Phillips owned Sun Records in Memphis and recorded Black blues musicians such as Howlin' Wolf and B. B. King. His office manager, Marion Keisker, said he often told her: "If I could find a white man who had the Negro sound and the Negro feel, I could make a billion dollars." "Negro" was a word of that time for Black people. Presley sang "That's All Right," a blues song by the Black singer Arthur Crudup, at Sun on the night of July 5, 1954, and Phillips recorded it.

**Arthur Crudup.** Crudup had recorded the song for RCA in 1946. He said later: "I realised I was making everybody rich, and here I was poor." In 1968 a publisher backed out of a $60,000 settlement with him at the last moment. In 1971 a songwriters' group got him more than $10,000 in royalties he had not been paid. He died in 1974.

**Colonel Parker's share.** Phillips and Presley's manager, Colonel Tom Parker, sold Presley's contract to RCA for $40,000 in November 1955. Parker took 25 percent of Presley's pay. Presley agreed in 1967 to raise Parker's share to 50 percent of some of his income. Parker sold RCA the rights to all of Presley's old records in 1973 for $5.4 million. Because of that sale, Presley's family got no royalties on those records after he died.

Blanchard Tual, a lawyer whom a judge had appointed, called the 50 percent share "extortionate" in 1981, meaning unfairly high. He called the 1973 sale "unethical and poorly handled." Presley served in the Army from 1958 to 1960. His record sales are estimated at about 500 million copies around the world. He died at Graceland on August 16, 1977, at 42.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elvis-presley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Motown, soul and gospel" -->
Berry Gordy Jr. of Detroit started a record company on January 12, 1959, with an $800 loan from his family's savings fund. It was later called Motown. From 1960 to 1969, 79 Motown songs reached the top ten of the national pop chart. The editors of *Black Enterprise* magazine began ranking the largest Black-owned businesses in 1973. Motown was first on that list, with $40 million in sales, and it stayed first until 1983. Gordy sold Motown in 1988 for $61 million.

**The Funk Brothers.** A group of Detroit studio musicians, later called the Funk Brothers, played on most Motown hits from 1959 to 1972. No Motown record listed their names until Marvin Gaye's album *What's Going On* in 1971. Gordy fined any of them he caught recording for other companies.

**Ray Charles.** He had been blind since he was about seven. In "I Got a Woman," from 1954, he mixed gospel with the blues, and some church people objected. He refused to play a dance in Augusta, Georgia, in 1961 when he learned that the audience would be split by race. The promoter sued him, and in 1962 a judge fined him $757. His 1959 contract with ABC-Paramount promised that he would own his master recordings, the original tapes that every copy is made from, in time.

**Sam Cooke.** A singer from Mississippi who had led the gospel group the Soul Stirrers, Cooke started his own record company and his own company to publish his songs. From 1963 his own company owned his master recordings, and he was among the first Black artists to control his own business.

A Los Angeles motel manager, Bertha Franklin, shot and killed him on December 11, 1964. A coroner's jury, a panel that decides how a person died, called it justifiable homicide, a killing the law excuses. His family has disputed that account.

**James Brown.** Brown paid for the recording of his 1963 album *Live at the Apollo* himself. Martin Luther King Jr. was shot and killed on April 4, 1968, and James Earl Ray later pleaded guilty to his murder. The next day, Brown gave a free concert in Boston that was shown on television. His song "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud" came out that August.

**By 1970:** Motown had put 79 songs in the pop top ten, Sam Cooke's own company owned his master recordings, and Ray Charles had a contract that promised him his.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="aretha-franklin" name="Aretha Franklin" movie="Amazing Grace (2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Aretha Franklin

> **Who:** A Black singer who recorded "Respect" in 1967.
> **When and where:** Born March 25, 1942, in Memphis, Tennessee. Grew up in Detroit, Michigan. Died there August 16, 2018.
> **Movie:** *Amazing Grace* (2018), a documentary film of her 1972 gospel concert in a Los Angeles church.

**She spelled it out: R-E-S-P-E-C-T.**

Aretha Franklin took Otis Redding's song "Respect" and turned it into a woman's demand, and it went to No. 1 on the pop chart in 1967. Her father, the Reverend C. L. Franklin, led New Bethel Baptist Church in Detroit, and she sang there as a child. At 14 she recorded a gospel album, *Songs of Faith*. She had her first child at 12 and her second at 15.

She recorded for Columbia from 1960 to 1966 and had few hits. Jerry Wexler then brought her to Atlantic Records. In January 1967 she recorded for one day at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, before a fight broke out in the studio.

**"Respect."** Redding had written and recorded "Respect" in 1965. In his song a man asks for respect when he comes home. Franklin recorded it in New York on February 14, 1967. She and her sister Carolyn made it a woman's demand. They spelled out the word "R-E-S-P-E-C-T" and added new lines.

It was No. 1 on the pop chart for two weeks and on the rhythm and blues chart for eight. It became a song of the women's movement and of the civil rights movement. Redding called it the song "that a girl took away from me."

**Angela Davis.** Franklin spoke up in 1970 for Angela Davis, a Black activist whom FBI agents had arrested that October. Davis was charged with kidnapping and with the murder of Judge Harold Haley, who was killed in an attack on a California courthouse. Franklin said, "Angela Davis must go free." An all-white jury found Davis not guilty in June 1972.

**Amazing Grace.** Franklin recorded the gospel album *Amazing Grace* at New Temple Missionary Baptist Church in Los Angeles in January 1972. It sold more than two million copies. The film made at the same time was not shown until 2018, because the sound and the pictures could not be matched. In 1987 she became the first woman named to the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. She died in Detroit on August 16, 2018.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="aretha-franklin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where did 'We Shall Overcome' come from?" -->
"We Shall Overcome" started as a church hymn, "I'll Overcome Some Day," that Charles Albert Tindley wrote in 1901. Tobacco workers went on strike at a cigar factory in Charleston, South Carolina, in 1945 and 1946. One of them, Lucille Simmons, led the strikers in singing "We Will Overcome." Zilphia Horton of the Highlander Folk School learned it from her and printed it in 1947.

The folk singer Pete Seeger changed "will" to "shall," because, he said, "'We shall' opens the mouth wider." From 1959 Guy Carawan taught the song to young civil rights workers.

**The March on Washington.** Joan Baez led "We Shall Overcome" at the March on Washington on August 28, 1963. Mahalia Jackson, Marian Anderson, Odetta, Bob Dylan, and Peter, Paul and Mary sang there too. During Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech, Jackson called out to him, "Tell them about the dream, Martin!"

**Selma.** About 600 marchers set out from Selma, Alabama, on March 7, 1965. At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, Alabama state troopers and other officers, led by Major John Cloud, attacked them with nightsticks and tear gas. At least 50 marchers went to the hospital. President Lyndon Johnson spoke to Congress on March 15 and used the song's words: "And we shall overcome." In January 2018, after a court case, the owners of the song's copyright gave it up. Since then anyone may use it freely.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" name="Bernice Johnson Reagon" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bernice Johnson Reagon

> **Who:** A Black student in Albany, Georgia, who led singing at civil rights meetings and sang with the SNCC Freedom Singers.
> **When and where:** Born October 4, 1942, in Albany, Georgia. Died July 16, 2024.

**"So instead I put in freedom," she said of an old song.**

Bernice Johnson Reagon led the singing at civil rights meetings in Albany, Georgia, and changed the words of old spirituals to fit the protests. She joined the NAACP Youth Council in Albany. Officials at Albany State College suspended her for her part in the protests.

At mass meetings she took old spirituals and put new words in them. Of one song she said: "So instead I put in freedom and by the second line everyone was singing."

**The Freedom Singers.** She joined the Freedom Singers of SNCC, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, with Cordell Reagon, Rutha Mae Harris and Chuck Neblett. She married Cordell Reagon, who had started the group. The group traveled 50,000 miles in nine months to sing about the movement, without taking a single flight, and its first tour raised nearly $50,000 for SNCC. "There weren't soloists, there were song leaders," she said. She called the group "a singing newspaper."

**Sweet Honey in the Rock.** She started Sweet Honey in the Rock in 1973, a group of African American women who sing without instruments. She later worked for the Smithsonian Institution as a curator, a person in charge of a museum's collections. Through radio, film and concerts, she helped people study the songs and stories that Black Americans passed down by word of mouth. She died on July 16, 2024.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bernice-johnson-reagon" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bob-dylan" name="Bob Dylan" movie="No Direction Home: Bob Dylan (2005)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bob Dylan

> **Who:** A songwriter of the folk music revival of the early 1960s and after, and winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature.
> **When and where:** Born Robert Allen Zimmerman on May 24, 1941, in Duluth, Minnesota. Grew up in Hibbing, Minnesota. Moved to New York in 1961.
> **Movie:** *No Direction Home: Bob Dylan* (2005), a documentary by Martin Scorsese.

**At the 1965 Newport Folk Festival he played an electric guitar.**

Bob Dylan won the Nobel Prize in Literature, a world prize for writers, in 2016 for his songs. The prize was "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition."

He moved to New York in January 1961 and visited Woody Guthrie, who was then in a mental hospital in New Jersey with Huntington's disease. He released the album *The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan* in 1963. At the March on Washington that August he sang "Only a Pawn in Their Game."

**Newport.** He played an electric guitar at the Newport Folk Festival in 1965. The leaders of the folk music world met him with hostility.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bob-dylan" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Country music from Nashville and Bakersfield" -->
Hank Williams learned guitar as a boy in Alabama from a Black street musician, Rufus "Tee-Tot" Payne, who taught him in return for money or meals. Williams had 12 No. 1 country records. He was born with a defect of the spine that caused him pain all his life, and he drank and took painkillers. Grand Ole Opry managers dropped him in August 1952 for drunkenness and missed shows. He died in the back seat of a car near Oak Hill, West Virginia, on January 1, 1953, at 29.

Patsy Cline recorded "Crazy," a song by Willie Nelson, in 1961. She died in a plane crash near Camden, Tennessee, on March 5, 1963, at 30. She became the first woman in the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1973.

**Bakersfield.** Buck Owens, Merle Haggard and other musicians in Bakersfield, California, played country music on electric guitars in the late 1950s, often two Fender Telecasters. Nashville records of those years added orchestras and a polished sound, and the Bakersfield musicians broke from it. Many of their listeners were the families of people who had come to California in the Dust Bowl years.

Johnny Cash recorded two concerts for prisoners at Folsom State Prison in California on January 13, 1968. The album ends with "Greystone Chapel," a song by a prisoner, Glen Sherley. It sold 500,000 copies by October 1968. Cash himself never served a prison sentence.

**"The Pill."** Loretta Lynn's song of 1975 is about a wife who is tired of being pregnant and starts taking birth-control pills. The people running a number of country radio stations refused to play it. It still reached No. 5 on the country chart.

**Charley Pride.** He became the first Black performer on the Grand Ole Opry in 1967 since DeFord Bailey last played there in 1941. Pride was one of eleven children of sharecroppers from Sledge, Mississippi, families who farmed another man's land for a share of the crop. His first records went to radio stations without his photograph. He had 30 No. 1 country records, and in 1971 he won the Country Music Association's award for Entertainer of the Year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hip-hop from the Bronx, 1973 onward" -->
Clive Campbell, a teenage DJ called Kool Herc, played records for dancers at a party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx, New York, on August 11, 1973. His sister Cindy Campbell planned the party to earn money for back-to-school clothes. City officials named the building the birthplace of hip-hop in 2007.

**The fires.** The first hip-hop DJs played in the Bronx in the same years that fires burned much of the South Bronx. Most of the fires were arson, fires set on purpose. Landlords paid people who lived nearby to set fires in the landlords' own buildings, so the landlords could collect insurance money. One landlord collected $125,000 from 21 fires.

After a study of the fire service by the RAND Corporation, a research company, city officials closed 12 fire companies in the South Bronx between 1974 and 1976, when the city was nearly out of money. Seven census tracts, small areas the census counts separately, lost more than 97 percent of their buildings to fire and abandonment. About 250,000 people lost their homes.

**"Rapper's Delight."** The Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" came out on Sugar Hill Records on September 16, 1979. Sylvia Robinson produced it and ran the company's work. It reached No. 36 on the national pop chart, and many writers call it the first rap record that sold widely. It used the music of Chic's song "Good Times." Chic's writers, Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards, got credit only after they threatened to sue. One of its rappers, Big Bank Hank, used rhymes written by Grandmaster Caz and did not credit him.

**"The Message."** The 1982 song by Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five described poverty in the inner city. Ed "Duke Bootee" Fletcher, Melle Mel, Clifton "Jiggs" Chase and Sylvia Robinson wrote it. In 2002 it became the first hip-hop record added to the National Recording Registry, the Library of Congress's list of recordings chosen to be kept for the future.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dj-kool-herc" name="DJ Kool Herc" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### DJ Kool Herc

> **Who:** Born Clive Campbell in Jamaica, the DJ at the 1973 Bronx party that people name as the start of hip-hop.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1955, in Kingston, Jamaica. Moved to the Bronx, New York, in November 1967, at 13.

**He played two copies of one record to stretch the drums.**

**The Merry-Go-Round.** At parties he played funk records. The "break" of a funk record is its drum part. Herc used two copies of the same record. When the break ended on one, he started it again on the other, so the drum part lasted longer. He called this the "Merry-Go-Round." He called the dancers who danced to the breaks "b-boys" and "b-girls."

Later someone stabbed Herc at a club called the Executive Playhouse while he was trying to stop a fight. No record names who stabbed him. After that he pulled back from playing, and by 1980 he had stopped working as a DJ. No surviving document shows what he earned from hip-hop.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dj-kool-herc" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Woodstock, music television, compact discs and the lyrics hearing" -->
Thirty-two acts played at the Woodstock music festival, on Max Yasgur's dairy farm in Bethel, New York, from August 15 to 18, 1969. The organizers expected about 50,000 people. Estimates of the crowd run from 400,000 to more than 460,000, and no record names who counted. The fences were not finished, so the festival became free. Three people died. Two died from taking too much of a drug, and a tractor ran over a 17-year-old as he slept. No record names the driver.

**MTV.** The MTV television channel began playing music videos on August 1, 1981. Its managers said they kept to a "rock format," and they played few Black artists. Walter Yetnikoff, president of CBS Records, threatened to pull CBS's videos unless MTV's managers played Black artists. They then put Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean" into heavy rotation from March 1983, which means they played it often. Compact disc players went on sale in Japan on October 1, 1982, and in the United States in March 1983.

**The lyrics hearing.** Tipper Gore, Susan Baker and two other women in Washington started a group called the Parents Music Resource Center in 1985. They made a list of 15 songs they objected to and called it the "Filthy Fifteen." It included Prince's "Darling Nikki." Members of the Senate Commerce Committee held a hearing on September 19, 1985. The musicians Frank Zappa, Dee Snider and John Denver spoke against labels. Zappa called the plan "an ill-conceived piece of nonsense," a badly thought-out idea.

Leaders of the RIAA, the record companies' own group, agreed on November 1, 1985, to put warning labels on some albums. Record companies began putting a standard "Parental Advisory" sticker on albums in 1990. Managers at Walmart and some other stores would not sell albums with the sticker.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native ceremonies and a 1978 law" -->
Many Native songs belong to religious ceremonies. Until 1978, federal officials could still enforce the rules against Native religious dances that Hiram Price, the head of the government's Indian office, had issued in 1883. In 1978 members of Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act. In it they wrote that Native "ceremonies have been intruded upon, interfered with, and in a few instances banned." The act does not name the people who did these things.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="selena-quintanilla" name="Selena Quintanilla" movie="Selena (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Selena Quintanilla

> **Who:** A Mexican American singer of Tejano music, the Spanish-language music of Texas.
> **When and where:** Born April 16, 1971, in Lake Jackson, Texas. Killed March 31, 1995, in Corpus Christi, Texas, at 23.
> **Movie:** *Selena* (1997), a Hollywood drama film about her, with Jennifer Lopez.

**The woman who ran her fan club shot her at 23.**

Selena Quintanilla sang with her brother and sister in their father Abraham Quintanilla Jr.'s band, Selena y Los Dinos, and learned the Spanish words of her songs by their sound, with her father's help. She won the Tejano Music Award for Female Vocalist of the Year in 1987 and in each of the next nine years. In 1994 she won a Grammy Award for her album *Live!*

**Yolanda Saldívar.** Saldívar ran Selena's fan club and managed her clothing stores. She had secretly taken money from the business, more than $30,000 or more than $60,000 by different counts. Selena meant to fire her.

**The shooting.** Saldívar shot Selena with a gun at a Days Inn motel in Corpus Christi, Texas, on March 31, 1995, as Selena tried to leave the room. The bullet hit her lower right shoulder. Selena reached the lobby and named Saldívar. She died at the hospital that afternoon from loss of blood. Saldívar gave herself up to the police more than nine hours later.

**The trial.** A jury in Houston found Saldívar guilty of murder with a deadly weapon on October 23, 1995, and Judge Mike Westergren sentenced her to life in prison. Members of the Texas parole board, who decide whether a prisoner may leave prison early, refused to let her out on March 27, 2025. They set her next review for March 2030.

Between 30,000 and 40,000 people came to see Selena's coffin. Her album *Dreaming of You* came out after her death. It made her the first Latin artist whose album entered the national album chart at No. 1.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 25 minutes**

Most Americans now stream their music, playing songs over the internet without buying copies, and a streaming service pays the owner of each recording a fraction of a cent a play. By 2025, 82 cents of every dollar that American record companies took in came from streaming.

Sales of recorded music in the United States had peaked in 1999, when most people bought CDs. Then a free program called Napster let people copy songs from each other's computers, and by the record companies' own count, sales fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015.

A streaming service pays the owner of the recording, usually a record company, and not the singer. In a 2018 survey, most working musicians said they earned money from playing live. Dolly Parton, a songwriter from the mountains of Tennessee, died on August 25, 2026, at 80. In September 2026 record companies and musicians were in court against companies whose computer programs make songs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Napster, the iPod and streaming, 1999 to 2025" -->
Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker started Napster in June 1999. It let people copy music files from each other's computers for free. Record companies sued in December 1999, and a federal judge ordered Napster to block copyrighted songs. Napster's owners could not do it, so they shut the service down in July 2001. At its peak, in February 2001, it had 26.4 million users by a checked count, though some estimates say 80 million.

**The iPod.** Apple's boss, Steve Jobs, showed the iPod, a pocket music player, on October 23, 2001. He said it held "1,000 songs in your pocket." Apple's iTunes Store opened on April 28, 2003, and sold most songs for 99 cents each. By 2014 it had sold 35 billion songs.

**Streaming.** Spotify, a company from Sweden, began streaming music in the United States in July 2011. By March 2026, the company said, Spotify had 300 million paying subscribers around the world.

**The numbers.** The Recording Industry Association of America, or RIAA, is the record companies' own group, and its staff count what the companies take in. Mark J. Perry of the American Enterprise Institute, a research group, used those figures in 2017. He found that sales peaked in 1999 at $20.9 billion, counted in 2016 dollars so that the rise in prices is taken out.

Sales then fell in 15 of the 16 years from 2000 to 2015. In 2016 they were $7.57 billion, a little more than a third of the 1999 figure. That year, for the first time, Americans paid more for streaming than for CDs, downloads and ringtones.

The RIAA reported in March 2026 that in 2025 the companies took in $11.5 billion at wholesale, the price the companies themselves are paid, which is lower than the price in stores. Counted that way, without taking out the rise in prices, the RIAA called 2025 the companies' best year. With the rise in prices taken out, the 1999 peak was still higher. Of the 2025 money, $9.5 billion came from streaming, and $1.04 billion came from vinyl records.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What does a stream pay?" -->
Spotify pays the owners of the music less than half a cent for each play, by one company's estimate. It pays about 70 percent of the money it takes in to the owners of the music, by its own account.

About 58.5 percent goes to whoever owns the recording, which is usually a record company. About 12 percent goes to whoever owns the song itself: the songwriter and the publisher, a company that manages the rights to the song. A singer who does not own the recording gets a share from the record company, under the singer's contract.

**The estimate.** Spotify does not pay a set price for each play. It pays each owner by that owner's share of all the plays. iMusician, a company that sends music to streaming services, put the average in February 2025 at $0.003 to $0.005 a play, and about $0.004 for a listener in the United States.

**The 1,000-play line.** From April 1, 2024, Spotify stopped paying anything for a recording played fewer than 1,000 times in a year. In a November 2023 statement, Spotify's managers wrote that recordings under that line had earned an average of about 3 cents a month, and that 99.5 percent of all plays were of recordings above the line.

Spotify's March 2026 report states that it paid the music business more than $11 billion in 2025. By its count, more than 1,500 artists earned over $1 million from it that year. The artist ranked 100,000th earned about $7,300.

**What musicians say.** A musicians' group, the United Musicians and Allied Workers, held protests in 31 cities in March 2021 and asked Spotify to pay one cent a play. A group called the Music Industry Research Association asked 1,227 American musicians about their work in 2018. Most earned money from playing live, and most could not live on music alone.

Of the women who answered, 72 percent said they had been treated unfairly because they were women, and 67 percent said they had been sexually harassed, which means they faced unwanted sexual remarks or touching. Of the musicians who were not white, 63 percent said they had been treated unfairly because of their race. The musicians who answered did not name the people who did it.

### Zoë Keating

> **Who:** A cellist and composer who sells her own recordings and publishes what streaming pays her.
> **When and where:** Born February 2, 1972, in Guelph, Ontario, Canada, to an English mother and an American father. Worked in San Francisco, California, and later Vermont.

**"206,011 Spotify streams. $753."**

Zoë Keating is a cellist who has published what streaming services paid her, so that other musicians could see real numbers. She posted those Spotify figures in September 2019. They came to a little more than a third of a cent for each play, and the company that sent her music to Spotify took no cut.

She played cello in the band Rasputina from 2002 to 2006. Alone, she uses a computer to record and repeat her own cello parts as she plays, so that one player sounds like many.

**On her own.** She released her album *One Cello x 16: Natoma* herself, without a record company. It reached No. 1 on the iTunes classical chart four times. She began publishing her income from sales and streaming in August 2012.

**Her husband.** Doctors found in May 2014 that her husband, Jeff Rusch, had stage 4 cancer, cancer that has spread through the body. Staff at their insurance company, Anthem, first refused to pay for his hospital stay. The records do not name them. Keating wrote about the refusal on her blog, as she publishes her pay. Anthem's staff reversed the refusal after local news stories about it. He died on February 19, 2015.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who owns a recording: masters and songs" -->
Every recorded song has two copyrights. One covers the song itself, its words and tune. The other covers the master, the first recording, which every copy is made from. Whoever owns the master is paid for every copy and every stream of that recording. Sam Cooke's own company owned his master recordings from 1963. Ray Charles's 1959 contract promised that he would own his in time.

**The Universal fire.** Jody Rosen, a writer for the New York Times Magazine, reported in 2019 that a fire in 2008 had destroyed 118,000 to 175,000 of Universal Music Group's master tapes. He wrote that they might include records by Chuck Berry, Muddy Waters and John Coltrane. A statement from Universal Music Group said the report held "numerous inaccuracies," meaning many mistakes. The company's leaders confirmed that the masters of one Soundgarden album had burned.

The fire began at Universal Studios Hollywood on June 1, 2008, when a worker used a blowtorch to heat roof shingles and left before they cooled. It spread across the studio's backlot, the outdoor area of sets and storage buildings.

Bob Dylan sold the rights to all of his songs to Universal Music Publishing Group in December 2020. Reporters at *The New York Times* estimated the price at more than $300 million, and other reports said about $400 million.

### Taylor Swift

> **Who:** A songwriter and singer who recorded four of her first six albums again after a music manager bought the master recordings.
> **When and where:** Born December 13, 1989, in West Reading, Pennsylvania. Moved to Hendersonville, Tennessee, at 14.
> **Movie:** *Miss Americana* (2020), a documentary about her.

**She recorded four of her albums again, so she would own them.**

Taylor Swift made new versions of four albums after a music manager bought the masters of her first six. Under her 2018 contract with Universal, she owns the masters of her new recordings.

At 14 her family moved to Hendersonville, Tennessee. That year a Nashville music publisher signed her as a songwriter, the youngest it had ever signed. After a show at Nashville's Bluebird Cafe on November 3, 2004, she signed with Scott Borchetta's new company, Big Machine Records. Big Machine owned the masters of her first six albums.

**The sale.** The music manager Scooter Braun bought Big Machine on June 30, 2019, for about $330 million, and with it her masters. Swift said she had tried for years to buy them. She said she was offered them only if she signed up to make six more albums for the company. Braun sold the masters to Shamrock Holdings in October 2020 for a reported $405 million. Shamrock is owned by the family of Roy E. Disney.

**Taylor's Version.** Swift recorded four of the albums again, and each came out as "Taylor's Version." *Fearless* came out on April 9, 2021, and *Red* on November 12, 2021. *Speak Now* followed on July 7, 2023, and *1989* on October 27, 2023.

**Buying them back.** Her Eras Tour ran from March 17, 2023, to December 8, 2024, with 149 shows. It took in $2.07 billion, more than any concert tour before it. On May 30, 2025, she said she had bought all six of the original masters from Shamrock. The price was not made public.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Music Modernization Act, 2018" -->
Members of Congress passed the Music Modernization Act in 2018. House members voted for it on April 25, and senators passed it on September 19 with no votes against. President Donald Trump signed it on October 11. Under the law, a nonprofit group, the Mechanical Licensing Collective, keeps a list of who owns each song and pays songwriters for streams. The group began paying out money in 2021 for songs whose owners nobody had identified.

**Older records.** Recordings made before February 15, 1972, had no federal copyright, so internet radio services did not have to pay for playing them. Many rhythm and blues records of the 1950s and 1960s were made before that date. Under the 2018 law, the singers and musicians on those older records are paid when digital radio plays them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hip-hop, Latin music and country radio" -->
Hip-hop and R&B made up 24.5 percent of all the music Americans bought or streamed in 2017, more than any other kind. Rock came second, with just under 21 percent. Analysts at Nielsen Music, a company that tracks sales and streams, made the count, and CNBC's reporters wrote about it in January 2018.

Sales of Latin music came to $1 billion in 2025, 8.8 percent of what the record companies took in across the United States. The RIAA reported in 2026 that Latin music's sales had reached a new high for the tenth year in a row.

**Country radio.** Jada Watson, a researcher, counts the songs played on country radio. She found that songs by women got 11 percent of the plays by 2022. She reported in 2023 that no song by a Black, Indigenous or other woman of color was in regular daytime rotation, the list of songs a station plays over and over each day.

**Beyoncé.** The Black singer Beyoncé sang her country song "Daddy Lessons" at the Country Music Association Awards on November 2, 2016. Some country fans said she did not belong there, and the association's staff deleted its online posts about her performance.

Her country album *Cowboy Carter*, from 2024, made her the first Black woman with a No. 1 album on the country album chart. The album got no nominations at the Country Music Association Awards that year. It won the Grammy Awards for Album of the Year and Best Country Album on February 2, 2025.

**By 2025:** Streaming made up 82 cents of every dollar that record companies took in, Latin music's sales reached a new high for the tenth year in a row, and Taylor Swift had bought back the masters of her first six albums.

### Kendrick Lamar

> **Who:** A Black rapper and songwriter, the first musician outside classical music and jazz to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music.
> **When and where:** Born June 17, 1987, in Compton, California.

**He won a Pulitzer Prize for an album of rap.**

Kendrick Lamar became the first musician outside classical music and jazz to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music, in 2018. His parents had moved to Compton, California, from the South Side of Chicago in 1984. When he was five, he saw a teenage drug dealer killed in a drive-by shooting outside his apartment. No record names the shooter. In seventh grade an English teacher, Regis Inge, introduced him to poetry.

**The albums.** He signed with the record label Top Dawg Entertainment in 2005. His albums include *Good Kid, M.A.A.D City* in 2012 and *To Pimp a Butterfly* in 2015. Protesters in the Black Lives Matter movement used his songs as anthems.

**The prize.** His album *DAMN.* won the Pulitzer Prize for Music on April 16, 2018. The Pulitzer board called it "a virtuosic song collection unified by its vernacular authenticity and rhythmic dynamism," praising the skill of the songs, their everyday speech and their strong rhythm. The prize was $15,000.

He performed at the Super Bowl halftime show on February 9, 2025. About 133.5 million people watched, more than had watched any halftime show before. By 2026 he had won 27 Grammy Awards, more than any other rapper.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Deaths at music festivals, 2017 and 2021" -->
More than 22,000 people were at the last night of the Route 91 Harvest country music festival in Las Vegas, Nevada, on October 1, 2017. At 10:05 p.m., while the country singer Jason Aldean was playing, Stephen Paddock, who was 64, began shooting into the crowd from his rooms on the 32nd floor of the Mandalay Bay hotel.

In ten minutes he fired more than 1,000 bullets. He killed 58 people that night and wounded at least 413 with gunfire, and about 867 people were hurt in all. Two wounded women died later, in 2019 and 2020, so the official count is 60 dead. Paddock shot himself. Investigators never found out why he did it.

**Astroworld.** About 50,000 people came to the Astroworld Festival in Houston, Texas, on November 5, 2021. When the rapper Travis Scott, who had started the festival, came on stage, the crowd pushed toward him. Ten people died of compressive asphyxiation: they were squeezed so hard they could not breathe. Thousands were hurt. The youngest to die was a boy of 9 or 10, and the reports differ on his age.

A Harris County grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether someone should be charged with a crime, decided in June 2023 not to charge Scott or five others. Among the five was Brent Silberstein, a festival manager for the concert company Live Nation. Families of people who died sued Scott and Live Nation.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Machines that make music" -->
Auto-Tune is a computer program that moves a singer's off-key notes onto the right pitch. The engineer Andy Hildebrand invented it, and the company Antares put it on sale on September 19, 1997. The singer Cher used it as a sound effect on her song "Believe" in 1998, the first record sold in stores to use it that way. The rapper and singer T-Pain used it that way on his 2005 album *Rappa Ternt Sanga* and after, and Kanye West used it on his 2008 album *808s & Heartbreak*.

**Songs made by programs.** Companies such as Suno and Udio sell computer programs that make whole songs. To train such a program, its makers feed recordings into it, and the program uses them to work out how to make new songs.

**The lawsuits.** Record companies, led by the RIAA, sued Suno and Udio in June 2024. They said the two companies had trained their programs on the record companies' recordings without permission.

**The settlements.** Some of the cases were settled in 2025 and 2026, which means the two sides ended them by agreement. Universal settled with Udio, and Udio's owners agreed to use only music they had permission to use. Warner and BMG settled with Suno and gave it permission to use their music.

**Still in court.** Universal and Sony kept suing Suno. In court papers, Suno's lawyers admitted that it had downloaded audio from YouTube. They argued that this was fair use, a use of someone else's work that the law allows in some cases.

Suno put out a new version of its program in September 2026, and its makers said they trained it on music from companies that had given permission. Universal and Sony sued again that month over 60,202 recordings, saying the new version grew out of the old ones. The songwriter Jason Isbell and three other musicians sued Suno that month too, and asked to speak for other artists as well. In October a music publisher sued Suno over 1,050 songs.

**No final ruling.** A separate case is about song lyrics. In March 2025 a federal judge, Eumi Lee, turned down music publishers' request to stop the company Anthropic from using lyrics to train its computer program while that case went on. As of October 26, 2026, no American court had given a final ruling on whether a company may train a song-making program on recordings without permission.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Kennedy Center, 2025 and 2026" -->
The Kennedy Center is a building for music, theater and dance in Washington, D.C., named for President John F. Kennedy. The National Symphony Orchestra plays there. Members of its board chose President Donald Trump as its chairman in February 2025. Trump then removed board members who had been appointed by Democrats and appointed his allies.

Board members voted on December 18, 2025, to add his name to the building, CBS News reporters wrote. Representative Joyce Beatty, a member of the board, said she had been muted on the call and that the vote had not been on the list of business for the meeting.

**In court.** Beatty went to court against the name change. A federal judge, Christopher Cooper, ruled on May 29, 2026, that the board could not rename the building. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," he wrote. He ordered the signs with Trump's name taken down within 14 days. Many performers canceled their shows at the center, saying it had become political, and ticket sales fell.

**Closed.** Board members voted to close the building on September 15, 2026, minutes after Judge Cooper again blocked the name change, Anastasia Tsioulcas of NPR reported. Trump wrote that repairs would not begin unless a higher court allowed his name. In a court filing, the center's executive director, Matt Floca, pointed to falling plaster and decay under the roof. He extended the closure to September 30, 2026, and said he would review it each week, reporters at WTOP, a Washington radio station, wrote.

The building did not reopen on September 30. Engineers had found rust and water damage on most of the panels under the building's overhanging roof, which could fall on people below.

NBC News and ABC News reported on October 1, 2026, an email in which Floca told the staff that the main building would "remain temporarily closed" while engineers checked whether people could safely use it and get out of it in an emergency. He expected that check to take one to two weeks, and he called that "an assessment timeline, not a reopening date." Judge Cooper asked for weekly reports on the repairs.

**The Honors moved.** The center's leaders moved the 2026 Kennedy Center Honors, a yearly award show, to Capital One Arena in Washington for December 7, Newsweek reported on October 2. It was the first time in nearly 50 years that the show would be held outside the Kennedy Center.

**The texts.** Beatty's lawyers asked Judge Cooper on October 25, 2026, to order the government's lawyers to hand over text messages they had kept back. They included 19 texts between Floca and Trump's assistant Natalie Harp and about 180 between Floca and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, the Associated Press reported. The judge did not rule right away. As of October 26, 2026, the main building was still closed, with no date set for it to reopen.

### Dolly Parton

> **Who:** A singer and songwriter from Sevier County, in the mountains of Tennessee, who wrote more than 3,000 songs and mailed free books to children.
> **When and where:** Born January 19, 1946, in a one-room cabin in Pittman Center, Sevier County, Tennessee. Moved to Nashville in 1964. Died August 25, 2026, in Nashville, at 80.
> **Movie:** *Dolly Parton: Here I Am* (2019), a documentary about her.

**She told Elvis Presley's manager no, and kept her song.**

Dolly Parton wrote more than 3,000 songs, and in 1974 she refused to give Elvis Presley's manager half the rights to one of them. She started a program that mails free books to children, one a month from birth to age five.

**The cabin.** She was the fourth of 12 children, born in a one-room cabin in Pittman Center, in Sevier County, Tennessee. Her father, Robert Lee Parton, farmed tobacco and could not read or write. He paid the doctor who delivered her with a sack of cornmeal. Her mother was Avie Lee Parton. From age six Dolly sang in her grandfather's church.

By ten she sang on a radio and television show in Knoxville. At 13 she made her first record and sang at the Grand Ole Opry. The day after she finished high school in 1964, she moved to Nashville.

**Porter Wagoner.** The country singer Porter Wagoner hired her for his weekly television show in 1967. Their duets made the country Top 10 for six years in a row. From 1969 Wagoner owned nearly half of Owe-Par, the company that she and her uncle Bill Owens had started to publish her songs.

Her first No. 1 single as a solo singer was "Joshua," which she wrote by herself. It reached the top of the country chart in February 1971. That same year she recorded "Coat of Many Colors," about her family's poverty. She wrote "Jolene" and "I Will Always Love You" within a few days of each other in 1973.

"I Will Always Love You" was her goodbye to Wagoner, and she left his show in 1974. The two later settled a lawsuit between them, and their 1980 album came out as part of that settlement.

**Saying no.** That year Elvis Presley wanted to record "I Will Always Love You." His manager, Colonel Tom Parker, told her that the songwriter always signed over half the publishing rights, the rights to the song itself, on any song Presley recorded. She said no. Whitney Houston recorded the song in 1992, and her record sold more than 24 million copies around the world. "I made enough money to buy Graceland," Parton said later, meaning Presley's home.

**Hits.** She wrote "9 to 5" for a 1980 film of the same name, in which she also acted. It reached No. 1 on the country and pop charts in February 1981. She sold more than 100 million records, had at least 25 No. 1 country songs, and won 11 Grammy Awards. She turned down the Presidential Medal of Freedom three times.

**Who owned what.** Ed Christman wrote in the magazine *Billboard* in August 2026 that Parton owned her own songs for her whole career. The records do not say how long Wagoner kept his share of Owe-Par, so they do not settle how that share fits with Christman's account. Her older recordings belong to Sony Music: the ones from her years with RCA, and probably from her Columbia years, about 23 albums in all. From 1995 she owned most of her new recordings, through her own record label.

**Dollywood.** She became a partner in a theme park in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, which reopened as Dollywood on May 3, 1986. It has about 4,000 to 4,500 workers, more than any other employer in Sevier County. From 2022 Dollywood paid college costs for workers who chose to go.

**The Imagination Library.** She started the Imagination Library in Sevier County in 1995. The program's website states that she started it because her father could not read or write. Each child who signs up gets one free book a month in the mail, from birth to age five.

Her foundation pays the program's running costs, and its staff bargain down the price of the books. Local partners raise the money for the books and the mailing, and families pay nothing. By 2026 more than 300 million books had gone out in five countries.

**Gifts.** After wildfires burned homes near Gatlinburg, Tennessee, in November 2016, she paid $1,000 a month for six months through her My People Fund to more than 900 families whose homes had burned, and $5,000 in the last month.

She gave $1 million to Vanderbilt University Medical Center in Nashville in the spring of 2020 for research on the coronavirus. Her longtime friend Dr. Naji Abumrad had told her that researchers there were making progress. A research paper on Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine listed her fund among its supporters. US government money for that vaccine came to about $2.5 billion. After Hurricane Helene in 2024, she and her businesses gave $2 million.

**Her death.** Her husband, Carl Thomas Dean, whom she married in 1966, died on March 3, 2025. She went into the Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center in Nashville on August 21, 2026, and died four days later. Her family did not say what kind of cancer she had. Her nephew Bryan Seaver, her head of security for more than 20 years, announced her death, as she had asked him to.

That day Representative Tim Burchett of Tennessee said, "Tennessee lost one of our greatest ambassadors in Dolly Parton." President Trump ordered flags at federal buildings flown at half-staff for a week. Her family asked people who wanted to honor her to support the Imagination Library. They held a private funeral on August 28, and she was buried beside her husband in Nashville.

**The three questions, answered**
- Where did the banjo and the blues come from? The banjo came from West African instruments, and enslaved Black people played it in the colonies from at least 1736, about a hundred years before a white man played it on a stage. The blues grew out of the work songs and field calls of enslaved people and the songs of Black workers in the South after slavery, between about 1870 and 1900.
- Why did so many musicians make hit songs and still die poor? Many were paid a set fee and no royalty, as Bessie Smith was at $125 a side, or someone else put his name on their song, as Don Robey did on "Hound Dog." Others, like Stephen Foster, sold their rights to future payments for money right away.
- Who tried to stop people from playing their own music, and why? South Carolina lawmakers banned drums and horns in 1740, saying the sound could call enslaved people together to rise up. Spanish friars burned Pueblo masks in the 1600s to punish Pueblo people for keeping their own religion. Richard Henry Pratt gave Carlisle students brass horns to stop their drumming, at a school whose own paper gave its aim as "the complete transformation of the Indians." Federal rules from 1883 to 1978 punished Native religious dances, which the Secretary of the Interior had called "heathenish."

**What changed:** Music in this land began as songs one singer taught to the next, and by 2026 most of it reached listeners as streams that paid the owner of a recording less than half a cent a play.

**Five dates to keep**
- May 10, 1740: South Carolina lawmakers ban enslaved people from using drums and horns.
- 1843: Four white men in blackface perform in New York as the Virginia Minstrels.
- October 6, 1871: The Fisk Jubilee Singers set out on their first tour.
- August 10, 1920: Mamie Smith records "Crazy Blues," the first blues record to become a big hit.
- August 11, 1973: Kool Herc plays records at the Bronx party that people name as the start of hip-hop.

**Not known:** No one knows who first played the blues, or when.

**Where to go:** *The Old Plantation*, the earliest known painting of a banjo, is at the Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Folk Art Museum in Williamsburg, Virginia.
