## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The best-known American explorers of the years 1900 to 1950 worked far from home. They traveled by dog sled toward the North Pole, flew toward both poles and across the oceans, and dug in the mountains of Peru and the deserts of Mongolia. Several of their biggest claims are still argued over. Historians dispute whether Robert Peary's party reached the North Pole in 1909 and whether Richard Byrd flew over it in 1926. In the places these explorers went, local people guided them and did much of the work. Inuit families worked on Peary's expeditions to the North Pole, and a local man led the way up to Machu Picchu.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-henson" name="Matthew Henson (with Robert Peary)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Henson (with Robert Peary)

> **Who:** A Black sailor and explorer from Maryland who was Robert Peary's navigator and dog-sled driver on seven Arctic voyages.
> **When and where:** 1887 to 1909. Northern Greenland and the Arctic Ocean.

Matthew Henson went with Robert Peary on the 1909 journey that Peary claimed reached the North Pole, Peary was honored in 1911, and Henson's honors came decades later. Henson was born on August 8, 1866, in Charles County, Maryland. His parents were sharecroppers, farmers who worked someone else's land for a share of the crop. They had been free before the Civil War. In 1867 the family left southern Maryland to escape attacks by the Ku Klux Klan, a group of white men who used violence to terrorize Black people.

At 12 Henson went to sea as a cabin boy. The ship's captain, a man named Childs, taught him to read and write. In November 1887 Henson was working in a clothing store in Washington, D.C., when he met Robert Peary, a Navy officer. Peary hired him as an aide for a survey in Nicaragua, where people were planning a canal. Their first Arctic trip together was in 1891 and 1892, and they made seven Arctic voyages over nearly 23 years.

Henson traded with the Inuit people of northern Greenland and learned their language. They called him Mahri-Pahluk. He was the only member of the expedition who was not Inuit to become skilled at driving and training dog teams the Inuit way. He was also Peary's navigator, the person who worked out where they were and which way to go.

In July 1908 Peary's ship, the *Roosevelt*, left New York. Inuit men, women and children from Cape York, in Greenland, joined the expedition. In February 1909 the sled parties set out across the frozen Arctic Ocean. There were 7 expedition members, 19 Inuit, 140 dogs and 28 sleds. They carried supplies forward in relays. By the end Peary could no longer walk, and he rode on a sled. He sent Henson ahead as a scout.

On April 6, 1909, Peary said that his party had reached the North Pole. The last group was six men: Peary, Henson and four Inuit men named Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah. Henson later said that he was the first of the party to reach the spot they believed was the pole.

The claim is disputed. Frederick Cook, who had been the doctor on Peary's trip of 1891 to 1892, said he had reached the pole first, in 1908. A committee of the National Geographic Society backed Peary. In 1989 the British explorer Wally Herbert studied Peary's records for the National Geographic Society. He concluded that the records were unreliable, that they showed the party moving faster than seemed possible, and that the party may have stopped 30 to 60 miles short of the pole. Neither Peary's claim nor Cook's is settled.

In 1911 Peary received the thanks of Congress and was made a rear admiral. Henson published a book in 1912 called *A Negro Explorer at the North Pole*. In 1937 he became the first African American life member of the Explorers Club, a society of explorers in New York. In 1944 he received a medal for the Peary expedition. Presidents Harry Truman and Dwight Eisenhower welcomed him to the White House. He died on March 9, 1955. In 1988 he and his wife were buried again at Arlington National Cemetery. In 2000 the National Geographic Society gave him its Hubbard Medal. In 2021 the International Astronomical Union, the scientists' group that names places in space, named a crater on the Moon after him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="matthew-henson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hiram-bingham" name="Hiram Bingham" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Hiram Bingham

> **Who:** A Yale history teacher, not a trained archaeologist, who made the Inca site of Machu Picchu famous.
> **When and where:** 1911 to 1915. The Andes Mountains of Peru.

Hiram Bingham made Machu Picchu famous around the world, and he did not discover it. He was born in Honolulu in 1875 and taught South American history at Yale University. He was not trained as an archaeologist, a scientist who studies the past by digging up what people left behind. In 1911 he led an expedition from Yale to Peru to look for the last capital of the Inca. The Inca ruled an empire in the Andes Mountains before the Spanish arrived.

On July 24, 1911, a local man named Melchor Arteaga led Bingham up to Machu Picchu. The people living in the valley next to it already knew the ruins. Explorers from the Peruvian city of Cusco are said to have reached it in 1901. One of them, Agustin Lizarraga, wrote "A. Lizarraga 1902" in charcoal on a wall there, and Bingham found the writing. A map from 1874 already shows the site. Bingham later wrote about the ruins as if he had found them himself.

Bingham believed Machu Picchu was the Incas' last capital, and he called it the "Lost City." He was wrong. The last capital was Vilcabamba. He visited it too, and he did not recognize it.

Bingham's team dug up about 40,000 objects at Machu Picchu, among them mummies, human bones and pottery. They shipped them to Yale under an order from Peru's government. Peruvian officials said the objects had only been lent. Leaders of Yale and Peru reached an agreement in 2007, and Yale officials began sending the objects back in 2012. Bingham later served one day as governor of Connecticut, in 1925, and then as a US senator until 1933.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hiram-bingham" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="roy-chapman-andrews" name="Roy Chapman Andrews" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Roy Chapman Andrews

> **Who:** A naturalist from Wisconsin who started as a janitor at the American Museum of Natural History and later ran it.
> **When and where:** 1922 to 1930. The Gobi Desert of Mongolia.

Roy Chapman Andrews led the expeditions that found the first nests of dinosaur eggs known to science. He was born in Beloit, Wisconsin, in 1884. He started work at the American Museum of Natural History in New York as a janitor. From 1922 to 1928 he led the museum's expeditions by car from Peking, in China, into the Gobi Desert of Mongolia. He was looking for the remains of the earliest humans. His teams found none.

They found other fossils instead. In 1922 they found the bones of a giant relative of the rhinoceros that had no horn. On July 13, 1923, they found nests full of fossil dinosaur eggs. Scientists first thought the eggs came from a dinosaur called *Protoceratops*. In 1995 scientists showed that they belonged to a meat-eating dinosaur called *Oviraptor*.

In 1928 Chinese officials seized what the expedition had found. The finds were later returned. In 1934 Andrews became director of the museum where he had started as a janitor.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roy-chapman-andrews" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="richard-byrd" name="Richard Byrd" movie="With Byrd at the South Pole (1930)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Richard Byrd

> **Who:** A US Navy pilot and navigator who led flights and expeditions to both poles.
> **When and where:** 1926 to 1935. The Arctic and Antarctica.
> **Movie:** *With Byrd at the South Pole* (1930), a documentary film of the trip.

Richard Byrd led the first flight to the South Pole, in 1929. His claim to have flown over the North Pole three years earlier is disputed. Byrd was born in Virginia in 1888 and became a Navy pilot in 1918.

On May 9, 1926, Byrd and the pilot Floyd Bennett flew a three-engine plane, the *Josephine Ford*, toward the North Pole. Members of Congress voted both men the Medal of Honor for the flight. Most researchers now believe the plane could not have carried enough fuel to fly as far as Byrd said. In 1958 another pilot, Bernt Balchen, said that Bennett had told him they never reached the pole. Byrd's diary was made public in 1996. In it, some of his navigation readings had been erased, and they differ from his official report. The historian Dennis Rawlins concluded that Byrd flew about 80 percent of the way and turned back because of an oil leak. Other researchers defend Byrd's claim.

In Antarctica, Byrd built a base camp called Little America on the Ross Ice Shelf, a huge sheet of ice floating on the sea. On November 28, 1929, Byrd, the pilot Bernt Balchen, the radio operator Harold June and the photographer Ashley McKinley took off for the South Pole. They flew there and back in 18 hours and 41 minutes. To make the plane light enough to climb onto the high ice of the Antarctic Plateau, they threw supplies out.

On his second expedition, in 1934, Byrd spent five months alone in a small weather hut. Fumes from a stove with a bad chimney gave him carbon monoxide poisoning. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no smell that stops the blood from carrying oxygen. He nearly died. Men from the base camp reached him on their third try. Byrd died in 1957.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="richard-byrd" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-lindbergh" name="Charles Lindbergh" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Lindbergh

> **Who:** An airmail pilot who made the first solo nonstop flight across the Atlantic Ocean.
> **When and where:** May 20 to 21, 1927. From Garden City, New York, to Paris, France.

In May 1927 Charles Lindbergh, an airmail pilot, became the first person to fly alone and without stopping across the Atlantic Ocean. A company in San Diego, Ryan Airlines, built his single-engine plane, the *Spirit of St. Louis*.

On May 20, 1927, Lindbergh took off from Roosevelt Field, in Garden City on Long Island, New York. He landed at Le Bourget airfield outside Paris 33 hours and 30 minutes later, after about 3,600 miles. He won the $25,000 Orteig Prize, which had been offered for the first nonstop flight between New York and Paris.

Lindbergh was not the first to fly across the Atlantic without stopping. Two British fliers, John Alcock and Arthur Brown, flew from St. John's, Newfoundland, to Clifden, Ireland, on June 14 and 15, 1919. Lindbergh was the first to make the crossing alone.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-lindbergh" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-earhart" name="Amelia Earhart" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Earhart

> **Who:** The first woman to fly alone and nonstop across the Atlantic Ocean.
> **When and where:** 1932, Newfoundland to Northern Ireland, and 1937, the central Pacific Ocean.

Five years after Lindbergh, Amelia Earhart made the same kind of crossing, alone and without stopping, the first woman to do it. She was born on July 24, 1897. In 1928 she crossed the Atlantic by air as a passenger, the first woman to do so. On May 20, 1932, when she was 34, she took off alone from Harbour Grace, in Newfoundland. She flew through strong winds and ice, and her engine gave her trouble. After 14 hours and 56 minutes she landed in a pasture in Northern Ireland.

In 1937 Earhart set out to be the first woman to fly around the world. Her route ran about 29,000 miles, near the equator. The navigator Fred Noonan flew with her. On June 29 they reached Lae, in New Guinea, with about 7,000 miles to go.

On July 2, 1937, they took off for Howland Island, a tiny island in the Pacific. A Coast Guard ship, the *Itasca*, waited there to guide them in by radio. The crew of the *Itasca* heard her clearly, but she could not hear the ship. The ship's equipment could not work out which direction her signal came from. At 7:58 in the morning she radioed that she could not hear the *Itasca*. Earhart and Noonan were never found. She was declared legally dead on January 5, 1939.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amelia-earhart" -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950 the places no person had ever seen were the deepest parts of the ocean and space beyond the Earth. On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first satellite to circle the Earth. A satellite is an object that travels in a path around a planet. The next year the United States set up NASA, its space agency. American and Soviet leaders then raced to send people into space and to the Moon. Between 1969 and 1972 twelve American men walked on the Moon.

Three American astronauts died in a fire on the launch pad in 1967, and seven died when the space shuttle *Challenger* broke apart in 1986. In the ocean, two men reached the deepest point on Earth in 1960, and scientists found animals living around hot springs on the sea floor in 1977.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="don-walsh" name="Don Walsh" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Don Walsh

> **Who:** A US Navy lieutenant who, with the Swiss scientist Jacques Piccard, made the first dive to the deepest point in the ocean.
> **When and where:** January 23, 1960. The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, near Guam in the Pacific Ocean.

On January 23, 1960, Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh and the Swiss ocean scientist Jacques Piccard reached the bottom of the deepest known place in the ocean. It is called the Challenger Deep, and it lies in the Mariana Trench, near the island of Guam in the Pacific. No craft of any kind, with people or without, had been there before.

They went down in the *Trieste*, a deep-diving craft called a bathyscaphe. Piccard's father, Auguste Piccard, had designed it, and it was built in Italy in 1953. The US Navy bought it. The trip down took 4 hours and 47 minutes. Below 30,000 feet, one of the outer window panes cracked, and the crack shook the whole craft.

At the time they measured the depth as 37,799 feet. The figure was later corrected to 35,814 feet, which is almost seven miles. Newer measurements give between 35,797 and 36,070 feet. The two men stayed on the bottom for twenty minutes, in a cabin that was 45 degrees Fahrenheit.

Walsh and Piccard reported seeing flatfish on the bottom. Scientists now doubt it, because fish are not known to live deeper than about 26,000 to 28,000 feet. Walsh later said they could have been mistaken.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="don-walsh" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Into space, 1957 to 1972" -->
NASA was set up on July 29, 1958, under a law called the National Aeronautics and Space Act, and began work that October. Its full name is the National Aeronautics and Space Administration. On April 12, 1961, a Soviet cosmonaut, Yuri Gagarin, circled the Earth, the first person to fly in space. Three weeks later, on May 5, 1961, Alan Shepard became the first American in space. His flight lasted 15 minutes. It went up into space and came straight back down without circling the Earth.

On May 25, 1961, President John F. Kennedy asked Congress to set the goal, "before this decade is out, of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to the Earth." The Moon program was called Apollo. At its peak it employed 400,000 people, and it cost $25.4 billion. On February 20, 1962, John Glenn circled the Earth three times in 4 hours and 55 minutes in a capsule called *Friendship 7*. He was the first American to orbit the Earth, which means to travel all the way around it. Two Soviet cosmonauts had orbited before him.

On January 27, 1967, three astronauts died in a fire during a practice countdown on the launch pad in Florida. They were Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee, the crew of Apollo 1. An electric spark set nylon material alight inside a cabin filled with pure oxygen. The hatch opened inward, and the pressure inside the cabin held it shut. It took workers on the launch pad five minutes to open all three layers of the hatch. The fire melted the men's suits and air hoses. Doctors who examined their bodies found that carbon monoxide gas from the fire had stopped their hearts. All three also had third-degree burns, burns that destroy the skin all the way through, most of them after they died.

In December 1968 the crew of Apollo 8 became the first people to orbit the Moon. Between December 1968 and December 1972, 24 astronauts flew around the Moon. Six Apollo crews landed on it, and twelve men walked on it. The last landing was Apollo 17, in December 1972. No one has walked on the Moon since.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="katherine-johnson" name="Katherine Johnson" movie="Hidden Figures (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Katherine Johnson

> **Who:** A Black mathematician from West Virginia who calculated the flight paths of the first American spaceflights.
> **When and where:** From 1953. The Langley laboratory in Hampton, Virginia.
> **Movie:** *Hidden Figures* (2016). The actor Taraji P. Henson plays Johnson.

Katherine Johnson worked out by hand the paths that carried the first American astronauts into space and back. She was born on August 26, 1918, in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia. Her county had no public high school for Black students. Her family sent her to one on the campus of West Virginia State College, and she started there at the age of ten. Later she was one of three Black students chosen to be the first to attend the graduate school of West Virginia University.

In June 1953 she was hired at the Langley laboratory, in Hampton, Virginia. The laboratory belonged to NACA, the government agency that came before NASA. Her job title was "computer." At that time a computer was a person who did long calculations by hand. Virginia's segregation laws, and the rules in federal workplaces, forced Johnson and the other Black women computers to work, eat and use restrooms apart from white workers. Segregation means keeping people apart by race. The sign on their office read "Colored Computers."

Johnson calculated the path and the launch time for Alan Shepard's flight in 1961. For John Glenn's flight in 1962, NASA officials used electronic computers for the first time to plan the orbit. Glenn asked for Johnson by name to check the machines' numbers. He refused to fly until she had checked them. She later worked on the paths that let Apollo spacecraft meet each other in space.

In 2015 President Barack Obama gave her the Presidential Medal of Freedom. Members of Congress awarded her the Congressional Gold Medal in 2019. She died on February 24, 2020, at the age of 101.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="katherine-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="apollo-11-crew" name="Apollo 11 crew" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Apollo 11 crew

> **Who:** Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins, the crew of the first spacecraft to land people on the Moon.
> **When and where:** July 16 to 24, 1969. From Florida to the Moon's Sea of Tranquility and back to the Pacific Ocean.

On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first people to land on the Moon. A Saturn V rocket had launched them and Michael Collins from Kennedy Space Center, in Florida, on July 16.

Armstrong and Aldrin flew the lunar module, a small landing craft named *Eagle*, down to a flat plain called the Sea of Tranquility. Collins stayed in orbit around the Moon in the command module, *Columbia*. About six hours after landing, Armstrong stepped out onto the Moon. Aldrin followed him 19 minutes later. An estimated 600 million people, about one in five people on Earth, watched Armstrong's first steps live on television. Another count gives 650 million.

The two men collected 47.5 pounds of rock and soil and spent more than 21 hours on the Moon's surface. The three astronauts splashed down safely in the Pacific Ocean on July 24, 1969.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="apollo-11-crew" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="apollo-13-crew" name="Apollo 13 crew" movie="Apollo 13 (1995)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Apollo 13 crew

> **Who:** Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise, a Moon-landing crew whose spacecraft was badly damaged by an explosion.
> **When and where:** April 11 to 17, 1970. Between the Earth and the Moon.
> **Movie:** *Apollo 13* (1995), based on Lovell's book about the flight, *Lost Moon*.

The crew of Apollo 13 survived an explosion on the way to the Moon by living in their small landing craft. They were Jim Lovell, the commander, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise. Swigert had taken the place of Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles, an illness that spreads from person to person.

The spacecraft launched on April 11, 1970. Two days later the crew stirred one of the oxygen tanks, a routine job. Damaged insulation on the wires inside the tank caught fire, and the tank exploded. The oxygen in both of the main spacecraft's tanks escaped into space. The main spacecraft lost the oxygen it needed and most of its electric power.

The Moon landing was called off. The three men shut down the main spacecraft to save its power for the return to Earth. They moved into the small lunar module, *Aquarius*, and used it as a lifeboat. It was cold and wet inside, and they ran short of drinking water. Carbon dioxide from their breathing built up in the air. The crew and the engineers on the ground worked out a way to make the main spacecraft's air filters fit the lunar module.

The crew swung around the Moon and headed home. They splashed down safely in the South Pacific on April 17, 1970. A review board later blamed the way the tank had been tested before the flight.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="apollo-13-crew" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sylvia-earle" name="Sylvia Earle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sylvia Earle

> **Who:** A marine biologist from New Jersey who set a deep-diving record and led the first all-woman team to live underwater.
> **When and where:** 1970 to 1992. The Virgin Islands and the waters off Hawaii.

Sylvia Earle walked on the sea floor deeper than any woman has walked since. She was born in New Jersey in 1935 and became a marine biologist, a scientist who studies life in the sea. In 1969 the Tektite project, which let scientists live in a laboratory under the sea, turned her down. By then she had spent more than 1,000 hours underwater doing research.

In 1970 Earle led the first all-woman team of aquanauts, people who live and work underwater. They lived in a laboratory 50 feet below the surface off the Virgin Islands. In 1979 she put on a hard diving suit that kept the water pressure away from her body. With no line connecting her to the surface, she walked on the sea floor off Oahu, Hawaii, 1,250 feet down. No woman has beaten that depth record. From 1990 to 1992 she was the first woman to be chief scientist of NOAA, the US government's ocean and weather agency.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sylvia-earle" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-ballard" name="Robert Ballard" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Ballard

> **Who:** A Navy oceanographer and sea-floor geologist from Kansas.
> **When and where:** 1977 to 1989. The Pacific floor near the Galapagos Islands and the North Atlantic.

Robert Ballard helped find living things where scientists had expected none, and later he found the wreck of the *Titanic*. He was born in Wichita, Kansas, in 1942. He became an oceanographer, a scientist who studies the ocean, and he worked at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

In 1977 Ballard was a diver on an expedition that used the small submarine *Alvin* on the sea floor near the Galapagos Islands, in the Pacific. Its members found hot springs on the sea floor. Animals lived all around them. They got their energy from chemicals in the water from the springs, not from sunlight. All the scientists on board were geologists, because they had been certain there would be no life to study.

In 1982 Ballard asked the Navy to pay for a search for the *Titanic*, using a new camera robot called *Argo* that a ship could tow along the sea floor. The Navy's leaders agreed, on one condition. First he had to use *Argo* to look in secret at the wrecks of two Navy nuclear submarines, the *Thresher* and the *Scorpion*, which had sunk in the 1960s. After that work, in 1985, Ballard and a French team found the *Titanic* on the floor of the North Atlantic. In 1989 he found the wreck of the German battleship *Bismarck*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-ballard" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sally-ride-mae-jemison" name="Sally Ride and Mae Jemison" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sally Ride and Mae Jemison

> **Who:** The first American woman in space and the first African American woman in space.
> **When and where:** 1983 and 1992, aboard the space shuttles *Challenger* and *Endeavour*.

In 1983 Sally Ride became the first American woman in space, and in 1992 Mae Jemison became the first African American woman in space. Ride was a physicist from California, a scientist who studies matter and energy. She joined NASA in 1978. On June 18, 1983, she flew on the space shuttle *Challenger*. She was the third woman in space. The first had been Valentina Tereshkova of the Soviet Union, in 1963. At 32 Ride was the youngest American astronaut to fly. On the flight she worked the shuttle's robot arm. Reporters sent more than 500 requests to interview her. Two months later, in August 1983, Guion Bluford became the first African American in space.

Ride later served on the group that investigated the *Challenger* explosion in 1986, and on the group that investigated the loss of the shuttle *Columbia* in 2003. She was the only person to serve on both. She died in 2012. Her obituary named Tam O'Shaughnessy as her partner of 27 years. Ride had kept the relationship private while she was alive. She is the first astronaut known to have been LGBTQ, which stands for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender or queer.

Mae Jemison was born in Decatur, Alabama, in 1956 and grew up in Chicago. She studied chemical engineering at Stanford University and then became a doctor. From 1983 to 1985 she worked as a doctor in Liberia and Sierra Leone, in West Africa. She went there with the Peace Corps, a US government program that sends volunteers to work in other countries. She joined NASA in 1987. From September 12 to 20, 1992, she flew aboard the space shuttle *Endeavour*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sally-ride-mae-jemison" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Challenger explosion, 1986" -->
On January 28, 1986, the space shuttle *Challenger* broke apart 73 seconds after it lifted off. All seven people on board died. They were the commander, Dick Scobee, the pilot, Michael Smith, and Ellison Onizuka, Judith Resnik, Ronald McNair, Gregory Jarvis and Christa McAuliffe, a schoolteacher. Many schools watched the launch live because a teacher was on board.

The shuttle broke apart because rubber rings, called O-rings, failed in a joint of one of its booster rockets. The rings sealed the joint so that hot gas could not escape. The record cold that morning had made them stiff. Tests going back to 1977 had shown the flaw in the seals. Two engineers at the Morton Thiokol company, Allan McDonald and Roger Boisjoly, had warned against launching in the cold. NASA managers ignored their warnings. Several of the crew are thought to have survived the breakup itself. Someone switched on the pilot's emergency air supply. The crew cabin then fell and struck the ocean.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, robot spacecraft steered from Earth have explored Mars and the outer solar system. Rovers drive across Mars, and probes have flown past Pluto and out beyond the planets. People have lived on the International Space Station without a break since November 2, 2000. On Earth, most of the deep ocean floor has still not been mapped in detail. In April 2026 an international mapping project reported that 28.7 percent had been mapped. That same month four astronauts flew around the Moon, the first people to travel there since 1972.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The loss of Columbia, 2003" -->
On February 1, 2003, the space shuttle *Columbia* broke apart over Texas and Louisiana as it came back down into the atmosphere, the layer of air around the Earth. All seven astronauts on board died. They were the commander, Rick Husband, the pilot, William McCool, and Michael Anderson, Kalpana Chawla, David Brown, Laurel Clark and Ilan Ramon, the first astronaut from Israel.

During the launch, a piece of foam from the shuttle's big fuel tank had broken off and hit the left wing. It damaged the panels that protected the wing from the heat of coming back through the atmosphere. Foam had struck shuttles before, on 65 of the 79 earlier launches that had been photographed. While *Columbia* was in orbit, a team of engineers asked several times for pictures of the wing, taken by military satellites or telescopes. Linda Ham, the manager who chaired the mission's management team, cancelled the request. The management team had decided it did not need the pictures.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Robots to Mars and beyond" -->
NASA's rover *Curiosity* landed in Gale crater on Mars on August 6, 2012. A rover is a robot vehicle that drives across the ground of another world. *Curiosity* was still working in September 2026. The rover *Perseverance* landed on February 18, 2021, in Jezero Crater. Scientists chose the crater because an ancient river once flowed into it and left a delta, a fan of mud and sand. *Perseverance* drills rock samples and stores them so that a later mission could bring them to Earth. In July 2024 it found a rock that shows a possible chemical sign of ancient life.

*Perseverance* carried a small helicopter named *Ingenuity*, the first aircraft to fly on another planet. It was built for five flights. It made 72 flights over nearly three years, until its rotor blades broke when it landed on January 18, 2024.

The probe *New Horizons* left Earth on January 19, 2006. On July 14, 2015, it passed 7,800 miles above Pluto, the first spacecraft to visit it. On January 1, 2019, it flew past a small icy world called Arrokoth, about 4 billion miles from the Sun.

Two older probes, *Voyager 1* and *Voyager 2*, were launched in 1977. On August 25, 2012, *Voyager 1* became the first spacecraft to reach interstellar space, the space between the stars, beyond the stream of particles that blows out from the Sun. *Voyager 2* followed on November 5, 2018. *Voyager 1* is the most distant object people have ever made. Both Voyagers get their power from plutonium, a radioactive metal that gives off heat. According to a NASA report of April 17, 2026, each loses about 4 watts of power a year. That day NASA engineers switched off another of *Voyager 1*'s instruments to save power. Two of its science instruments still work.

The James Webb Space Telescope was launched on December 25, 2021, from French Guiana, in South America. It cost just under $10 billion. It was built to see light from about 180 million years after the universe began, when the first stars and galaxies were forming.

### James Cameron

> **Who:** A Canadian film director who dove alone to the deepest point in the ocean.
> **When and where:** March 26, 2012. The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, in the western Pacific Ocean.

On March 26, 2012, James Cameron, a film director from Canada, became the first person to dive alone to the Challenger Deep, the deepest point in the ocean. He steered a one-person submarine, the *Deepsea Challenger*, to the bottom. The craft was built in Sydney, Australia, with the National Geographic Society.

The trip down took 2 hours and 37 minutes, about twice as fast as the *Trieste* in 1960. The deepest reading on the dive was 35,787 feet. Cameron spent about three hours exploring the bottom, the first person to spend that long there. A problem with the craft's hydraulic system, which used fluid under pressure to work its equipment, made it hard to use the equipment for collecting samples.

Cameron was only the third person ever to reach the Challenger Deep, 52 years after Don Walsh and Jacques Piccard. The *Deepsea Challenger* was later given to the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, where Robert Ballard had worked.

### Artemis II crew

> **Who:** Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch of NASA and Jeremy Hansen of Canada, the first people to travel to the Moon since 1972.
> **When and where:** April 1 to 10, 2026. From Florida around the Moon and back to the Pacific Ocean off San Diego.

In April 2026 four astronauts flew around the Moon, the first people to go there in more than 50 years. They were the commander, Reid Wiseman, the pilot, Victor Glover, and Christina Koch of NASA, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. They launched on April 1, 2026. It was the first flight with a crew for NASA's Orion spacecraft and its SLS rocket. The crew circled around the Moon but did not land.

According to NASA's announcement of April 10, 2026, the crew traveled 252,756 miles from Earth at their farthest point. That broke the record of 248,655 miles set by the crew of Apollo 13 in 1970. The Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum states that Koch became the first woman, Glover the first Black man and Hansen the first Canadian to travel that far from Earth. Koch had already spent 328 days in orbit on the International Space Station, ending in February 2020. It was the longest single spaceflight by a woman up to that time.

Some experts outside NASA objected to flying this mission with Orion's heat shield as it was. After Orion's test flight without a crew in November 2022, NASA engineers found that its heat shield had worn away in ways they had not expected. The heat shield protects the spacecraft from the heat of coming back through the atmosphere. NASA's leaders kept the same shield for Artemis II and changed the path the spacecraft would take on the way down. The outside experts wanted a redesigned shield.

The crew splashed down safely in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego at 5:07 p.m. Pacific time on April 10, 2026, after a trip of nearly ten days.
