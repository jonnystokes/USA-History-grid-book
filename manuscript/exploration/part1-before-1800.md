## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first people to explore this land came from Asia during the last ice age. At that time a strip of dry land joined Siberia to Alaska. Human footprints at White Sands, in New Mexico, show that people were here at least 21,000 years ago. Over thousands of years their descendants spread across the whole continent, into its deserts, mountains, forests, coasts and plains.

By 1500 hundreds of Native nations lived here. Nobody knows how many people lived north of Mexico at that time. Estimates run from about 1 million to about 18 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The footprints at White Sands" -->
White Sands National Park in New Mexico holds fossil human footprints. Scientists dated seeds of a water plant found in the layers just above and just below the prints. The seeds gave ages of about 21,000 and about 23,000 years.

For a long time scientists had thought people first arrived only about 13,500 to 16,000 years ago. Some scientists questioned the new dates. A water plant can take in old carbon from the rock around it, and that can make its seeds test older than they are. The National Park Service states that the latest research shows people have lived in North America for at least 23,000 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trade routes and Pacific voyaging" -->
Native people knew routes across the continent long before any European arrived. Traders carried copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf of Mexico, turquoise from the Southwest and obsidian from the West. Obsidian is a natural glass formed by volcanoes. Archaeologists have found each of these goods hundreds of miles from where it came from. Chert is a hard stone that can be shaped into tools. The people of Cahokia, a city near present-day St. Louis, controlled a kind called Mill Creek chert. Hoe blades made of it turn up more than 400 miles away.

Polynesian sailors crossed thousands of miles of open Pacific Ocean to reach the Hawaiian Islands. The archaeologist Patrick Kirch reviewed 150 years of study and put their first arrival and settlement between about the years 1000 and 1200. Scholars before him had put it much earlier, between about the years 300 and 750.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="leif-erikson" name="Leif Erikson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Leif Erikson

> **Who:** A Norse sailor from Greenland, son of Erik the Red, who led one of the first European voyages to North America.
> **When and where:** About the year 1000, and a Norse camp dated to 1021. L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, Canada.

Norse sailors from Greenland reached North America about 500 years before Columbus. Two Icelandic sagas, the *Saga of the Greenlanders* and the *Saga of Erik the Red*, tell the story. A saga is an old Icelandic story. The sagas say that Leif Erikson, son of Erik the Red, reached a land he called Vinland.

The sagas also describe the people the Norse met there, whom the Norse called Skraelings. The two groups traded. They also fought, and the sagas say the fights led the Norse to leave. The sagas do not say which nation these people belonged to.

In 1960 a local man, George Decker, led the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad to some old mounds near the village of L'Anse aux Meadows. People there called the place the "old Indian camp." Helge Ingstad and the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad dug there for seven seasons, from 1961 to 1968. They found a Norse camp.

In 2021 scientists worked out the exact year when some of the wood at the camp was cut. A storm on the sun in the year 993 left a mark in the rings of trees growing on Earth. The scientists found that mark in pieces of wood cut with metal blades, and counted the rings out to the bark. The trees were cut down in 1021. That is 471 years before Columbus reached the Bahamas.

Historians disagree about how long the Norse stayed. Estimates run from a few years to about twenty. One scholar thinks the camp was only a place to repair boats. Native people had lived on the same spot long before the Norse came. Five earlier groups had lived there, the oldest about 6,000 years before. Dorset people lived there about 300 years before the Norse.

The camp proves that the Norse reached Newfoundland. It does not prove that this camp was Leif's own.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s European sailors mapped the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, and Spanish armies marched far inland. Every place they reached was already the home of a Native nation. Native guides showed several of them the way, including the way to the Grand Canyon. The explorers also took people captive, seized stored food and attacked towns that refused to let them in.

In 1565 Spanish colonists built St. Augustine in Florida, inside a Timucua town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="christopher-columbus" name="Christopher Columbus" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Christopher Columbus

> **Who:** A sea captain from Genoa, in Italy, who sailed west for Spain looking for a sea route to Asia.
> **When and where:** Four voyages from 1492 to 1504, to the Bahamas, the Caribbean islands and the coasts of Central and South America.

Christopher Columbus crossed the Atlantic four times, and he never set foot in what is now the United States. He was born in Genoa in 1451. He sailed for the king and queen of Spain. He believed that by sailing west he could reach Asia.

At about 2 in the morning on October 12, 1492, a lookout named Rodrigo de Triana saw land from the ship *Pinta*. It was an island in the Bahamas. Its people, the Lucayan Taino, called it Guanahani. Columbus named it San Salvador. On that first voyage he took Taino people captive and carried them to Spain to show the king and queen.

On the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, in 1494 and 1495, Columbus's soldiers made war on the Taino until they surrendered and agreed to pay a tribute. A tribute is a payment that a conquered people are forced to hand over. Every Taino aged 14 or older had to bring in a small measure of gold dust every six months. A priest named Bartolome de las Casas wrote that the Spaniards cut off the hands of people who failed to pay. Historians dispute how often that was done.

In February 1495 Columbus's men rounded up about 1,500 Taino. They shipped about 500 of the strongest to Spain to be sold as slaves. About 200 of them died on the voyage. Within a few decades, disease, war and forced labor had killed most of the Taino on Hispaniola. Historians disagree about how many Taino lived there when Columbus arrived.

In 1500 the Spanish rulers sent an investigator, Francisco de Bobadilla, to Hispaniola. Bobadilla arrested Columbus and sent him back to Spain in chains. Bobadilla's report took statements from 23 witnesses. It records that Columbus punished a man who stole corn by having his ears and nose cut off, and then sold him as a slave. The report was found again in 2005 in a Spanish archive in the city of Valladolid.

Columbus died in Valladolid on May 20, 1506. He never said in public that he had reached a new continent. Historians disagree about what he believed in private. The name "America" came from another Italian sailor, Amerigo Vespucci. Vespucci wrote in 1503 that the lands across the Atlantic were a new continent. In 1507 a German mapmaker, Martin Waldseemuller, printed a world map that named the new land America.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="christopher-columbus" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sailing the coasts, 1497 to 1542" -->
John Cabot was an Italian, born Giovanni Caboto, who sailed for King Henry VII of England. On June 24, 1497, his small ship, the *Matthew*, reached the coast of North America with a crew of 18 to 20 men. Historians still argue about where he landed. Some say Newfoundland and some say Cape Breton Island. Cabot went ashore only once, and he went no farther inland than a crossbow could shoot. King Henry gave him 10 pounds, which was about two years' pay for a laborer.

Juan Ponce de Leon sailed from Puerto Rico in March 1513 with three ships and 65 people. Among them was Juan Garrido, a free African man. Ponce de Leon named the land La Florida. His was the first European expedition to Florida that anyone wrote down. A map made in 1502, called the Cantino map, already shows a shape like Florida, so Spanish slave raiders may have reached it first without leaving a record. On the Gulf coast the Calusa people of southwest Florida refused to trade. Calusa warriors in canoes drove his ships away, and in the fights that followed his men captured eight Calusa, four men and four women.

Ponce de Leon came back in 1521 to build a colony on Calusa land. Calusa warriors fought the newcomers, and they wounded him. He died of the wound in Cuba that July. Many people have heard that he was searching for a Fountain of Youth. No writing from his own time supports that story, and most historians call it a myth.

In 1524 Giovanni da Verrazzano, an Italian sailing for the king of France, followed the Atlantic coast from Florida to what is now New Brunswick, in Canada. In New York Bay about 30 canoes of Lenape people came out to meet his ship. In Narragansett Bay, Wampanoag and Narragansett people visited him. Verrazzano wrote to the French king about the voyage himself. In his letter he described what about twenty of his men did on the peninsula now called Delmarva, south of New York Bay. They found an old woman, a young woman and some children hiding in the grass. He wrote: "We took the boy from the old woman to carry back to France, and we wanted to take the young woman, who was very beautiful and tall, but it was impossible to take her to the sea because of the loud cries she uttered." The boy was about eight years old. The letter does not give his name or his people, and no record tells what happened to him in France.

Jacques Cartier, sailing for France, explored the St. Lawrence River in present-day Canada from 1534 to 1542. In the winter of 1535 to 1536, Iroquoian people at the town of Stadacona gave his men a medicine made from evergreen trees, which saved them from scurvy. Scurvy is a disease caused by a severe lack of vitamin C, which comes from fresh food. It makes people weak, damages their gums and makes them bleed under the skin. In 1536 Cartier kidnapped Donnacona, the Iroquoian leader, along with Donnacona's two sons and seven other people. The French never brought any of them home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cabeza-de-vaca-estevanico" name="Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico

> **Who:** Two of the four survivors of a Spanish expedition who crossed the Southwest on foot. Alvar Nunez Cabeza de Vaca was a Spanish officer. Estevanico was an enslaved African man.
> **When and where:** 1528 to 1539. Florida, Texas, the Southwest and northern Mexico.

Four men out of an army of hundreds walked from the coast of Texas to Mexico City between 1534 and 1536. Two of them were Alvar Nunez Cabeza de Vaca and a man the Spanish called Estevanico, or Esteban.

The army belonged to Panfilo de Narvaez. It left Spain in June 1527 with about 600 men. In April 1528 Narvaez landed near Tampa Bay, in Florida, with 300 men. Attacks, disease and hunger wore them down. By September the survivors had built rafts to try to reach Mexico by sea. In November 1528 a storm washed about 80 of them onto Galveston Island, off the coast of Texas.

Estevanico was born about the year 1500 in Azemmour, in Morocco, in North Africa. Cabeza de Vaca described him as a Black man who spoke Arabic. In 1522 he was sold as a slave to a Spanish nobleman, Andres Dorantes, who brought him on the expedition.

On the Texas coast, Native people held the survivors as slaves for years. In 1534 the last four escaped: Cabeza de Vaca, Dorantes, Alonso del Castillo Maldonado and Estevanico. They walked west. Along the way they worked as healers among the nations they met. In July 1536 they reached Mexico City. Cabeza de Vaca published an account of the journey in 1542.

After the journey the Spanish viceroy in Mexico, Antonio de Mendoza, bought Estevanico from Dorantes or received him as a gift. A viceroy is the king's governor of a colony. Some evidence suggests that Mendoza freed him.

In 1539 Mendoza sent Estevanico north as the guide for a friar named Marcos de Niza. A friar is a member of a Catholic religious order. Estevanico went ahead of the friar with Native guides from Sonora, in northern Mexico. He was killed at Hawikuh, a Zuni town. Several accounts of his death survive, and they disagree about how and why it happened.

Estevanico was not the first African to explore this land. Juan Garrido had sailed with Ponce de Leon to Florida in 1513.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cabeza-de-vaca-estevanico" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Armies inland: de Soto and Coronado, 1539 to 1542" -->
Two Spanish armies marched deep into the continent between 1539 and 1542. Both fed themselves on the stored food of the towns they passed through.

Hernando de Soto landed in Florida in May 1539 with 620 men and 220 horses. For three years he marched through the Southeast. From October 1539 to March 1540 his army lived in Anhaica, the capital of the Apalachee people, where Tallahassee stands today. His men ate the Apalachee's stored corn. De Soto seized dozens of men, women and children to carry his army's supplies. He took local leaders hostage so that their people would let the army pass. He held the leader of the Coosa people hostage to get through the Coosa towns. At a town called Hymahi, de Soto took all the food. The National Park Service records that he later burned at least one person from the town to death while questioning people about a town farther on, called Cofitachequi.

On October 18, 1540, de Soto's army reached Mabila, in present-day Alabama. It was a town surrounded by a palisade, a wall of tall upright logs. Its leader was a man the Spanish called Tascalusa. A Spanish officer decided that a man of Mabila had not shown him respect. The argument ended with the man's arm cut off, and a battle began. De Soto's men fought for about nine hours and set the town on fire with people still inside. One of the expedition's own record keepers wrote: "We killed them all either with fire or the sword." The National Park Service gives about 2,000 people of Mabila killed and over 200 Spanish. Other accounts give about 20 Spanish killed and 250 wounded. The site of Mabila has never been found.

De Soto died of fever beside the Mississippi River on May 21, 1542. His men sank his body in the river. They did not want the nations they had fought to learn that he could die. The survivors reached Mexico in September 1543.

Francisco Vazquez de Coronado left Compostela, in Mexico, in February 1540. He led about 400 European soldiers, 1,300 to 2,000 Native allies from Mexico and four friars. They were looking for cities of gold. In July 1540, close to starving, they reached the Zuni town of Hawikuh and demanded to be let in. The Zuni had drawn lines of sacred cornmeal on the ground and warned the Spaniards not to cross them. When the Spaniards crossed, the Zuni shot arrows. Coronado's men attacked the town, and the Zuni left it after a short fight.

Coronado sent out scouting parties from there. Pedro de Tovar's men forced their way into a Hopi town that had refused them. Hopi guides then led Garcia Lopez de Cardenas and his men to the South Rim of the Grand Canyon. They were the first Europeans to see it, and Hopi men showed them the way.

In the winter of 1540 to 1541 the army camped along the Rio Grande among the Tiguex pueblos. A pueblo is a town of connected houses built of stone or dried mud. Coronado's men took over the pueblo of Coofor, near present-day Bernalillo, New Mexico, and forced its people out. The National Park Service states that the fighting began after "a Spanish soldier assaulted a Pueblo woman, wife to a Tiguex man." In the war that followed, Coronado's soldiers killed hundreds of Tiguex people and burned their villages. At a pueblo called Arenal they executed dozens of captives. They made Tiguex women and children slaves.

In the spring of 1541 a Native guide the Spanish called "the Turk," probably a Wichita or Pawnee man, led the army out onto the Great Plains. He told them of a rich land called Quivira. At Quivira, in present-day central Kansas, Coronado found the grass houses of Wichita towns and no gold. Before he left, Coronado ordered the Turk garroted. That means he was strangled to death with a cord or an iron collar that was twisted tight around his neck. The army went back to Mexico in 1542.

Spanish officials later charged Coronado and Cardenas with crimes against Native people on the march. Coronado's friends sat on the court in Mexico that heard his case, and they cleared him. A royal council in Spain found Cardenas guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine, 1565" -->
In September 1565 Pedro Menendez de Aviles and about 800 colonists landed on the coast of Florida. The colonists included 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. They settled at the town of Seloy, a Timucua chief, and used Seloy's council house as their first fort. That same month Menendez's men destroyed a French settlement nearby, Fort Caroline. At Matanzas Inlet they killed more than a hundred Frenchmen who had surrendered.

The Timucua soon wanted the Spaniards gone from their town. In 1566 Timucua warriors shot flaming arrows at the fort. Nine months after they arrived, the Spaniards moved their settlement across the bay to Anastasia Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English, Dutch, French and Spanish colonists of the 1600s built small settlements on the Atlantic coast and along the Rio Grande. English colonists built James Fort, at Jamestown in Virginia, in 1607. The Spanish governor Pedro de Peralta founded Santa Fe, in New Mexico, in about 1610. Almost everything between the coasts was Native land that no European had mapped.

The explorers of this century traveled by water, in ships and in canoes. Native guides and hosts told several of them where the rivers went.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Champlain and the lake, 1609" -->
Samuel de Champlain, a Frenchman, explored the coast of New England as far south as Cape Cod in 1605 and 1606. In 1608 he founded Quebec, on the St. Lawrence River in present-day Canada. That first winter, 16 of his 25 men died of scurvy.

In June 1609 Champlain went south with two other Frenchmen and a party of Wendat, Algonquin and Montagnais warriors. The Wendat are also called the Huron. They took him to the lake that now carries his name, between present-day New York and Vermont. In late July, at Ticonderoga, they met a party of Haudenosaunee men. The Haudenosaunee are also called the Iroquois. In the fight that followed, according to the historian Marcel Trudel, Champlain killed two of the Haudenosaunee men.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-hudson" name="Henry Hudson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Hudson

> **Who:** An English sea captain hired by the Dutch East India Company to look for a sea route to Asia.
> **When and where:** 1609 to 1611. The Hudson River in New York, then Hudson Bay in Canada.

In September 1609 Henry Hudson sailed the Dutch ship *Half Moon* up a river in what is now New York. The Lenape called it Muhheakantuck, and the Mohicans called it Mahicannittuk. Today it is called the Hudson River. Hudson was not the first European in its harbor. Verrazzano had been there in 1524. The Lenape and Munsee people lived along the lower river, and the Mohicans lived along the upper river.

Hudson's mate, Robert Juet, kept a journal of the voyage, and most of what is known comes from it. On September 6 two canoes carrying 26 men attacked the ship's small boat. An arrow hit a sailor named John Colman in the throat and killed him. Two other sailors were hurt. On September 9 the crew seized two Native men who had come aboard. They dressed them in red coats to keep them on the ship. Juet's journal does not say what happened to the two men after that.

On the way back down the river, Hudson's crew killed about 10 to 12 Native men, by Juet's own count. On October 1 a man in a canoe climbed in through the cabin window. He took a pillow, two shirts and two belts for carrying bullets. The ship's mate shot him in the chest and killed him. Another man swam up and grabbed the side of the ship's small boat. The ship's cook cut off one of the man's hands with a sword, and the man drowned.

On October 2 men in two canoes shot arrows at the ship. The crew fired their muskets and killed two or three of them. About a hundred men on a point of land shot arrows too. Juet fired a small cannon at them and killed two. He fired again at a canoe with nine or ten men in it and killed one, and the crew shot three or four more. Then the ship anchored near the island that Juet called "Manna-hata." Juet did not write down which nation the dead men belonged to.

On his next voyage Hudson sailed for England in a ship called the *Discovery*. He searched for a northern sea passage in the great bay in Canada that now carries his name. Ice trapped the ship through the winter in James Bay. In June 1611 most of the crew mutinied, which means they rose up against their captain. They put Hudson, his teenage son John and seven sick or loyal men into a small open boat and sailed away. No European saw any of them again. A survivor, Abacuk Pricket, named Henry Greene and Robert Juet as the leaders. Historians question his account, because he blamed men who were already dead. Only eight of the thirteen mutineers lived to reach England. Some were put on trial there, and none was punished.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-hudson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marquette-and-jolliet" name="Marquette and Jolliet" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Marquette and Jolliet

> **Who:** Jacques Marquette, a French Jesuit priest, and Louis Jolliet, a French fur trader.
> **When and where:** May to autumn 1673. From St. Ignace, Michigan, down the Mississippi River to the mouth of the Arkansas River.

In 1673 Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet paddled down the Mississippi River far enough to learn that it flows to the Gulf of Mexico and not to the Pacific Ocean. Native people had told them about the river first. Illinois people had described it to Marquette and invited him to their towns.

The two men left St. Ignace, in present-day Michigan, on May 17, 1673. They had two canoes and five paddlers of French and Native ancestry. On the river, Michigamea warriors almost attacked them. The town's elders stopped the attack and fed the travelers. Farther south the Quapaw people hosted them for two nights near the mouth of the Arkansas River.

On July 17 they turned back, about 435 miles from the Gulf. They feared the Spanish. They had also been told that the nations farther south were well armed and would attack anyone who got in the way of their trade. On the way home they took a shorter route up the Illinois River, which their Native hosts showed them.

Near Montreal, Jolliet's canoe tipped over in the Lachine Rapids, and his notes were lost. Marquette's account became the main record of the journey. Marquette died of dysentery on May 18, 1675, in present-day Michigan. Dysentery is an infection of the gut that causes severe, bloody diarrhea.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marquette-and-jolliet" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-de-la-salle" name="Robert de La Salle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert de La Salle

> **Who:** A French fur trader and explorer who claimed the whole Mississippi River valley for the king of France.
> **When and where:** 1682 to 1687. The lower Mississippi River and the coast of Texas.

Robert de La Salle was the first European known to follow the Mississippi River all the way down to the sea. In early 1682 he led a party by canoe from the mouth of the Illinois River down the Mississippi to the Gulf of Mexico. On the way, his men built a small fort near present-day Memphis, Tennessee.

On April 9, 1682, at the mouth of the river, La Salle claimed the whole Mississippi River basin for France. A river basin is all the land whose rain drains into one river. He named it Louisiana, after King Louis XIV. Many Native nations lived in that basin. None of them was asked. In 1803 French officials sold the claim that began that day to the United States.

In July 1684 La Salle sailed from France with four ships and 300 colonists to build a colony at the river's mouth. The ships missed the river and landed at Matagorda Bay, in Texas, on the land of the Karankawa people. Pirates took one ship. Another sank in the bay. The last, *La Belle*, ran aground. The colonists built their settlement on Garcitas Creek, near present-day Victoria, Texas.

On March 19, 1687, near present-day Navasota, Texas, one of La Salle's own men, Pierre Duhaut, killed him from an ambush. Another man, Jean L'Archeveque, had drawn La Salle into talking with him so that Duhaut could strike. Another man in the party then shot Duhaut. In 1688 Karankawa-speaking men killed the 20 adults still at the colony and took five children captive. Archaeologists found the wreck of *La Belle* in Matagorda Bay in 1995.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists did little exploring beyond the Appalachian Mountains between 1700 and 1750. The journeys into country that no European had mapped came from outside the English colonies. A Russian navy expedition reached the coast of Alaska in 1741. French traders crossed the northern Great Plains in 1742 and 1743, with Mandan guides.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="vitus-bering" name="Vitus Bering" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Vitus Bering

> **Who:** A Danish-born officer in the Russian navy who led Russia's first voyages to Alaska.
> **When and where:** 1728 and 1741. The Bering Strait and the coast of Alaska.

Vitus Bering led the Russian voyage that reached Alaska in 1741. He was born in Horsens, in Denmark, in 1681, and he served as an officer in the navy of Russia. In August 1728 he sailed north through the narrow strip of sea between Asia and North America. It is now called the Bering Strait.

Before the second voyage, men from a detachment of Bering's expedition in Kamchatka, in eastern Siberia, killed several Koryak people in the settlement of Utkolotsk and made the rest slaves. The naturalist on the voyage, Georg Steller, wrote that he was horrified to see Koryak people tortured. A naturalist is a scientist who studies plants and animals.

In 1741 Bering commanded the ship *St. Peter*. On July 16 his crew sighted Mount Saint Elias on the Alaska coast, and they landed briefly at or near Kayak Island. That coast was Native land. Steller described animals that European scientists had not recorded before, among them a bird now called Steller's jay and a sea animal now called Steller's sea cow.

Bering's second ship, the *St. Paul*, was under the command of Aleksei Chirikov. On July 15, 1741, Chirikov's crew sighted land in southeast Alaska. He sent a boat ashore to look for a safe place to anchor. After a week it had not come back, so he sent his second boat, and that one did not come back either. On July 27 Chirikov sailed west without them. What happened to the men in the two boats is not known.

On the way home, scurvy spread through the crew of the *St. Peter*. The ship was wrecked on an island near Kamchatka, now called Bering Island. Bering died there on December 19, 1741. About 28 of his men also died. At the time, people blamed scurvy for all of these deaths. In 1991 a team of Russian and Danish scientists dug up Bering's remains and studied his teeth and bones. They concluded that his own death was more likely caused by heart failure.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="vitus-bering" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The La Verendrye brothers on the northern Plains, 1742 to 1743" -->
Pierre La Verendrye was a French fur trader in what is now Canada. On April 29, 1742, two of his sons and two other Frenchmen left Fort La Reine, at present-day Portage la Prairie, Manitoba. The son who kept the journal, known as the Chevalier, was most likely Louis-Joseph La Verendrye. They reached the Mandan towns on the upper Missouri River on May 19 and waited there two months. On July 23 they set out west with two Mandan guides.

For months they traveled with Plains nations that the French called the Horse People, the Bow People and the Little Cherry People. Historians still disagree about which nations these were. The Bow People may have been Pawnee, and the Little Cherry People were probably Arikara. The Horse People told them that two years before, a nation the French called the Snake People had destroyed seventeen of their villages. The Snake People had killed the men and the old women and taken the young women to sell. Which nation the Snake People were is not certain.

The brothers may have been the first Europeans to see the Rocky Mountains, probably in what is now Wyoming. Their journal is hard to follow, so nobody knows their route for certain. It lay in French archives until it was found in 1851.

On March 30, 1743, the brothers buried a lead plate at the Little Cherry People's fort beside the Missouri River. The plate is six by eight inches. One side has a Latin inscription stamped into it that names King Louis XV, their father and the year 1741. On the other side someone scratched the date and the names of the men who placed it. The plate was dug up at Pierre, South Dakota, in 1913. It is kept in the South Dakota Cultural Heritage Center.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
American and British diplomats signed a peace treaty in Paris on September 3, 1783. It set the western border of the new United States at the Mississippi River. Native nations lived on almost all of the land between the Appalachian Mountains and that river. The diplomats did not invite them to the talks.

Before and after that treaty, colonists from Virginia and North Carolina pushed through the Appalachians into Kentucky, which the Shawnee used as a hunting ground. On the Pacific coast, Spanish soldiers, a British navy captain and an American fur trader reached bays, islands and a river mouth that Europeans had never mapped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-boone" name="Daniel Boone" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Boone

> **Who:** A hunter from North Carolina who marked the first road for colonists through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky.
> **When and where:** 1767 to 1778. The Appalachian Mountains and Kentucky.

Daniel Boone marked the road that colonists used to cross the Appalachian Mountains into Kentucky. He did not discover the way. The Cumberland Gap is a low pass through the Appalachians. Native nations had used it long before any colonist. In 1750 a Virginia doctor named Thomas Walker told colonists about it, and he named the Cumberland River. In 1769 Joseph Martin built a fort near the gap for people who claimed land there, and Native men chased Martin and his men out.

Kentucky was the hunting ground of the Shawnee. The Cherokee and the Haudenosaunee also claimed it. In a treaty signed at Fort Stanwix in 1768, Haudenosaunee leaders gave up their claim to Kentucky to the British. The Shawnee did not sign that treaty.

Boone and his brother Squire first crossed into Kentucky in 1767. In May 1769 Boone set out with five other men on a hunting and trapping trip that lasted two years. One of them, John Findley, had told him about the Cumberland Gap. On December 22, 1769, a Shawnee party caught Boone and his brother-in-law, John Stewart. The Shawnee took all their skins and told them to leave and never come back.

In 1773 Boone led his family and about 50 other people toward Kentucky to settle there. On October 9 a small group left the main party to get supplies. It included Boone's oldest son, James, several white men and two enslaved Black men named Charles and Adam. A band of Delaware, Shawnee and Cherokee men attacked them. The historian John Mack Faragher writes that the band had decided "to send a message of their opposition to settlement." They tortured and killed James Boone and Henry Russell, the son of another settler. They captured Charles. Adam hid in a pile of driftwood and saw what happened. The families turned back.

In 1774 Virginians fought the Shawnee in a war called Dunmore's War. After the Virginians won a battle at Point Pleasant, Shawnee leaders gave up their claims to Kentucky. Then Richard Henderson, a judge from North Carolina, hired Boone to help start a colony in Kentucky, which Henderson called Transylvania. In March 1775 Boone invited leaders of Cherokee towns to a meeting at Sycamore Shoals, where Henderson bought the Cherokee claim to Kentucky.

Boone then led a work party that cut a trail through the Cumberland Gap into central Kentucky. It was later called the Wilderness Road. The work party included Sam, an enslaved Black man, and other enslaved workers. Near present-day Richmond, Kentucky, Native men attacked the camp and killed Sam and his owner. Boone built a settlement called Boonesborough on the Kentucky River.

On July 14, 1776, a war party captured Boone's daughter Jemima and two other girls outside Boonesborough. Boone's men caught up two days later and freed them. In 1778 Shawnee warriors captured Boone himself. He lived among the Shawnee for months before he escaped.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pacific: California, Hawaii and the Columbia, 1769 to 1792" -->
In 1769 a Spanish official, Jose de Galvez, sent an expedition north from San Diego to survey California and to hold it for Spain. The commander was Gaspar de Portola. His party of 74 men included soldiers, an engineer, two friars and Christian Native men from Baja California. They walked past Monterey Bay without recognizing it. In early November 1769 Sergeant Jose Francisco Ortega led a group of scouts over Montara Mountain, south of present-day San Francisco. A huge bay blocked their way north. Ortega's scouts, not Portola himself, were the first Europeans known to see San Francisco Bay.

The expedition turned back on November 11. The expedition's members reported that most of their meetings with Native people along the way were peaceful, and that people gave them directions and food. On November 30 about a dozen Rumsen people brought the starving soldiers pinole, which is flour made from ground seeds.

The British navy captain James Cook reached the Hawaiian Islands on January 18, 1778. He and his crews were the first Europeans known to see them. They landed at Waimea, on the island of Kauai, and on Niihau. While he was ashore collecting supplies, one of Cook's crew, John Williamson, shot and killed a Hawaiian man. Cook's crews were also the first Europeans to bring some diseases to the islands.

Cook came back in late November 1778. At Kealakekua Bay, on the island of Hawaii, about 10,000 people and 1,000 canoes met the ships, and the high chief Kalaniopuu welcomed him. On February 13, 1779, Hawaiians took one of the ships' boats. The next day Cook went ashore to kidnap Kalaniopuu and hold him until the boat was returned. Crowds gathered. Cook fired a warning shot, then shot one Hawaiian man dead. In the fight that followed, Hawaiians killed Cook and four marines, and Cook's men killed seventeen Hawaiians. The accounts of Cook's last moments contradict each other.

Robert Gray was a sea captain from Rhode Island. In 1787 a group of Boston merchants sent him and another captain, John Kendrick, to the Pacific Northwest to buy sea otter furs and sell them in China. In 1790 Gray finished the first voyage by an American ship around the world. On his first trip, in August 1788, his men fought the Tillamook people at Tillamook Bay, in Oregon. Marcus Lopez, Gray's Black cabin boy and cook from the Cape Verde Islands, was killed in the fighting.

On the evening of May 11, 1792, Gray's crew found a safe channel over the sandbars at the mouth of a great river. His ship, the *Columbia Rediviva*, sailed in. Gray named the river the Columbia, after his ship. He was the first navigator known to enter it who was not Native.

The same year, Gray attacked Native villages along the coast. In April 1792 he ordered his men to destroy Opitsaht, a Nuu-chah-nulth village of about 200 houses on Vancouver Island, in present-day Canada. The village was empty at the time. In May he ordered an attack on another Nuu-chah-nulth village, and his men killed seven people and took their sea otter furs. Later in 1792, at Grays Harbor in present-day Washington State, Gray fired on a group of Chinook people and killed 20. One of his officers, John Boit, wrote that Gray "had let his passions go too far." In 2005 Gray's descendants formally apologized for the destruction of Opitsaht.
