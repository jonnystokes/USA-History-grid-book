## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
On April 30, 1803, American diplomats in Paris agreed to pay France $15 million for the right to take Louisiana from the Native nations who lived on most of it. Diplomats are officials who speak for their countries. The United States could now take that land by treaty or by war, and no other European country could get in the way. French officials controlled only a small part of Louisiana. It covered about 828,000 square miles, most of the land west of the Mississippi River whose rain drains into that river. With it, the land the United States claimed nearly doubled.

Over the next fifty years Army officers, fur trappers and a Navy squadron crossed and mapped lands where Native nations already lived. Native guides showed several of them the way. Crow people drew a map that led trappers over South Pass, two men who had run away from Spanish missions, church settlements run by Catholic priests, led a party across the Mojave Desert, and an Ojibwe guide led an American to the source of the Mississippi. Some of these explorers killed the people whose land they crossed.

### Meriwether Lewis and William Clark

> **Who:** Two Army officers sent by President Thomas Jefferson to cross the Louisiana Purchase to the Pacific Ocean.
> **When and where:** May 1804 to September 1806. From near St. Louis up the Missouri River, over the Rocky Mountains and down the Columbia River to the Pacific coast of Oregon.
> **Movie:** *The Far Horizons* (1955), a film about the expedition.

Meriwether Lewis and William Clark led an Army expedition from near St. Louis, through the new Louisiana Purchase and beyond it, to the Pacific Ocean and back. President Thomas Jefferson gave them written orders. They were to find "the most direct & practicable water communication across this continent for the purpose of commerce." In plain words, Jefferson wanted a river route that traders could use to cross the continent. Members of Congress voted $2,324 for supplies.

Jefferson also ordered them to tell the Native nations they met that their land now belonged to the United States. They were to say that the president in Washington, whom they called "their great father," now ruled over them. Lewis and Clark carried silver peace medals with Jefferson's face on them. They carried an air rifle to show off American weapons. American officials used the expedition's maps, medals and flags as legal steps to claim each nation's land. They relied on an idea in American law called the doctrine of discovery. Under it, the country whose explorers claimed a land first had the right, ahead of every other European or American country, to take it from the nations living there.

On May 14, 1804, Clark and about 30 men left Camp Dubois, in Illinois, near St. Louis. Lewis joined them at St. Charles, Missouri. In all the party had as many as 45 members. They included officers, soldiers, volunteers and York, a man Clark held as a slave. Sergeant Charles Floyd died on August 20, 1804, probably of appendicitis, an infection of a small part of the gut. He was the only member of the party to die on the journey.

On September 25, 1804, near present-day Fort Pierre, South Dakota, Lakota chiefs, among them Black Buffalo, demanded gifts before they would let the boats pass. Lakota warriors took hold of a boat and an oar. Lewis ordered his men to aim their guns, and Clark drew his sword. Black Buffalo ordered his warriors back, and nobody was killed. In his journal Clark called the Lakota the "vilest miscreants of the savage race," meaning the worst villains.

The party spent the winter of 1804 to 1805 at Fort Mandan, beside the Mandan and Hidatsa towns in present-day North Dakota. There Lewis and Clark, the expedition's two captains, hired a French-Canadian trader, Toussaint Charbonneau, and met Sacagawea, a young Shoshone woman he held as his wife. In the spring they went on up the Missouri to the streams where it begins. They crossed the Continental Divide at Lemhi Pass. The Continental Divide is the line of high ground that separates rivers flowing east from rivers flowing west. They crossed the Bitterroot Mountains at Lolo Pass and then went down the Clearwater, Snake and Columbia rivers by canoe. They found no water route across the continent. What they found instead was a Native trail that linked the Missouri River to the Columbia.

They first saw the Pacific on November 7, 1805. On November 24 Clark wrote down every member's choice for a place to spend the winter, including the choices of York and of Sacagawea. Accounts differ about whether this counts as a vote. The party built Fort Clatsop near present-day Astoria, Oregon. Food ran short that winter, and they had too little money to buy enough food from the Native nations nearby.

On the way home the party split up. Lewis took three men: George Drouillard and the brothers Joseph and Reubin Field. On the night of July 26, 1806, the four camped with some Blackfeet men on the Two Medicine River in present-day Montana. At dawn the Blackfeet men took the Americans' guns and tried to drive off their horses. Reubin Field stabbed one of the Blackfeet men to death. Lewis shot another man in the belly. The National Park Service says two or three Blackfeet were killed. Lewis wrote down no names for them. He left a peace medal around the neck of a dead man "that they might be informed who we were." It was the only fight on the expedition in which anyone was killed.

The expedition reached St. Louis on September 23, 1806. The captains had met more than 70 Native nations and drawn about 140 maps. Every man in the party except York received double pay and 320 acres of land.

### Sacagawea

> **Who:** A Lemhi Shoshone woman who interpreted for Lewis and Clark while carrying her baby son.
> **When and where:** 1805 to 1806. From Fort Mandan in North Dakota to the Pacific and back.

Sacagawea was the interpreter who spoke for the expedition with her own Shoshone people. An interpreter is a person who puts one language into another. She was born about 1788 near present-day Salmon, Idaho, among the Lemhi Shoshone. Most accounts say she was Shoshone. A Hidatsa account written in 2021, *Our Story of Eagle Woman*, states that she was Hidatsa by birth.

In 1800, when she was about 12, Hidatsa raiders captured her and several other children. The raiders killed four Shoshone men, four women and several boys. She was held at a Hidatsa village in present-day North Dakota. At about 13 she was forced into marriage with Toussaint Charbonneau, a French-Canadian trader. She did not agree to it. Accounts say he either bought her and another Shoshone girl, Otter Woman, from the Hidatsa, or won Sacagawea gambling. Records of the expedition describe Charbonneau as her husband and her owner.

Her son, Jean Baptiste, was born at Fort Mandan on February 11, 1805. Lewis gave her crushed rattlesnake rattle to speed the birth. She carried her son on the whole journey.

In August 1805 the party met a band of Shoshone. Its leader, Cameahwait, turned out to be her brother. Sacagawea interpreted, and the Shoshone traded horses to the party for the crossing of the mountains. She also met a woman who had been taken prisoner at the same time as she was.

She was mainly the expedition's interpreter, not its guide. On the way home she did show the way in country she knew. On July 13, 1806, she told Clark to cross into the valley of the Yellowstone River through the pass now called Bozeman Pass, in Montana. Clark wrote that she "has been of great service to me as a pilot through this Country." In the journals the men called her "the wife of our interpreter" and "the Snake woman." Snake was their name for the Shoshone. They also called her by an insulting word for a Native woman.

A clerk at a fur post on the Missouri River, John Luttig, wrote that "the wife of Charbonneau" died of a fever on December 20, 1812, at about age 25. The post was in present-day South Dakota. Most accounts accept that record. In Shoshone tradition, she lived until 1884 and died on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming. A reservation is land the US government set aside for a Native nation. The Hidatsa account also disputes the 1812 record. In 1813 William Clark became the legal guardian of her son and her daughter, Lizette. That meant the law made him responsible for the children.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="york" name="York" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### York

> **Who:** A Black man held as a slave by William Clark, who took him on the Lewis and Clark expedition.
> **When and where:** 1804 to about 1815. The journey to the Pacific, then Kentucky and Missouri.

York crossed the continent with Lewis and Clark as a slave, and he came home still a slave. He was born into slavery in Caroline County, Virginia. His parents, Old York and Rose, were enslaved by John Clark III. In 1799 William Clark inherited York under his father's will. Before the expedition York married a woman who was enslaved by a different owner, near Louisville, Kentucky.

On the journey York hunted with a gun. Enslaved men were usually not allowed to carry one. On November 24, 1805, Clark wrote down York's choice for the winter camp alongside everyone else's.

After the expedition every other man received double pay and 320 acres of land. York received nothing. He asked Clark for his freedom because of his services on the journey. He also asked to be hired out near his wife in Louisville, or to be sold to someone there. Clark refused, and he wanted York hired out to "a Severe Master." In 1808 Clark wrote that York had "Such a notion about freedom and his emence Services." He meant that York believed his great services on the journey had earned him his freedom. Clark did not agree. In July 1809 Clark whipped York, giving him what Clark called "a Severe trouncing," and had him put in jail.

Clark rented York out to work for other people as a wagon driver in Louisville. The last record of him in that work is from 1815. In 1811 his wife's owner planned to move to Natchez, Mississippi, far to the south, and York probably never saw her again. The National Park Service states that York was freed sometime after 1815. No paper freeing him has been found. Nobody knows for certain when or how he died.

In 2001 President Bill Clinton made York an honorary sergeant in the US Army.

### Zebulon Pike

> **Who:** A US Army lieutenant sent to explore the northern and southern edges of the Louisiana Purchase.
> **When and where:** 1805 to 1807. Minnesota, then Colorado, New Mexico and northern Mexico.

Zebulon Pike led two Army expeditions to the edges of the Louisiana Purchase. His commander, General James Wilkinson, sent him out. Pike left St. Louis on August 9, 1805, to find where the Mississippi River begins. On September 21, 1805, at the mouth of the Minnesota River, he bought land from the Dakota people for a future Army fort. He named Cass Lake, in present-day Minnesota, as the river's source, but the true source is a lake farther up. An Ojibwe guide showed it to an American in 1832.

In 1806 Wilkinson sent him west to find where the Arkansas and Red rivers begin, near the Spanish lands of New Mexico and Texas. In November 1806 Pike's men tried to climb the tall peak in present-day Colorado that later took his name. They turned back in snow up to their waists. They had gone almost two days without food.

Pike's party crossed into Spanish land. No one knows whether he got lost or went there on purpose. On February 26, 1807, Spanish soldiers arrested Pike and some of his men. The soldiers took them to Santa Fe and then to Chihuahua, in Mexico. There the Spanish commander, Nemesio Salcedo, questioned Pike and took his papers. The Spanish released Pike at the Louisiana border on July 1, 1807. Accounts still differ about whether Pike was an explorer or a spy for Wilkinson.

Pike was killed on April 27, 1813, in the War of 1812, during an American attack on the town of York, now Toronto, in Canada. By then he was a general. As British soldiers pulled back, they blew up their store of gunpowder, and the blast killed Pike and 37 other American soldiers.

### John Colter

> **Who:** A soldier of the Lewis and Clark expedition who stayed in the mountains to trap.
> **When and where:** 1806 to 1809. Montana and Wyoming.

In the winter of 1807 to 1808 John Colter walked alone through the country that is now Yellowstone National Park. He was the first person of European descent known to see it. He had been a member of the Lewis and Clark expedition. On August 13, 1806, at the Mandan towns, the captains let him leave early to guide two trappers back up the Missouri River.

In 1807 Colter joined a fur party led by the trader Manuel Lisa. In October 1807 Lisa sent him out alone to find the Crow people and ask them to trade. That winter Colter walked more than 500 miles through the country that is now Yellowstone and Grand Teton national parks. He came back in March or April 1808. Few people believed his reports of hot springs, and they called the place "Colter's Hell."

In 1808 Colter fought beside Flathead and Crow men against the Blackfeet, and he was wounded in the leg. Writers have long blamed Lewis's fight of 1806 for the Blackfeet's anger at Americans, and others dispute that. In 1808 or 1809 he and another trapper, John Potts, were paddling a canoe on the Jefferson River in present-day Montana. Several hundred Blackfeet men ordered them ashore. Colter came ashore, and the Blackfeet took his weapons and stripped him naked. Potts refused to come ashore. Blackfeet men shot and wounded him. He shot one of them, and then they killed him with arrows.

The Blackfeet told Colter to run, and they chased him. He ran about five miles to the Madison River. He killed the one man who caught up with him, using the man's own spear. Then he hid in a beaver lodge and walked for eleven days to a trading fort. All of this comes from Colter's own telling, as other people retold it later.

Colter died of jaundice in 1812 or 1813. Jaundice is an illness that turns the skin and eyes yellow. The sources disagree on the date.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-colter" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first recorded climb of Pikes Peak, 1820" -->
On July 14, 1820, three men from an Army expedition led by Major Stephen Long reached the top of the mountain now called Pikes Peak. One of them was Edwin James, a 22-year-old naturalist, a scientist who studies plants and animals. It is the first recorded climb to the top. The Ute people call the mountain Tava-kaavi. Their tradition says that Ute ancestors were the first to climb it. Long called the high plains a "Great American Desert."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Trappers and the fur trade, 1822 to 1840" -->
In 1822 two fur traders, William Ashley and Andrew Henry, put an advertisement in a St. Louis newspaper. They wanted one hundred "enterprising young men" to go up the Missouri River to its source and work there for one, two or three years. They paid $200 a year. The men who answered became known as Ashley's Hundred. Jedediah Smith, Jim Bridger and Hugh Glass were among the men who joined Ashley's company.

In late May 1823 Ashley's boats reached the towns of the Arikara people on the Missouri River, in present-day South Dakota, to buy horses. The Arikara were angry after a fight with men of another fur company, and they wanted a trading post of their own. On June 2, 1823, Arikara men attacked Ashley's party. Records give two counts of Ashley's men killed, 12 and 13. In August an Army officer, Henry Leavenworth, led about 230 soldiers, 750 Lakota and Yankton warriors and about 50 trappers against the Arikara towns. His men fired cannon at the towns. Leavenworth and the Arikara then agreed to a peace, and the Arikara left their towns at night. Men of the Missouri Fur Company then burned one of the towns. No reliable count of the Arikara killed has survived.

From 1825 to 1840 the trappers met every summer at a gathering they called the rendezvous, in the mountains of present-day Wyoming, Utah or Idaho. The word is French for a meeting. There the trappers sold their furs and bought supplies for the next year. Native people came too, and so did the Native wives and children of many trappers.

In 1832 an Ojibwe guide named Ozaawindib led Henry Rowe Schoolcraft to the true source of the Mississippi River. Schoolcraft was an Indian agent, a US official who dealt with Native nations, at Sault Ste. Marie, in Michigan. The source was a lake that the Ojibwe called Elk Lake. Schoolcraft renamed it Lake Itasca. He made up the name from pieces of the Latin words for "truth" and "head."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Hugh Glass

> **Who:** A fur trapper who survived a grizzly bear attack after the men left to watch over him abandoned him.
> **When and where:** 1823. Northwestern South Dakota.
> **Movie:** *The Revenant* (2015), a film based on his story. An earlier film, *Man in the Wilderness* (1971), is based on it too.

Hugh Glass crawled and walked about 200 miles to safety after a grizzly bear tore the flesh from his body. The two men left to care for him had taken his gun and gone. He was born about 1783. In 1823 he joined Ashley's fur company, and he was shot in the leg in the Arikara attack of June 2. A letter he wrote to the parents of John Gardner, a trapper killed that day, still survives.

Late that summer, near the forks of the Grand River in present-day South Dakota, Glass surprised a mother grizzly with two cubs. The bear bit him and slashed him with her claws. Another trapper later said she had "torn the flesh from the lower part of the body, and from the lower limbs," and torn his neck badly. His leg was broken. The cuts on his back were so deep that his ribs showed through.

Andrew Henry, the leader of the party, asked for two men to stay with Glass until he died and then bury him. John Fitzgerald and a man written down as "Bridges" stayed. They dug a grave. Then they left, taking Glass's rifle and knife, and told the others he was dead. Whether "Bridges" was Jim Bridger is still argued. Bridger later denied it.

Glass set his own broken leg and wrapped himself in the bear's hide. He crawled and walked about 200 miles to Fort Kiowa on the Missouri River, and part of the way he floated on a raft down the Cheyenne River. The journey took six weeks. He let maggots eat the dead flesh in his wounds, to keep the wounds from turning to gangrene. Gangrene is the rotting of dead flesh in a living body. Glass later found "Bridges" and forgave him. Fitzgerald had joined the Army, and Glass got his rifle back.

The story was first printed in 1825, in a Philadelphia magazine called *The Port Folio*. A writer named James Hall wrote it, and other accounts dispute parts of it. No diary by Glass tells the story. It comes from other people's later retellings. In early 1833 Arikara warriors killed Glass and two other trappers, Edward Rose and Hilain Menard, on the Yellowstone River.

### Jedediah Smith

> **Who:** A fur trapper from New York who led the first party of US citizens known to reach California over land.
> **When and where:** 1823 to 1831. The Rocky Mountains, California and Oregon.

Jedediah Smith led the first party of US citizens known to reach California over land. He was born in New York in 1799 and answered Ashley's advertisement in 1822. In the autumn of 1823, in the Black Hills region, a grizzly bear broke his ribs and took his head in its mouth. It tore off his scalp and one ear. Smith told another trapper, Jim Clyman, how to sew them back on, and Clyman did. Afterward Smith wore his hair long to hide the scar.

In 1824, in the Wind River valley of present-day Wyoming, Crow people drew a map in sand and on a buffalo hide to show Smith's men the way west. The map led them over South Pass, a crossing of the Rocky Mountains in present-day Wyoming. The Crow already used the pass. A trader named Robert Stuart had crossed it in 1812, but his crossing was kept secret. Smith's party is credited with making the pass known to Americans.

In August 1826 Smith led 15 men southwest toward California. Mojave people near present-day Needles, California, sheltered them. Two men who had run away from the Spanish missions guided them west across the Mojave Desert. On November 27, 1826, they reached Mission San Gabriel. California then belonged to Mexico. The Mexican governor, Jose Maria Echeandia, arrested Smith as a suspected spy and held him for about two weeks. In 1827 Smith's party became the first US citizens known to cross the Sierra Nevada mountains and the Great Basin desert, going east.

In August 1827 Smith came back with 18 men and two French-Canadian women. As they crossed the Colorado River, Mojave men attacked them. Trappers from Taos, in New Mexico, had fought the Mojave, and the Mojave were set on revenge. They killed ten of Smith's men, among them Silas Gobel, and took the two women captive. Smith's men shot and killed two Mojave men.

On July 11, 1828, on the Umpqua River in present-day Oregon, an Umpqua man took an axe from Smith's camp. Smith and another man tied a cord around the Umpqua man's neck while the rest of the party held their guns ready. On July 14 Umpqua men attacked the camp while Smith and two others were away. They killed 15 of Smith's men. One man, Arthur Black, escaped.

On May 27, 1831, Smith left a wagon train on the Santa Fe Trail to look for water near the Cimarron River, in present-day Kansas. He did not come back. Weeks later his partners learned that Comanche men had killed him. His body was never found.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jedediah-smith" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jim-bridger" name="Jim Bridger" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jim Bridger

> **Who:** A trapper and guide from Virginia who never learned to read or write.
> **When and where:** 1823 to 1881. Wyoming, Utah and Montana.

Jim Bridger was one of the first two men who were not Native known to reach the Great Salt Lake. He was born in Richmond, Virginia, in 1804. His parents died when he was 13, and he never learned to read or write. In 1823, at 19, he joined Ashley's trappers.

In the fall of 1824 Bridger floated down the Bear River in a bull boat, a boat made of buffalo hide, and reached the Great Salt Lake. A French-Canadian trader, Etienne Provost, reached the lake at about the same time. In October 1824 Shoshone men attacked Provost's party at the Jordan River and killed eight of his men. The Shoshone, the Ute and other nations already lived around the lake.

In 1843 Bridger and his partner Louis Vasquez built Fort Bridger, a trading post on the Oregon Trail in present-day Wyoming. In 1850 Bridger found a crossing of the mountains now called Bridger Pass. He later worked as a scout for the Army. By 1875 he was blind, and he died on July 17, 1881. He may have been the "Bridges" who left Hugh Glass in 1823. He denied it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jim-bridger" -->

### Charles Wilkes

> **Who:** A US Navy lieutenant who led the country's first large exploring voyage paid for by the US government.
> **When and where:** August 1838 to June 1842. The Pacific islands, Antarctica and the Pacific Northwest.

Charles Wilkes commanded the United States Exploring Expedition, the first large exploring voyage paid for by the US government. Members of Congress approved it in May 1836. Wilkes was only a lieutenant, and he got the command in April 1838 after more senior officers turned it down. On August 18, 1838, he left Virginia with six ships and nearly 350 men. They included naturalists, a mineralogist, who studies rocks, and a scholar of languages. One ship, the *Sea Gull*, went missing off South America in 1839.

On January 16, 1840, two officers on the ship *Peacock* sighted mountain peaks in the far south. The ships followed the coast. In February Wilkes wrote that they had "discovered the Antarctic Continent." His ships charted about 1,500 miles of that coast, which is now called Wilkes Land. French and British ships were exploring the same waters that year.

In the Fiji Islands in 1840, Wilkes's men burned villages and killed Fijians. On July 12 Wilkes had his men burn the villages of Solevu and Tye after people from Solevu took one of his boats. An officer, William Hudson, took a Fijian chief named Vendovi prisoner by holding his brothers hostage. Americans blamed Vendovi for killing American sailors in 1834. Vendovi died in the Navy hospital in New York in June 1842, soon after the ships came home. The expedition's surgeons cut off his head, and his skull became part of the first collections of the Smithsonian Institution. In December 2025 his remains were returned to his home district of Rewa, in Fiji, and buried in the chiefs' burial ground at Lomanikoro.

Later in July 1840, off Malolo Island, Lieutenant Joseph Underwood held the son of a village chief hostage while he bargained for food. The young man jumped from the boat, and the Americans fired over his head. Fighting began. Fijians killed Underwood and Wilkes's nephew, Midshipman Wilkes Henry. The Americans killed about ten Fijians that day. Accounts disagree over whether the escape or the shot started the fight.

The next day Wilkes landed about 70 men on Malolo. He ordered them to kill the men, to burn the villages and to "spare only the women and the children." His men burned the villages of Sualib and Arro and killed between 74 and 104 Fijians. In 1841 men from the *Peacock* fought about 700 islanders on Drummond's Island, in the Gilbert Islands, killed twelve and burned two villages.

In 1841 Wilkes's ships mapped Puget Sound, in present-day Washington State, and his men mapped more than 800 miles of Oregon. The *Peacock* was wrecked on the sandbar at the mouth of the Columbia. The expedition came home to New York on June 10, 1842. It had sailed about 87,000 miles and lost two ships and 28 men. It brought back more than 60,000 plant and bird specimens, which helped start the collections of the Smithsonian Institution in Washington. It also brought 254 live plants, which started what became the US Botanic Garden.

Back home, Navy officers tried Wilkes in a court-martial, a trial held by the military. They cleared him of losing the *Peacock* and of mistreating his officers. They found him guilty of illegally punishing sailors in his squadron. His punishments had included flogging, which means beating a man's bare back with a whip. Navies of that time used a whip of nine knotted cords, called a cat-o'-nine-tails, which was made to tear the skin and cause great pain. One charge named flogging round the fleet, a sentence under which a sailor was whipped aboard each ship of the squadron in turn.

### John C. Frémont

> **Who:** An Army mapmaker, later known as "the Pathfinder," whose reports and maps guided thousands of travelers west.
> **When and where:** 1842 to 1849. The Oregon Trail, the Great Basin, the Sierra Nevada, California and Oregon.

John C. Frémont led four expeditions to map the West, and on the third one his men killed at least 120 Wintu people in California. He was born in 1813. In 1841 he married Jessie Benton, the daughter of Senator Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri. Benton got the government to pay for Frémont's expeditions. A mountain man, a trapper who lived in the western mountains, named Kit Carson guided him. Frémont and Jessie wrote the reports of the expeditions together.

In 1842 Frémont led 25 men up the Platte River to South Pass. In 1843 and 1844 he went on to the Columbia River with almost 40 men and a cannon. Then he turned south and, in winter, crossed the Sierra Nevada into California. He was one of the first Americans to see Lake Tahoe. He showed that no river drains the Great Basin to the sea. The Great Basin is a huge dry region between the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada. Members of the Senate and of the House each ordered 10,000 copies of his report on this trip. In 1846 members of the Senate had seven maps from his work printed. Thousands of American travelers heading west used them as a guide.

In 1845 Frémont set out again, with an armed party of about 60 men. Officials of the War Department secretly told him to act as a soldier if war with Mexico began. On April 5, 1846, some settlers claimed that Native people were about to attack them. Frémont's party attacked a camp of Wintu people on the Sacramento River, near present-day Redding, California. Most of the people in the camp were women and children, and they were trapped against the river. One of Frémont's men, Thomas Breckenridge, wrote that "the order was given to ask no quarter and to give none." That meant no one would be spared, even people who tried to surrender.

Frémont's men fired their rifles in volleys, then charged with swords and pistols. Men on horseback chased and killed people who ran. Riflemen shot people who were swimming in the river. Breckenridge counted 120 to 150 dead, and another of Frémont's men, Thomas Martin, counted over 175. An eyewitness, William Isaac Tustin, said at least 600 to 700 were killed on land and 200 or more in the water. Kit Carson, who took part, later called it "a perfect butchery." None of Frémont's men was killed or wounded. Nobody was ever charged.

After that, Frémont's men shot Native people on sight. Breckenridge said the men "had orders while in camp or on the move to shoot Indians on sight." On the night of May 9, 1846, Klamath men attacked Frémont's camp and killed two or three of his party. On May 12 Kit Carson led an attack on Dokdokwas, a Klamath village at the mouth of the Williamson River, in present-day Oregon, and killed at least 14 people. The people of Dokdokwas may have had nothing to do with the first attack. Nobody was charged for these killings either. In June 1846 Frémont joined a revolt by American settlers against Mexican rule in California, the start of his part in the war with Mexico.

In the winter of 1848 to 1849 Frémont tried to find a route for a railroad through the San Juan Mountains of Colorado. By December 12, 1848, the party needed ninety minutes to move 300 yards, and the mules had begun to die. In the end ten of the men died. Survivors partly ate at least one of the bodies. Ute warriors later killed two of the men, Benjamin Kern and "Old Bill" Williams, when they went back for the party's gear. In 1856 members of the new Republican Party made Frémont their first candidate for president. He died in 1890.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900 American explorers turned from finding routes across the West to measuring it in detail. After the Civil War members of Congress paid for four survey teams to map the western lands. Native nations lived on the land the surveyors mapped, and one survey leader's orders told him to count them and to choose places for Army forts. In 1867 Secretary of State William Seward agreed to buy Alaska from Russia, and in 1885 an Army officer crossed part of its interior.

By 1890 the superintendent of the census wrote that there could "hardly be said to be a frontier line" left between settled and unsettled land. American explorers had already turned toward the North Pole. Two of their voyages ended with most of the men dead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jim-beckwourth" name="Jim Beckwourth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jim Beckwourth

> **Who:** A Black fur trapper, born into slavery, who lived with the Crow and found a low pass through the Sierra Nevada.
> **When and where:** 1824 to 1866. The Rocky Mountains, California and Colorado.

Jim Beckwourth is credited with finding the lowest pass through the Sierra Nevada mountains in California. He turned an old Native path over it into a trail and was never paid for the work. He was born into slavery in Frederick County, Virginia, in 1798 or 1800. His father, Jennings Beckwith, was a white planter who held Beckwourth's mother, a Black woman, as a slave. His father freed him in papers filed in court in 1824, 1825 and 1826.

In 1824 Beckwourth joined Ashley's fur company. For eight or nine years he lived with a band of Crow people, who named him "Bull's Robe." He married the daughter of a chief and rose from warrior to chief. His own book says he became the Crow's highest war chief. Other accounts suggest his fur company planned his stay so that it could win the Crow people's trade.

In 1850 Beckwourth was credited with finding the pass now named for him. At 5,221 feet above sea level, it is the lowest crossing of the Sierra Nevada in California. In 1851 he improved an old Native path over it into a trail that ran down to the town of Marysville. It saved gold seekers and settlers about 150 miles and kept them off steep passes such as Donner Pass.

By Beckwourth's own account, the merchants of Marysville had promised to pay him for the trail. When he came to collect in 1851, two large fires had burned the town, and its leaders were unable to pay him. In 1996 the largest park in Marysville was named Beckwourth Riverfront Park, to recognize the debt the town still owed him.

In the winter of 1854 to 1855 Beckwourth told the story of his life to a writer, Thomas Bonner. Bonner published it as a book in 1856 and never paid Beckwourth his half of the money. How much of the book is accurate is in doubt.

In 1864 Colonel John Chivington hired Beckwourth as a scout. On November 29, 1864, Chivington's soldiers attacked a camp of Cheyenne people at Sand Creek, in Colorado. The commander of a nearby Army fort had told the Cheyenne they would be safe there, and the camp was flying an American flag. Chivington's soldiers killed an estimated 70 to 163 Cheyenne. Other counts give about 230 dead, most of them women, children and old people, among the Cheyenne and Arapaho in the camp. Soldiers cut scalps and other parts from the bodies of the dead. After that the Cheyenne refused to trade with Beckwourth. He died in a Crow village in Montana in 1866. Jim Bridger and a newspaper editor, William Byers, said the Crow poisoned him. Severe high blood pressure is the more likely cause.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jim-beckwourth" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Surveys of the West, 1867 to 1879" -->
In 1867 the geologist Clarence King began a survey along the 40th parallel, from eastern California to Wyoming. A parallel is an east-west line on a map. A geologist is a scientist who studies rocks and the earth. In 1872 King's team found a field in Colorado where diamonds had supposedly been found, and showed that the diamond field was a fake.

In 1871 members of Congress gave Ferdinand Hayden $40,000 to survey the Yellowstone country. His party of 32 included the photographer William Henry Jackson and the painter Thomas Moran. The railroad financier Jay Cooke arranged for Moran to go. Cooke wanted to make Yellowstone known to help the Northern Pacific Railroad. Jackson took the first known photographs of Yellowstone's waterfalls. The pictures and Hayden's report helped persuade members of Congress to make Yellowstone a public park. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the law on March 1, 1872.

A band of Mountain Shoshone called the Tukudika had lived in the Yellowstone country for thousands of years. In the fall of 1879 the park's superintendent, Philetus Norris, found a Tukudika camp and asked the US Indian agent at Fort Washakie for help. The agent sent a party of Shoshone men to take the Tukudika to the Wind River Reservation, in Wyoming.

From 1872 to 1879 Army Lieutenant George Wheeler mapped the land west of the 100th meridian. A meridian is a north-south line on a map, and the 100th meridian runs through the Great Plains. His orders told him to map at eight miles to the inch. They also told him to "discover the numbers, habits, and disposition of Indians in the section" and to "select sites for future military installations." In plain words, he was to count the Native people, learn how they lived and whether they were friendly, and pick places for Army forts.

In 1879 members of Congress ended the Wheeler, King and Powell surveys and moved their work into a new agency, the US Geological Survey. Clarence King was its first director.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alaska, 1867 to 1885" -->
At 4 in the morning on March 30, 1867, Secretary of State William Seward and a Russian diplomat, Eduard de Stoeckl, signed a treaty. Under it, Seward agreed to pay $7.2 million for Alaska, about 2 cents an acre, for 586,412 square miles, and de Stoeckl agreed for Russia to sell it. Most reactions in the United States were positive. Some opponents called it "Seward's Folly" or "Seward's Icebox." Seward's own figures counted about 8,000 Native people under Russian rule and about 50,000 more living beyond it. Neither Seward nor the Russian officials asked any of them.

In the spring of 1885 Army Lieutenant Henry Allen set out to cross Alaska with two soldiers, Sergeant Cady Robertson and Private Fred Fickett, and $2,000. They went up the Copper River. At the Native village of Taral a prospector, John Bremner, joined them. A prospector is a person who searches for gold or other minerals. The people of Taral showed them how to build and use boats made of animal skins. The men ate rotten meat full of worms. They crossed the Alaska Range by a pass that Allen named Miles Pass and went down the Tanana River and up the Koyukuk, about 1,500 miles in all. Two of the men got scurvy, a disease caused by a severe lack of vitamin C, which comes from fresh food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-wesley-powell" name="John Wesley Powell" movie="Ten Who Dared (1960)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Wesley Powell

> **Who:** A Civil War officer who had lost an arm and who led the first recorded boat journey by white men through the whole Grand Canyon.
> **When and where:** May to August 1869. The Green and Colorado rivers in Wyoming, Utah and Arizona.
> **Movie:** *Ten Who Dared* (1960), a Disney film about the expedition.

John Wesley Powell had only one arm when he led the first recorded boat trip by white men through the whole Grand Canyon. He was born in 1834. At the Battle of Shiloh, in April 1862, he was giving the order to fire when a rifle bullet hit his right arm. He lost the arm below the elbow. The raw nerve endings hurt him for the rest of his life. He went back to the Army and fought at Vicksburg.

On May 24, 1869, Powell and nine other men set out from Green River, in Wyoming Territory, in four wooden boats. At a rapid they named Disaster Falls they wrecked and lost one boat, the *No Name*. They named other places along the way, among them Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon. One man, Frank Goodman, left the trip in July.

On August 28 three men, Oramel Howland, his brother Seneca Howland and William Dunn, left the river. They were afraid of the rapids ahead, and they climbed out of the canyon. They were never seen again. Shivwits Paiute men later told Powell's interpreter that they had killed the three by mistake. The Shivwits said they had taken them for prospectors who had raped and killed a Shivwits woman. Other accounts say Mormon settlers from Toquerville, Utah, killed them instead. Mormons are members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. No one knows for certain.

Powell and five men reached the Mormon settlement of St. Thomas, Nevada, on August 30, 1869. They had traveled almost 930 miles in 98 days. They were the first white men recorded passing through the whole canyon. Paiute people and other Native nations had lived along it long before them.

Powell went down the river a second time in 1871 and 1872. From 1881 to 1894 he led the US Geological Survey.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-wesley-powell" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Toward the North Pole, 1879 to 1898" -->
In 1879 Navy Lieutenant George De Long sailed the *Jeannette* north through the Bering Strait with 33 men. James Gordon Bennett Jr., the owner of the *New York Herald* newspaper, paid for the voyage. De Long hoped to find an open sea around the North Pole. Ice trapped the ship for nearly two years and crushed it on June 13, 1881. The men dragged their boats across the ice and then sailed for the Lena River in Siberia. One boat, under Lieutenant Charles Chipp, was lost with all eight men in it. De Long's own party landed in the river's delta and starved to death there. His last journal entry, on October 30, 1881, lists the dead and ends "Mr Collins dying." Twenty of the 33 men died, De Long among them. The voyage proved that there is no open sea at the pole.

In 1881 Army Lieutenant Adolphus Greely took 25 men to Ellesmere Island, in Canada's far north. They were to record the weather as part of the First International Polar Year, when scientists from several countries studied the far north and far south at the same time. Two of his men, Lieutenant James Lockwood and Sergeant David Brainard, set a new record for the farthest north anyone had reached. Two Greenland Inuit dog drivers, Jens Edward and Thorlip Frederik Christiansen, went with them. Supply ships failed to reach the party in 1882 and again in 1883. The men spent their last winter at Cape Sabine with about 40 days of food. Private Charles B. Henry stole food again and again. On June 6, 1884, Greely wrote an order: "Private Henry will be shot to-day." The order warned that Henry was stronger than any two of the other men. Three men, David Brainard, Francis Long and Julius Fredericks, shot him at 2 in the afternoon. On Greely's order, two of their guns held bullets and one held a blank. When rescue ships arrived on June 22, 1884, seven men were still alive. One died soon after, so six of the 25 lived to go home. Apart from Henry, the dead died of hunger, cold and drowning. In August 1884 doctors examined the body of one dead officer, Lieutenant Frederick Kislingbury, and found flesh cut from the bones. The survivors said they knew nothing about anyone eating the dead.

Robert Peary was a Navy officer who later led expeditions toward the pole. In 1894 he reached the Cape York meteorite in northern Greenland. A meteorite is a rock that falls to Earth from space. Inuit people had relied on it to make tools. Peary took it. In 1896 Franz Boas, a scientist at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, asked Peary to bring an Inuk, one of the Inuit, to the museum. In 1897 Peary deceived six Inuit into sailing with him to New York. He promised they could go home within a year with tools, weapons and gifts, and he did not keep that promise. The six arrived in September 1897. Staff at the museum kept them in its basement, and 20,000 people paid to look at them.

By November 1 all six had tuberculosis, a lung disease that spreads through the air. Four died in a New York hospital in 1898: Qisuk on February 17, Atangana and Nuktaq that spring, and a girl, Aviaq, on May 24. Qisuk's son, Minik, who was about seven, survived. The museum's scientific staff wanted to keep Qisuk's body for study. They filled a coffin with stones, laid a stuffed shape covered with a cloth on top, and held a fake burial so that Minik would believe his father had been buried. The museum's building manager, William Wallace, had Qisuk's flesh stripped from the bones at his workshop, and the skeleton was put on display in the museum. Wallace then adopted Minik.

Of the two who lived, a young man named Uisaakassak demanded to go home to Greenland. He sailed home on the ship *Windward* in July 1898. Minik stayed in the United States. About 1906 New York newspapers reported that his father's skeleton was on display, and Minik learned it from his classmates. He died in the flu epidemic of 1918. In 1993 a Canadian writer, Kenn Harper, got the remains of Qisuk and the three others returned to Greenland, where they were buried at Qaanaaq.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1898: islands in two oceans" -->
In 1898 President William McKinley, members of Congress and American diplomats took control of islands in the Pacific Ocean and the Caribbean Sea. Members of Congress voted to annex the Republic of Hawaii, which means to take it over as part of the United States, and McKinley signed their resolution on July 7, 1898. Hawaii's former ruler, Queen Liliuokalani, protested to Congress. On December 10, 1898, after a war between Spain and the United States, diplomats from the two countries signed a treaty in Paris. Under the treaty, Spanish officials gave up Cuba and handed Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines to the United States. American officials agreed to pay Spain $20 million for the Philippines.
