## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Christmas in American towns changed in the first half of the 1800s, from a noisy street holiday into a day for families and children. A poem printed in 1823 gave Americans their picture of St. Nicholas coming down the chimney. Thanksgiving was still a New England day, set each year by each governor. Sarah Josepha Hale, a magazine editor, began asking governors and presidents to make it one day for the whole country.

In the South, enslavers gave enslaved people a few days off at Christmas. On New Year's Day, enslavers hired many of them out to other white men or sold them away from their families. Free Black Americans chose days of their own to celebrate: January 1, 1808, July 5, 1827, in New York, and August 1 after 1834. In 1811 the city leaders of Albany, New York, banned Pinkster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas becomes a children's day" -->
In the early 1800s, Christmas in American towns still meant heavy drinking, begging from door to door and rowdy parades. Wealthier families and newspaper editors feared that these crowds threatened public order. They worked to turn Christmas into a quiet day at home, centered on children.

On December 23, 1823, the *Troy Sentinel*, a newspaper in Troy, New York, printed a poem called "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas." It carried no author's name. It begins "'Twas the night before Christmas," and it describes stockings hung by the chimney for St. Nicholas to fill.

In 1837 a book called the *New York Book of Poetry* listed Clement Clarke Moore, a New York professor, as the author. Moore put the poem in his own book of poems in 1844. The descendants of Henry Livingston Jr., a New York farmer and poet, say he wrote it as early as 1808. The question has never been settled.

A few years after the poem appeared, Catharine Maria Sedgwick of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, published a story about children who found a Christmas tree with gifts around it. Shopkeepers began to advertise gifts for children.

By the 1840s several states had made Christmas a legal holiday. Alabama is often called the first state, in 1836, but Alabama's state archivists searched and found no law from that year. In 1848 Alabama's lawmakers made Christmas a bank holiday, a day when payments on loans were not due.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and New Year's Day for enslaved people" -->
Enslavers in the South gave most enslaved workers a few days off at Christmas. Edwin Epps, who held Solomon Northup on a cotton plantation in Louisiana in the 1840s, gave three days. Northup wrote that other enslavers gave four, five or six.

Northup wrote that the planters on Bayou Boeuf took turns holding a "Christmas supper" for enslaved people from nearby plantations. Three to five hundred people came, and white people came to watch them eat. Northup wrote that Christmas was "three days in the year." The rest of the year, he wrote, was "days of weariness, and fear, and suffering, and unremitting labor." Unremitting means without a break.

Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. In 1845 he wrote that enslavers gave the days from Christmas to New Year's as holidays because it "would be unsafe to deprive them of it." To deprive someone of a thing is to take it away. Douglass called the holidays "safety-valves." By that he meant that the days off let out the anger that could lead enslaved people to rebel.

In Edenton, North Carolina, enslaved men from the plantations marched through town in costume on Christmas morning. Harriet Jacobs, who was enslaved in Edenton, called these groups the "Johnkannaus." The men beat a drum made from a box covered in sheepskin. They sang songs they had made up during the month before, and they asked at every door for money or rum.

In Jacobs's town, January 1 was hiring day. On that day enslavers rented enslaved people to other white men for the coming year. The next day, each person had to go to the new master. Enslavers had anyone who refused whipped or locked in jail until that person agreed. After the hiring, enslavers sold people.

Jacobs wrote that on one New Year's Day she watched a mother lead her seven children to the auction block, the platform where people were sold. A slave trader bought all seven children. A man from the town bought the mother. Jacobs did not give the mother's name. On another New Year's Day, Jacobs's friend Fanny was sold at auction to pay her enslaver's debts. One buyer took Fanny. Another master, far away, bought her four daughters.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Days Black Americans chose to celebrate" -->
On January 1, 1808, a federal law made it illegal to bring enslaved people into the United States from other countries. That day Absalom Jones preached a sermon of thanks at St. Thomas's African Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. He asked Black Americans to keep January 1 every year as a day of thanks. On that day they would teach their children "the history of the sufferings of our brethren, and of their deliverance." Deliverance means being set free. Enslavers inside the country kept buying and selling people after the law.

On March 31, 1817, New York's lawmakers voted to end slavery in the state on July 4, 1827. On that day about 4,600 people were freed. Black New Yorkers held church services on July 4, 1827, and paraded on July 5. Nathaniel Paul had led a meeting in Albany that voted to celebrate on the fifth, because the fourth was the day white citizens celebrated. People feared that white crowds celebrating the Fourth would attack them. In New York City, between 2,000 and 4,000 people marched on July 5 behind their marshal, Samuel Hardenburgh.

On August 1, 1834, a law passed by Britain's Parliament began to end slavery in Britain's colonies in the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean Sea. Slavery there was fully ended in 1838. Black Americans in New York City held a celebration on August 1, 1834. For about thirty years after that, August 1 was the largest yearly freedom celebration of Black Americans in the North. They held parades, picnics and speeches against American slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster banned in Albany, 1811" -->
By the late 1700s, Pinkster drew large crowds of enslaved and free Black people to Albany, New York. White city leaders worried about so many Black people gathering in one place. In 1811 the members of the Albany Common Council, the city's governing board, banned the celebration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and the Fourth in the new states" -->
Thanksgiving in these years was a New England custom. Each governor named the day each year. Most people in other regions did not keep it.

People in New York, Philadelphia and Richmond held balls, dinners and parades on Washington's birthday, February 22. The balls and dinners were for rich families, and the parades filled the streets for everyone else. The day was not yet an official holiday anywhere.

John Adams and Thomas Jefferson both died on July 4, 1826. It was fifty years to the day after the Declaration of Independence was adopted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-josepha-hale" name="Sarah Josepha Hale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Josepha Hale

> **Who:** A magazine editor who asked governors and presidents for years to make Thanksgiving one national holiday.
> **When and where:** New England and Philadelphia, 1827 to 1879.

Sarah Josepha Hale spent decades asking governors and presidents to make Thanksgiving one holiday for the whole country, on the same Thursday in every state. She was born in Newport, New Hampshire, on October 24, 1788. Her husband, David Hale, died after nine years of marriage and left her with five children. She wrote poems and books to support them. One of her poems for children was "Mary Had a Little Lamb."

In her 1827 novel *Northwood*, she described a Thanksgiving dinner in New Hampshire. Roast turkey sat at the head of the table. Of all the pies, "the pumpkin pie occupied the most distinguished niche," which means the place of honor.

In 1837 she became the editor of *Godey's Lady's Book*, a women's magazine read across the country. She held the job until 1877. She used the magazine and her letters to ask for a national Thanksgiving. Accounts disagree about when she began. Plimoth Patuxet Museums gives the 1830s. The National Women's History Museum gives 1846. In 1863 Hale herself wrote that she had asked "for the last fifteen years," which points to about 1848. She sent her articles to the governors of every state and territory.

Some Southern leaders saw Thanksgiving as a Northern holiday. Governor Henry Wise of Virginia wrote back to Hale that he wanted nothing to do with "this theatrical national claptrap of Thanksgiving." Northern ministers preached against slavery on the day, and that was what Wise objected to.

Hale supported ending slavery. Later she backed a plan to send freed Black Americans to Africa. She opposed giving women the vote.

She got her national day in 1863. After that, each president still had to proclaim it every year. In May 1877, when she was 88, her eyes were so weak that a helper wrote her letters for her. She asked George Washington Warren of Massachusetts to write a resolution for Congress. It would make the last Thursday in November a legal holiday. She died in 1879.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans got their first national holidays between 1863 and 1894. President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving in 1863. In 1870 the members of Congress made New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Thanksgiving and Christmas holidays for federal workers in Washington, D.C. Federal workers are people who work for the national government. The members of Congress added Washington's birthday in 1879, Decoration Day in 1888 and Labor Day in 1894.

Freed Black Americans made days of their own after the Civil War. In Texas they celebrated June 19, the day in 1865 when a Union general announced that slavery had ended there. In Charleston, South Carolina, freed people reburied Union prisoners of war and decorated their graves. White mobs attacked some of these celebrations.

In 1890 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs ordered the government's Indian schools to celebrate the anniversary of a law that broke up reservation land. In 1891 a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men. The next year, President Benjamin Harrison proclaimed the first national Columbus Day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving for the whole country, 1863" -->
In the 1850s, some Southern leaders called Thanksgiving a Northern holiday. In 1856 the Richmond *Whig*, a Virginia newspaper, printed an attack on it. The editors wrote that the day cost working men a day's pay and led to drunkenness.

On September 28, 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. She asked him to proclaim the last Thursday in November as a national day of thanksgiving. Getting every state to pass a law, she wrote, "would require years." A proclamation is a public order or announcement by a president or governor.

On October 3, 1863, Lincoln did it. Secretary of State William Seward wrote the proclamation, and Lincoln signed it. It asked Americans everywhere to keep the last Thursday in November as a day of thanks. It also asked them to pray for "all those who have become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers" in the war.

After 1863 every president proclaimed a Thanksgiving Day each year. Most chose the last Thursday in November. Andrew Johnson chose the first Thursday in December in 1865. Ulysses S. Grant chose the third Thursday in November in 1869. In Texas, Governor Oran Roberts, who had been a Confederate officer, still refused to proclaim Thanksgiving in the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal holidays, 1870 to 1888" -->
On June 28, 1870, the members of Congress passed the first law that gave federal workers in Washington, D.C., holidays. The days were New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Christmas Day and whatever day the president named for thanksgiving. Bankers and businessmen in the city had asked for the law. Representative Burton Cook of Illinois said it would match the holidays of the states around the city.

In January 1879 they added Washington's birthday, February 22. The main purpose was to close the banks that day. Lawmakers in Massachusetts had already made Christmas, Washington's birthday and the Fourth of July state holidays in 1856. Irish Catholic voters in the state helped pass that law. Federal workers outside Washington, D.C., got no paid holidays until a law of January 6, 1885.

In 1888 the members of Congress made Decoration Day, May 30, a holiday for federal workers in the city. Many of those workers were Union veterans. Before the law, they had lost a day's pay whenever they marched in the Decoration Day ceremonies.

During the war, on January 3, 1863, a magazine called *Harper's Weekly* printed a drawing by Thomas Nast, who had come to America from Bavaria, in Germany. It showed Santa Claus giving out presents in a Union army camp. Santa wore a coat covered in stars and striped trousers. In his hands was a toy of the Confederate president, Jefferson Davis, with a rope around its neck.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Juneteenth, 1865" -->
On June 19, 1865, the Union general Gordon Granger announced in Galveston, Texas, that slavery had ended there. The Confederate armies in Virginia had surrendered two months earlier. Granger arrived with about 1,800 soldiers, according to the historian Henry Louis Gates Jr. Other accounts give about 2,000.

Granger issued General Orders No. 3. It stated that "all slaves are free." The same order told the freed people to stay in their homes and work for wages for their former enslavers. It said they would not be allowed to gather at army posts.

About 250,000 people were enslaved in Texas. More than 150,000 of them had been brought there during the war by enslavers from other Southern states. The enslavers wanted to keep them out of reach of the Union army.

Many enslavers in Texas did not tell the people they held that they were free until after the harvest was in. One formerly enslaved Texan remembered an enslaver who did not tell them "til a whole year after we were." In Galveston, the mayor, a former Confederate, forced freed people back to work.

Susan Merritt had been enslaved in Texas. She said that right after freedom, she saw many Black people hanging from trees in the Sabine River bottom, the low land along the river. She said white men caught them swimming across the river and shot them. She did not name the people who were killed, and no record names the killers. Katie Darling kept working for her former mistress for six more years. Darling said the woman whipped her after the war just as she had before.

The Freedmen's Bureau was the federal office set up to help freed people. Its officials did not reach Texas until September 1865. A committee of the Texas constitutional convention of 1868 counted 939 murders in the state between 1865 and 1868. In 379 of them, white people killed Black people. Outlaw gangs and armed groups such as the Ku Klux Klan attacked freed people and people who had supported the Union.

Freed people in Texas celebrated the day a year later, on June 19, 1866. They called it Juneteenth, from the words June and nineteenth. Some early celebrations were political meetings, where freedmen learned about their new right to vote. In some towns, officials made Black people hold their celebrations outside the town. Black Texans raised money to buy land of their own for the day. They named many of these places Emancipation Park. Emancipation means being freed from slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-yates-holidays" name="Jack Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Yates

> **Who:** A Baptist minister, born enslaved, who led Black Houstonians in buying land for their Juneteenth celebrations.
> **When and where:** Virginia, then Houston, Texas, 1828 to 1897.

In 1872 Jack Yates and three other men paid $800 for ten acres in Houston, Texas, so that the city's Black people had a place of their own for Juneteenth. The other three men were Richard Allen, Richard Brock and Elias Dibble. The members of two churches raised the money. One was Antioch Baptist Church, where Yates was the pastor. The other was Trinity Methodist Episcopal Church.

They named the land Emancipation Park. It is the oldest park in Houston. For parts of the years of legal segregation, it was the only public park in the area that Black people were allowed to use. Segregation was the system of laws that kept Black people and white people apart. Juneteenth celebrations are still held there.

Yates was born enslaved in Gloucester County, Virginia, on July 11, 1828. His full name was John Henry Yates. His enslaver's child taught him to read, which was against the law. He married Harriet Willis, who was enslaved on a nearby farm, and they had eleven children.

In about 1863, Harriet's enslaver moved to Matagorda County, Texas. Yates did not want to be separated from his wife and children, so he asked to go too. The Emancipation Park Conservancy, the group that runs the park today, says he had already gained his freedom. By its account, he went back into slavery to stay with his family.

When freedom came in June 1865, the family moved to Houston. Yates hauled goods by day and preached at night and on Sundays. In 1866 he became the first pastor of Antioch Missionary Baptist Church, the first Black Baptist church in Houston. He bought land for his own house in 1869. In 1885 he started Houston Academy, a school for Black children. He died on December 22, 1897.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-yates-holidays" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom celebrations, and the attacks on them" -->
Black Americans marked freedom on days of their own, and white men attacked some of those celebrations. Before the war, on July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass spoke in Rochester, New York. He asked, "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?" He answered that the day showed an enslaved person "the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim." The Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863. That night Douglass and other Black leaders in the North held celebrations at midnight.

In April 1866, the members of Congress passed a Civil Rights Act over President Andrew Johnson's veto. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a law. Congress can still pass the law if enough of its members vote for it again. On April 16, Black residents of Norfolk, Virginia, marched to celebrate the law. Black Union soldiers marched with them in uniform, carrying their guns.

Someone fired a shot. In the chase that followed, a white Confederate veteran was shot and killed. The records do not say who fired either shot. White men then attacked Black people. They killed at least two Black people, badly wounded six, and destroyed property that belonged to Black people. The attacks went on through the night until United States Army soldiers stopped them. The records do not give the names of the dead.

Joseph T. Wilson was a Black Union veteran who published a newspaper in Norfolk, the *True Southerner*. He printed his side of the story in it. After that issue came out, white men broke into his printing office and destroyed his press.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Decoration Day: who started it" -->
Several towns claim to have held the first day for decorating soldiers' graves. The National Cemetery Administration, the federal office that runs the national military cemeteries, lists these claims. Historians still disagree about which came first. They also disagree about whether the later ceremonies copied the one in Charleston.

In October 1864, three women in Boalsburg, Pennsylvania, decorated the graves of soldiers who had died.

In Charleston, South Carolina, Confederate soldiers had held Union prisoners of war at the Washington Race Course, a track for horse racing. The prisoners were kept outdoors in the middle of the track. According to the historian David Blight, at least 257 of them died of exposure and disease. Exposure means being left outside with no shelter from cold and weather. They were buried in a mass grave behind the stands.

In April 1865 freed people and Black workmen dug up the bodies and buried each man in his own grave. They built a fence and an arch over the gate that read "Martyrs of the Race Course." A martyr is a person who dies for a cause. On May 1, 1865, freed people and white allies from the North held a ceremony there. Blight writes that as many as 10,000 people came. Among them were 3,000 Black schoolchildren, who carried flowers and sang the song "John Brown's Body."

On April 25 and 26, 1866, white women in Columbus, Mississippi, and Columbus, Georgia, held days to put flowers on soldiers' graves. Mary Ann Williams of the Ladies Memorial Association in Columbus, Georgia, had asked for the day in a letter to the newspaper. Supporters of the Confederacy in the South kept a separate Memorial Day of their own.

On May 5, 1868, General John A. Logan gave an order to the Grand Army of the Republic, the organization of Union veterans, which he led. He ordered its members to decorate the graves of Union soldiers on May 30, 1868. The first national Decoration Day was held that day at Arlington National Cemetery in Virginia. The cemetery stands on land that had been the home of the Confederate general Robert E. Lee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Labor Day, 1882 to 1894" -->
Union workers in New York City held the first Labor Day parade on Tuesday, September 5, 1882. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain for better pay and conditions. The city's Central Labor Union, a group of unions, planned the parade. Two men have been given credit for the idea: Peter J. McGuire of the carpenters' union and Matthew Maguire, a machinist. The US Department of Labor states that recent research favors Maguire.

Oregon made Labor Day a state holiday on February 21, 1887. By 1894, 23 more states had done the same. A senator brought a bill for a national Labor Day to Congress in August 1893. Senators passed it in June 1894, and members of the House passed it on June 26. President Grover Cleveland signed it on June 28, 1894. It gave federal workers the first Monday in September off.

Cleveland signed it in the first days of a national railroad strike. On June 26, the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs, had begun refusing to run trains that pulled Pullman sleeping cars. They did it to support the workers at the Pullman factory, who were on strike. On July 3, five days after he signed the bill, Cleveland sent federal soldiers to Chicago to break the strike. Cleveland left no letter or statement that explains why he signed the Labor Day bill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day, 1892, and the New Orleans lynching" -->
The first national Columbus Day came a year and a half after a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men. A lynching is a killing by a mob, without a trial. Some historians say President Benjamin Harrison meant the holiday partly to calm Italy and Italian Americans after the killings.

In October 1890, someone shot and killed David Hennessy, the police chief of New Orleans. Police arrested many Italian Americans, and 19 were charged. Nine were tried. The jury found six of them not guilty and could not agree about the other three. No solid evidence had been shown against them.

The next day, March 14, 1891, thousands of people gathered in the city. A lawyer, William S. Parkerson, told the crowd to "set aside the verdict." A verdict is a jury's decision. Parkerson led an armed group into the city jail with three other men: Walter Denegre, James D. Houston and John C. Wickliffe.

The armed men killed eleven Italian American men. They shot and clubbed nine of them to death inside the jail. They hanged Emmanuele Polizzi from a lamppost and shot him. They hanged Antonio Bagnetto from a tree and shot him.

The eleven men were Antonio Bagnetto, James Caruso, Loreto Comitis, Rocco Geraci, Joseph Macheca, Antonio Marchesi, Pietro Monasterio, Emmanuele Polizzi, Frank Romero, Antonio Scaffidi and Charles Traina. Three had been found not guilty. Three had had a mistrial, which means the jury could not agree. Five had never been tried.

A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether someone should be charged with a crime. The members of the grand jury said they could not identify the killers, and they charged no one. Italy's leaders called their ambassador home from the United States. President Harrison agreed to pay $25,000 to the dead men's families.

On July 21, 1892, Harrison proclaimed October 21, 1892, a national holiday. It was the 400th anniversary of Columbus's landing in the Americas. He asked schools to lead the celebrations and to fly the flag over every schoolhouse. The proclamation does not mention Italy or New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Holidays in the government boarding schools" -->
In the 1880s and 1890s, federal officials sent Native children to government boarding schools to make them give up their nations' languages and ways. In 1890 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Thomas J. Morgan, ordered these schools to celebrate a new holiday. It marked the anniversary of the Dawes Act of 1887. Under that law, federal officials broke up reservation land, the land set aside for Native nations, into farms for single families.

In 1883, at the request of Secretary of the Interior Henry Teller, federal officials made the Sun Dance and other Native ceremonies "Indian offenses" on the reservations. Teller had called them "old heathenish dances." Heathen was an insulting word for people who were not Christian.
