## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Christmas in American towns changed in the first half of the 1800s, from a noisy street holiday into a day for families and children. In a poem printed in 1823, St. Nicholas comes down the chimney to fill the children's stockings. Thanksgiving was still mostly a New England day. Sarah Josepha Hale, a magazine editor, began asking governors and presidents to make it one day for the whole country.

In the South, enslavers gave enslaved people three to six days off at Christmas. In Edenton, North Carolina, January 1 was hiring day. Enslavers rented many enslaved people out to other white men for the year and sold others away from their families. Free Black Americans chose days of their own to celebrate: January 1, 1808, July 5, 1827, in New York, and August 1 after 1834. In 1811 the city leaders of Albany, New York, banned Pinkster, a spring holiday that African and Dutch New Yorkers had kept since the 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas becomes a children's day" -->
In the early 1800s, Christmas in American towns still meant heavy drinking, begging from door to door and rowdy parades. Wealthier families and newspaper editors feared that these crowds threatened public order. They worked to turn Christmas into a quiet day at home, centered on children.

On December 23, 1823, the *Troy Sentinel*, a newspaper in Troy, New York, printed a poem called "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas." It carried no author's name. It begins "'Twas the night before Christmas," and it describes stockings hung by the chimney for St. Nicholas to fill. "Down the chimney St. Nicholas came with a bound," the poem says. Eight reindeer pull his sleigh, and in the 1823 printing two of them are named Dunder and Blixem.

In 1837 a book called the *New York Book of Poetry* listed Clement Clarke Moore, a professor at a school for ministers in New York City, as the author. Moore put the poem in his own book of poems in 1844. The descendants of Henry Livingston Jr., a New York farmer and poet, say he wrote it as early as 1808. Accounts still differ about who wrote it.

A few years after the poem appeared, Catharine Maria Sedgwick of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, published a story about children who found a Christmas tree with gifts around it. Shopkeepers began to advertise gifts for children.

By the 1840s many states had begun to make Christmas a legal holiday, a holiday set by law. Alabama is often called the first state, in 1836, but Alabama's state archivists searched and found no law from that year. In 1848 Alabama's lawmakers made Christmas a bank holiday, a day when payments on loans were not due.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and New Year's Day for enslaved people" -->
Enslavers in the South gave enslaved workers three to six days off at Christmas. Edwin Epps, who held Solomon Northup on a plantation on Bayou Boeuf in Louisiana in the 1840s, gave three days. A bayou is a slow, swampy stream. Northup wrote that other enslavers gave four, five or six.

Northup wrote that the planters on Bayou Boeuf took turns holding a "Christmas supper" for enslaved people from nearby plantations. Three to five hundred people came, and white people came to watch them eat. The supper and the days off were, Northup wrote, "southern life as it is, three days in the year." The other 362 days, he wrote, were "days of weariness, and fear, and suffering, and unremitting labor." Unremitting means without a break.

Frederick Douglass was enslaved in Maryland. In 1845 he wrote that enslavers gave the days from Christmas to New Year's as holidays. He wrote that they did it because they knew it "would be unsafe to deprive them of it." To deprive someone of a thing is to take it away. Douglass called the holidays "safety-valves." By that he meant that the days off let out the anger that could lead enslaved people to rebel.

In Edenton, North Carolina, enslaved men from the plantations went from door to door in costume on Christmas Day, until noon. Harriet Jacobs, who was enslaved in Edenton, called these groups the "Johnkannaus." Each group had about a hundred men. Two of them wore calico covered with a net of bright stripes, with cows' tails and horns. The men beat a drum made from a box covered in sheepskin, and others played triangles and jawbones. They sang songs they had made up during the month before. At every door they asked for a penny or a glass of rum, and a group could collect twenty or thirty dollars.

In Jacobs's town, January 1 was hiring day. On that day enslavers rented enslaved people to other white men for the coming year. The next day, each person had to go to the new master. Jacobs wrote that anyone who refused "is whipped, or locked up in jail, until he consents to go." She did not name who did the whipping. After the hiring, enslavers sold people.

Jacobs wrote that on one of these sale days she watched a mother lead her seven children to the auction block, the platform where people were sold. The mother knew some of them would be taken from her, but the buyers took all seven. A slave trader bought the children. A man from the town bought the mother. Jacobs did not give the mother's name. Jacobs's friend Fanny was put up for auction on another New Year's Day to pay her enslaver's debts. One master bought Fanny, and another master, far away, bought her four daughters. Fanny later escaped.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Days Black Americans chose to celebrate" -->
The members of Congress had voted to ban bringing enslaved people into the United States from other countries, and the ban took effect on January 1, 1808. That day Absalom Jones preached a sermon of thanks at St. Thomas's African Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. He asked Black Americans to keep January 1 every year as a day of thanks. On that day they would teach their children "the history of the sufferings of our brethren, and of their deliverance." Brethren means brothers and sisters, and deliverance means being set free. Enslavers inside the country kept buying and selling people after the law.

On March 31, 1817, New York's lawmakers voted to end slavery in the state on July 4, 1827. On that day about 4,600 people were freed. Some of them still had to work for their former enslavers for a set number of years under written contracts called indentures. Black New Yorkers held church services on July 4, 1827, and paraded on July 5. Nathaniel Paul had led a meeting in Albany that voted to celebrate on the fifth, because the fourth was the day white citizens celebrated. The people at the July 4 church services went home quietly. They feared that white men celebrating Independence Day would pick fights with them. In New York City, between 2,000 and 4,000 people marched on July 5 behind Samuel Hardenburgh, the marshal who led the parade.

The members of Britain's Parliament, the British lawmakers, set August 1, 1834, as the day slavery would begin to end in Britain's colonies in the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean Sea. On that day enslaved people in Antigua, Montserrat and Bermuda went free. In the other colonies they became "apprentices." Apprentices still had to work about 40 hours a week for their former enslavers, without pay. Their protests helped end that system in 1838.

Black Americans in New York City held a celebration on August 1, 1834. After freedom was complete in 1838, Arthur Tappan's antislavery newspaper, *The Emancipator*, called for a yearly holiday. In Oberlin, Ohio, Sabram Cox, who had escaped slavery, led the town's first August 1 celebration in 1842. For about thirty years, the historian Jeffrey Kerr-Ritchie writes, August 1 was the most important yearly celebration of freedom among Black people in the northern United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster banned in Albany, 1811" -->
By the late 1700s, large crowds of enslaved and free Black people gathered in Albany, New York, for Pinkster. City leaders worried about so many Black people gathering in one place. In 1811 the members of the Albany Common Council, the city's governing board, banned the celebration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and the Fourth in the new states" -->
Thanksgiving in these years was a New England custom. People in other parts of the country did not keep it.

People in New York, Philadelphia and Richmond held balls, dinners and parades on Washington's birthday, February 22. The balls and dinners were for rich families, and the parades filled the streets for everyone else. Massachusetts lawmakers made it a state holiday in 1856.

John Adams and Thomas Jefferson both died on July 4, 1826. It was fifty years to the day after the Declaration of Independence was adopted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-josepha-hale" name="Sarah Josepha Hale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Josepha Hale

> **Who:** A magazine editor who asked governors and presidents for years to make Thanksgiving one national holiday.
> **When and where:** New England and Philadelphia, 1827 to 1879.

Sarah Josepha Hale spent decades asking governors and presidents to make Thanksgiving one holiday for the whole country, on the same Thursday in every state. She was born in Newport, New Hampshire, on October 24, 1788. Her husband, David Hale, died after nine years of marriage and left her with five children. She wrote poems and books to support them. One of her poems for children was "Mary Had a Little Lamb."

In her 1827 novel *Northwood*, she described a New England Thanksgiving dinner. Roast turkey sat at the head of the table. Of all the pies, "the pumpkin pie occupied the most distinguished niche," which means the place of honor.

In 1837 she became the editor of *Godey's Lady's Book*, a women's magazine read across the country. She held the job until 1877. She used the magazine and her letters to ask for a national Thanksgiving. Accounts disagree about when she began. Plimoth Patuxet Museums gives the 1830s. The National Women's History Museum gives 1846. In 1863 Hale herself wrote that she had asked "for the last fifteen years," which points to about 1848. She sent her articles to the governors of every state and territory.

Some Southern leaders saw Thanksgiving as a Northern holiday. Governor Henry Wise of Virginia wrote back to Hale that he wanted nothing to do with "this theatrical national claptrap of Thanksgiving." Claptrap means silly talk meant to win cheers. Northern ministers preached against slavery on the day, and that was what Wise objected to.

Hale supported ending slavery, and later she backed a plan to send freed Black Americans to Africa. She did not support the movement to give women the vote.

She got her national day in 1863. After that, each president proclaimed it each year. To proclaim a day is to name it in a public order. No law yet made Thanksgiving a holiday. In May 1877, when she was 88, her eyes were so weak that a helper wrote a letter for her to George Washington Warren. In it she was still asking for "a Resolution of Congress making the last Thursday in November a legal holiday." She died in 1879.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans got their first national holidays between 1863 and 1894. President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving in 1863. In 1870 the members of Congress made New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Thanksgiving and Christmas holidays for federal workers in Washington, D.C. Federal workers are people who work for the national government. The members of Congress added Washington's birthday in 1879, Decoration Day in 1888 and Labor Day in 1894. Decoration Day was a day for putting flowers on soldiers' graves.

Freed Black Americans made days of their own after the Civil War. In Texas they celebrated June 19, the day in 1865 when a Union general announced that slavery had ended there. In Charleston, South Carolina, freed people reburied Union prisoners of war and decorated their graves. In Norfolk, Virginia, in 1866, white men attacked a march held to celebrate a new civil rights law.

In 1890 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the official who ran the government's office for Native affairs, ordered the government's Indian schools to celebrate the anniversary of the Dawes Act. Under that law, federal officials broke up the land set aside for Native nations. In 1891 a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men. The next year, President Benjamin Harrison proclaimed the first national Columbus Day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving for the whole country, 1863" -->
In the 1850s, some Southern leaders called Thanksgiving a Northern holiday. In 1856 the Richmond *Whig*, a Virginia newspaper, printed an attack on the thanksgiving day in the District of Columbia. The editors wrote that the day cost working men a day's pay and led to drunkenness. They called New England a "crazy society."

On September 28, 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. She asked him to proclaim the last Thursday in November as a national day of thanksgiving. Getting every state to pass a law, she wrote, "would require years." A proclamation is a public order or announcement by a president or governor.

Lincoln did it on October 3, 1863. Secretary of State William Seward wrote the proclamation, and Lincoln signed it. In it Lincoln invited Americans everywhere, even those at sea and in other countries, to keep the last Thursday in November as a day of thanks. He also asked them to pray for "all those who have become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers" in the war.

After 1863 every president proclaimed a Thanksgiving Day each year. Most chose the last Thursday in November. Two presidents chose other days: Andrew Johnson the first Thursday in December in 1865, and Ulysses S. Grant the third Thursday in November in 1869. In Texas, Governor Oran Roberts, who had been a Confederate officer, still refused to proclaim Thanksgiving in the 1880s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal holidays, 1870 to 1888" -->
On June 28, 1870, the members of Congress passed the first law that gave federal workers in Washington, D.C., holidays. The days were New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Christmas Day and any day the president named for fasting or thanksgiving. Researchers at the Congressional Research Service write that the law was apparently an answer to a written request from bankers and businessmen in the city. Representative Burton Cook of Illinois said it would match the holidays of the states around the city.

In January 1879 the members of Congress added Washington's birthday, February 22. The main purpose was to make it a bank holiday. Lawmakers in Massachusetts had already made Christmas, Washington's birthday and the Fourth of July state holidays in 1856. Irish Catholic voters in the state helped pass that law. A law of January 6, 1885, gave paid holidays to federal workers paid by the day, all over the country.

In 1888 the members of Congress made Decoration Day, May 30, a holiday for federal workers in the city. Many of those workers belonged to the Grand Army of the Republic, the organization of Union veterans. Before the law, they lost a day's pay when they went to the Decoration Day ceremonies.

During the war, on January 3, 1863, a magazine called *Harper's Weekly* printed a drawing by Thomas Nast, who had come to America from Bavaria, in Germany. Smithsonian Magazine calls it Union propaganda, a picture made to win people to the Union side. It showed Santa Claus visiting a Union army camp. Santa wore a coat covered in stars and striped trousers. In his hands was a toy of the Confederate president, Jefferson Davis, with a rope around its neck.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Juneteenth, 1865" -->
On June 19, 1865, the Union general Gordon Granger announced in Galveston, Texas, that slavery had ended there. The Confederate general Robert E. Lee had surrendered his army in Virginia on April 9, 1865, ten weeks earlier. Granger arrived with about 1,800 soldiers, according to the historian Henry Louis Gates Jr. Other accounts give about 2,000.

Granger issued General Orders No. 3. It stated that "all slaves are free," and that former masters and the people they had enslaved now stood as "employer and hired labor." The order advised the freed people "to remain quietly at their present homes and work for wages." It said they would not be allowed to gather at army posts, and that they would not be "supported in idleness," which meant fed without working.

About 250,000 people were enslaved in Texas. Enslavers from other Southern states had forced more than 150,000 of them to move there after 1862. The enslavers were running from the Union army and wanted to keep the people they held out of its reach.

Many enslavers in Texas did not tell the people they held that they were free until after the harvest was in. One formerly enslaved Texan remembered an enslaver who did not tell them "til a whole year after we were." In Galveston, the mayor, a former Confederate, forced freed people back to work.

Susan Merritt had been enslaved in Texas. She said that right after freedom, she saw many Black people hanging from trees in the Sabine River bottom, the low land along the river. She said white men caught them swimming across the river and shot them. She did not name the people who were killed, and no record names the killers. Katie Darling kept working for her former mistress for six more years. Darling said the woman whipped her after the war just as she had before.

The Freedmen's Bureau was the federal office set up to help freed people. Its officials did not reach Texas until September 1865. In 1868 Texans met to write a new state constitution, the state's basic set of laws. A committee of that meeting counted 939 murders in the state between 1865 and 1868. In 379 of them, white people killed Black people. Outlaw gangs and armed groups such as the Ku Klux Klan, a secret group of white men who used terror against Black people, attacked freed people and Union soldiers.

Freed people in Texas celebrated the day a year later, on June 19, 1866. The day came to be called Juneteenth, a word made from June and nineteenth. Some early celebrations were political meetings, where freedmen learned about their new right to vote. In some towns, city officials made Black people hold their celebrations outside the town. Black Texans raised money to buy land of their own for the day. In Houston they bought ten acres in 1872, and the land became Emancipation Park. Emancipation means being freed from slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-yates-holidays" name="Jack Yates" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack Yates

> **Who:** A Baptist minister, born enslaved, who led Black Houstonians in buying land for their Juneteenth celebrations.
> **When and where:** Virginia, then Houston, Texas, 1828 to 1897.

In 1872 Jack Yates and three other men paid $800 for ten acres in Houston, Texas, so that the city's Black people had a place of their own for Juneteenth. The other three men were Richard Allen, Richard Brock and Elias Dibble. The members of two churches bought the land for the Black people of Houston. One was Antioch Baptist Church, where Yates was the pastor. The other was Trinity Methodist Episcopal Church.

The land became Emancipation Park. It is the oldest park in Houston. For parts of the years of legal segregation, it was the only public park in the area that Black people were allowed to use. Segregation was the system of laws that kept Black people and white people apart. Juneteenth celebrations are still held there.

Yates was born enslaved in Gloucester County, Virginia, on July 11, 1828. His full name was John Henry Yates. His enslaver's child taught him to read, which was against the law. He married Harriet Willis, who was enslaved on a nearby farm, and they had eleven children.

In about 1863, Harriet's enslaver moved to Matagorda County, Texas. Yates could not bear to be separated from his wife and children, so he begged to go along. The Emancipation Park Conservancy, a group that works to keep up the park, says he had already gained his freedom. By its account, he went back into slavery to stay with his family.

When freedom came in June 1865, the family moved to Houston. Yates hauled goods by day and preached at night. In January 1866 freed people organized Antioch Missionary Baptist Church, the first Black Baptist church in Houston. Yates became its first pastor, in 1868 by one account. He bought land for his own house in 1869. In 1885 he started Houston Academy, a school that taught formerly enslaved people reading, writing, arithmetic and trades. He died on December 22, 1897.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-yates-holidays" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom celebrations, and the attacks on them" -->
Black Americans marked freedom on days of their own, and in Norfolk, Virginia, white men attacked one of those celebrations. Before the war, on July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass spoke in Rochester, New York. He asked, "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?" He answered that the day showed an enslaved person "the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim." The Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863. It was President Lincoln's order freeing the enslaved people in the states fighting against the Union. On the night it took effect, Douglass and other Black leaders in the North led large celebrations that began at midnight.

In April 1866, the members of Congress passed a Civil Rights Act over President Andrew Johnson's veto. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a law. Congress can still pass the law if enough of its members vote for it again. On April 16, Black residents of Norfolk, Virginia, marched to celebrate the law. Black Union soldiers marched with them in uniform, carrying their guns.

Someone fired a shot. In the chase that followed, a white Confederate veteran was shot and killed. The records do not say who fired either shot. White men then attacked Black people. They killed at least two Black people, badly wounded six, and destroyed property that belonged to Black people. The attacks went on through the night until United States Army soldiers stopped them. The records do not give the names of the dead.

Joseph T. Wilson was a Black Union veteran who published a newspaper in Norfolk, the *True Southerner*. He printed his side of the story in it. After that issue came out, white men attacked his printing office and destroyed his press.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Decoration Day: who started it" -->
People in several towns say their town held the first day for decorating soldiers' graves. The National Cemetery Administration, the federal office that runs the national military cemeteries, lists these claims. Accounts still differ about which came first, and about whether the later ceremonies copied the one in Charleston.

In October 1864, three women in Boalsburg, Pennsylvania, decorated the graves of soldiers who had died.

In Charleston, South Carolina, Confederate soldiers had held Union prisoners of war at the Washington Race Course, a track for horse racing. The prisoners were kept outdoors in the middle of the track. According to the historian David Blight, at least 257 of them died of exposure and disease. Exposure means being left outside with no shelter from cold and weather. They were buried quickly in a mass grave, one pit for many bodies, behind the stands. The records do not say who buried them.

In April 1865 freed people and Black workmen dug up the bodies and buried each man in his own grave. They built a fence and an arch over the gate that read "Martyrs of the Race Course." A martyr is a person who dies for a cause. On May 1, 1865, freed people and white allies from the North held a ceremony there. Blight found the account of it in old papers at Harvard and in Charleston. The parade is believed to have included as many as 10,000 people. Among them were 3,000 Black schoolchildren, who sang the song "John Brown's Body."

On April 25 and 26, 1866, women in Columbus, Mississippi, and Columbus, Georgia, held days to put flowers on the graves of Confederate soldiers. In Columbus, Mississippi, they put flowers on Union soldiers' graves too. Mary Ann Williams of the Ladies Memorial Association in Columbus, Georgia, had asked for the day in a letter to the newspaper. Supporters of the Confederacy in the South kept a separate Memorial Day of their own.

General John A. Logan led the Grand Army of the Republic. On May 5, 1868, he gave an order to its members. He ordered its members to decorate the graves of Union soldiers on May 30, 1868. The first national Decoration Day was held that day at Arlington National Cemetery in Virginia. The cemetery stands on land that had been the home of the Confederate general Robert E. Lee.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Labor Day, 1882 to 1894" -->
Union workers in New York City held the first Labor Day parade on Tuesday, September 5, 1882. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain for better pay and conditions. The city's Central Labor Union, a group of unions, planned the parade. People have credited two men with the idea: Peter J. McGuire of the carpenters' union and Matthew Maguire, a machinist, a worker who made and repaired machines. The US Department of Labor states that recent research favors Maguire.

Oregon's lawmakers made Labor Day a state holiday on February 21, 1887. The Department of Labor says lawmakers in 23 more states did the same by 1894. The House of Representatives' history office counts 23 states in all from 1887 to 1894. A senator brought a bill for a national Labor Day to Congress in August 1893. Senators passed it in June 1894, and members of the House passed it on June 26. President Grover Cleveland signed it on June 28, 1894. It gave federal workers the first Monday in September off.

Cleveland signed it in the first days of a national railroad strike. On June 26, members of the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs, had begun refusing to run trains that pulled Pullman sleeping cars. They did it to support the workers at the Pullman factory, who were on strike. On July 3, five days after he signed the bill, Cleveland sent federal soldiers to Chicago to break the strike. Cleveland left no letter or statement that explains why he signed the Labor Day bill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day, 1892, and the New Orleans lynching" -->
The first national Columbus Day came a year and a half after a mob in New Orleans killed eleven Italian American men. A lynching is a killing by a mob, without a trial. Some accounts say President Benjamin Harrison meant the holiday partly to calm Italy and Italian Americans after the killings.

In October 1890, someone shot and killed David Hennessy, the police chief of New Orleans. Police arrested Italian Americans, and 19 were charged, among them a 14-year-old boy. Nine were tried. The jury found six of them not guilty and could not agree about the other three. No solid evidence had been shown against them.

On March 14, 1891, after the verdicts, thousands of people gathered in the city. A lawyer, William S. Parkerson, told the crowd to "set aside the verdict." A verdict is a jury's decision. Parkerson led an armed group into the city jail with three other men: Walter Denegre, James D. Houston and John C. Wickliffe.

The armed men killed eleven Italian American men. They shot and clubbed nine of them to death inside the jail. Outside, they hanged Emmanuele Polizzi from a lamppost and Antonio Bagnetto from a tree, and shot both men. Among the mob were John M. Parker, who later became governor of Louisiana, and Walter C. Flower, who later became mayor of New Orleans.

The eleven men were Antonio Bagnetto, James Caruso, Loreto Comitis, Rocco Geraci, Joseph Macheca, Antonio Marchesi, Pietro Monasterio, Emmanuele Polizzi, Frank Romero, Antonio Scaffidi and Charles Traina. Three had been found not guilty. Three had had a mistrial, which means the jury could not agree. Five had never been tried.

A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether someone should be charged with a crime. The grand jury worked under Judge Robert H. Marr, a friend of several men in the mob. Its members said they could not identify the killers. They called the mob "several thousands of the first, best, and even the most law-abiding, of the citizens," and they charged no one. Italy's leaders called home their ambassador, the official who spoke for Italy in the United States. President Harrison agreed to pay $25,000 to the dead men's families. Members of Congress objected, and the United States paid $2,211.90 to each family.

Harrison proclaimed October 21, 1892, a national holiday on July 21, 1892. It was the 400th anniversary of Columbus's landing in the Americas. He asked schools to lead the celebrations and to fly the flag over every schoolhouse. The proclamation does not mention Italy or New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Holidays in the government boarding schools" -->
From 1879 on, federal officials took Native children from their families and put them in government boarding schools. The schools were meant to make the children give up their nations' languages and ways. Richard Henry Pratt, the Army officer who opened the first one at Carlisle, Pennsylvania, described its aim in 1892: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." In 1891 the members of Congress let officials make rules to force Native children to attend. In 1893 they let officials take away food rations, clothing and payments from parents who refused. Some government agents sent reservation police to seize children. At the schools, staff cut the children's hair, took their clothes and gave them English names. Staff beat children for speaking their own languages, shut them alone in rooms, kept food from them and washed their mouths out with soap. A federal investigation later counted at least 973 children who died at the schools, and its report says the true number is higher.

In 1890 the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Thomas J. Morgan, ordered the boarding schools to celebrate a new holiday. It marked the anniversary of the Dawes Act of 1887. Under that law, federal officials broke up reservation land, the land set aside for Native nations, into farms for single families, and sold the land left over. Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887. By 1934 they held about 48 million. They had lost about 90 million acres, two-thirds of their land.

In 1883 Commissioner Hiram Price issued rules that made the Sun Dance and other Native ceremonies "Indian offenses" on the reservations. He wrote them after a letter from Secretary of the Interior Henry Teller, who had called the ceremonies "old heathenish dances." Heathen was an insulting word for people who were not Christian. Under the rules, government agents punished a person who held one of these dances by taking away the family's food rations for up to ten days. For a second offense they could take the rations for up to thirty days or put the person in the agency jail for up to thirty days. The government had promised those rations by treaty, in return for land it had taken.
