## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Native nations here marked their year with ceremonies. Each ceremony came at a set point in the year. It might come when a group of stars rose, when the sun reached the end of its path along the horizon, when the corn ripened, or when the salmon swam back up the river. Many of these ceremonies gave thanks for food as it came in, such as maple sap, strawberries, the first ripe corn and the first salmon.

Nobody here wrote with an alphabet before 1500, so every written description comes from later. Outsiders wrote some of them down in the 1700s. The nations have written others themselves, and they still hold many of these ceremonies today. Their own histories place the ceremonies far earlier than the first written accounts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Giving thanks through the year in the Northeast" -->
The Onondaga Nation is one of the Haudenosaunee nations, in what is now New York. It lists seven ceremonies in its year: Midwinter, Maple Sap, Planting, Bean, Strawberry, Green Corn and Harvest. Men and women called Faith Keepers decide when each one takes place. They go by the seasons and the moon, so the dates change from year to year. Midwinter lasts 21 days, and on each day people give thanks for different things.

Haudenosaunee people open and close their gatherings with the Thanksgiving Address. They also call it the Words That Come Before All Else. The speaker thanks the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the food plants and the medicine plants, one at a time, and goes on from there. Each part ends with the words "Now our minds are one." An English translation of the Mohawk version was published in 1993.

Farther east lived the Wampanoag. Nancy Eldredge, a Nauset Wampanoag writer, wrote in 1998 that her people held thanksgiving ceremonies in early spring, for a good growing season, and in the fall, for the harvest. They also gave thanks for strawberries and cranberries as each one ripened, through the thirteen moons of their year. The Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), on the island of Martha's Vineyard, still holds Cranberry Day each October. The day opens with a greeting at sunrise. People pick cranberries, share a meal and set aside a share for the elders.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Green Corn Ceremony in the Southeast" -->
The Muscogee (Creek) and other nations of the Southeast held the Green Corn Ceremony in midsummer, when the first corn was ripe. It lasted four or eight days. Men fasted, which means they went without food. The historian Eric Bowne gives the men's fast as one and a half to two days. Women, children and old people could eat certain foods.

People put out every fire in the town. Then they lit a new fire in the town square, and each household took its fire from that one. The people of each town forgave every crime except murder.

William Bartram, a naturalist from Philadelphia, traveled through Creek towns in the 1770s. In a book printed in 1791, he wrote that the Creek had a feast for almost every month of the year. He called the Green Corn Ceremony the busk and said it was the most important one. Each town held its own busk in August, when its own corn was ripe.

Bartram described what people did to get ready. Families made new clothes, pots and pans. They swept their houses and the whole town. They piled up their worn-out clothes and the old grain and burned the pile. Bartram gives the fast as three days. On the fourth morning, the high priest made a new fire by rubbing dry wood together, and every house took its fire from it. Then the women brought in the new corn, and people danced in the square at night.

The first written accounts of the ceremony come from three men who saw it in the 1700s: James Adair, William Bartram and Benjamin Hawkins. Creek and Yuchi towns in Oklahoma still hold the ceremony at their stomp grounds, the grounds each town keeps for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Watching the sun in the Southwest" -->
In the Pueblo towns of the Southwest, a man called the Sun-priest watches where the sun comes up over the horizon. He watches at sunrise from a spot near the town. The point where the sun rises moves along the horizon through the year, so from his sightings he can tell the time of year. From them he sets the planting calendar and the dates of the ceremonies.

Among the Hopi of northern Arizona, the ceremonial year opens with the Soyal ceremony at the winter solstice in December. The winter solstice is the shortest day of the year. After Soyal, each village holds a ceremony about once a month until the Niman, or Home Dance, in late July.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The First Salmon Feast on the Columbia River" -->
The Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs and Nez Perce peoples live along the Columbia River in the Northwest. Each spring they hold a First Salmon Feast before the fishing season opens. Their tradition says that when the Creator was about to bring people onto the earth, Salmon offered his body to feed them.

At the feast, people bless water and drink it first. Then they eat the salmon and the other foods. The feast at the Celilo Longhouse in Oregon is held on the second weekend of April.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Makahiki in Hawaii" -->
Hawaii became part of the United States much later, and its people kept their own calendar. Their new year began when the Pleiades rose at sunset, in late October or early November. The Pleiades are a small group of stars that Hawaiians call Makaliʻi. The season that followed was called Makahiki.

The Hawaiian historian David Malo was born in about 1793. He wrote that four months were set aside for Makahiki. Priests carried an image of the god Lono around each island. At the edge of each district, the local chief handed over the taxes owed to the king: pigs, taro, sweet potatoes, feathers, kapa cloth and mats. Chiefs were not allowed to make war during the season. People held races, boxing and wrestling matches, and games of skill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, Spanish and French colonists brought the Christian church year to a few places on the coast of Florida and along the Rio Grande. That year was built around Sundays, Easter, Christmas and the feast days of saints. The colonists named places after those days. When they landed, they held church services to give thanks. Several towns today call one of those services the "first Thanksgiving." None of these services became a yearly holiday. Across the rest of the land, the Native nations kept their own ceremonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Places named for holy days" -->
Juan Ponce de León claimed the coast of what is now Florida for Spain on April 2, 1513. It was the Easter season, which Spanish speakers called *Pascua Florida*. He named the land *La Florida* after it.

Pedro Menéndez de Avilés sighted the same coast on August 28, 1565. That was the feast day of Saint Augustine, a day the church set aside to honor him. Menéndez named the town he founded St. Augustine.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Services of thanks on the Florida coast, 1564 and 1565" -->
French Protestants, called Huguenots, built Fort Caroline in June 1564, near what is now Jacksonville. The land belonged to the Timucua people. The Huguenots' leader, René de Laudonnière, wrote that on June 30, 1564, he had his people gather on the beach. There they sang and prayed to thank God for their safe arrival.

On September 8, 1565, Menéndez and about 800 Spanish settlers came ashore at Seloy, a Timucua town. A priest, Father Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, said a Mass of thanksgiving. A Mass is the main church service of the Catholic Church. Afterward, the priest wrote, Menéndez "had the Indians fed and dined himself."

A National Park Service page describes this Mass and meal as "the first community act of religion and thanksgiving in the first permanent European settlement" in North America. It came 56 years before the English colonists' harvest meal at Plymouth, in New England. The same page states that the day "did not become the origin of a national annual tradition."

Twelve days after the Mass, on September 20, 1565, Menéndez's soldiers captured Fort Caroline and killed most of the men there. Other French survivors surrendered to the Spanish at an inlet south of St. Augustine. Menéndez's soldiers killed them there: 111 men in late September and 134 more in October, by the Park Service's count. Menéndez's own letter gives different numbers. The Spanish named the inlet Matanzas, which means "slaughters."

In a letter of October 15, 1565, Menéndez told King Philip II of Spain why he had killed them. He wrote that they "spread the Lutheran sect," which meant the Protestant faith. He also wrote that they had settled "without permission of Your Majesty."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A thanksgiving on the Rio Grande, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate led about 500 colonists north across the Chihuahuan Desert to colonize New Mexico for Spain. They brought about 7,000 cattle and other animals. The crossing took about 50 days. They reached the Rio Grande near what is now San Elizario, Texas.

On April 30, 1598, Oñate ordered a day of thanksgiving. Franciscan friars said Mass. The colonists ate game they had hunted and fish that local Native people brought them. Then Oñate read out a claim to all the land along the river for King Philip II.

Some Texans call this day the first Thanksgiving. Others have made the same claim for a feast held by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's men in Palo Duro Canyon in 1541. Historians later questioned that claim.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A harvest dance at Secotan, 1585" -->
In July 1585 the English artist John White visited Secotan, an Algonquian town in what is now North Carolina. He painted a large ceremony there. Men and women from other towns danced around a ring of carved wooden posts. Thomas Hariot traveled with White. He wrote that each visitor had marks on his back to show which town he came from.

Encyclopedia Virginia describes the ceremony as "perhaps connected to the corn harvest." The records that survive do not say what the ceremony was called or what it meant to the people of Secotan.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
English colonists in the 1600s did not agree about holidays. In Virginia they kept Christmas the way people did in England. In New England the Puritan leaders kept Sundays and no other holiday on a fixed date. The Puritans were English Protestants, and their leaders ran the New England colonies. Those leaders called a day of fasting and prayer in times of drought or war. They called a day of thanksgiving, spent in church, after rain, a good harvest or a victory. In 1659 the lawmakers of Massachusetts Bay made keeping Christmas a crime.

In the fall of 1621, the colonists at Plymouth held a harvest meal. About ninety Wampanoag men joined them, and the meal went on for three days. Americans now call it the first Thanksgiving. Nobody in print gave it that name until 1841.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas and thanksgiving in Virginia" -->
In the winter of 1608 to 1609, John Smith and his men were caught by bad weather at Kecoughtan, a Native town in Virginia. They stayed six or seven days, over Christmas. Smith wrote that they "were never more merry." The people of Kecoughtan fed them oysters, fish, meat, wild birds and bread.

On December 4, 1619, the ship *Margaret* landed 35 settlers and their captain, John Woodlief, at Berkeley Hundred on the James River. The land there was home to Native nations, among them the Chickahominy, the Paspahegh and the Weyanock. The London investors who sent the settlers had given them an order. The day of arrival "shall be yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of Thanksgiving to Almighty God." The settlers held a service of thanks that day. The records do not show whether they held it again in the next two years.

On March 22, 1622, Powhatan men attacked the English settlements along the James River. They killed eleven people at Berkeley. Virginians began holding the Berkeley thanksgiving again in 1958.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The harvest meal at Plymouth, 1621" -->
The Plymouth colonists built their town in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. An epidemic between 1616 and 1619 had killed most of the people of Patuxet. An epidemic is a disease that spreads to many people at once. Of the 102 people who came on the *Mayflower*, 53 were still alive by the fall of 1621: 24 men, 5 women and 24 children and teenagers.

Only two people who were there wrote about that fall's harvest. One was Edward Winslow, in a letter of December 1621 that was printed in London in 1622. The book it was printed in is now called *Mourt's Relation*. The other was William Bradford, the governor, who wrote a history of the colony years later.

Winslow wrote that after the harvest, the governor sent four men to hunt birds "so we might after a special manner rejoice together." Massasoit, the leader the English called the Wampanoag's "greatest king," came "with some ninety men." The English "entertained and feasted" them for three days. The Wampanoag men went out, killed five deer, and gave them to the governor and other leaders.

Massasoit's own name was Ousamequin. The two sides had made an alliance earlier that year. To the Wampanoag, an alliance was a spoken agreement sealed by giving gifts. Plimoth Patuxet Museums states that the three days may have been Ousamequin's show of strength, and a renewal of the alliance.

Neither Winslow nor Bradford called the meal a thanksgiving. To the colonists, a thanksgiving was a day of prayer in church. By 1816 New Englanders already credited the Pilgrims with starting their yearly thanksgiving. That year the *Salem Gazette* called it the festival that "our Pilgrim forefathers" began. In 1841 a Boston minister, Alexander Young, printed Winslow's letter with a note: "This was the first Thanksgiving, the harvest festival of New England." Plimoth Patuxet Museums identifies that note as the first time anyone called the 1621 meal the first Thanksgiving.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### William Bradford

> **Who:** Governor of Plymouth Colony, who wrote the colony's history, *Of Plimoth Plantation*.
> **When and where:** Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts, 1621 to 1623.

As governor of Plymouth, William Bradford decided how the colony kept its days, and he wrote down what he did. In the fall of 1621 he sent four men to hunt birds for the harvest meal. The Wampanoag men who came with Massasoit gave him and other leaders five deer.

On Christmas Day 1621, Bradford called the men of Plymouth out to work. Most of the men who had just arrived on the ship *Fortune* said that working on Christmas went against their consciences. Bradford let them stay behind. At noon he came back and found them playing games in the street. Some were throwing a heavy iron bar. Others were playing stool-ball, a game played with a bat and a ball.

Bradford took their equipment away. He told them that if keeping the day was a matter of faith, they could keep it by praying in their houses. Nobody would play in the streets while others worked.

In the summer of 1623, a drought was killing Plymouth's corn. Bradford wrote that the colonists held a day of humiliation, which meant a day of fasting and prayer. Rain came, and the harvest was good. Then they held a day of thanksgiving.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-bradford-holidays" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fast days and thanksgiving days in New England" -->
The Puritan leaders of New England called a fast or a thanksgiving when something happened. Each one was a day of church services, so the dates changed from year to year. In Massachusetts Bay, the members of the General Court, the colony's lawmaking body, set these days together with the elders who led the churches.

In September 1638 the members of the Court asked the elders to keep a thanksgiving on the last Thursday of the eighth month. Their year began in March, so the eighth month was October. The thanks were for the many ships that had arrived safely that year and for the harvest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving days after the Pequot War, 1637" -->
The Massachusetts Bay colonists held days of thanksgiving in 1637 for their victory over the Pequot, a war in which colonists killed hundreds of Pequot at Mystic. Before dawn on May 26, 1637, Connecticut colonists under Captain John Mason, with Mohegan and Narragansett allies, attacked the Pequot town at Mystic. The town had a wall of wooden posts around it. Mason's men set it on fire and shot the people who tried to escape. Between 400 and 700 Pequot were killed, most of them old men, women and children. Those figures come from the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation's own history.

In August 1637 the members of the General Court asked the magistrates to set a day of thanksgiving with the church elders for the soldiers' return. Magistrates were the colony's judges and officials. They also asked each town to hold a feast for its soldiers. Then they ordered that October 12, 1637, be kept as a day of thanksgiving to God. The order gave thanks for "subdewing the Pecoits," meaning defeating the Pequot, and for "bringing the soldiers in safety." It also gave thanks for the end of a church meeting and for good news from Germany.

William B. Newell, a Penobscot man, said the first official Thanksgiving Day "commemorated the massacre of 700 Indian men, women and children during one of their religious ceremonies." The records of the attack do not mention a ceremony. The historian Jeremy Bangs disputes Newell's claim. The court records show that Massachusetts Bay held thanksgiving days for the victory over the Pequot. They also show many other thanksgiving days, before 1637 and after it, for ships, harvests and rain.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas made a crime, 1659 to 1681" -->
On May 11, 1659, the members of the General Court of Massachusetts Bay passed a law against Christmas. Anyone found "observing any such day as Christmas or the like, either by forbearing of labor, feasting, or any other way" had to pay a fine of five shillings. Forbearing of labor meant not working.

The Puritans said nothing in the Bible told Christians to keep Christmas. The minister Increase Mather called Christmas "a profane and superstitious custom."

King Charles II's officials in London pressed the leaders of Massachusetts to bring the colony's laws in line with England's. In 1681 the members of the General Court repealed the Christmas law, which means they canceled it. Most people in Massachusetts still did not celebrate Christmas for many years after that.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ceremonies banned in New Mexico" -->
In New Mexico, Spanish church leaders and governors tried to end the Pueblo people's own ceremonies. From 1656 to 1665, the head of the Franciscan friars there, Alonso de Posada, forbade those ceremonies. He ordered the friars to go into the kivas, the underground rooms where the Pueblo people worship. There the friars were to burn the masks and destroy the sacred objects.

In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested for what he called "sorcery." Four were sentenced to death by hanging. Treviño's government hanged three of them, and the fourth killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the rest in public and put them in prison. Then Pueblo men came to Santa Fe and forced Treviño to free them. One of the men freed was Po'pay. In 1680 he led the Pueblo Revolt against the Spanish.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750 the colonies still shared no holiday. Each colony kept its own days, and many of them came from Britain. New England governors ordered days of thanksgiving and days of fasting, and on those days nobody was allowed to work. In Boston, boys and young men paraded through the streets every November 5, on Pope's Night. In New York, enslaved and free Black people gathered for Pinkster, a spring holiday the Dutch had brought. In New England, Black communities began choosing their own governors and kings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving and fast days by the governor's order" -->
In New England, the governor chose each thanksgiving and each fast day, with the advice of his council. Printers published his order on a large single sheet of paper called a broadside. The order called on people to go to worship that day.

On October 14, 1721, Governor Gurdon Saltonstall of Connecticut set Wednesday, November 8, as a day of thanksgiving. Smallpox, a disease that passes from person to person and can kill, was then spreading in Boston. Saltonstall's order gave thanks that Connecticut had escaped it. It also gave thanks for the peace and for that year's harvest.

Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts ordered a thanksgiving for Thursday, August 14, 1746. The Duke of Cumberland's army had defeated a rebellion in Scotland. Shirley's order ended with the words "all servile Labour is prohibited on said Day." Servile labor meant ordinary work. Massachusetts governors also ordered fast days. Jonathan Belcher ordered one in 1734, and Spencer Phips ordered another in 1745.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night in Boston" -->
Pope's Night began with a plot in England. In the early hours of November 5, 1605, guards in London found a man named Guy Fawkes under the Parliament building with fuses and matches. He and a group of other English Catholics had planned to kill King James I when Parliament opened. Parliament then passed a law for a public thanksgiving every November 5. In New England the day became Pope's Night, and crowds burned figures of the Pope, the head of the Catholic Church.

In Boston, young men and older boys built wagons that carried moving figures of the Pope, the Devil and other enemies of the king. They dressed up, paraded through town and asked homeowners for money. The night ended with a meal outdoors and a bonfire.

Boston had two gangs, one from the North End and one from the South End. Each gang built its own Pope wagon and tried to capture the other gang's wagon. The winners burned the captured figures on Copp's Hill or on the Common, a large open field in the middle of Boston.

The printer Isaiah Thomas, born in 1749, remembered the crowds as a boy. They blew on conch shells, beat on the sides of houses with clubs and shouted "North end forever" or "South end forever." Thomas once got too close to a wagon, and a brick hit him and knocked him out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pinkster in New York" -->
Pinkster is the Dutch name for Pentecost, a Christian feast held seven weeks after Easter. Dutch settlers brought it to their colony of New Netherland in the 1620s. That colony later became New York. The oldest known written account of Pinkster appeared in the *New-York Weekly Journal* in March 1737. It describes a Pinkster gathering in the fields outside New York City, with African-style musical instruments.

Enslavers gave enslaved men and women several days off at Pinkster. Many walked from farms into the city to see family members who lived apart from them. They sold berries, herbs, sassafras bark, drinks and oysters, and spent the money at the celebration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black governors and kings in New England" -->
Black communities in New England held elections of their own. Where white men elected their governor, as in Connecticut and Rhode Island, Black men elected a Black governor. Where the king of England appointed the governor, as in Massachusetts and New Hampshire, Black men elected a king.

The first name known today comes from New London, Connecticut, in 1749. A man named Hercules served as governor there. He is known from the gravestone of his wife, Florio.

In Connecticut the Black election came the day after the white men's election, and the celebration went on for a week. People wore their best clothes, and some of them borrowed clothes from the people who enslaved them. The enslaver of the man who won was expected to pay for the food, the decorations and the liquor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="holidays" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans began celebrating their independence on its first anniversary, July 4, 1777. Within a few years they also celebrated George Washington's birthday. Neither day was a national holiday yet. In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official holiday. The members of the Continental Congress, and later President Washington, asked Americans to keep days of thanksgiving on dates they chose, but none of them set a yearly date.

Older customs changed during the Revolution. Boston's crowds turned Pope's Night into a protest against British taxes, and General Washington banned it in his army. In the South, most enslaved people got a few days off at Christmas. The enslaved people who worked in the house got extra work, serving their enslavers' guests.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pope's Night, 1764 to 1775" -->
On Pope's Night, November 5, 1764, one of the Pope wagons in Boston ran over and killed a boy named Brown. Accounts give his age as five to nine. The records that survive do not give his first name or say who was driving. The boy died early in the day. The parades and the fight between the gangs went on that night.

In August 1765, crowds in Boston protested the Stamp Act, a British tax on printed paper. They used the customs of Pope's Night: they hanged and burned figures, marched through town and lit bonfires. They broke into three or four large houses and smashed the furniture. Ebenezer Mackintosh, a shoemaker and the captain of the South End gang, led crowds at the protests.

On November 5, 1765, Mackintosh and Henry Swift, the captain of the North End gang, agreed to march together instead of fighting. Wealthy merchants paid for a dinner for the two gangs.

Ten years later, on November 5, 1775, General George Washington ordered his soldiers not to keep "that ridiculous and childish Custom of burning the Effigy of the pope." An effigy is a figure made to look like a person. Washington wrote that the Americans were asking Catholics in Canada to join them. Insulting their religion at such a time, he wrote, was wrong. Pope's Night ended in Boston during the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first Fourth of July, 1777" -->
The members of the Continental Congress voted for independence on July 2, 1776. The next day John Adams wrote to his wife, Abigail. He wrote that the day "ought to be solemnized with Pomp and Parade, with Shews, Games, Sports, Guns, Bells, Bonfires and Illuminations from one End of this Continent to the other." Shews meant shows. Illuminations were candles lit in the windows. The members of Congress formally adopted the Declaration of Independence on July 4, and that is the date Americans celebrate.

In July 1776, crowds greeted the public readings of the Declaration with parades, bonfires and gunfire. The first planned Fourth of July celebrations came a year later, in Philadelphia and in Boston.

In Philadelphia on July 4, 1777, the armed ships in the Delaware River were hung with flags. At one o'clock each ship fired thirteen cannon, one for each state. That night bells rang and fireworks went up over the city's common, and people lit candles in their windows. A Philadelphia newspaper, the *Pennsylvania Evening Post*, reported the day. In 1781 Massachusetts became the first state to make the Fourth of July an official state holiday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-wight-holidays" name="Henry Wight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Henry Wight

> **Who:** A minister and Revolutionary War veteran who started a Fourth of July celebration that has been held every year since.
> **When and where:** Bristol, Rhode Island, from 1785.

Henry Wight started the Fourth of July celebration in Bristol, Rhode Island, in 1785. The town has held it every year since then. The town's leaders call it the oldest Fourth of July celebration in the United States that has never stopped.

Wight was born in Medfield, Massachusetts, on May 26, 1752. He left his studies at Harvard to serve in the Revolutionary War. One account says he served as a soldier. Another says he served as a surgeon, a doctor who operates on the body.

In January 1785 the First Congregational Church of Bristol hired him as its minister. The church had 36 members.

Bristol stands on Mount Hope, called Montaup in the Pokanoket language. The Pokanoket Wampanoag lived there. Metacom, a Wampanoag leader, had his base there during King Philip's War. On August 12, 1676, Metacom was killed there in an ambush led by the English captain Benjamin Church. After the war, four Boston merchants bought the land as part of Plymouth Colony and started the town.

The people of Bristol had fired cannon on the Fourth before Wight came. In 1777 they fired 13 cannon at dawn, one for each state. In 1785, his first year in Bristol, Wight led the town's first Patriotic Exercises on the Fourth of July. He gave a speech that honored the veterans and celebrated the new country's freedoms. Bristol's parade probably began in the early 1800s, as the walk to the Patriotic Exercises. Wight died in 1837.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-wight-holidays" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving days for the new country" -->
In October 1777 the American army forced a British army to surrender at Saratoga, New York. The members of the Continental Congress then set December 18, 1777, as a day of thanksgiving for the whole country. Over the war years they named days of fasting in 1775, 1776, 1778, 1779, 1780, 1781 and 1782, and days of thanksgiving in 1777, 1778, 1781 and 1783.

In 1789 Elias Boudinot, a member of the House of Representatives from New Jersey, asked the other members of Congress to request a day of thanksgiving for the new Constitution. The members of both houses agreed. On October 3, 1789, President George Washington named Thursday, November 26, 1789, as a day of thanksgiving.

Washington sent his order to the state governors and asked them to announce it. On the day, he went to church at St. Paul's Chapel in New York City. He sent beer and food to the men held in the city jail because they owed money. On January 1, 1795, Washington named a day of thanksgiving in February. It gave thanks that the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania was over. None of these days became a yearly holiday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Washington's birthday" -->
On February 22, 1778, soldiers at the army's winter camp at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, gathered to wish George Washington a happy birthday. In 1779 people in Milton, Massachusetts, held a public celebration of the day.

Washington was not born on February 22 by the calendar used in his time. When he was born, the date was February 11, 1731. Britain and its colonies later changed to the calendar used today. To catch up with it, they dropped eleven days from September 1752. The calendar jumped from September 2 to September 14. Under the new calendar, Washington's birth date became February 22, 1732.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas on the plantations" -->
At Mount Vernon, George Washington's plantation in Virginia, most enslaved workers had four days off at Christmas. They used the days to rest and to visit family, at Mount Vernon and on nearby plantations. Enslaved cooks, housemaids and waiters had more work than usual over Christmas, because the Washingtons had extra guests.

Philip Vickers Fithian was a young tutor from New Jersey. He spent Christmas 1773 at Nomini Hall, the Virginia plantation of Robert Carter. He wrote in his journal that guns fired all around the house woke him that morning.

Before Fithian was dressed, several enslaved men and boys came to his room. They wished him a merry Christmas and asked for a "Christmas Box," a small gift of money. Nelson was the boy who made Fithian's fire and blacked his shoes. Tom was the coachman, and Dennis was a boy who waited at table. Another man lit the fire in the schoolroom, and Fithian did not write his name. Fithian gave them five bits in all, about three shillings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black governors in wartime Hartford, 1776" -->
Black men in Connecticut kept electing Black governors through these years. In Hartford, London was governor in 1755, while Thomas Seymour held him enslaved. Quaw, governor in 1760, was enslaved by George Wyllys, the secretary of the colony. Cuff was governor from 1766 to 1776.

These governors had little power of their own. A Black governor had no power to punish a white person who wronged a Black person.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### John Anderson

> **Who:** An enslaved man who became Hartford's Black governor in 1776, in a dispute that white officials investigated as a possible plot.
> **When and where:** Hartford, Connecticut, 1776 to 1780.

John Anderson became the Black governor of Hartford in May 1776, two months before the Declaration of Independence. White officials investigated his choice as a possible British plot.

That May, white men in Connecticut elected Jonathan Trumbull governor again. Soon after, Cuff, the Black governor of Hartford, resigned. He named John Anderson as governor in his place, without an election. A Hartford man, Major French, recorded it in his journal on May 11, 1776.

Anderson was enslaved by Philip Skene, who had been a major in the British army. The Americans held Skene as a prisoner, because they suspected he was loyal to Britain. White people in Hartford feared that Skene planned to use Anderson to bring Black people over to the British side. A committee of the Connecticut government investigated.

Two accounts disagree about what the committee found. Chris Menapace writes that Skene had tried to pay voters, and that many Black people refused to back an enslaved man owned by a Loyalist. A Loyalist was a colonist who stayed on the king's side. Skene told the committee he had done it for amusement. Andy Piascik writes that the committee cleared Anderson, but found that Anderson had paid some Black people to support him. By Piascik's account, Connecticut officials approved a four-year term for Anderson. He served as governor until 1780.
