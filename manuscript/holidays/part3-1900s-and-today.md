## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1908 to 1941, presidents and members of Congress added new national days and fixed the dates of old ones. After a campaign by Anna Jarvis, Mother's Day became a national day in 1914. She spent the rest of her life fighting the businesses that sold flowers and cards for it. After the First World War, Americans marked November 11 as Armistice Day. In 1938 the members of Congress made it a legal holiday dedicated to world peace. From 1934, presidents proclaimed Columbus Day each October.

Store owners used the holidays to sell. Macy's department store began its Thanksgiving parade in 1924. In 1939 President Franklin Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving a week earlier to give stores more shopping days before Christmas. Governors in sixteen states refused to follow him, and in 1941 the members of Congress fixed the date by law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mother's Day, 1908 to 1914" -->
Other Americans had proposed a day for mothers before Anna Jarvis did. In 1872 Julia Ward Howe, who wrote "The Battle Hymn of the Republic," started a Mothers' Peace Day. In 1904 Frank Hering of the Fraternal Order of Eagles gave a speech that called for a national day to honor mothers.

On May 8, 1914, the members of Congress passed a joint resolution naming the second Sunday in May as Mother's Day. A joint resolution is a measure that both houses of Congress pass together. The next day, President Woodrow Wilson asked Americans to fly the flag on that day "as a public expression of our love and reverence for the mothers of our country." The resolution gave no one a day off, because the day was a Sunday.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anna-jarvis" name="Anna Jarvis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anna Jarvis

> **Who:** The woman who campaigned to create Mother's Day and then fought the businesses that made money from it.
> **When and where:** West Virginia and Philadelphia, 1905 to 1948.

Anna Jarvis campaigned for years to create Mother's Day, and then spent her money and the rest of her life fighting the people who sold things for it. She was born in Webster, West Virginia, on May 1, 1864, during the Civil War. Her mother, Ann Reeves Jarvis, ran Mothers' Day Work Clubs. The clubs cared for sick and wounded soldiers from both armies.

Her mother died in 1905. To honor her, Anna Jarvis began asking for a national day for mothers. She chose the second Sunday in May, because her mother had died near that date.

On May 10, 1908, she sent 500 white carnations to her mother's church in Grafton, West Virginia, for the first Mother's Day service. She herself was in Philadelphia that day. She spoke at a Mother's Day gathering in the department store of John Wanamaker. National Geographic reports that 15,000 people came, and each one was given a carnation.

She wrote letters to business owners, politicians, ministers and newspaper editors across the country. In 1914 the members of Congress and President Wilson made the second Sunday in May a national Mother's Day.

Florists raised the price of carnations before Mother's Day, and card companies sold printed Mother's Day cards. Jarvis wanted people to write their own letters to their mothers and to visit them. In the 1920s she wrote that she did not want Mother's Day to become a "burdensome, wasteful, expensive gift day."

In 1925, when she was 61, she was arrested for disturbing the peace. She had been protesting at a convention of the American War Mothers, a group that sold carnations for Mother's Day. She sued businesses that used the name "Mother's Day," and she spent her money on the fight.

In 1943 she started a petition to end the holiday. That same year she was placed in the Marshall Square Sanitarium, a hospital in West Chester, Pennsylvania. She stayed there until she died. The records do not say who placed her there. The historian Katharine Antolini found that people connected with the flower and greeting-card businesses paid her bills there. Jarvis died on November 24, 1948, at 84, with no money left. She never had children.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anna-jarvis" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Armistice Day, 1919 and 1938" -->
The fighting in the First World War stopped at 11 o'clock in the morning on November 11, 1918. It stopped under an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting. In November 1919 President Wilson asked Americans to mark the first anniversary as Armistice Day. He asked for parades, public meetings and a short stop in work at 11 a.m.

By 1926 the lawmakers of 27 states had made November 11 a holiday. That year the members of Congress asked the president to call for flags and for ceremonies of "friendly relations with all other peoples."

On May 13, 1938, the members of Congress made Armistice Day a legal holiday, "dedicated to the cause of world peace." By then all 48 states kept the day. Groups of First World War veterans backed the law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving, stores and the date fight" -->
On November 27, 1924, workers at Macy's department store in New York City marched to the store in costume. They brought floats and animals from the Central Park Zoo. At the end of the parade, Santa Claus arrived. The store's owners made the parade a yearly event.

In 1939 the last Thursday in November fell on November 30. President Franklin Roosevelt was worried that a short shopping season before Christmas would slow the country's recovery from the Great Depression. The Great Depression was the long period of hard times in the 1930s, when many Americans had no work. Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving a week earlier, to November 23, to help stores.

The governors of 32 states followed him. The governors of 16 states kept the last Thursday. For two years, Americans kept two different Thanksgivings. In 1941 the members of Congress set Thanksgiving by law as the fourth Thursday in November. Roosevelt signed the law on December 26, 1941.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day, 1905 to 1937" -->
Angelo Noce, an Italian immigrant in Denver, asked Colorado's leaders to honor Columbus. In 1905 Colorado's governor, Jesse McDonald, proclaimed the first state Columbus Day.

The Knights of Columbus, a Catholic men's organization, and Generoso Pope, an Italian American leader in New York City, asked Congress for a national day. On April 30, 1934, the members of Congress asked the president to proclaim October 12 each year as Columbus Day, with flags on government buildings. President Roosevelt did so that September, and again in 1937.

It was a day of flags and school ceremonies. Federal workers did not get the day off. Many Italian Americans kept it as a celebration of their own heritage.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Christmas, Labor Day, Juneteenth and Memorial Day" -->
In November 1923 the public schools of Washington, D.C., asked to put up a tree near the White House, and Grace Coolidge, the president's wife, agreed. On Christmas Eve 1923 President Calvin Coolidge lit it. It was the first National Christmas Tree, a fir 48 feet tall from Vermont. About 3,000 people watched.

The 1894 Labor Day law gave the day off only to federal workers. As late as the 1930s, unions told their members to strike if their employers made them work that day.

Juneteenth celebrations grew smaller in the early 1900s. Schools taught that the Emancipation Proclamation had ended slavery, and they did not teach Juneteenth. During the Great Depression, many people had to take work in cities. Employers there did not want to give workers the day off.

On Memorial Day 1937, Chicago police shot and killed ten people who were marching in support of striking steelworkers at the Republic Steel plant.

Federal officials in the 1920s kept using rules against Native ceremonies. In 1921 Charles Burke, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, sent out an order that let the officials in charge of reservations shut down Native dances. He added to it in 1923.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950, Americans argued over whose days the country should mark, and the calendar changed. In 1968 the members of Congress moved three holidays to Mondays, so that workers got more three-day weekends, and made Columbus Day a federal holiday. Black Americans started Kwanzaa in 1966 and brought Juneteenth back. They also campaigned for 15 years for a holiday for Martin Luther King Jr., and President Ronald Reagan signed that law in 1983. Some state leaders refused the King holiday for years. The last state named a day for King in 1999.

Native people began to mark Thanksgiving as a National Day of Mourning in 1970. In 1992 the members of the city council of Berkeley, California, renamed Columbus Day for Indigenous peoples. Indigenous peoples are the first peoples of a place and their descendants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Veterans Day and the Monday holidays, 1954 to 1975" -->
On June 1, 1954, the members of Congress renamed Armistice Day as Veterans Day. By then Americans had also fought in the Second World War and the Korean War. The new name honored the veterans of every war. In 1966 the members of Congress and President Lyndon Johnson named Waterloo, New York, the "birthplace" of Memorial Day. Other towns still claim the first one.

In 1968 Representative Robert McClory, a Republican from Illinois, steered the Uniform Monday Holiday bill through the House. Four business groups backed it: the US Chamber of Commerce, the National Association of Manufacturers, the National Association of Travel Organizations and the National Retail Federation. McClory said it would give families more time together and more chances to visit historic places. Representative H. R. Gross of Iowa asked whether store clerks would get the days off or spend them selling. McClory answered that labor unions supported the bill.

President Johnson signed it on June 28, 1968, and it took effect in 1971. It moved Washington's Birthday to the third Monday in February, Memorial Day to the last Monday in May and Veterans Day to the fourth Monday in October. It also made Columbus Day a federal holiday, on the second Monday in October.

McClory had also wanted to rename Washington's Birthday "Presidents' Day." He dropped the idea to win the votes of Virginia's lawmakers. No law has ever changed the federal name, although many people and stores call it Presidents' Day.

Veterans' groups objected to moving Veterans Day, and 46 states kept November 11 or went back to it. In 1975 the members of Congress put Veterans Day back on November 11.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Kwanzaa, from 1966" -->
Maulana Karenga, a Black activist in Los Angeles, created Kwanzaa in 1966. It runs for seven days, from December 26 to January 1. Each day honors one of seven principles that Karenga wrote in 1965. One of them is Umoja, the Swahili word for unity. Swahili is a language spoken in East Africa. The name Kwanzaa comes from a Swahili phrase meaning "first fruits."

At first Karenga said that Kwanzaa gave Black Americans an alternative to Christmas. In 1997 he wrote that it "was not created to give people an alternative to their own religion or religious holiday." Many families keep both. The US Postal Service issued the first Kwanzaa stamp in 1997. Nobody has made a firm count of how many Americans celebrate Kwanzaa. One estimate in 2009 put the number between 500,000 and 2 million.

In 1971 a California court convicted Karenga of felony assault and false imprisonment for torturing two women at his home. False imprisonment means holding people against their will so they cannot leave. The two women, Deborah Jones and Gail Davis, were members of his group. Jones testified that at Karenga's home, the two women were ordered to take off their clothes. Then they were whipped with an electrical cord and beaten with a karate baton. She testified that a hot soldering iron, a tool that heats metal to join wires, was put in Davis's mouth and pressed against Davis's face. One of Jones's own big toes was squeezed in a vise, a clamp that tightens with a screw. Jones said Karenga also put detergent and running water hoses in their mouths.

Karenga was sentenced to one to ten years in prison and was released on parole in 1975. Parole is an early release from prison under rules. He has always denied the charges, and he calls the case political persecution by the FBI.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Juneteenth comes back, 1968 to 1980" -->
Juneteenth returned during the civil rights movement. In Atlanta, students in the movement wore Juneteenth Freedom buttons. The Poor People's March on Washington ended on June 19, 1968. The marchers took the celebration home to their own cities, among them Milwaukee and Minneapolis. Those two cities still hold some of the largest Juneteenth events.

Al Edwards, a state representative and a Democrat from Houston, wrote a bill to make Juneteenth a Texas state holiday. The members of the Texas legislature passed it in 1979, and Governor Bill Clements signed it. Texas held its first state Juneteenth celebration in 1980.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thanksgiving as a day of mourning, from 1970" -->
On Thanksgiving Day, November 26, 1970, the Wampanoag leader Frank James and other Native people gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth, above Plymouth Rock, for the first National Day of Mourning. About 500 Native people came.

The gathering has been held every Thanksgiving since then. United American Indians of New England organizes it. Many who come go without food from the evening before.

On Thanksgiving Day 1997, Plymouth police stopped the marchers from walking through the center of town. The police used pepper spray on the marchers. Pepper spray is a spray made from hot peppers that burns the eyes and skin. One witness, Chris Groden, said police sprayed it straight into several people's eyes. The police arrested 25 marchers for disorderly conduct and unlawful assembly. The records do not name the officers.

In 1998 Plymouth officials dropped the charges. They agreed that the marchers could walk on Thanksgiving without a permit, forever. They also paid for two plaques about Native history. One stands on Cole's Hill. The other stands in Post Office Square in Plymouth and tells of Metacom, the Wampanoag leader. After the English killed him in 1676, they put his head on a pole in that square and left it there for 25 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frank-james-wamsutta" name="Frank James (Wamsutta)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Frank James (Wamsutta)

> **Who:** A Wampanoag leader whose speech was stopped in 1970 and who started the National Day of Mourning.
> **When and where:** Massachusetts, 1970.

Frank James started the National Day of Mourning in 1970, after the organizers of a state dinner for a Pilgrim anniversary rejected the speech he had written for it. He lived from 1923 to 2001. He was Aquinnah Wampanoag, and he led the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head. He took the name Wamsutta, the name of an earlier Wampanoag leader.

In September 1970, the organizers of the state's dinner for the 350th anniversary of the Pilgrims' landing invited him to speak. He wrote his speech from the Pilgrims' own records, including William Bradford's history and *Mourt's Relation*. It began: "I speak to you as a man, a Wampanoag Man."

In the speech, James wrote that within four days of landing, the Pilgrims had dug up Wampanoag graves and taken the people's stored corn and beans. He wrote that "before 50 years were to pass, the Wampanoag would no longer be a free people."

The organizers read the speech in advance. A state commerce official told him that "the theme of the anniversary celebration is brotherhood and anything inflammatory would have been out of place." Inflammatory means likely to make people angry. The organizers gave him a speech written by their public relations staff. He refused to read it and did not speak at the dinner.

On Thanksgiving Day 1970, he spoke instead on Cole's Hill in Plymouth, beside the statue of the Wampanoag leader Massasoit. Accounts differ on whether he read the same speech or a shorter one. That year he founded United American Indians of New England.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frank-james-wamsutta" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Martin Luther King Jr. Day, 1968 to 1983" -->
It took 15 years of campaigning to make Martin Luther King Jr.'s birthday a national holiday. James Earl Ray, a white man, shot and killed King in Memphis on April 4, 1968. Ray pleaded guilty in 1969. Four days after the killing, Representative John Conyers of Michigan brought a bill to Congress for a national holiday on King's birthday, January 15. The committee in charge of the bill never acted on it.

In the early 1970s, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, the group King had led, gathered 3 million signatures on petitions. In 1973 lawmakers in Illinois made the first state King holiday. State lawmaker Harold Washington wrote the bill.

In 1979 Coretta Scott King, King's widow, spoke for the holiday before Congress. In November 1979 the members of the House voted 252 to 133 for it. Under the rules for that vote, the bill needed two-thirds of the votes, and it fell short. The Congressional Research Service counts it as four votes short. The King Institute at Stanford University counts five.

In 1980 the singer Stevie Wonder released "Happy Birthday," a song asking for the holiday. His money paid for a lobbying office, an office whose staff pressed lawmakers to vote for the bill. Petitions reached more than 6 million signatures. On January 15, 1983, more than 100,000 people rallied at the Washington Monument.

The members of the House passed the bill 338 to 90 on August 2, 1983. In the Senate, Jesse Helms of North Carolina led the opposition. He called for the FBI's secret recordings of King to be made public. A court had sealed them until 2027. He also gave senators a 300-page paper that claimed King had ties to communists. Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan called it a "packet of filth" and stamped on it. Helms's plan to open the FBI records lost, 3 votes to 90. Other senators said another paid day off would cost too much.

Senators passed the bill 78 to 22 on October 19, 1983. Reagan had wanted a day of remembrance, not a paid holiday. He signed the law on November 2, 1983. The first King holiday was kept on January 20, 1986.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The states that held out, 1986 to 1999" -->
The 1983 law gave the day off only to federal workers. Each state's leaders decided for themselves. By January 1989, 44 states kept a King holiday.

Some state lawmakers joined King's day to a day for Confederate generals. Alabama's holiday was named for Robert E. Lee and King, and Mississippi's for King and Lee. Arkansas combined King and Lee from 1985. Virginia called its day Lee-Jackson-King Day, for Lee, the Confederate general Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson and King.

In Arizona, Governor Bruce Babbitt made King's day a paid state holiday by his own order in 1986. His successor, Evan Mecham, canceled it in January 1987. Mecham said Babbitt had no legal power to create it. Groups across the country moved their conventions out of Arizona. Stevie Wonder and other musicians refused to perform there.

In November 1990, Arizona voters turned down two ballot measures for a King holiday. The National Football League then took the 1993 Super Bowl away from Tempe, Arizona, and moved it to Pasadena, California. Phoenix had expected about $200 million from the game. In November 1992, 61 percent of Arizona voters approved Martin Luther King Jr./Civil Rights Day. Arizona is the only state whose voters approved the holiday themselves.

New Hampshire's lawmakers made a "Civil Rights Day" in 1991. In June 1999 they renamed it for King. New Hampshire was the last state to do so.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="liz-byrd" name="Liz Byrd" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Liz Byrd

> **Who:** A Cheyenne schoolteacher and lawmaker who brought the King holiday bill to Wyoming's legislature nine times.
> **When and where:** Wyoming, 1981 to 1990.

Liz Byrd brought a bill for a Martin Luther King Jr. holiday before the Wyoming legislature nine times before it passed. Her full name was Harriett Elizabeth Byrd, and she was born on April 20, 1926. Her grandfather Charles Rhone had come to Wyoming Territory as a boy in 1876.

The University of Wyoming turned down her application. Her son believes it was because she was Black. She earned her teaching degree at West Virginia State College in 1949. The school district in Cheyenne would not hire her at first. The district's officials hired her in 1959, and she became the first fully certified full-time Black teacher in Wyoming. She taught second grade for most of her 37 years as a teacher.

She served in the Wyoming House of Representatives from 1981 to 1988. In 1989 she became the first Black member of the Wyoming Senate.

One Republican who voted against her King holiday bill was Loren Willford. He said a Wyoming holiday should honor someone who had been in Wyoming, such as Chief Washakie, a Shoshone leader. In 1990 the lawmakers passed the holiday with a longer name, "Martin Luther King, Jr./Wyoming Equality Day." Her son said she never liked the added words but took them rather than lose the bill again. Another account gives the year as 1991.

She died on January 27, 2015, at 88. Governor Matt Mead ordered flags lowered in her honor.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="liz-byrd" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Columbus Day renamed, and Pride marches, 1970 to 1999" -->
In 1977, people at a United Nations conference in Geneva, Switzerland, discussed replacing Columbus Day with a day for Indigenous peoples. The year 1992 was the 500th anniversary of Columbus's first voyage. That year a group from the San Francisco Bay Area called Resistance 500 asked the city council of Berkeley, California, to act. The council members renamed the day Indigenous Peoples Day.

On June 28, 1970, several thousand people marched from Greenwich Village to Central Park in New York City. The march came one year after police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in the city. The organizers' counts ranged from 3,000 to 20,000. Craig Rodwell and three others had proposed a yearly march in 1969. Marchers repeated it every June. On June 11, 1999, President Bill Clinton proclaimed June "Gay and Lesbian Pride Month." It was the first time a president had proclaimed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000 the members of Congress have added one federal holiday, Juneteenth, in 2021. Presidents have disagreed about which days to honor. Virginia and Arkansas stopped joining King's day to days for Confederate generals, although Alabama and Mississippi still honor Robert E. Lee on the same Monday as King. In 2021 President Joe Biden became the first president to proclaim Indigenous Peoples' Day. In 2025 President Donald Trump proclaimed Columbus Day only, and he issued no Juneteenth proclamation. States and cities also added days for groups that had never had one, such as Lunar New Year and Diwali. As of September 2026, Juneteenth, King's day and Columbus Day are all still federal holidays.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of most combined days, 2000 to 2020" -->
In May 2000, South Carolina's governor, Jim Hodges, signed a law that gave all state workers King's day off. South Carolina was the last state to do so. Before then, state workers there could choose King's day or one of three Confederate holidays.

From 1984 to 2000, Virginia kept Lee-Jackson-King Day, which honored the two Confederate generals and King on the same day. In 2000 Virginia's lawmakers split it into two holidays. In April 2020 Virginia's governor, Ralph Northam, signed a law that ended Lee-Jackson Day and made Election Day a state holiday. Northam said the old day "commemorates a lost cause." CNN reported that both generals had enslaved people and had fought to keep slavery.

Arkansas stopped honoring Lee on King's day in 2017. Alabama and Mississippi still honor Lee's birthday on the same Monday as King's day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Juneteenth becomes a federal holiday, 2021" -->
Juneteenth became a federal holiday in 2021. On May 25, 2020, Derek Chauvin, a Minneapolis police officer, killed George Floyd. That June, many Juneteenth gatherings called for changes in the way police do their work.

In June 2021 the senators passed the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act. On June 16, 2021, the members of the House passed it 415 votes to 14, according to the House's own record of the vote. All 14 votes against it came from Republicans. President Biden signed it on June 17, 2021. It was the first new federal holiday since King's day in 1983.

The leaders of each state still decide whether state workers get the day off. In 2025 the Congressional Research Service, which studies laws for Congress, counted 31 states and Washington, D.C., with a permanent paid Juneteenth holiday. PolitiFact, a fact-checking news site, reported that count in June 2026. Alabama was the most recent state to add it, in May 2025.

On Juneteenth 2025, President Trump signed no proclamation for the day. He wrote that there were "too many non-working holidays," CBS News reported. In December 2025, CBS News reported that officials of the National Park Service had dropped Martin Luther King Jr. Day and Juneteenth from their list of days with free entry to the parks in 2026. They added June 14, which is Flag Day and President Trump's birthday. The change affects only park fees. Both days are still federal holidays.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="opal-lee" name="Opal Lee" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Opal Lee

> **Who:** A retired Fort Worth teacher who organized Juneteenth celebrations for about 40 years and campaigned for it to become a national holiday.
> **When and where:** Fort Worth, Texas, 1939 to today.

Opal Lee led the campaign that made Juneteenth a national holiday, and she stood with President Biden when he signed the law on June 17, 2021. She was born Opal Flake in Marshall, Texas, on October 7, 1926. When she was nine, her family moved to Fort Worth.

In June 1939, her parents bought a house at 940 East Annie Street in Fort Worth, in a white neighborhood. On June 19, 1939, when she was 12, a white mob of about 500 people drove the family out. The mob dragged out their furniture and burned the house to the ground. Lee remembered that police officers were there and did not stop the mob. She said that when her father came home from work with a gun, the police told him that if he fired, they would let the mob have the family. No record names anyone in the mob, and no record shows that anyone was arrested.

She became a teacher after she graduated from Wiley College in 1953. When she retired in 1977, she helped found the Tarrant County Black Historical and Genealogical Society. She began planning Fort Worth's Juneteenth celebrations, and she led them for about 40 years.

In 2016, when she was 89, she organized a walk of 1,400 miles from Fort Worth to Washington, D.C., to ask for a national Juneteenth holiday. Her family worried about her health. So she walked about 300 miles herself, in pieces of 2.5 miles each. The 2.5 miles stand for the two and a half years it took for the news of freedom to reach Texas. Her petition for the holiday gathered 1.6 million signatures, according to the National Women's History Museum.

On May 3, 2024, President Biden gave her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest honor the country gives to a person who is not in the military. That year builders put up a new house for her on the lot where her family's house had burned. She paid $10 for the land, KERA News reported in 2024.

On Juneteenth 2026, nearly 600 people walked Opal's Walk for Freedom in Fort Worth, the Fort Worth Report counted. Lee did not attend. Her family led the walk while she rested, a few months before her 100th birthday.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="opal-lee" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigenous Peoples' Day and Columbus Day, 2021 to 2026" -->
On October 8, 2021, President Biden proclaimed Indigenous Peoples' Day. He wrote that for generations "Federal policies systematically sought to assimilate and displace Native people and eradicate Native cultures." To assimilate people means to make them give up their own ways and take on someone else's. To eradicate means to wipe out. Biden also proclaimed Columbus Day each year from 2021 to 2024. In 2020 Colorado's lawmakers replaced Columbus Day in their state with Cabrini Day.

On October 9, 2025, President Trump proclaimed Columbus Day and did not mention Indigenous Peoples' Day. His proclamation said, "Left-wing radicals toppled his statues, vandalized his monuments, tarnished his character." NPR reported that in 2025 the Pew Research Center, a group that studies public life, counted 30 states and three territories that recognize Columbus Day in some way. Its researchers also counted 17 states and Washington, D.C., that honor Indigenous people on that day. Some states do both.

The National Day of Mourning is still held at noon on Cole's Hill in Plymouth every Thanksgiving. The most recent one was on November 27, 2025. Only Native people speak, and many of those who come go without food from the evening before. Frank James's granddaughter, Kisha James, helps organize it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pride Month, and new days for more communities" -->
President Barack Obama proclaimed Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual and Transgender Pride Month in 2009. President Biden added more groups to the name in 2022.

Other communities' days also spread. Cinco de Mayo, the fifth of May, marks the Mexican army's defeat of French troops at Puebla, Mexico, on May 5, 1862. Latinos in California began celebrating it during the Civil War, according to David Hayes-Bautista, a scholar at the University of California, Los Angeles. It is kept far less in Mexico than in the United States.

In September 2023, New York's governor, Kathy Hochul, signed a law that closes all the state's public schools for Lunar New Year. Lunar New Year is the start of the year in calendars based on the moon. In October 2025, California's governor, Gavin Newsom, signed a law making Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights, a state holiday from 2026. California was the third state to do so, after Pennsylvania and Connecticut.

Halloween, on October 31, is not a legal holiday, but Americans spend billions of dollars on it. The National Retail Federation, a trade group for stores, surveys shoppers each year. Its 2025 survey forecast a record $13.1 billion in Halloween spending. Its survey in September 2026 forecast $13.5 billion for 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
