## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Slavery was against the law after 1865, but in the first half of the 1900s, Southern lawmakers, sheriffs, judges and companies built a system that took its place. Lawmakers kept Black citizens apart from white citizens by law and kept almost all of them from voting. Sheriffs, judges and mine companies kept Black prisoners and people in debt working without pay. White mobs lynched thousands of Black people.

The last people born into slavery were growing old. From 1936 to 1938, writers paid by the federal government interviewed more than 2,300 of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Segregation and the stolen vote" -->
After the Supreme Court's *Plessy* ruling of 1896, lawmakers across the South passed laws that kept Black and white people apart in schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, courtrooms and cemeteries, and at water fountains. Keeping people apart by race is called segregation. These laws became known as Jim Crow laws.

Southern lawmakers also took away the vote from Black men. The delegates who wrote Mississippi's constitution of 1890 set the pattern. They required a poll tax, a fee a man had to pay before he could vote. They required a literacy test, a reading test. They added an understanding clause, under which a man had to explain a part of the constitution to the satisfaction of the official who registered voters. In 1898 the delegates who wrote Louisiana's new constitution added a grandfather clause. It let a man skip the tests if his father or grandfather had been able to vote in the years before Black men could.

In Louisiana, 130,344 Black voters were registered in 1896. By 1904 the number was 1,342.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Lynching" -->
A lynching is the killing of a person by a mob, with no trial. The Equal Justice Initiative has documented more than 4,400 lynchings of Black people carried out as racial terror between 1877 and 1950. Other counts, which use different years and different rules for what counts, give different totals. White mobs used lynching to hold Black people at the bottom of the South's system of work and of racial rank. Black Southerners left for the North and the West to get away from the terror, in a movement known as the Great Migration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Forced labor after slavery" -->
Convict leasing and debt peonage kept Black people working without pay well into the 1900s. Debt peonage meant forcing a person to work to pay off a debt. Douglas Blackmon told this history in his book *Slavery by Another Name*, which won the Pulitzer Prize. PBS made it into a film in 2012.

Alabama kept leasing its prisoners to mine companies until 1928. In 1911, 123 Black county prisoners leased to the Banner Mine were killed in an explosion there. In 1924 a prisoner named James Knox, from Mobile, died at the Flat Top mine. His death was first reported as a suicide by poison. An autopsy, an examination of the body after death, found that he had died of heart failure and that the poison had been put into his body after he died. He had been whipped with a steel wire and lowered into hot water. The historian Mary Ellen Curtin describes it as a vat of boiling water. The accounts do not name the men who did it. On June 30, 1928, Alabama became the last state to end convict leasing.

Alonzo Bailey was a farm worker in Alabama. He signed on to work for the Riverside Company for $12 a month and took $15 in advance. After about a month he left. Under Alabama law, leaving a job after taking an advance was a crime, and the law barred him from telling the court why he had left. He was fined $30 and sentenced to 136 days of hard labor. On January 3, 1911, in *Bailey v. Alabama*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 7 to 2 that the law broke the 13th Amendment. Booker T. Washington secretly helped pay for Bailey's lawyers. Other Southern states kept laws like it for decades. Georgia's law was struck down in 1942 and Florida's in 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The last voices" -->
From 1936 to 1938, the Federal Writers' Project, a program of the federal government that gave jobs to writers, sent interviewers to talk with people who had been born into slavery. They worked in 17 states. They wrote down more than 2,300 life stories and took 500 photographs. The Library of Congress keeps them in a collection called "Born in Slavery."

The Library of Congress also keeps sound recordings of people who were born into slavery, made between 1932 and 1975. The collection is called "Voices Remembering Slavery."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="fountain-hughes" name="Fountain Hughes" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Fountain Hughes

> **Who:** A man born into slavery in Virginia, one of the few people born enslaved whose own voice survives on a recording.
> **When and where:** Born in Charlottesville, Virginia. Recorded by the Library of Congress in Baltimore, Maryland, on June 11, 1949.

Fountain Hughes was recorded talking about slavery in 1949, and the recording still exists. On June 11, 1949, in Baltimore, Hermond Norwood of the Library of Congress interviewed him. Hughes began: "My name is Fountain Hughes. I was born in Charlottesville, Virginia." He said he was 101 years old, which would mean he was born in about 1848. Some reference books give 1854, and some historians argue for a later year.

His grandfather, Wormley Hughes, was the enslaved head gardener at Monticello, Thomas Jefferson's plantation. Fountain Hughes said his father "got killed in the Army," and that left the children "to live on whatever people choose to give us."

He was never sold, he said, because "I was too young to sell." But he described how others were sold: "They'd sell us like they sell horses and cows and hogs and all like that. Have an auction bench, and they'd put you on up on the bench and bid on you." He could not go anywhere without his enslaver's permission: "I couldn't go from here across the street, or I couldn't go through nobody's house without I have a note or something from my master."

He said of being enslaved: "You're nothing but a dog. You're not a thing but a dog!"

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The 14th Amendment of 1868 promised Black Americans equal protection of the laws, and the 15th Amendment of 1870 promised them the vote. For almost a hundred years, federal officials did not enforce those promises in the South. Between 1954 and 1965, a Supreme Court ruling and two laws passed by Congress gave federal officials the tools to enforce them. In 1976 Alex Haley's book *Roots* led many Black families to start tracing their ancestors back to slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The amendments enforced" -->
On May 17, 1954, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." Inherently means by their very nature. The ruling overturned the rule of *Plessy v. Ferguson* for public schools.

The Civil Rights Act became law on July 2, 1964, and the Voting Rights Act on August 6, 1965. These laws gave federal officials the power to enforce the 14th and 15th Amendments in the South. They came ninety-nine years after the 13th Amendment ended slavery.

In 1995 the members of Mississippi's legislature voted to approve the 13th Amendment, 130 years after it became part of the Constitution. Their vote was never officially filed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Roots and the search for family" -->
In 1976 Alex Haley published *Roots*, a book about his family, which he traced back to an African man enslaved in America. In January 1977 the ABC television network showed *Roots* as a miniseries, a story told over several nights. An estimated 130 million people watched it, the largest audience a television series had ever had. Afterward, many Black families began searching for their own ancestors.

Parts of Haley's work were later challenged. In 1978 Haley paid the writer Harold Courlander $650,000 to settle a lawsuit claiming that Haley had copied passages from Courlander's novel *The African*. Professional genealogists, people who trace family histories from records, have challenged links in the chain of records behind Haley's family story.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alex-haley" name="Alex Haley" movie="Roots (1977)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alex Haley

> **Who:** A writer who traced his family back to an enslaved African ancestor in the book *Roots*, and whose work led many Black Americans to search for their own families. Parts of his research and writing have been challenged.
> **When and where:** Born in Ithaca, New York, in 1921. *Roots* published in 1976. Died in Tennessee in 1992.
> **Movie:** *Roots* (1977), a television miniseries on ABC based on Haley's book. It tells his family's story as drama. It is not a documentary about Haley.

Alex Haley wrote *Roots*, a book about his family's history from Africa through slavery. He was born in Ithaca, New York, on August 11, 1921. He spent part of his childhood in Henning, Tennessee, his mother's hometown. There his aunts and grandmothers told the family's stories. One story was about an African ancestor named Toby, who "insisted that his real name was Kin-te."

Haley served twenty years in the United States Coast Guard, and he became a writer while he was in it. Later he traveled to Gambia, in West Africa. There a griot, a keeper of history who tells it from memory, told him the story of "his ancestor Kunta Kinte, captured by slave traders."

*Roots* came out in 1976. It won a special Pulitzer Prize in 1977. The television miniseries ran on eight nights in a row in January 1977 and won 145 awards, including nine Emmys.

In 1978 Haley agreed to pay Harold Courlander $650,000 to settle Courlander's lawsuit. Genealogists have questioned whether the records support every link Haley drew between his family and Kunta Kinte. Haley died in a Tennessee hospital in February 1992, of problems caused by diabetes.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have built museums about slavery, opened its records to the public and argued over what the country owes the descendants of enslaved people. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture opened in Washington in 2016. In a project begun in 2015, volunteers made about 1.8 million names from the Freedmen's Bureau records searchable online. In 2025 and 2026, federal officials ordered exhibits about slavery removed from some national parks, and judges were still deciding in September 2026 whether that was lawful.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Museums, a holiday and a late vote" -->
The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture opened in Washington, D.C., on September 24, 2016. Its history galleries begin with the slave trade. Among the things on display is a cabin where enslaved people lived. The International African American Museum opened in Charleston, South Carolina, on June 27, 2023. It stands at Gadsden's Wharf, one of the main places where slave ships landed their captives.

On June 17, 2021, the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act became law. It made June 19, the day in 1865 when Union soldiers announced freedom in Galveston, Texas, a federal holiday.

In 2012 two Mississippi residents found that the paperwork for the state's 1995 vote approving the 13th Amendment had never been filed. It was officially recorded on February 7, 2013.

On December 20, 2024, Missouri's governor, Mike Parson, pardoned Celia, the enslaved woman hanged in 1855 for killing the man who had raped her for five years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The records opened" -->
For millions of Black Americans, the records of the Freedmen's Bureau are the first papers in which their ancestors appear by name, according to FamilySearch and the Smithsonian. The records include marriage registers, work contracts and school lists. In a project begun in 2015, volunteers for FamilySearch, a genealogy group, indexed about 1.8 million names from these records. The names can be searched at discoverfreedmen.org.

In 1838 the Maryland Jesuits, priests of a Catholic religious order, sold 272 enslaved people to plantations in Louisiana. They sold them in part to keep Georgetown College, now Georgetown University, from running out of money. It is one of the best-documented mass sales of people in American history. The people sold are now called the GU272. In 2016 Georgetown's leaders announced that descendants of the GU272 would get special consideration when they applied, and they renamed buildings. Genealogists have traced thousands of descendants, according to the Georgetown Memory Project in 2021. In 2021 the Jesuits and leaders of the descendants set up the Descendants Truth and Reconciliation Foundation, and the Jesuits pledged $100 million to it. It is the largest pledge of its kind by a religious group in the United States, according to news reports.

The Cherokee Nation's treaty of 1866 had promised its Freedmen "all the rights of native Cherokees." In 2017, in the case *Cherokee Nation v. Nash*, a federal court ruled that the Cherokee Nation was bound by that treaty. Descendants of Cherokee Freedmen had a right to full citizenship in the nation. The Cherokee Nation accepted the ruling. In 2021 the nation's own Supreme Court struck the words "by blood" out of its rules on citizenship.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="melisande-short-colomb" name="Mélisande Short-Colomb" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mélisande Short-Colomb

> **Who:** A descendant of two people sold by the Maryland Jesuits in 1838, who enrolled at Georgetown University at the age of 63.
> **When and where:** From New Orleans. Entered Georgetown University in Washington, D.C., in the fall of 2017.

Mélisande Short-Colomb enrolled at Georgetown University in 2017, at 63 years old, 179 years after the Jesuits of Maryland sold her ancestors to keep the college going. She was a retired chef from New Orleans.

She descends from Abraham Mahoney and Mary Ellen Queen. Both were among the 272 people the Jesuits sold in 1838. She grew up hearing family stories from her grandmother and her great-aunts. In the summer of 2016 she learned that Mahoney and Queen had been part of the 1838 sale. In April 2017 Georgetown held a Liturgy of Remembrance, a religious service honoring the people who were sold. After that, she applied to the university.

At Georgetown she wrote a play, *Here I Am*, about her ancestors and the university.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="melisande-short-colomb" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Repair debated" -->
Reparations are payments or other actions meant to make up for a wrong. Since 1989 members of Congress have argued over whether the United States should pay reparations for slavery.

Representative John Conyers first introduced a bill called H.R. 40 in 1989. It would set up a commission to study slavery and its effects and to recommend ways to repair them. The number 40 comes from the promise of forty acres. In 2021 the members of the House Judiciary Committee voted to move it forward for the first time. On January 3, 2025, Representative Ayanna Pressley of Massachusetts introduced it again. As of September 2026, neither the House nor the Senate had passed it, according to the records of Congress.

In 2019 the city council of Evanston, Illinois, voted to create the first reparations program paid for by a city. Council member Robin Rue Simmons led the effort. City officials set aside $10 million and in March 2021 approved its first grants of $25,000 for housing. The program is for Black residents who lived in Evanston between 1919 and 1969, when city policies discriminated against Black people in housing. By August 2026, according to the Evanston RoundTable newspaper, 137 people had received a total of $3.47 million. In May 2024 a conservative group called Judicial Watch sued the city, arguing that paying people based on race breaks the 14th Amendment. On June 16, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice asked to join the lawsuit against the city. The case was still open in September 2026.

In California, a state task force delivered its final report on June 29, 2023. According to the state's Department of Justice, it made more than 115 recommendations, including a formal apology. Governor Gavin Newsom signed the state's apology in September 2024. On October 10, 2025, he signed a law creating a state Bureau for Descendants of American Slavery. The bureau is to check who qualifies and to recommend forms of repair. The law provides for no payments.

In Maryland, Governor Wes Moore vetoed a bill for a state reparations commission in May 2025. On December 16, 2025, the members of the General Assembly voted to override his veto, 31 to 14 in the Senate and 93 to 35 in the House. The 23-member commission is to study state and local policies from 1877 to 1965.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the national parks may show, 2025 and 2026" -->
On March 27, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an executive order called "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, who is in charge of the national parks, then ordered a review of how the parks describe the history of the United States.

In September 2025, officials ordered a copy of the 1863 photograph of Peter's scarred back removed from an exhibit at Fort Pulaski National Monument, near Savannah, Georgia. The Washington Post and the New York Times first reported the order. In January 2026, officials removed the exhibits about slavery at the President's House site in Philadelphia, the house Ona Judge escaped from in 1796. On February 16, 2026, Judge Cynthia M. Rufe ordered them put back. The government appealed, and an appeals court later ruled that the administration had the power to replace that exhibit.

In a separate case, Judge Angel Kelley of the federal court in Massachusetts ruled on June 12, 2026, that the order could not be carried out at any of the park system's 433 sites. She ordered the removed exhibits put back by July 3. On July 2, 2026, the First Circuit Court of Appeals paused her order, so removals could go on while the case continued. By the government's own count in a June 2026 court filing, exhibits had been removed or changed at 37 park sites. The case was still undecided in September 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joycelyn-davis" name="Joycelyn Davis" movie="Descendant (2022)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Joycelyn Davis

> **Who:** A descendant of Charlie and Maggie Lewis. Charlie Lewis was one of the Africans carried to Mobile, Alabama, on the *Clotilda* in 1860.
> **When and where:** Africatown, in Mobile, Alabama, where she has lived all her life.
> **Movie:** *Descendant* (2022), a documentary by Margaret Brown about the *Clotilda*, Africatown and the survivors' families. Joycelyn Davis appears in it.

Joycelyn Davis descends from people who survived the last known slave ship to reach the United States, and she has worked to keep their memory alive in the town they founded. Her ancestors Charlie and Maggie Lewis were among the Africans carried to Mobile on the *Clotilda*. She has lived in Africatown all her life. As a child she visited her great-grandmother in Lewis Quarters, one of the first pieces of land the *Clotilda* survivors bought. It was named for Charlie and Maggie Lewis.

She went to the school and the church that her ancestors founded. In 2017 she sued the International Paper Company over pollution from its old mill near Mobile. She is a cancer survivor.

While divers were still searching for the wreck of the *Clotilda*, she said: "Our community is suffering, and you're asking about a ship?" After the wreck was confirmed in 2019, she came to see the ship as a way to bring money and attention to Africatown. She organized a reunion of *Clotilda* descendants in 2019. Of the crowd that came, she told National Geographic: "I never in a million years thought it would be that big." She also organized a yearly festival called Spirit of Our Ancestors. The 2021 festival, the third, honored the women who survived the *Clotilda*.

She appears in *Descendant*, the documentary by Margaret Brown. It won a special jury award at the Sundance Film Festival in January 2022 and was released in theaters and on Netflix on October 21, 2022.
