## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
On January 1, 1808, it became illegal to bring enslaved people into the United States from abroad. The number of people held in slavery kept growing anyway, mostly through children born into it. The census counted 697,624 enslaved people in 1790 and 3,953,760 in 1860. Planters in the new cotton states of the Deep South bought more than a million people from Virginia, Maryland and the other older slave states. Those sales split families apart.

Enslaved people resisted every day, and some rose up in armed revolt. Thousands escaped to the free states and to Canada. In the slave states, lawmakers made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to read.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton" -->
In 1793 Eli Whitney built a cotton gin, a machine that pulls the seeds out of cotton. The gin made it pay to grow upland cotton, the kind that grows away from the coast, and slavery spread west across the cotton lands. Cotton growers in the United States produced fewer than 9,000 bales a year around 1790. By 1800 they produced more than 200,000 bales, and by 1860 about 4 million. The number of slave states grew from six in 1790 to fifteen in 1860. By 1860 the South grew about 75 percent of the world's cotton. Cotton made up more than half of everything the United States sold to other countries, measured in dollars.

By 1860 the people who held four million people in slavery valued them at $3 billion or more. That was more than all the money invested in the country's railroads and factories put together. Enslavers bought, sold, priced and mortgaged people as property.

The cotton land of these years was Native land. In September 1830, in the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, the Choctaw gave up more than 10 million acres in Mississippi and Alabama. It was the first treaty signed after members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act of 1830. The Chickasaw gave up their land in the Treaty of Pontotoc Creek in 1832. Both nations were forced to move west under the Removal Act. Enslavers then brought in enslaved people to clear and plant the land the Choctaw and Chickasaw had lost. Mississippi's cotton crop grew from about 20 million pounds in 1820 to 193.2 million pounds in 1839. The number of enslaved people in Mississippi grew from 65,659 in 1830 to 195,211 in 1840.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The slave trade inside the country" -->
Between about 1790 and 1860, slave traders moved more than one million people from the Upper South to the cotton lands of the Deep South. From 1820 on, they moved about 200,000 people every ten years. They marched people overland in coffles, lines of people chained together. They also shipped people along the coast to New Orleans, which had the largest slave market in the country.

The biggest trading firm belonged to Isaac Franklin and John Armfield. From 1828 to 1836 they ran it from 1315 Duke Street in Alexandria, Virginia. Their compound took up half a city block, with separate pens for men and for women. At their busiest they moved between 1,000 and 1,500 people a year, and about 8,500 in the nine years of the partnership. They marched people to Natchez, in Mississippi, and shipped others to New Orleans on their own ships. Franklin and Armfield also sold enslaved women to buyers who wanted them for sex. This was known as the "fancy" trade.

Sale broke families apart all the time. The Equal Justice Initiative, a group that studies racial injustice, has added up the effects. The trade separated nearly half of the people it moved from a husband, a wife or a parent. About one enslaved marriage in three ended because a husband or wife was sold away. Traders sold children away from their mothers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Work, food and home" -->
Enslaved people worked from sunup to sundown, six days a week. On cotton and sugar plantations, an overseer drove them in gangs, row by row across the fields. This was called the gang system. On the rice plantations of the Lowcountry, each person was given a set task for the day. When it was done, the rest of the day was the person's own. This was called the task system.

Solomon Northup picked cotton in Louisiana in the 1840s. In his book *Twelve Years a Slave* (1853), he wrote: "An ordinary day's work is two hundred pounds." Every night the cotton was weighed. A person who picked less was whipped. A lash is one stroke of the whip. Northup wrote that "Twenty-five are deemed a mere brush," fifty lashes were "the ordinary penalty," and "one hundred is called severe."

Frederick Douglass lived as an enslaved child on a plantation in Maryland. In his *Narrative* (1845), he recorded the food allowance there for one month: eight pounds of pork, or the same amount of fish, and one bushel of cornmeal. The clothing given out each year was so thin that small children too young for field work often had only a long shirt to wear. Enslaved people added to their food with gardens, fishing and trapping where their owners allowed it.

Formerly enslaved people interviewed in the 1930s remembered cabins of one room, with dirt floors and beds made of boards. The law did not recognize their marriages. An owner could sell a husband, a wife or a child on any day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Punishment" -->
Owners and overseers flogged enslaved people for picking too little cotton, for arriving late and for running away. A flogging meant being tied and struck again and again on the bare body with a whip. The whip named most often in Southern sources is the cowskin, also called the cowhide. It was a whip made of dried, twisted cow's hide. Each stroke cut the skin. Deep or repeated whipping left raised scars for life.

Frederick Douglass described a flogging he saw as a child. His enslaver, Aaron Anthony, stripped Douglass's Aunt Hester from her neck to her waist. He tied her crossed hands to a hook in a ceiling beam, so that she stood on her toes. Then he whipped her with "the heavy cowskin" until her blood dripped to the floor.

In 1839 the abolitionist Theodore Weld published *American Slavery As It Is*, a collection of reports from eyewitnesses and of advertisements from Southern newspapers. One witness saw an overseer whip a man named Joe "with a heavy cow-hide full six feet long" for arriving five minutes late.

The advertisements were written by the enslavers themselves. On July 18, 1838, Micajah Ricks of Nash County, North Carolina, placed one in the Raleigh *Standard*. It began: "Ranaway, a negro woman and two children." It went on: "a few days before she went off, I burnt her with a hot iron, on the left side of her face, I tried to make the letter M." The woman's name is not given. Other advertisements of the 1830s describe runaways' backs as "much marked by the whip."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sexual violence" -->
Enslaved women and girls had no protection under the law from the men who owned them. Harriet Jacobs later wrote a book about what one of those men did to her.

Jacobs was born enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in 1813. In 1825 she was willed to a three-year-old girl, Mary Matilda Norcom, which put her under the control of the girl's father, Dr. James Norcom. From her early teens, Norcom tried to force her into sex. He owned her, and for years she could not get away from him. To protect herself she began a relationship with a white lawyer, Samuel Tredwell Sawyer, and had two children with him, Joseph and Louisa.

In 1835 she ran away. She hid in a crawl space above a storeroom in her grandmother's house for seven years. The space was so small she could barely sit up. In 1842 she escaped north by boat. Ten years later the wife of her employer, Cornelia Grinnell Willis, bought her freedom. Jacobs told her story in *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl*, dated 1861 and published under the name "Linda Brent." Historian Jean Fagan Yellin later proved from Jacobs's letters that she wrote it herself. She died in Washington, D.C., in 1897.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Churches and hush harbors" -->
Richard Allen was born enslaved. In Philadelphia, he founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, known as the AME Church. It was formally organized in 1816, and it was the first Black church denomination in the country. A denomination is a group of churches that share the same beliefs and leaders.

In the slave states, enslaved people also worshipped out of their owners' sight. They met in brush arbors, shelters built of branches, which they called "hush harbors." They sang spirituals, religious songs that carried their faith and also hidden messages.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reading made a crime" -->
South Carolina had banned teaching enslaved people to write in 1740, and Georgia followed in 1755. After 1829 more slave states passed such laws or made them harsher. Georgia passed a new law in December 1829. Louisiana and North Carolina followed in 1830, Virginia in 1831 and Alabama in 1832. South Carolina made its law harsher in 1834. Most of the slave states had such a law, but not all of them.

The punishments depended on who broke the law. A white person who taught was fined. In North Carolina the fine was $200. Georgia and other states added jail. Black teachers and learners were whipped. North Carolina set the punishment at 39 lashes, and in Virginia a person caught at a school could get up to 20 lashes. Several of these laws covered free Black people as well as the enslaved.

Enslavers had several fears. A person who could write could forge a pass, a note from an owner that allowed an enslaved person to travel. A person who could read could read the newspapers of the abolitionists, the people working to end slavery. A reader could also read the Bible without an owner choosing which parts to hear. After Nat Turner's revolt in 1831, enslavers feared organized revolt most of all.

People learned anyway. Historians estimate that about 5 to 10 percent of enslaved people could read by 1860. Some estimates for the last years of slavery run as high as 20 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-douglass-slavery-freedom" name="Frederick Douglass" movie="Becoming Frederick Douglass (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A man born enslaved in Maryland who taught himself to read against the wishes of his enslaver, escaped, and became the best-known abolitionist of his century.
> **When and where:** Born in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. Escaped on September 3, 1838. Died in 1895.
> **Movie:** *Becoming Frederick Douglass* (2022), a documentary by Stanley Nelson, shown on PBS. It is about Douglass's life.

Frederick Douglass learned to read while he was enslaved, after his enslaver forbade anyone to teach him. He was born in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. No record gives the exact day. He chose February 14 as his birthday.

As a boy he lived in Baltimore, in the household of Hugh and Sophia Auld. Sophia Auld began to teach him the alphabet. Hugh Auld stopped her. Douglass wrote in his *Narrative* (1845) that hearing Auld forbid it was "the first decidedly antislavery lecture" he ever heard.

He kept learning in secret. He traded bread with hungry white boys in the streets for reading lessons. At about age twelve he bought a book of speeches called *The Columbian Orator*.

On September 3, 1838, Douglass escaped. He rode a train north through Baltimore, dressed in a sailor's clothes and carrying a sailor's papers that he had borrowed. Anna Murray, a free Black woman, helped him, and he married her.

In 1845 he published *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*. In 1847 he started a newspaper, the *North Star*. For the rest of the century he spoke and wrote against slavery, and more people heard him speak than any other abolitionist. He was also photographed more than any other abolitionist. He died in 1895.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-douglass-slavery-freedom" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="milla-granson" name="Milla Granson (Lilly Ann Granderson)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Milla Granson (Lilly Ann Granderson)

> **Who:** An enslaved woman who ran a secret night school in Natchez, Mississippi, and by her own count taught hundreds of people to read and write.
> **When and where:** Born in Petersburg, Virginia, about 1816. Enslaved in Kentucky, then near Natchez, Mississippi. Died in 1889.

Milla Granson ran a school at night in Natchez, Mississippi, while she was enslaved. Her pupils were enslaved people who came after a full day's work. What is known about the school comes from one source. In March 1864, she told her story to Laura Haviland, a Quaker teacher, who printed it in a book in 1881.

Granson was born in Petersburg, Virginia, in about 1816. She was enslaved in Kentucky, where the children of her enslaver taught her to read and write. When that enslaver died, she was sold south to an owner near Natchez and put to work in the fields. She could not keep up with the field hands, and the overseer whipped her. She told Haviland: "sometimes I thought I would die from such cruel whippings upon my bared body." The record does not give the overseer's name.

The school opened "at eleven or twelve o'clock at night, and closed at two o'clock A. M." Every door and window was shut so no one would find out. Her pupils came into a back alley, "each with a bundle of pitch-pine splinters for lights." She taught twelve at a time. When those twelve could read and write, she sent them off and took twelve more, "until she had graduated hundreds." Some of her pupils wrote their own passes and set out for Canada. She never heard from them again, and she believed they got away.

After slavery ended, she pushed for the founding of Natchez Seminary, the school that became Jackson State University. She married Oliver Granderson. She died in 1889 and is buried in the Natchez City Cemetery.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="milla-granson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Resistance and revolt" -->
Enslaved people resisted slavery in small ways every day. They worked slowly, broke tools, pretended to be sick and took food. Most of all, they ran away. Southern newspapers were full of advertisements for runaways, placed by enslavers who wanted them back. Some of the ads warn that the person "can read," or "can write and may forge a pass." A project at Cornell University called Freedom on the Move is putting thousands of these ads online.

In 1800 an enslaved blacksmith named Gabriel planned to lead a march on Richmond, Virginia. People who knew of the plan told white authorities a few hours before it was to begin, and a storm scattered those who had gathered. Virginia officials hanged 26 men, Gabriel among them.

In January 1811, Charles Deslondes led an uprising on the German Coast of the Territory of Orleans, upriver from New Orleans. Between 64 and 125 enslaved people began it, and accounts put the number who joined at 200 to 500. They marched about twenty miles down the river road toward New Orleans and killed two white men, Gilbert Andry and François Trépagnier.

Governor William C. C. Claiborne, General Wade Hampton with U.S. Army troops, and a militia of planters led by Manuel Andry and Charles Perret put the uprising down. On January 10 they killed 40 to 45 rebels in the fighting. Planters sat as judges in special courts called tribunals and had 44 more put to death. About 95 rebels were killed in all. The militia cut off the heads of the dead and set nearly 100 of them on poles along the levee, the river wall, from New Orleans upriver. Deslondes was captured on January 11 and given no trial. Militiamen cut off his hands, shot him several times and burned him alive.

In 1822 Denmark Vesey, a free Black carpenter in Charleston, South Carolina, was accused of planning a revolt to free thousands of enslaved people. No revolt ever took place, and no white person was harmed. Officials in Charleston put Vesey and 34 other men to death. They claimed the city's African Methodist Episcopal church had helped plan the revolt. They ordered its members to scatter and destroyed the church. The Equal Justice Initiative says they burned it to the ground. Other accounts say it was torn down. Soon afterward, Black churches were outlawed in Charleston. The congregation later became the church known today as Mother Emanuel.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nat-turner" name="Nat Turner" movie="The Birth of a Nation (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nat Turner

> **Who:** An enslaved preacher who led the slave revolt in which rebels killed the most white people in United States history.
> **When and where:** Southampton County, Virginia, August 21 to 23, 1831. Hanged on November 11, 1831.
> **Movie:** *The Birth of a Nation* (2016), a feature film about Turner's revolt. It is a different film from a 1915 film with the same title.

Nat Turner was an enslaved preacher in Southampton County, Virginia. From August 21 to 23, 1831, he led a revolt in which the rebels killed at least 55 white people.

After the revolt, militias and bands of armed white men killed more than 100 Black people. Many of them had nothing to do with the revolt. Some estimates put the number killed at 200.

Turner hid until he was caught on October 30. About 30 people were tried in Southampton County. Of those convicted, 19 were put to death. Turner was hanged on November 11, 1831. While he was in jail, a lawyer named Thomas R. Gray took down his account, and Gray published it that year as *The Confessions of Nat Turner*.

Lawmakers in Virginia and other slave states answered the revolt with new laws against teaching enslaved people to read and against their gathering in groups.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nat-turner" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sengbe-pieh" name="Sengbe Pieh (Joseph Cinqué)" movie="Amistad (1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sengbe Pieh (Joseph Cinqué)

> **Who:** A Mende man who led the captives of the ship *Amistad* in taking control of it, and who won freedom for them in the United States Supreme Court.
> **When and where:** From the Mende country, in the region of Sierra Leone in West Africa. Took the ship on July 1, 1839. Freed by the Supreme Court in 1841. Home in 1842.
> **Movie:** *Amistad* (1997), a feature film about the case.

Sengbe Pieh led 53 kidnapped Africans in taking over the ship that held them, and they won their freedom in an American court. Spanish slave traders had carried them, most of them Mende people from the region of Sierra Leone, to Cuba. There the 53 were bought for a Cuban plantation and put aboard a schooner, the *Amistad*. On July 1, 1839, they rose up and took control of the ship. The Spanish called Pieh Joseph Cinqué.

In late August 1839, the U.S. Navy brig *Washington*, commanded by Lieutenant Thomas R. Gedney, seized the *Amistad* off Long Island. Officials took the Africans to New London, Connecticut, and jailed them. Some died at sea or in prison while they waited for trial.

Their case went all the way to the Supreme Court. Former president John Quincy Adams argued part of it for them. On March 9, 1841, the justices set them free. In 1842 the 35 who had survived sailed home to Sierra Leone. They had never been enslaved on American soil.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sengbe-pieh" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="solomon-northup" name="Solomon Northup" movie="12 Years a Slave (2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Solomon Northup

> **Who:** A free man from New York who was kidnapped and held in slavery in Louisiana for twelve years, and who named the people who did it.
> **When and where:** Kidnapped in Washington, D.C., in 1841. Freed on January 4, 1853. Where and when he died is not known.
> **Movie:** *12 Years a Slave* (2013), directed by Steve McQueen. It won the Academy Award for Best Picture.

Solomon Northup was born free and was kidnapped into slavery. He was born in 1808 in Minerva, New York. He was a farmer and a fiddler. In 1841 two white men who called themselves Merrill Brown and Abram Hamilton offered him work playing music for a circus. They took him to Washington, D.C. There they gave him a drink that left him "insensible," or unconscious. He woke up "alone, in utter darkness, and in chains."

He was in Williams's slave pen, called the Yellow House, near the U.S. Capitol. A slave trader named James H. Burch held him there. When Northup said he was a free man, Burch beat him. Northup described the paddle Burch used: "a piece of hard-wood board, eighteen or twenty inches long." Burch also used a cat-o'-nine-tails, a whip of rope with many strands, each with a knot tied at the end. Burch told Northup that if he ever again said he was free, what came next would be worse. "He swore that he would either conquer or kill me."

In New Orleans, Burch's partner Theophilus Freeman sold him under the name Platt to William Ford. Ford sold him to a carpenter, John Tibeats, in the winter of 1842. Edwin Epps held him for almost ten years on a cotton plantation on Bayou Boeuf, in Louisiana.

A Canadian carpenter named Samuel Bass mailed letters for Northup to people who knew him in New York. Henry B. Northup, a member of the family Solomon's father had once worked for, got help from the governor of New York, Washington Hunt. Solomon Northup was freed on January 4, 1853.

That year he published *Twelve Years a Slave*, which sold about 30,000 copies. Burch was tried in Washington in 1853 and found not guilty, because the law did not allow Northup, a Black man, to testify against a white man. The case in New York against the kidnappers was dropped in 1857. No one was ever convicted. After about 1857, Northup disappears from the records. Where and when he died is not known.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="solomon-northup" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Underground Railroad and the abolitionists" -->
The Underground Railroad was a network of people who helped enslaved people escape to the free states and to Canada. It had guides and safe houses. Much of the work was done by free Black communities and their churches. Historians estimate that it helped about 100,000 people escape over several decades. Many more tried to escape and were caught.

In the free states, Black people built their own institutions. On March 16, 1827, Samuel Cornish and John Russwurm published the first issue of *Freedom's Journal* in New York. It was the first newspaper in the United States owned by Black people. Free Black communities in the North ran vigilance committees, groups that hid people who had escaped and helped them move on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sojourner-truth-slavery-freedom" name="Sojourner Truth" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sojourner Truth

> **Who:** A woman who walked away from slavery in New York, then sued to get back her son, who had been sold south against the law, and won.
> **When and where:** Ulster County, New York. Walked to freedom in 1826. Won her son's return in 1828. Died in 1883.

Sojourner Truth went to court to get her son back and won. She was born enslaved in about 1797 in Ulster County, New York, and her name then was Isabella Baumfree. In 1826 she walked away from her enslaver, carrying her baby daughter. That was one year before New York's law freed all its enslaved people, on July 4, 1827.

Her son Peter was five years old. He was sold south to Alabama, which was against New York law. She sued, and in 1828 she won his return. The Historical Society of the New York Courts says she was the first Black woman to win a case of this kind against a white man in an American court.

In 1843 she took the name Sojourner Truth. Her life story, the *Narrative of Sojourner Truth*, was published in 1850.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sojourner-truth-slavery-freedom" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harriet-tubman" name="Harriet Tubman" movie="Harriet (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Harriet Tubman

> **Who:** A woman who escaped slavery in Maryland and went back about 13 times to lead about 70 people out.
> **When and where:** Born in Dorchester County, on Maryland's Eastern Shore, in March 1822. Escaped in September 1849. Died in 1913.
> **Movie:** *Harriet* (2019), a feature film about her life, with Cynthia Erivo as Tubman.

Harriet Tubman escaped from slavery and then went back into Maryland about 13 times to lead others out. She brought out about 70 people, most of them family and friends, among them her parents, Rit and Ben. An old story says she made 19 trips and freed 300 people. The National Park Service says that story is a legend. The numbers 13 and 70 come from her own statements and the records.

She was born in March 1822 in Dorchester County, on Maryland's Eastern Shore. Her name then was Araminta Ross. In 1835, when she was 13, she was at a store when an enslaved person tried to escape. An overseer threw a two-pound weight. It hit her in the back of the head and broke her skull. For the rest of her life she had pain and "sleeping spells," times when she fell asleep suddenly. The accounts of her injury do not name the overseer.

In September 1849, she escaped to Philadelphia. Between 1850 and 1860 she made her trips back. During the Civil War she worked for the Union army as a scout. She died of pneumonia in 1913, at about 91 years old, in a home for elderly Black people.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1850 the members of Congress passed a law that forced people in the free states to help catch those who had escaped slavery. Fifteen years later, after the Civil War, slavery became illegal under the 13th Amendment to the Constitution, and about four million people became free. Many of them had already freed themselves by escaping to the Union army during the war.

Freedom came with work to do. Freed people searched for family members who had been sold away. They married in law, chose their own names, built churches and schools, voted and served in Congress. White men answered with new laws that forced Black people to work, with whippings and burnings, and with massacres. In 1877 federal troops were withdrawn from the last Southern statehouses, and the period of rebuilding called Reconstruction came to an end.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Fugitive Slave Act, 1850" -->
On September 18, 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act became law. A fugitive is a person who has escaped. Under the act, federal officials called commissioners decided whether a captured person was a runaway slave. There was no jury, and the captured person was not allowed to testify. A commissioner was paid $10 for ruling that a person was a slave and $5 for ruling that the person was free. Officials could force bystanders to help catch people. The law applied in every free state, and free Black people in the North fled to Canada.

Anthony Burns escaped from slavery in Virginia by stowing away on a ship. On May 24, 1854, he was arrested in Boston. After a hearing that lasted a week, federal troops marched him through the city on June 2, 1854, to a ship that took him back to Virginia. He passed crowds draped in black, the color of mourning.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Celia, 1855" -->
In 1855 a Missouri judge refused to let a jury treat an enslaved woman as a woman with the right to defend herself against rape. Her name was Celia. In 1850 Robert Newsom, a farmer in Callaway County, Missouri, bought her when she was 14 years old. From 1850 to 1855 he raped her again and again. She had two children, and at least one was his. In 1855 she was pregnant again.

On the night of June 23, 1855, Newsom told Celia he would come to her cabin. She had put a large stick in the corner. When he came, she struck him on the head with it twice, and the second blow killed him. She burned his body in her fireplace.

Missouri's law made it a crime to force "any woman" into sex against her will, and it allowed a woman to use force to resist. At Celia's trial in October 1855, her lawyer, John Jameson, asked Judge William Hall to tell the jury that Celia could be found not guilty if she had been resisting rape. Hall refused. That left the jury no way to treat the killing as self-defense. A jury of twelve white men convicted her of murder. The Missouri Supreme Court turned down her appeal on December 14, 1855. She was hanged on December 21, 1855.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dred-harriet-scott" name="Dred and Harriet Scott" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Dred and Harriet Scott

> **Who:** An enslaved couple who sued for their freedom and lost in the United States Supreme Court, which ruled that no Black American could be a citizen.
> **When and where:** Suit filed in St. Louis, Missouri, in 1846. Supreme Court decision on March 6, 1857. Freed in May 1857.

Dred and Harriet Scott sued for their freedom in 1846 and lost in the Supreme Court eleven years later. Dred Scott was born enslaved in Virginia in about 1800. Peter and Elizabeth Taylor Blow held him. The Blows moved to St. Louis in 1830 and sold him to Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon.

Emerson took Scott to Fort Armstrong, in Illinois, in 1833, and then to Fort Snelling, in Wisconsin Territory, in 1836. Slavery was banned in both places. At Fort Snelling, Dred Scott married Harriet Robinson, who was also enslaved. They had two daughters, Eliza and Lizzie. Eliza was born on a steamboat in free waters. After Emerson died in 1843, his widow, Irene Emerson, claimed the family as her property.

On April 6, 1846, the Scotts sued for their freedom in St. Louis. They argued that living where slavery was banned had made them free. On January 12, 1850, a jury ruled in their favor. On March 22, 1852, the Missouri Supreme Court reversed that ruling, and the Scotts were enslaved again.

The case reached the United States Supreme Court. On March 6, 1857, the justices ruled against the Scotts, 7 votes to 2. Chief Justice Roger Taney wrote that Black Americans were not and could never be citizens. He wrote that Black people "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect." The justices also ruled that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories of the West.

Irene Emerson's second husband, Calvin Chaffee, handed ownership of the family to Taylor Blow. Taylor Blow freed them on May 26, 1857. Dred Scott worked as a porter at Barnum's Hotel in St. Louis and helped Harriet with her laundry work. He died of tuberculosis on September 17, 1858.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dred-harriet-scott" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sold and smuggled, 1858 to 1860" -->
On March 2 and 3, 1859, Pierce Mease Butler sold between 429 and 436 men, women and children at a racetrack near Savannah, Georgia, to pay his gambling debts. It was the largest single sale of people recorded in the United States. The slave dealer Joseph Bryan ran it. He advertised it for weeks in newspapers from Charleston to Richmond. The sale brought in $303,850. A reporter from the *New York Tribune*, Mortimer Thomson, went disguised as a buyer and wrote about it. Black Georgians remembered those two days as "the weeping time."

Bringing captives from Africa had been against the law since 1808, but some men kept doing it. In 1858 Charles A. L. Lamar led a group of investors who paid for a ship called the *Wanderer* to bring captives from Africa. Its captain, William R. Corrie, bought 487 Africans at the Congo River. About 409 of them were still alive when the ship landed at Jekyll Island, Georgia, on November 29, 1858. The others died on the voyage. Six trials followed. None of them ended in a conviction.

In 1860 Timothy Meaher, a businessman and shipbuilder in Mobile, Alabama, bet that he could sneak a ship full of captives into Mobile Bay. Captain William Foster sailed the schooner *Clotilda* to West Africa. He bought 125 people and carried 110 of them across the ocean. On July 8, 1860, the ship entered the Gulf of Mexico. Later that month Foster anchored it near Twelve-Mile Island and set it on fire to destroy the evidence. The only punishment anyone received was a $1,000 fine charged to Foster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="oluale-kossola" name="Oluale Kossola (Cudjo Lewis)" movie="Descendant (2022)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Oluale Kossola (Cudjo Lewis)

> **Who:** A man carried to Alabama on the *Clotilda*, the last known slave ship to reach the United States, who helped found the town of Africatown after he was freed.
> **When and where:** Born about 1841 in what is now Benin, in West Africa. Landed near Mobile, Alabama, in July 1860. Freed in 1865. Died in 1935.
> **Movie:** *Descendant* (2022), a documentary by Margaret Brown about the *Clotilda*, Africatown and the families of the survivors.

Oluale Kossola was one of the people carried to Alabama on the *Clotilda* in 1860, and he lived long enough to tell his story to a writer in the 1920s. He was born in about 1841 in what is now the country of Benin, among the Yoruba Isha people.

In April 1860, the army of Ghezo, the King of Dahomey, attacked his town. Kossola was captured and held in a barracoon, a pen for captives, at the port of Ouidah. There he was sold to William Foster, the *Clotilda*'s captain. The crossing took 45 days. He was one of 110 people on board.

In Mobile, James Meaher, Timothy Meaher's brother, enslaved him. Kossola worked on a steamship and lived with others from the *Clotilda* under Meaher's house, which stood high off the ground. In 1865 he was freed. He took the name Cudjo Lewis and married Abile, a woman who had also been on the *Clotilda*.

The people from the *Clotilda* could not afford to sail home. They chose Kossola to speak for them and asked Timothy Meaher for land. Meaher refused. So they bought land from him and from others, and about 30 of them founded Africatown, north of Mobile. Kossola and Abile had six children, and all six died young. In 1902 he was hurt in a train accident and sued the railroad company. He became the caretaker of the Africatown church.

In 1927 the writer Zora Neale Hurston interviewed him. Her book about his life, *Barracoon*, was not published until 2018. Kossola died on July 26, 1935, at about 94. In 2019 the Alabama Historical Commission announced that the burned wreck of the *Clotilda* had been found in the Mobile River.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="oluale-kossola" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom taken during the war" -->
Enslaved people did not wait for the government to free them. On May 23 and 24, 1861, three enslaved men, Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory and James Townsend, rowed to Fort Monroe, a Union fort at Old Point Comfort in Virginia. It was the same ground where the *White Lion* had landed Africans in 1619. They were about to be taken south to build batteries, gun positions, for the Confederate army. General Benjamin Butler refused to send them back. He called them "contraband of war," meaning property of the enemy that an army may seize.

Word spread. Enslaved people walked, rowed and rode to Union lines across the South. By the end of the war, about half a million people had freed themselves by reaching the Union army. The people who escaped to Fort Monroe called it "Freedom's Fortress."

Union army and Freedmen's Bureau officials ran camps for people who had escaped slavery, called contraband camps. The camps were crowded and dirty. Between 1862 and 1868, more than 60,000 freed people died of smallpox, according to the historian Jim Downs. Doctors knew how to prevent the disease by vaccination and by keeping the sick apart. The quarantine tents for freed people were made of old, ripped army cloth.

Philip Reid was an enslaved craftsman at the foundry of Clark Mills in Washington, D.C. A foundry is a workshop where metal is melted and cast. Mills owned him. Mills's workers were casting the Statue of Freedom for the top of the Capitol dome. When the paid foreman demanded more money before he would take apart the plaster model, Reid worked out how to separate it with a pulley and tackle. He was paid $1.25 a day, but he kept only the pay for Sundays. On April 16, 1862, a law freed the enslaved people of Washington, D.C., and Reid was free. The statue was set on the dome on December 2, 1863. In his later life Reid signed his name Reed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-smalls" name="Robert Smalls" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Smalls

> **Who:** An enslaved harbor pilot who sailed himself, his family and his crew out of slavery on a Confederate ship, and later served five terms in Congress.
> **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, before dawn on May 13, 1862. Later Beaufort, South Carolina. Died in 1915.

Robert Smalls freed himself, his family and his crew by taking a Confederate ship out of Charleston harbor. He was 23 years old and enslaved. He worked as the pilot, the man who steered the ship, on a Confederate transport ship called the *Planter*.

Before dawn on May 13, 1862, Smalls took the *Planter* out with an enslaved crew and their families aboard. Smalls wore the captain's straw hat, and he gave the right signals as they passed Fort Sumter. Out at sea he surrendered the ship to the Union navy ships that were blockading, or closing off, the harbor.

Smalls went on to become a ship's captain in United States service. After the war he served in the South Carolina legislature. He was elected to the United States House of Representatives five times. He died in 1915.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-smalls" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Emancipation Proclamation, 1863" -->
President Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation in two steps. He announced it on September 22, 1862, and it took effect on January 1, 1863. Emancipation means setting people free from slavery.

The proclamation declared free the enslaved people in the states that were fighting against the United States. Wherever the Union army advanced after that, its soldiers carried freedom with them. The proclamation also opened the army to Black men.

It did not free everyone. It left out the slave states that had stayed loyal to the Union: Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri. It also left out Tennessee and the parts of Virginia and Louisiana that the Union army already held. Enslaved people in those places had to wait for their own states to act or for the 13th Amendment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black soldiers and the scourged back" -->
About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army in the regiments called the U.S. Colored Troops. By the end of the war, about one Union soldier in ten was Black. About 19,000 more Black men served in the navy. On September 29, 1864, at the Battle of New Market Heights in Virginia, fourteen Black soldiers earned the Medal of Honor, the highest award for bravery in battle.

In March 1863, a man named Peter escaped from the 3,000-acre plantation of Captain John Lyons in St. Landry Parish, Louisiana. He reached Union troops at Baton Rouge. Army doctors examined him before he joined the army. His back was covered in thick raised scars, called keloid scars, which grow over a healed wound. Peter said the overseer, Artayou Carrier, had whipped him in October 1862. He said he lay in bed for two months, sore "from the whipping and salt brine" that was poured into the wounds.

About April 2, 1863, the photographers William D. McPherson and J. Oliver photographed his back. *Harper's Weekly* printed pictures made from the photograph on July 4, 1863, under the name "Gordon." Historians now think that the *Harper's* story mixed up three different men. No record shows what became of Peter after he joined the Union army.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-harvey-carney" name="William Harvey Carney" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### William Harvey Carney

> **Who:** A man born enslaved in Virginia who carried the flag at Fort Wagner with the 54th Massachusetts, a Black regiment. His actions there were the earliest act of bravery by a Black American to be recognized with the Medal of Honor.
> **When and where:** Born in Norfolk, Virginia, on February 29, 1840. Fort Wagner, South Carolina, July 18, 1863. Died on December 9, 1908.

William Harvey Carney carried the flag of the United States through a battle while badly wounded, and he did not let it fall. He was born enslaved in Norfolk, Virginia, on February 29, 1840. Accounts differ about how he became free. Some say he escaped on the Underground Railroad. Others say his father escaped and then bought his freedom. The family settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts.

In February 1863 Carney joined Company C of the 54th Massachusetts, a regiment of Black soldiers. He became a sergeant in March. On July 18, 1863, the 54th attacked Fort Wagner, in South Carolina. The soldier carrying the flag was shot. Carney took the flag and went forward with several serious wounds. He planted it on the wall of the fort and then carried it back to the Union lines. The National Park Service quotes his words afterward. He said, "Boys, I did but my duty," and then, "the dear old flag never touched the ground!" The attack appears in the film *Glory* (1989), which is about the 54th Massachusetts.

He received the Medal of Honor in May 1900. The National Park Service says his actions were "the earliest act of African American bravery to be recognized with the medal." He was one of 22 African Americans to receive it for service in the Civil War. He died at Boston City Hospital on December 9, 1908.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="william-harvey-carney" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Combahee Ferry, Juneteenth and the 13th Amendment" -->
On the night of June 2, 1863, Harriet Tubman guided Union gunboats and soldiers of the 2nd South Carolina Volunteers up the Combahee River in South Carolina. More than 700 enslaved people escaped to freedom on the boats that night.

On June 19, 1865, Major General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas, with about 2,000 federal soldiers. He issued General Order No. 3. It said that "all slaves are free," with "absolute equality of personal rights." More than 250,000 people were still enslaved in Texas, two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation, because the Union army had not been there to enforce it. June 19 became a day of celebration, called Juneteenth.

On January 31, 1865, the members of Congress passed the 13th Amendment. It became part of the Constitution on December 6, 1865, when enough states had approved it. Its text forbids slavery everywhere in the United States, "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." That exception mattered. It still allowed forced labor for people convicted of crimes.

Some Native nations had also held Black people in slavery. The Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations were known as the Five Tribes. At emancipation the Cherokee Nation held as many as 2,500 enslaved people. In 1866 the leaders of each of these nations signed treaties with the United States that freed the people they had enslaved and promised them citizenship in the nation. The Cherokee treaty's Article 9 promised the freed people, called Freedmen, "all the rights of native Cherokees."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Freedom, day to day" -->
For decades after slavery ended, freed people searched for family members who had been sold away. They placed advertisements in Black newspapers, under headings such as "Information Wanted," asking for news of mothers, children, husbands and wives. The searches ran into the 1900s. Few ended in a reunion, and when one did, people wrote about it with joy. A project called "Last Seen: Finding Family After Slavery," at Villanova University with Mother Bethel AME Church in Philadelphia, has put more than 5,000 of these ads online.

Enslaved people's marriages had never counted in law. After the war, officers of the Freedmen's Bureau, the federal office set up to help freed people, recorded thousands of marriages. Couples who had been together for decades stood up to be married in law. The Bureau's marriage registers survive at the National Archives.

Freed people chose and registered their own last names. Some kept a former owner's name, so that family members who had been sold away could find them. Others chose new names. Freeman was one.

Congregations that had met in hush harbors built their own churches. Within ten years of the war, the AME and AME Zion churches had spread across the South. Freed people used their churches as schools, as meeting halls and as places to organize for elections.

The Freedman's Savings Bank was chartered in 1865 to hold the savings of freed people. The bank's managers ran it badly and lost money on risky deals. It failed in June 1874. About 61,000 depositors lost about $3 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thornton-copeland" name="Thornton Copeland" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Thornton Copeland

> **Who:** A freed man who placed a newspaper advertisement in 1865 searching for his mother, from whom he had been sold away 21 years earlier.
> **When and where:** The *Colored Tennessean*, a newspaper in Nashville, Tennessee, October 1865. He had been sold in Richmond, Virginia, in 1844.

Thornton Copeland was sold away from his mother in 1844. In October 1865, after slavery ended, he placed an advertisement in the *Colored Tennessean*, a Black newspaper in Nashville, to look for her. Her name was Betty.

His advertisement reads: "INFORMATION is wanted of my mother, whom I left in Fauquier county, Va., in 1844, and I was sold in Richmond, Va., to Saml. Copeland." He had been held before that by a man named Robert Rogers.

His advertisement is item 918 in the "Last Seen" collection. No record shows whether he ever found his mother.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thornton-copeland" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools, the moment freedom came" -->
People who had been forbidden to read started schools as soon as they were free. Schools opened in churches, in army camps and in buildings the Union army had taken over, as soon as the Union lines arrived. Grandparents and grandchildren learned in the same rooms. Grown men and women came to night classes after a full day's work.

Freed people paid for schools themselves wherever they could. They paid school fees out of their first wages, and they gave land and their own labor to build schoolhouses. Northern aid societies helped, the American Missionary Association most of all. Thousands of teachers came south, many of them women, and a growing number of them Black. By 1870 the Freedmen's Bureau counted more than 2,600 day and night schools with about 150,000 students. Counts that include every kind of school are higher.

New colleges opened for freed people. Fisk, in Nashville, opened in January 1866. Members of Congress chartered Howard University in 1867. By 1872 there were about 25 colleges for freed people.

In 1868 and 1869, delegates in the Southern states wrote new state constitutions. Their conventions were the first public bodies in United States history with many Black members. The delegates created the South's first statewide systems of free public schools, open to white children too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="susie-king-taylor" name="Susie King Taylor" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Susie King Taylor

> **Who:** A girl who learned to read in secret schools in Savannah, escaped at 13, and became the first Black teacher of freed children in a school working openly in Georgia.
> **When and where:** Born in Liberty County, Georgia, on August 6, 1848. Taught on St. Simons Island, Georgia, in 1862. Died in 1912.

Susie King Taylor was a teacher of freed people at 14, while the Civil War was still going on. As a child in Savannah, Georgia, she went to two secret schools run by Black women. Two white young people also taught her, breaking the law to do it.

In April 1862, at 13, she escaped with others to St. Simons Island, which the Union army held. She turned 14 that August. There she became the first Black teacher of freed students in a freedmen's school working openly in Georgia. She taught about 40 children by day and grown people at night, until the island was emptied in October 1862.

She married Edward King, a Black noncommissioned officer in the army. She served with the 33rd U.S. Colored Troops as a nurse and laundress, and she taught soldiers to read. In 1902 she published a book about those years, *Reminiscences of My Life in Camp*. She died in 1912.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="susie-king-taylor" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Memphis and New Orleans, 1866" -->
In early May 1866, white mobs in Memphis, Tennessee, attacked the city's Black neighborhoods. The mobs included city policemen, firemen and some businessmen. The attacks began after a clash between the police and Black Union soldiers who had just left the army. General George Stoneman had taken the soldiers' guns away. The mobs killed 46 Black people. Two white people also died.

A committee of Congress investigated. It counted 75 people injured, 100 robbed and five women raped. The mobs burned 91 homes, 4 churches and 8 schools. That was every Black church and school in the city. No one was ever punished for the killings, as far as the records show.

Frances Thompson testified to the committee. She said that on May 1, 1866, seven white men broke into her home and raped her and her roommate, 16-year-old Lucy Smith. Accounts of her testimony differ on whether two or three of the men were police officers. The men also stole about $300. In July 1876, Memphis police arrested Thompson for wearing women's clothing. Dr. Joseph Nuttall, who worked for the city, and three other doctors examined her body against her will and declared that she was a man. She was sentenced to 100 days on the chain gang, cleaning streets. Her enemies used the arrest to attack her testimony about the rapes. She died of dysentery, a disease of the gut, in the city hospital on November 1, 1876.

On July 30, 1866, delegates met at the Mechanics' Institute in New Orleans to reopen Louisiana's constitutional convention and give Black men the vote. Mayor John T. Monroe had declared the meeting unlawful. About 200 Black men, most of them Union veterans, marched in support. Police deputized by Sheriff Harry T. Hays, many of them former Confederate soldiers, attacked with white mobs and firemen. They killed 34 Black people and wounded 119. They killed 3 white delegates and wounded 17. General Philip Sheridan called it "an absolute massacre by the police."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black political power" -->
The 15th Amendment became part of the Constitution on February 3, 1870. It states that the right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Abridged means cut down. In the ten years that followed, 16 Black men served in Congress, 14 in the House and 2 in the Senate. All of them were Republicans from the South. Joseph Rainey of South Carolina became the first Black member of the House of Representatives on December 12, 1870. Robert Smalls served five terms in the House.

Across the South, about 2,000 Black men held public office during Reconstruction, some elected and some appointed. The historian Eric Foner has found more than 1,500 of them by name and listed them in his book *Freedom's Lawmakers*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="revels-bruce" name="Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce

> **Who:** The first two Black United States senators. Revels was the first to serve. Bruce, born enslaved, was the first to serve a full six-year term.
> **When and where:** Both represented Mississippi. Revels served from 1870 to 1871. Bruce served from 1875 to 1881.

Hiram Revels became the first Black United States senator on February 25, 1870. Blanche K. Bruce became the first Black senator to serve a full term, from 1875 to 1881. Both men represented Mississippi.

Revels was born free on September 27, 1827, in Fayetteville, North Carolina. He was a minister in the African Methodist Episcopal Church and a teacher. During the Civil War he served as a chaplain, a minister to soldiers, for Black regiments. In 1869 he won a seat in the Mississippi state senate. On January 20, 1870, the members of the Mississippi legislature voted 85 to 15 to send him to the United States Senate. Democrats in the Senate argued against seating him. Some said he had not been a citizen until the 14th Amendment was adopted in 1868, so he had not been a citizen long enough to serve. The senators voted 48 to 8 to seat him. He served until March 3, 1871. In 1871 he became the first president of Alcorn University. He died in 1901.

Bruce was born enslaved on March 1, 1841, near Farmville, Virginia. Pettus Perkinson held him in slavery. When the Civil War began, Bruce escaped to Lawrence, Kansas, where he taught school. After the war he moved to Mississippi. He was a sheriff and a tax collector in Bolivar County and the county's superintendent of education. On February 4, 1874, he was elected to the Senate. He served from March 4, 1875, to March 3, 1881. Later he served as Register of the Treasury, an office in the federal government. He died in Washington, D.C., in 1898.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="revels-bruce" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The promises broken" -->
Several promises made to freed people were broken within a year of the war's end.

On January 16, 1865, the Union general Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15. In it, Sherman set aside about 400,000 acres of coastal land, from Charleston to northern Florida, for freed families in plots of forty acres. About 40,000 people settled on the land. In the fall of 1865, President Andrew Johnson reversed the order and gave the land back to its former owners. The phrase "forty acres and a mule" comes from this broken promise.

In November 1865, Mississippi's lawmakers passed the first Black Codes, and lawmakers in other Southern states followed. Under vagrancy laws, a Black person who could not prove he had a job could be arrested. Under apprenticeship laws, officials could bind Black children to work for white employers. Other laws required licenses to work in certain jobs. People arrested under these laws were hired out to work. The members of Congress answered with the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the 14th Amendment, which became part of the Constitution on July 9, 1868.

Without land of their own, many freed families became sharecroppers. A sharecropper farmed a landlord's fields in exchange for a share of the crop. The landlord advanced tools, seed and food, charged at the high prices of the plantation store, and took the cost out of the family's share. The debt carried over from year to year, and families could not leave the land until it was paid.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convict leasing" -->
The 13th Amendment allowed forced labor as a punishment for crime. Southern officials used that exception. Sheriffs and county courts arrested Black men, often for vagrancy or small crimes under the new codes, and sentenced them. State officials then leased the prisoners, or rented them out, to planters, mine owners and railroads. The prisoners worked without pay. This was called convict leasing.

Alabama leased its prisoners from 1875 to 1928. State officials signed the leases, and company managers ran the prison camps. Most of the prisoners were sent to coal mines run by companies such as the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company, Sloss Iron and Steel, and Pratt Coal and Iron. More than 95 percent of the county prisoners and 90 percent of the state prisoners were Black. In one count from the 1890s, 181 of 745 county prisoners in the mines died within two years. County prisoners also had to work off their court costs, which made their sentences longer. In 1912 prison mining brought the state of Alabama more than $1 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Terror" -->
In December 1865, Confederate veterans founded the Ku Klux Klan in Pulaski, Tennessee. Klansmen, armed and in disguise, whipped, burned out and murdered freed people and the white people who worked with them. They attacked mostly at night.

Klansmen went after schools and teachers. In late 1869, at Greensboro, Georgia, they drove away a white teacher named R. H. Gladding. They dragged Abram Colby, a Black man just elected to the Georgia legislature who supported the school, out of his house and beat him nearly to death. In Alabama, Klansmen burned many Black schools and churches. On July 11, 1870, at Cross Plains, Alabama, they killed a teacher of freed people, William Luke, a preacher from Ontario in Canada. Accounts say six or eight people were killed there.

Under the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871, federal officials broke up the Klan for a time.

On Easter Sunday, April 13, 1873, white men carried out a massacre at Colfax, Louisiana. The historian Eric Foner calls it the bloodiest single act of racial violence in Reconstruction. After a disputed election, Black militiamen and Republicans held the courthouse of Grant Parish. Christopher Columbus Nash, who claimed to be the sheriff, led more than 150 armed white men against them. Other accounts say about 300. The attackers fired a small cannon, set the courthouse on fire and shot the men who came out. That night some of the attackers killed their prisoners, most of them with shots to the head. Estimates of the number of Black men killed run from about 60 to 153. Two white men died.

A jury convicted three of the attackers of conspiracy. On March 27, 1876, in the case *United States v. Cruikshank*, the justices of the Supreme Court threw out the convictions. They ruled that the rights at stake protected people only against actions by governments, not against private men. No one was ever punished for the killings. In 1950 Louisiana officials put up a marker calling it a riot in which "three white men and 150 Negroes were slain." It was taken down in 2021.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The end of Reconstruction, 1877" -->
The presidential election of 1876 ended in dispute. In the Compromise of 1877 that settled it, the last federal troops were withdrawn from Southern statehouses. The last ones left New Orleans on April 24, 1877. The 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments stayed in the Constitution, but federal officials stopped enforcing them in the South.

On May 18, 1896, in the case *Plessy v. Ferguson*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 7 to 1 that states could keep Black and white people separate by law, under the rule known as "separate but equal." Justice Harlan was the only one to disagree. Separating people by race is called segregation. With that ruling, the justices approved the segregation laws of the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
