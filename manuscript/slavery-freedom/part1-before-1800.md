## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, some Native nations in North America took captives in war. In most of those nations, a captive could become a member of a family. A captive's children did not automatically become captives too. On the Northwest Coast, the Tlingit, the Haida and their neighbors held people as slaves. They bought and sold them, and the children of slaves were slaves too.

Holding a person as property for life, and passing that status on to the person's children, is called chattel slavery. Chattel is an old word for property. Later, Europeans wrote chattel slavery into the laws of the colonies they set up here.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Captives and slaves" -->
Warriors in some Native nations took captives in raids on their enemies. Among the Haudenosaunee, the nations also called the Iroquois, a family that had lost someone in war could adopt a captive to take the dead person's place. Historians call this practice the mourning war. It is the best-described case. In most of these nations, a captive could stop being a captive. The captive's children did not automatically become captives.

The Northwest Coast was different. There, among the Tlingit, the Haida and the nations near them, slaves were property. Their owners traded them, and a slave's children were born slaves. Leland Donald studied this system in a 1997 book and found that it was slavery in the full sense of the word.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The first people enslaved by Europeans in what is now the United States were Native people. From the early 1500s, Spanish raiders took Native people captive on the coasts of Florida and the Carolinas. In 1526 a Spanish colonist brought about 100 enslaved Africans to a colony on the Atlantic coast. That October they rebelled and escaped, in the first known revolt of enslaved Africans in what became the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish slavery and the first revolt" -->
In their colonies, the Spanish ran a system called the encomienda. Under it, Spanish colonists forced Native people to work for them and to pay them goods. On November 20, 1542, King Charles I of Spain issued the New Laws. They said that no one could enslave Native people, and that an encomienda ended when its holder died. Spanish colonists opposed the New Laws and kept forcing Native people to work.

In 1526 Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón founded a colony called San Miguel de Gualdape on the coast of what is now Georgia or South Carolina. Nobody knows the exact spot. Between 600 and 700 colonists came with him, and about 100 of them were enslaved Africans. Ayllón died, and the colonists fought among themselves. In October 1526 the enslaved Africans rose up and escaped. Historians believe they found shelter with Native people nearby, most likely the Guale. Within months the surviving colonists gave up the colony.

On September 8, 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and about 800 Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine, in Florida. They settled in the town of a Timucua chief named Seloy and used his council house as their first fort. Africans were among the town's first residents, some free and some enslaved. They worked as craftsmen, farmers and house servants. Spanish law gave enslaved people rights that English law later did not. Families were not supposed to be split up. Enslaved people could join the Catholic Church and marry in it, and the law set out ways for them to become free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="estevanico-slavery-freedom" name="Estevanico" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Estevanico

> **Who:** An enslaved man from North Africa who survived a Spanish shipwreck, crossed the south of the continent on foot, and was killed while guiding a Spanish expedition.
> **When and where:** Born in Azemmour, Morocco. Shipwrecked on the Texas coast in 1528. Reached Mexico in 1536. Killed at the Zuni town of Hawikuh in 1539.

Estevanico was an enslaved man who guided Spanish expeditions through North America. He was born in about 1500 in Azemmour, in Morocco. In 1522 he was sold as a slave to a Spanish nobleman named Andrés Dorantes de Carranza.

In 1528 Dorantes and Estevanico were part of a Spanish expedition that was wrecked on the coast of Texas. Native people on that coast held the survivors, Estevanico among them, as slaves for several years. In 1534 four of the survivors escaped: Estevanico, Dorantes, Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca and Alonso del Castillo Maldonado. They walked west, working as healers along the way, and reached Mexico City in July 1536.

After that, the Spanish official who ruled Mexico, Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza, got Estevanico from Dorantes. He either bought him or received him as a gift. Some evidence suggests Mendoza freed him. On March 7, 1539, Estevanico set out as the guide for Marcos de Niza, who was heading north. Marcos was a friar, a member of a Catholic religious order. Estevanico went ahead of the friar with Native men from Sonora. The people of Hawikuh, a Zuni town, killed him there in 1539. Several accounts of his death survive, and they do not agree. Zuni tradition and the Spanish accounts give different reasons for why he was killed.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1600s, English colonists built slavery into their laws step by step, through court cases and new laws. When about twenty Africans reached Virginia in 1619, Virginia's law had no category for a person held as property for life. Between 1640 and 1705, judges and elected lawmakers in Virginia, Massachusetts and Carolina created that category. In 1662 Virginia's lawmakers ruled that a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life, whoever the father was.

Over the whole Atlantic slave trade, ship crews loaded about 12.5 million captive Africans onto ships bound for the Americas. About 1.8 million of them died on the way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Point Comfort, 1619" -->
In late August 1619, an English ship called the *White Lion* landed at Point Comfort, in Virginia. It carried "20. and odd Negroes," in the words of the colonist John Rolfe. The *White Lion* was a privateer, a privately owned armed ship that attacked the ships of enemy countries. Its crew had taken these Africans from a Portuguese slave ship. They came from the Ndongo and Kongo region of West Central Africa. Sources give different days for the landing. The National Park Service says late August. Some writers give August 20, and the Hampton historian Beth Austin argues for about August 25.

Point Comfort was Native land. It belonged to the Kecoughtan, one of the Powhatan nations, a people who spoke an Algonquian language and lived where the city of Hampton stands today. English colonists built a fort beside their main village in 1609. On July 9, 1610, soon after Lord De La Warr arrived, the colonists seized the village. They drew the people out with a man playing a tambourine and then attacked them. The records do not say how many Kecoughtan they killed. The survivors fled to other towns of the Powhatan nations.

In 1619 Virginia's law did not yet have a category for slaves. Historians still argue whether the colonists held the Africans of 1619 as servants for a set number of years or as slaves for life. The records that survive do not settle it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Middle Passage" -->
Ship crews took captive Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas. That crossing is called the Middle Passage. Over the whole history of the trade, ship crews loaded about 12.5 million Africans onto slave ships. About 10.7 million lived through the crossing. About 1.8 million died at sea. These figures come from the SlaveVoyages database of slave-ship voyages.

The average voyage took just over two months. The trip from Upper Guinea, in West Africa, took about three weeks. Crews chained the captives below the deck, in spaces too low for a person to stand up. They held the women apart from the men and kept the men in chains for long periods so they could not revolt. Crew members often raped the women. Rape means forcing a person into sex. About one captive in four was a child.

People died of disease and of thirst, and some killed themselves. The crew threw the bodies of the dead into the sea. Historians at the College of Charleston report that 13 to 19 percent of the people on board died during a crossing.

Ship crews landed most of the captives who survived in the Caribbean and in Brazil. Fewer than 4 percent came to mainland North America. Crews brought about 305,000 people there straight from Africa. Counting those who came by way of the Caribbean, the total is close to 388,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One survivor's account" -->
Slave traders carried Olaudah Equiano across the ocean on a slave ship when he was a child. In 1789 he published a book in London about his life, *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano*. In it he described the crossing.

The crew put Equiano below deck, and he was too sick to eat. Two white crewmen tied him across the windlass, the winch that pulled up the anchor chain, and flogged him for refusing food. Flogging means that a person is tied down and struck over and over on the bare body with a whip. Each stroke cuts the skin, and deep or repeated flogging leaves raised scars for life. Equiano wrote that captives were "hourly whipped for not eating."

He wrote that the hold was "so crowded that each had scarcely room to turn himself." The heat and the foul air made people sick, "of which many died." Children fell into the tubs the captives used as toilets and nearly smothered. One day two men who were chained together broke through the netting around the deck and jumped into the sea. A third man followed them. The crew lowered a boat. The two chained men drowned. The crew pulled the third man out "and afterwards flogged him unmercifully."

The ship landed at Barbados, an island in the Caribbean. From there, traders sold him on to Virginia. In 2005 the historian Vincent Carretta reported two records that give Equiano's birthplace as Carolina: a 1759 baptism record in London and a 1773 Royal Navy crew list. Equiano's own book says he was born in Africa. Historians still argue over which is true.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->


<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-punch" name="John Punch" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Punch

> **Who:** The first African known to have been sentenced by a Virginia court to serve for life.
> **When and where:** Virginia. Sentenced on July 9, 1640.

On July 9, 1640, the judges of the General Court of Virginia sentenced John Punch to serve his master for the rest of his life. The same judges gave the two white men who had run away with him four more years. Punch was the only African among the three.

The three men were servants of a man named Hugh Gwyn. The court record calls them "Victor, a dutchman, the other a Scotchman called James Gregory, the third being a negro named John Punch." They ran away together toward Maryland and were caught.

The judges ordered that "the said three servants shall receive the punishment of whipping and to have thirty stripes apiece." A stripe is one stroke of the whip. Victor and James Gregory then had to finish their time with their master, serve him one more year, and serve the colony for three years after that.

The judges ordered that John Punch "shall serve his said master or his assigns for the time of his natural Life here or elsewhere." His assigns meant anyone the master sold or handed him to. It is the first known sentence of lifetime slavery in Virginia.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-punch" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From servant to slave for life, 1640 to 1705" -->
In the early years of Virginia, many workers were indentured servants. An indentured servant was bound by a contract to work for a master for a set number of years and then went free. Between 1640 and 1705, judges and lawmakers made a different rule for Africans. They could be held for life.

In 1654 Anthony Johnson went to court in Northampton County, Virginia. Johnson was an African who had arrived in about 1621, gained his freedom and become a landowner on the Eastern Shore. He sued his white neighbor Robert Parker over a Black man named John Casor. Casor said the time on his contract had run out and he was free. In 1655 the county judges ruled that Casor was Johnson's servant for life. It was the first court ruling in the English mainland colonies that a person who had committed no crime could be held for life.

Lawmakers in New England wrote slavery into law too. On December 10, 1641, the members of the Massachusetts Bay General Court, the colony's lawmaking body, adopted a code of laws called the Body of Liberties. In it they wrote that slavery was allowed for "lawful captives taken in just wars, and such strangers as willingly sell themselves or are sold to us." In plain words, people captured in a war the colonists called fair, and outsiders who were sold to them, could be held as slaves. The Massachusetts Bay lawmakers were the first in any English colony on the mainland to put slavery into a written law.

In 1662 the members of Virginia's House of Burgesses, the colony's elected assembly, passed a law about children. Under the old rule in English law, a child's status came from the father. Virginia's lawmakers broke with that on purpose. They ruled that a child's status followed the mother's. A child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life, whoever the father was. Lawyers called the rule by a Latin phrase, *partus sequitur ventrem*, which means "the child follows the womb."

Some colonists were unsure whether baptism, the ceremony that made a person a Christian, set an enslaved person free. In September 1667, the members of Virginia's General Assembly passed a law that said the opposite. It stated that "the conferring of baptisme doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome." In other words, being baptized as a Christian did not set an enslaved person free. The lawmakers gave their reason in the law. With that doubt gone, they wrote, masters would be more willing to let enslaved people be baptized.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The slave codes" -->
A slave code is a set of laws about enslaved people: what they could not do, how they could be punished, and what their owners could do to them. In 1705 the members of Virginia's General Assembly gathered the colony's earlier slave laws into one code. Under it, enslaved people counted as real estate, the same kind of property as land. The code said that baptism did not free anyone. A master who killed an enslaved person while "correcting" him, which meant punishing him, faced no penalty.

In Carolina, the members of the colony's assembly, its lawmakers, passed their first slave law in 1690. The Lords Proprietors, the English noblemen who owned the colony, struck that law down. The assembly's members passed a new code in 1696. They based both laws on the slave code that lawmakers in Barbados, an English island in the Caribbean, had written in 1661.

The 1690 Carolina law already allowed branding, nose-slitting and castration as punishments. Branding meant pressing a metal iron, heated red-hot, into the person's skin. The iron burned a mark, often a letter, that stayed on the body as a scar for life. Nose-slitting meant cutting the person's nose open. Under the Barbados code, an enslaved person who hit a Christian a second time was to be whipped hard, have the nose slit and be burned on the face with a hot iron. Castration meant cutting off a man's testicles, which left him unable to father children.

No lawmakers in any colony passed a law against teaching enslaved people to write in the 1600s. South Carolina's lawmakers passed the first one in 1740.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, colonial lawmakers wrote slave codes that ordered whipping, branding and cutting off ears as punishments, and enslaved people rose up in both the South and the North. South Carolina planters grew rice to sell overseas, a crop that enslaved West Africans already knew how to grow. In 1739 about 20 enslaved men near Charleston took up arms and marched toward Spanish Florida. The king of Spain had promised freedom to people who escaped there from the English colonies.

The English colonists enslaved Native people as well as Africans. In New York City in 1712, about one person in five was enslaved.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carolina's trade in Native captives" -->
English traders in Carolina ran a large business in enslaved Native people. Between 1670 and 1715, they enslaved between 24,000 and 51,000 Native people of the Southeast. That range comes from the historian Alan Gallay. The traders shipped many of the captives to the Caribbean. In those years, more enslaved Native people were shipped out of Charleston than enslaved Africans were brought in.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ships from Rhode Island" -->
Merchants in the northern colonies sent out slave ships too. Rhode Island merchants paid for about 1,000 slaving voyages. Rhode Island ports sent out about half of all the slave ships that left mainland North America. The historian Jay Coughtry counted 934 of these voyages, which carried about 106,000 Africans. In the 1700s, Rhode Island merchants controlled between 60 and 90 percent of the American trade in African captives.

The DeWolf family of Bristol, Rhode Island, sent out twice as many slaving voyages as any other family. Bringing captives into the United States from abroad became illegal in 1808. George DeWolf kept sending out slave ships anyway, against the law, until 1825.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York, 1711 and 1712" -->
Slavery was part of life in the northern colonies too. In 1700 New York City had about 5,000 people, and at least 750 of them were enslaved. On November 30, 1711, the city's aldermen, the men who governed the city, set up a market at the foot of Wall Street. There, enslaved people were sold, rented out and inspected by buyers. The market stood from 1711 to 1762. The accounts of the market do not name the men who bought and sold people there.

On April 6, 1712, more than 20 enslaved people in New York City set fire to a building on Maiden Lane. Historians believe most of them were Coromantee, Akan people from the Gold Coast of West Africa, which is Ghana today. When white people came to fight the fire, the rebels attacked them. They killed 8 or 9 white people and wounded 6 or 7. Six of the rebels killed themselves before they could be captured.

Governor Robert Hunter called out the militia, the colony's armed citizens. Officials arrested more than 70 Black people. They put 27 on trial, found 21 guilty and put all 21 to death. One account says 20 were burned alive and one was broken on the wheel. Breaking on the wheel was a public execution. The person was tied to a large wheel, and the executioner broke the person's arms and legs with an iron bar and left the person to die. Other accounts say only that the punishments ran from burning alive to breaking on the wheel. Afterward, the members of the colony's assembly made it illegal for enslaved people to gather in groups of more than three or to own guns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rice and the Lowcountry" -->
The Lowcountry is the coast of South Carolina around Charleston. Rice planters there relied on the skill and the labor of West Africans from rice-growing parts of Africa. Planters paid more for captives from those coasts. Rice shipped out of Carolina rose from about 268,602 pounds a year around 1700 to more than 30 million pounds a year around 1740. Charleston became the largest port on the mainland where slave ships landed their captives.

Ships arriving at Charleston stopped first at Sullivan's Island, in the harbor. The island had a quarantine station, called the pest house, where captives who might carry disease were held apart from everyone else. Many accounts say about 40 percent of all the enslaved Africans brought to mainland North America passed through the island itself. The Charleston historian Nic Butler and others say that figure is too high, because many ships landed elsewhere in the harbor. They agree that Charleston took in more captives than any other port on the mainland.

The rice plantations stand on Native land. When English colonists settled Charleston in 1670, about fifteen Native groups lived in the Lowcountry. Among them were the Stono, the Kiawah, the Etiwan, the Edisto and the Combahee. By the middle 1700s these groups were nearly gone. Many of their people had died of disease and in wars, and English colonists had taken their land.

In June 1712, the members of South Carolina's assembly passed a law that listed punishments for people who ran away. The law applied to any enslaved person who stayed away for twenty days or more. For a first escape, it ordered a public whipping of up to forty lashes. A lash is one stroke of the whip. For a second escape, it ordered the person "branded with the letter R, on the right cheek." For a third, it ordered forty lashes and one ear cut off. A man caught a fourth time was to be castrated. A woman caught a fourth time was to be whipped, branded with an R on the left cheek, and have her left ear cut off. The records of the law do not name the people who carried out these punishments.

For a fifth escape, the law let two justices of the peace and three landowners decide. A justice of the peace was a local judge. They could order the person killed. Or they could order the person's Achilles tendon, the cord just above the heel, cut through. After that the person could not run. From 1723 on, South Carolina's codes left punishment to the enslaver instead of listing it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Mose, 1738" -->
Florida belonged to Spain. In 1693 the king of Spain promised freedom to enslaved people who escaped from the English colonies and reached Florida, if they became Catholics. Men also had to serve in the militia.

In 1738 Florida's governor, Manuel Montiano, set up a town for these free people about two miles north of St. Augustine. Its Spanish name was Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose. People call it Fort Mose. It was the first free Black town in what is now the United States to be recognized by law. About 100 people lived there, in more than 20 households.

In 1740 British forces surrounded St. Augustine and attacked it. Fort Mose was destroyed in the fighting, which people remember as the battle of Bloody Mose. The people rebuilt it in 1752. In 1763, when the Spanish left Florida, the people of Mose left with them for Cuba. The site is now Fort Mose Historic State Park.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="francisco-menendez" name="Francisco Menéndez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Francisco Menéndez

> **Who:** A man from West Africa who escaped slavery in Carolina and became captain of the free Black militia at Fort Mose.
> **When and where:** Born in the Gambia region of Africa. Enslaved in Carolina. Led the militia at Fort Mose, Florida, from 1738.

Francisco Menéndez commanded the free Black militia at Fort Mose, the town of people who had escaped slavery in the English colonies. He was a Mandinga man, born in the Gambia region of West Africa. Slave traders carried him to Carolina, where he was enslaved.

He escaped south to Spanish Florida. There he was baptized as a Catholic and took the name Francisco Menéndez. From 1738 he led the militia at Mose, the armed men who defended the town. Spanish colonial records document his life. Of all the people who lived at Mose, he is the one the records describe most fully.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="francisco-menendez" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Stono Rebellion, 1739" -->
On September 9, 1739, about 20 enslaved men rose up at the Stono River, south of Charleston. It was the largest uprising of enslaved people in the mainland colonies before the American Revolution. Their leader was a man named Jemmy. A report written in 1739 describes them as Angolan and says "one who was called Jemmy was their Captain." They seized weapons and marched south toward Spanish Florida. Others joined them along the way. The same report says they were "calling out Liberty" and marched "with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating." The rebels killed about 25 white colonists.

Lieutenant Governor William Bull met the rebels on the road that day. He rode off to warn the planters and raised the militia, which attacked the rebels the same day. Bull also hired Chickasaw and Catawba men, and some enslaved men, to hunt down those who got away. About a week later, the militia fought a group of rebels again, about 30 miles from the first fight.

The militia killed between 35 and 50 rebels in the first fight. A historical marker says at least 54. The 1739 report gives its own count: "about 40 Negroes and 20 whites were killed." The report says the militia "shot on the Spot" rebels they took in the field. Colonists cut off the heads of rebels and set them on stakes along the main roads as a warning to others. The colonists put most of the captured rebels to death. One account says the militia took 43 rebels who had no weapons and hanged them. The colonists sold the survivors to the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Negro Act of 1740" -->
On May 10, 1740, the members of South Carolina's assembly passed the Negro Act in answer to Stono. It barred enslaved people from gathering in groups, growing their own food, earning money and moving about freely. It made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to write. That was the first law in what became the United States against teaching enslaved people. It named writing, not reading.

Under the Negro Act, a white person who murdered an enslaved person was guilty only of a misdemeanor, a minor crime punished by a fine. Any white person, not only an owner, could arrest and punish a Black person who broke the act. The act allowed the death penalty for plotting a revolt, running away, setting fires, or teaching others about poisonous plants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York, 1741" -->
In March 1741, a string of fires began in New York City with a fire at Fort George, the governor's home. City officials claimed that enslaved people and poor white people had plotted to burn the city. Three judges of the colony's Supreme Court heard the cases: James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse. The trials began on May 11, 1741.

The main witness was Mary Burton, a young servant bound to a tavern owner named John Hughson. Judge Horsmanden offered her an end to her contract and a reward in money.

About 200 people were tried. The New York courts' historical society counts 30 people sentenced to death and 70 sentenced to be sold into slavery in the Caribbean. The historian Jill Lepore counts 34 put to death between May 11 and August 29, 1741. By her count, officials burned 13 Black men at the stake and hanged 17 Black men, two white men and two white women. John Hughson, his wife Sarah and their daughter Sarah were among the white people put to death. In 1744 Horsmanden published his record of the trials.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="slavery-freedom" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1776 the Declaration of Independence stated "that all men are created equal." In 1790 the first census, a count of every person in the United States, found 697,681 people held in slavery. Some enslaved people went to court and used the words of the new state constitutions to win their freedom. Elizabeth Freeman won hers in a Massachusetts court in 1781. About 20,000 Black people joined or fled to the British during the war, after Virginia's royal governor promised freedom to enslaved men who fought for Britain. In 1796 Ona Judge escaped from President George Washington's household.

Between 1777 and 1804, lawmakers and judges in Vermont and six northern states set out to end slavery. Most of their laws worked slowly and freed only children born after a set date, once they grew up. The men who wrote the Constitution in 1787 protected slavery in three of its clauses without once using the word. A clause is one part of a written law or constitution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="phillis-wheatley-slavery-freedom" name="Phillis Wheatley" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Phillis Wheatley

> **Who:** A girl kidnapped from West Africa who became the first African American to publish a book of poetry.
> **When and where:** Landed in Boston in 1761. Her book was published in London on September 1, 1773. Freed by November 1773. Died in 1784, at about age 31.

Phillis Wheatley was the first African American to publish a book of poetry. She was about seven years old when slave traders kidnapped her from West Africa. In 1761 she landed in Boston on a slave ship called the *Phillis*. The name of the ship became her first name. Her last name came from the Wheatley family of Boston, who enslaved her.

Her book, *Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral*, was published in London on September 1, 1773. The Wheatleys freed her by November 1773, shortly after it appeared.

In 1778 she married John Peters. She died poor in 1784, at about 31 years old.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="phillis-wheatley-slavery-freedom" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two promises of freedom in the Revolution" -->
On March 5, 1770, British soldiers fired on a crowd of colonists in King Street, in Boston. People call it the Boston Massacre. Crispus Attucks was the first person killed. He was a sailor with African and Wampanoag ancestors. Most accounts say he had escaped from slavery in Framingham, Massachusetts, around 1750. Historians disagree about whether he was legally free by 1770 or still an escaped slave.

On November 7, 1775, Virginia's royal governor, Lord Dunmore, made a promise to the enslaved men of Virginians who were fighting the British. Any of them who joined the British army would be free. About 300 men joined his Ethiopian Regiment within a month, and it grew to about 800. Over the whole war, historians estimate that about 20,000 Black people joined or fled to the British. About 9,000 served the American side, the Patriots. About 5,000 of those fought as soldiers. Both sides promised freedom to some of the enslaved men who fought for them, and neither side kept those promises to all of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first states to end slavery" -->
In 1777 Vermont's constitution banned holding adults in slavery. Vermont was then an independent republic, and it joined the United States in 1791. It was the first constitution to ban slavery.

On March 1, 1780, Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed the Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery. To abolish something means to end it. No elected lawmakers anywhere in the world had passed a law to end slavery before. The act freed no one right away. Children born after it would be freed when they turned 28.

In Massachusetts, juries and judges ended slavery in court cases decided between 1781 and 1783. Lawmakers in Connecticut and Rhode Island passed gradual laws in 1784. New York's lawmakers passed one in 1799, and the last enslaved people in New York were freed in 1827. New Jersey's lawmakers passed theirs last, in 1804. Under these gradual laws, people in the North stayed enslaved for decades.

On July 13, 1787, the members of Congress passed the Northwest Ordinance, a law for the lands north of the Ohio River. It banned slavery there.

### Elizabeth Freeman ("Mum Bett")

> **Who:** An enslaved woman who sued for her freedom under the new Massachusetts constitution and won.
> **When and where:** Sheffield and Great Barrington, Massachusetts. Jury verdict in August 1781. Died in 1829.

Elizabeth Freeman went to court in 1781 and won her freedom. Before that she was known as Bett, or Mum Bett. She was enslaved in the household of Colonel John Ashley in Sheffield, Massachusetts.

The new Massachusetts constitution of 1780 states that "all men are born free and equal." Bett and an enslaved man named Brom sued Ashley for their freedom. Their lawyer, Theodore Sedgwick, argued that those words in the constitution made slavery illegal.

In August 1781, a jury in Great Barrington set them free. The jury also ordered Ashley to pay them 30 shillings for the harm done to them. The case was called *Brom and Bett v. Ashley*, which means Brom and Bett against Ashley. That case and the court cases of an enslaved man named Quock Walker, decided from 1781 to 1783, ended slavery in Massachusetts.

Bett took the name Elizabeth Freeman. For the rest of her life she worked for pay as a housekeeper and a nurse. She died in 1829.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-freeman" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sally Hemings and Thomas Jefferson" -->
Thomas Jefferson was the main author of the Declaration of Independence. Over his lifetime he owned more than 600 enslaved people. One of them was Sally Hemings. Jefferson raped her when she was a girl, and she had his children.

Sally Hemings was born enslaved in 1773. Her mother, Elizabeth Hemings, was enslaved. Her father was most likely John Wayles, the man who owned her mother. By law Wayles owned Elizabeth Hemings, and she could not refuse him. Wayles was also the father of Jefferson's wife, Martha. That made Sally Hemings the half-sister of Jefferson's wife.

In 1787 Jefferson was the United States minister to France, the country's top official there, living in Paris. That year Sally Hemings went to Paris with his daughter Polly. Sally was 14 and enslaved by Jefferson. He was 44. By law he owned her, and she could not refuse him. He had the legal power to sell her or her family. Her son Madison Hemings said in 1873 that in Paris she "became Mr. Jefferson's concubine," an old word for a woman a man kept for sex without marrying her. He said she was pregnant when they came back to Virginia. An enslaved girl had no safe way to say no to the man who owned her. Historians describe what Jefferson did as sexual exploitation, and as rape.

Under French law she could have claimed her freedom in Paris. Madison Hemings said Jefferson promised that her children would be freed at age 21 if she went back with him. She had children from 1790 to 1808. Four lived to be adults: Beverly, Harriet, Madison and Eston.

In 1998 scientists compared DNA and found that a man of the Jefferson family was the father of Eston Hemings. In 2000 a committee of the Thomas Jefferson Foundation, which runs Jefferson's home at Monticello, concluded that Jefferson "was probably the father" of her children. By 2018 the foundation's historians treated this as a fact. In September 2026 a study paid for by the Smithsonian tested DNA from a lock of Jefferson's hair, cut on the day he died. Descendants of Madison and Eston Hemings carried more of Jefferson's DNA than the scientists expected, and the study concluded that Jefferson fathered her children. Jefferson died in 1826. Neither he nor anyone after him ever legally freed Sally Hemings. His daughter Martha Jefferson Randolph let her live in Charlottesville with her sons. The census of 1833 listed her as free. She died in 1835.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Constitution's protections for slavery, 1787" -->
The men who wrote the Constitution in 1787 put three protections for slavery into it. None of the three clauses uses the word slave.

Under the three-fifths clause, each enslaved person counted as three-fifths of a person. That count decided how many seats a state got in the House of Representatives. Enslaved people could not vote, but their owners' states got extra seats for them. In 1793 the slave states held 47 of the 105 seats in the House. Counting only their free people, they would have had 33. The fugitive clause said that a person "held to Service or Labour" who escaped to another state had to be handed back. The slave-trade clause barred Congress from ending the import of enslaved people before 1808.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved builders of the capital" -->
Enslaved people worked at every stage of building the White House and the Capitol in Washington. The cornerstone of the White House was laid on October 13, 1792, and that of the Capitol on September 18, 1793. Enslaved men cut sandstone at the quarries on Aquia Creek, the open pits where the stone was dug. They also cut timber, made bricks, sawed lumber and did carpentry. The officials in charge of the building hired them from their owners, and the owners kept the pay.

The payroll of the President's House for May 1795 names five enslaved carpenters: Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry and Daniel. The architect James Hoban held four of them, and his assistant Pierce Purcell held one. The payroll records the days four of the men worked that month: Peter 28, Ben 28, Daniel 25 and Harry 9. Hoban signed for their wages.

### Ona Judge

> **Who:** An enslaved woman who escaped from President George Washington's household and was never caught.
> **When and where:** Walked out of the President's House in Philadelphia on May 21, 1796. Lived free in New Hampshire. Died in 1848.

Ona Judge escaped from President George Washington and his wife, Martha, and lived the rest of her life free. She was enslaved as Martha Washington's personal servant.

On May 21, 1796, while the Washingtons were eating dinner in the President's House in Philadelphia, Ona Judge walked out. She boarded a sloop, a small sailing ship, called the *Nancy*, and sailed to Portsmouth, New Hampshire.

On May 24, 1796, a runaway advertisement ran in the *Philadelphia Gazette*. It offered ten dollars for her capture. George Washington tried for years to get her back. She refused an offer to return. She married and lived in New Hampshire until she died in 1848.

In the 1840s she told her own story to newspaper interviewers, including one from *The Granite Freeman* in 1845. She told them that she was poor and that she was free.
