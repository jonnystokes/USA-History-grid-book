## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 25 minutes**

The Galveston hurricane of September 1900 killed between 6,000 and 12,000 people, more than any other natural disaster in the country's history. In the fifty years after it, many more people died because of choices other people made.

Witnesses said the owners of a New York blouse factory kept an exit door locked. Near Los Angeles the city's chief engineer and his assistant judged a leaking dam safe, and in Texas members of a school board piped waste gas into a school to save money. After the Triangle fire, the *Titanic*, the New London school explosion and the Cocoanut Grove fire, lawmakers wrote new safety rules for factories, ships, gas lines and nightclubs.

In three disasters, the people in charge treated Black survivors and Black victims worse than white ones. In Galveston, members of an armed militia forced Black workers at gunpoint to bury the dead. In the 1927 Mississippi flood, the local leaders who ran many relief camps gave supplies to white people first and forced Black people to work. After the 1928 hurricane in Florida, officials saved the few coffins for white victims, and the bodies of Black victims were burned in piles or thrown into mass graves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Galveston, September 8, 1900" -->
A hurricane flooded Galveston, Texas, on September 8, 1900, and killed more people than any other natural disaster in the country's history. Galveston was a port city of about 38,000 people on an island in the Gulf of Mexico. At about 6:30 that evening, a storm wave put the city under as much as 15 feet of water. The highest water mark was 15.7 feet. The storm destroyed a third of the city, including between 2,636 and about 3,600 homes by different counts.

Between 6,000 and 8,000 people died in the city. Estimates for the whole island and the nearby coast run from 10,000 to 12,000. The *Galveston News* printed a list of 4,263 dead in early October, months before searchers found the last body.

Searchers found about 70 bodies a day during the first month. The last body was found on February 10, 1901. Galveston's leaders put the city under martial law, which means soldiers or armed guards take over from the police and courts. A volunteer militia, a group of armed citizens, patrolled the streets against looters, people who steal from damaged buildings. Some accounts say that men accused of looting were shot, but the records do not give their names or a count.

**Forced at gunpoint.** Too few volunteers came forward to handle the dead, so armed men rounded up other men at gunpoint or bayonet point to do it. The writer Elizabeth Blasius found that members of the volunteer militia did this, and that many of the men they forced were Black workers. About one fifth of the people of Galveston were Black. Even before the storm, the city's segregation rules, which kept people apart by race, kept Black Galvestonians from drinking at the public fountains or going to the public schools.

At first, workers loaded bodies onto barges, tied heavy rocks to them and dropped them into the Gulf. Days later, some of the bodies floated back to shore. After that, workers burned the dead in large fires. The fires burned into November.

Clara Barton, 78, arrived with Red Cross workers on September 17, nine days after the storm. Mayor Walter C. Jones led a Central Relief Committee that included the banker John Sealy. The committee's members spent its money on 483 new houses and on partial help toward 1,114 more.

**The seawall.** Afterward, workers built a seawall that stood 17 feet above the low-tide line. Its first section ran three and a half miles. Workers then raised the ground under the city by as much as 17 feet. They lifted about 2,000 buildings on jacks first. St. Patrick's Church, which weighed 3,000 tons, went up five feet.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="isaac-cline" name="Isaac Cline" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Isaac Cline

> **Who:** The chief of the U.S. Weather Bureau office in Galveston, whose pregnant wife died in the 1900 hurricane.
> **When and where:** Galveston, Texas, September 8, 1900.

**He had called a hurricane harming Galveston "a crazy idea."**

Isaac Cline took charge of the Galveston weather office in 1889, when the Army's Signal Service still ran the country's weather service. The office passed to the new Weather Bureau in 1890, and his brother Joseph joined him there in 1892. Isaac wrote an article for the *Galveston Daily News* in 1891, nine years before the storm. In it he called the thought of a hurricane seriously harming Galveston "a crazy idea." Many people in the city had asked for a seawall. His article was one reason no seawall was built before the storm.

**The flags.** On September 8, 1900, water began flooding the low parts of the city. Around noon, Cline raised the hurricane warning flags without waiting for approval from the Weather Bureau's main office in Washington. Weather Bureau rules required that approval.

Cline later wrote that he drove a horse-drawn cart along the beach and warned people to leave. No eyewitness ever reported seeing him do it. The writer Erik Larson argued that Cline warned no one before his office sent out its official warning.

Cline's wife, Cora, was pregnant with their fourth child, and she died in the storm. Cline nearly drowned. He saved his youngest daughter, Esther, who was 6. Joseph saved the two older girls, Allie May, 12, and Rosemary, 11.

Cline moved to New Orleans in 1901. Between 1900 and 1924 he charted the paths of 16 storms.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-cline" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="San Francisco, 1906: the fire after the earthquake" -->
Fire did most of the damage after the San Francisco earthquake of April 18, 1906. The earthquake struck at 5:12 in the morning. It had a magnitude, the number scientists use for an earthquake's strength, of about 7.9, and it shook the city for about 42 seconds. It broke gas pipes, and within three days more than 30 fires had spread across the city. Between 80 and 95 percent of the damage came from the fires.

Firefighters tried to blow up buildings with dynamite to make empty gaps the fire could not cross. They had no training with dynamite, and some of their blasts started new fires. The city's fire chief, Dennis T. Sullivan, had been hurt in the earthquake itself, when a building next door fell onto the fire station where he lived. He died four days later.

General Frederick Funston sent in Army soldiers. Some building owners set fire to their own damaged buildings, because their insurance paid for fire damage and not for earthquake damage.

More than 80 percent of the city was destroyed. Between 227,000 and 300,000 people lost their homes, out of about 410,000. The first counts of the dead ran from 375 to more than 500. Those counts left out hundreds of deaths in Chinatown that no one recorded. In 2005 the members of the Board of Supervisors, San Francisco's city lawmakers, voted to accept more than 3,000 as the official count. They voted for a written statement by the novelist James Dalessandro and the city historian Gladys Hansen.

On the day of the earthquake, Mayor Eugene Schmitz announced that he had given soldiers and police permission "to kill any and all persons found engaged in Looting or in the Commission of Any Other Crime." The records do not say how many people they killed. People also accused soldiers themselves of looting.

**Chinatown.** About 15,000 Chinese people lived in Chinatown, and they lost nearly everything. Even before the earthquake, some city officials had wanted to move the Chinese out of Chinatown to Hunters Point. After it, they tried to use the ruins to take Chinatown's land for business buildings.

The Chinese were the only group that officials kept apart in the camps for people who had lost their homes. Army officers moved the Chinese survivors to camps farther and farther from Chinatown. Most Chinese survivors left for Oakland, and only about 400 stayed in the city. The officials' plan to move Chinatown failed. Chinese residents rebuilt Chinatown.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hugh-kwong-liang" name="Hugh Kwong Liang" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hugh Kwong Liang

> **Who:** A 15-year-old in San Francisco's Chinatown who lived through the 1906 earthquake and fire.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, April 1906.

**He was 15, and the building across the street fell down.**

Hugh Kwong Liang was 15 when the earthquake woke him in Chinatown. He is one of the few Chinese survivors whose own account of the earthquake has lasted to today. He remembered seeing "pieces of plaster falling down like water." He ran out into Washington Street, and the building across from his home fell down.

As the fire spread, city officials who were directing the people fleeing told him and his family to go to the open ground of the Presidio. The Presidio was an Army post by the Golden Gate, the entrance to San Francisco Bay. He remembered the moment he left: "I turned away from my dear old Chinatown for the last time."

At the Presidio, Army officers kept the Chinese survivors apart from everyone else. They moved them from camp to camp. The last camp stood in a cold, windy corner of the Presidio near Fort Point.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hugh-kwong-liang" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Triangle fire, March 25, 1911" -->
A fire at the Triangle Waist Company in New York City killed 146 workers on March 25, 1911. Witnesses said the owners kept one of its exit doors locked. The company made women's blouses on the top three floors of the ten-story Asch Building. Most of its workers were young immigrant women, and many were teenagers. The youngest who died, Kate Leone and Rosaria Maltese, were 14.

The fire started on the eighth floor just before the 4:45 p.m. quitting time. Workers ran down the Greene Street stairs, crowded into the elevators, and climbed onto the one fire escape, which collapsed.

**The locked door.** On the ninth floor, the door to the Washington Place stairs would not open. Witnesses told the court that the owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, kept it locked so workers could not leave with stolen blouses. Harris later admitted that all the blouses ever stolen were worth less than 25 dollars.

The firefighters' ladders reached only to the seventh floor. Workers trapped on the ninth floor could not get past the fire, and many jumped from the windows. A crowd in the street watched 62 people jump or fall to their deaths. Joseph Flecher, who worked in the company office, watched from the roof and saw his workers "hit the sidewalk spread out and still."

On the tenth floor, workers climbed to the roof. A law professor at New York University, Frank Sommer, and his students found ladders that painters had left and set them across to the school's roof. Workers climbed over them to safety.

**The trial.** Blanck and Harris were put on trial for manslaughter, the crime of killing a person without meaning to, through carelessness. Judge Crain told the jury it had to find that the owners knew the door was locked. On December 27, 1911, after less than two hours, the jury found them not guilty. One juror, Victor Steinman, said he believed the door was locked, "but we couldn't find them guilty unless we believed they knew the door was locked."

The families in 23 lawsuits against the building's owner settled on March 11, 1914, which means they agreed to take money and end the lawsuits. They got an average of 75 dollars for each life.

New York lawmakers set up a nine-member Factory Investigating Commission. From 1911 to 1914 they passed 36 new laws to change the state's rules for workplaces. A state law already in force at the time of the fire had required factory doors to open outward where possible and to stay unlocked during working hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kate-alterman" name="Kate Alterman" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kate Alterman

> **Who:** A worker who escaped the Triangle fire and told the jury how another worker, Margaret Schwartz, died at the locked door.
> **When and where:** New York City, March 25, 1911, and the trial in December 1911.

**With her coat turned inside out, she ran through the flames.**

Kate Alterman saw another worker, Margaret Schwartz, catch fire at the locked Washington Place door, and she told the jury what she saw. Margaret died in the fire. Kate worked on the ninth floor. She had lived in Philadelphia for nine years, and she had been in New York and worked at the Triangle factory for four months.

When the fire reached the ninth floor, the two women ran to the Washington Place door. The manager's brother, whose last name was Bernstein, tried it and could not open it. Margaret tried next. Then Kate pulled the handle "in and out, all ways," and it still would not open.

Kate saw Margaret kneel down by the door. She saw the hem of Margaret's dress and the ends of her loose hair start to burn. Margaret screamed, "Open the door! Fire! I am lost, there is fire!"

Kate turned her coat inside out and put it over her head with the fur against her face. She covered her head with a bunch of dresses. Then she ran out through the flames. "The whole door was a flame right to the roof," she said.

**In court.** At the trial the prosecutor, Charles Bostwick, used her account to show that the locked door caused Margaret Schwartz's death. The owners' lawyer, Max Steuer, made her tell the story again and again. She used almost the same words each time. In this way Steuer made her story seem less believable without ever saying she was lying. She said she told it the same way "because he asked me the very same story over and over."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kate-alterman" -->

**By the end of 1911:** Thousands had died in Galveston and San Francisco, a jury had found the Triangle owners not guilty, and New York lawmakers were writing new rules for the state's factories.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ships: the Titanic, 1912, and the Eastland, 1915" -->
The *Titanic* carried lifeboats for only some of the people on board, and United States senators blamed British officials for that. The *Titanic* was a British passenger ship. It hit an iceberg in the North Atlantic and sank on April 15, 1912. More than 1,500 passengers and crew died. The ship *Carpathia* picked up about 710 survivors.

The ship had too few lifeboats for everyone, but it met the rules of the British Board of Trade, the government office in charge of British ships. Senator William Alden Smith of Michigan led a Senate investigation. It began in New York on April 19, four days after the sinking. The senators found that Captain Edward Smith's "indifference to danger" was one of the direct causes of the deaths. They blamed the Board of Trade's officials for the missing lifeboats.

The senators called for ships to slow down near ice, to carry enough lifeboats for all on board, and to keep a radio operator on duty day and night. In March 1915, after the *Titanic* sinking, members of Congress passed the Seamen's Act, pushed by Senator Robert La Follette of Wisconsin.

**The *Eastland*.** The Great Lakes passenger ship *Eastland* rolled over on July 24, 1915, while it was tied to a dock in the Chicago River. It was taking workers of the Western Electric Company and their families to a company picnic in Indiana, and it had already filled to its limit of 2,500 passengers.

The ship had tipped badly before, including in 1906. Under the new law, its owners had added a full set of lifeboats so it could carry more people. That added weight may have made the ship more dangerous. At 7:28 a.m. it rolled onto its side and settled on the river bottom, only 20 feet down. In all, 844 people died. Many of the dead were immigrants, and 220 of them were Czech.

A deckhand, Peter Boyle, drowned while saving people. A nurse, Helen Repa, led much of the rescue work on shore. An Illinois grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence for a trial, charged the president of the steamship company and three other company officers with manslaughter. It charged the captain and the engineer with criminal carelessness.

**No trial.** The men were outside Illinois, and a federal court refused to send them there for trial. The court found "barely a scintilla of proof," meaning almost no evidence, because the four company officers had not been on the ship. None of the men was ever tried in Illinois.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who was helped first in the 1927 flood?" -->
About 637,000 people had to leave their homes in the Mississippi River flood of 1927. In the relief camps, local leaders gave supplies to white people first and forced Black people to work. In the spring the river broke through its levees, walls of earth built along a riverbank to hold the water in, in at least 145 places. Water covered 27,000 square miles, as deep as 30 feet. About 500 people died.

**The dynamite.** A group of New Orleans bankers arranged to set off about 30 tons of dynamite on the levee at Caernarvon, Louisiana, below their city, on April 29. They wanted to let the river out there to keep it from badly damaging New Orleans. The water flooded St. Bernard Parish and part of Plaquemines Parish, as Louisiana calls its counties. Blowing up the levee turned out to be unnecessary. The New Orleans businessmen never paid the people whose homes and land they flooded.

**In the camps.** About 325,000 people lived in relief camps, and 69 percent of them were Black. Near Greenville, Mississippi, more than 13,000 people escaped to the top of a levee that had held. Many of them were stranded there for days without food or clean water.

Local leaders from the countryside ran many of the camps. They gave supplies and boats to white people first, and Black people got what was left. A Black person got supplies only by giving the name of a white employer. Black people in the camps were forced to work and were not allowed to leave. The records do not name the camp leaders who gave these orders.

A white police officer ordered a Black man at gunpoint to unload a relief boat. The man refused, and the officer shot and killed him. The records do not give the name of the man who was killed.

Near Helena, Arkansas, a plantation overseer, the man who bossed the workers, tried to force a Black man, Owen Flemming, to rescue the plantation owner's mules. Flemming killed the overseer. Then he was lynched, which means he was killed by a mob without a trial. The records do not name the men who killed him.

Herbert Hoover, the Secretary of Commerce, led the national government's relief work. A group of Black leaders headed by Robert Russa Moton studied the camps and reported what was happening in them. Hoover asked that their reports be kept out of the newspapers, and they were. After the flood, members of Congress passed the Flood Control Act of 1928.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two disasters of 1928: the St. Francis Dam and Lake Okeechobee" -->
The St. Francis Dam in northern Los Angeles County broke just before midnight on March 12, 1928. That same day its builder had looked at a new leak and judged it safe.

William Mulholland was the chief engineer of the city's Bureau of Water Works and Supply. His workers had built the dam from 1924 to 1926. Leaks and cracks showed up in 1927 and 1928. That morning the dam keeper, Tony Harnischfeger, found a new leak. Mulholland and his assistant, Harvey Van Norman, looked at it and decided it was not dangerous.

The dam gave way at about 11:57 that night. Harnischfeger and his family were probably among the first people killed. The flood ran 54 miles to the Pacific Ocean and killed at least 431 people. Among them were 84 workers of the Southern California Edison company at a construction camp beside the river.

At the investigation afterward, the *Express* newspaper reported that Mulholland said, "the only ones I envy are those who are dead." The *Los Angeles Times* printed his words as "on an occasion like this I envy the dead."

**The jury's finding.** A coroner's jury, a group of citizens who decide how people died, ruled on the collapse on April 12, 1928. The jurors found an error in engineering judgment about the rock under the dam, and they put that blame on Mulholland's bureau and its chief engineer. They found a second error, in the rules meant to keep the public safe. They put that blame on the people the chief engineer answered to: the city's water and power commissioners, the lawmakers of the city and the state, and the public.

The jurors wrote that a great dam should never be left "to the sole judgment of one man, no matter how eminent," meaning no matter how famous and respected. They found no crime and recommended that no one be charged. Mulholland's career as chief engineer ended after the dam broke.

**Lake Okeechobee.** Six months later, early on September 17, 1928, a hurricane came ashore near West Palm Beach, Florida. It had already killed 312 people in Puerto Rico. The storm pushed the water of Lake Okeechobee over the low dike, a bank of earth built to keep water back, at the lake's south end. Floods more than 20 feet deep covered the farm towns of Belle Glade, Canal Point, Chosen, Pahokee and South Bay.

At least 2,500 people died in the United States. For many years the official count was the Red Cross estimate of 1,836. About three out of four of the dead were migrant farm workers, people who moved from farm to farm to pick crops. Most of them were Black.

Black workers did most of the cleanup after the storm. Officials saved the few coffins they had for the bodies of white people. White victims were buried with a service, in a shared grave at Woodlawn Cemetery in West Palm Beach. The bodies of Black victims were burned in piles or thrown into mass graves. One mass grave at Port Mayaca holds 1,600 bodies. The records do not name the officials who made these decisions.

Robert Hazard of West Palm Beach started a group to win recognition for the Black victims buried in the city's mass grave. The city bought the grave site back in 2000. In 2003 a state historical marker was put up there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**By 1929:** The Mississippi flood had driven about 637,000 people from their homes, the St. Francis Dam and the Okeechobee hurricane had killed thousands more in 1928, and members of Congress had passed the Flood Control Act of 1928.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Sunday, April 14, 1935" -->
A wall of blowing dirt swept across the southern Great Plains on April 14, 1935, with winds of up to 60 miles an hour. It reached Beaver, Oklahoma, at about 4 p.m. and Boise City, Oklahoma, at about 5:15 p.m. For a short time the sky was so black that people could not see their own hands in front of their faces. The day became known as Black Sunday.

An Associated Press reporter, Robert Geiger, and a photographer, Harry Eisenhard, were caught in the storm six miles from Boise City. The next day Geiger wrote a news story that began, "Residents of the southwestern dust bowl marked up another black duster today." A duster was a dust storm. His story is generally accepted as the first use of the words "dust bowl."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why natural gas smells: New London, 1937" -->
A gas explosion destroyed a school in New London, Texas, on March 18, 1937, and killed about 298 people, most of them children. Members of the school board had piped in the gas to save money. Early that year, board members canceled the school's contract for natural gas. They had plumbers connect the school to a pipe of leftover waste gas from a nearby company, the Parade Gasoline Company.

Natural gas that has not been treated has no smell and no color. Gas leaked from the new connection and built up in the closed crawlspace, the low space under the floor, which ran the whole 253-foot length of the building's front. Students had been complaining of headaches for some time, but few people paid attention.

About 500 students and 40 teachers were in the building that afternoon. At 3:17 p.m. Lemmie R. Butler, who taught shop, turned on an electric sander. A spark from its switch probably set off the gas. The *Handbook of Texas*, published by the Texas State Historical Association, records about 298 dead. Other counts run from 294 to more than 400, and the exact number is not known.

More than 70 lawsuits were filed. Judge Robert T. Brown dismissed the ones that came to trial, for lack of evidence, and no one was ever found responsible. The school superintendent, W. C. Shaw, whose son was killed, resigned. Texas lawmakers gave the state's Railroad Commission the power to require a chemical with a strong smell in natural gas, so that people can smell a leak. Within weeks of the explosion, that chemical was being mixed into the gas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hindenburg, 1937, and the Cocoanut Grove, 1942" -->
The German passenger airship *Hindenburg* caught fire on May 6, 1937, as it came in to land at the Naval Air Station in Lakehurst, New Jersey. An airship is a giant aircraft held up by a gas lighter than air, with engines to push it and a cabin for passengers. The *Hindenburg* was filled with hydrogen, a gas that burns very easily. Of the 97 people on board, 35 died. A man working on the ground, Allen Hagaman, died too.

Cameramen from four newsreel companies, which made short news films shown in movie theaters, were filming the landing and filmed the fire. None of them caught the moment the fire started. The radio reporter Herbert Morrison recorded what he saw as it happened. What caused the first spark has never been settled. Passenger travel by giant airship ended after the fire.

**The Cocoanut Grove.** Fire spread through the Cocoanut Grove nightclub in Boston at about 10:15 p.m. on November 28, 1942, and killed 492 people. Many of them were trapped behind doors that were locked, hidden or blocked. More than 1,000 people were inside a club that was allowed to hold 460. The owner, Barnett Welansky, had locked some exits, hidden others behind curtains, and bricked one up.

The main entrance was a single revolving door. Bodies piled up on both sides of it and jammed it until it broke. Other doors opened inward, so people could not open them against the crush of the crowd. Fire officials later told the court that if the doors had swung outward, at least 300 lives could have been saved. How the fire started is not known.

Welansky was found guilty of manslaughter and spent nearly four years in prison. Lawmakers then passed new laws that banned decorations that burn easily. The laws required emergency exits to stay unlocked, and a revolving door could no longer be a building's only exit.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Explosions at the docks: Port Chicago, 1944, and Texas City, 1947" -->
Two ships being loaded with ammunition blew up at the Port Chicago Naval Magazine in California, a Navy base for storing explosives, on July 17, 1944. The blast killed 320 men, and 202 of them were Black sailors. Navy officers had given Black sailors the job of loading the ships without enough training or equipment.

**Texas City.** A fire broke out in the cargo of the French ship *Grandcamp* at the docks of Texas City, Texas, on the morning of April 16, 1947. The ship carried about 2,300 tons of ammonium nitrate, a chemical used as fertilizer that can explode.

The ship's captain ordered his crew not to use water on the fire, so that the cargo would not be ruined. The crew closed the hatches and pumped in steam. The unusual smoke drew a crowd of about 500 people to the shore to watch, many of them children.

At 9:12 a.m. the ship exploded. Flying pieces of metal set fire to oil refineries and chemical tanks along the waterfront. The Monsanto chemical plant beside the ship took the worst of the blast, and seven broken oil tanks at the Republic Refinery caught fire. Another ship, the *High Flyer*, which carried 961 tons of ammonium nitrate, blew up at 1:10 the next morning.

At least 581 people died. The first explosion killed 26 of the town's volunteer firefighters, all but one member of the fire department, and destroyed all of the city's firefighting equipment. The anchor monument in Texas City lists 576 people known to have died. Sixty-three of them were buried without names.

More than 8,000 people sued the United States government. They used a 1946 law that let people sue the government for harm caused by the carelessness of its workers. In 1953 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled against them, 4 to 3. In 1955 members of Congress voted to pay the claims anyway. The government paid 1,394 awards, worth nearly 17 million dollars in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1950" -->
**If you remember one thing:** People's choices turned fires, floods and gas leaks into mass deaths in these fifty years, and of all the owners and officials blamed, one went to prison: Barnett Welansky, who locked the Cocoanut Grove's exits.
In 1900: Galveston had no seawall, and natural gas had no smell to warn anyone of a leak.
In 1950: Galveston stood behind a seawall 17 feet high, Texas officials could require a strong smell in natural gas, and a revolving door could no longer be a building's only exit.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 17 minutes**

Hurricane Audrey killed at least 416 people on the Louisiana coast in June 1957. Warnings got faster after 1950, and help got organized. Weather Bureau forecasters began giving hurricanes human names in 1953. In 1979 President Jimmy Carter put much of the national government's disaster work under one new office, the Federal Emergency Management Agency, called FEMA.

Choices made by owners and engineers still killed hundreds of people. A coal company's waste dam in West Virginia broke in 1972 and killed 125. Two hotel walkways in Kansas City fell in 1981 and killed 114, after an engineer approved a design change without checking the math. News reporters broadcast a rescue from a frozen river in Washington, D.C., live as it happened. After Hurricane Andrew in 1992, Florida got a single building code, a set of rules for how strong buildings must be.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricanes with names: Audrey, 1957, and Camille, 1969" -->
By the time Hurricane Audrey struck in 1957, hurricanes had names. In 1953 forecasters of the U.S. Weather Bureau began giving human names to storms. In 1956 Weather Bureau officials opened a hurricane center in Miami.

Forecasters warned the whole Louisiana coast at 10 a.m. on June 26, 1957, that a hurricane was coming. About 75,000 people left. Audrey came ashore the next day near Cameron, about 24 hours after the warning. The storm had sped up and arrived sooner than forecasters or residents expected.

Its storm surge, the wall of seawater that a hurricane's winds push onto land, rose as high as 12 feet. The water did damage as far as 25 miles inland. At least 416 people died, most of them in Cameron Parish. The final count may never be known.

**Camille.** Hurricane Camille came ashore near Bay St. Louis, Mississippi, late on August 17, 1969, with winds of 175 miles an hour. The water rose 24.6 feet at Pass Christian, Mississippi. Camille killed 172 people in Mississippi and 9 in Louisiana. As the storm moved north, its rain caused floods in Virginia that killed more than 153 people there. At least 343 people died in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Alaska earthquake, March 27, 1964" -->
The strongest earthquake ever recorded in North America struck southern Alaska at 5:36 p.m. on March 27, 1964. Most of the people it killed drowned in the giant waves it caused. The earthquake had a magnitude of 9.2 and lasted four minutes and 38 seconds.

The shaking and underwater landslides sent tsunamis, giant waves set off by an earthquake or a landslide under the sea, into the coast. Scientists of the U.S. Geological Survey count 131 dead: 122 killed by the tsunamis and 9 by the shaking. Other counts reach 139. At Valdez, 32 people died when the town's harbor and docks collapsed. Tsunamis also reached Oregon and California. In Crescent City, California, one killed 10 people.

**Chenega.** Chenega was a village of Chugach Alutiiq people, also called Sugpiaq, on an island in Prince William Sound. Their people had lived there since before Russians came to Alaska. About four minutes after the shaking began, a wave 27 feet high struck the village. It knocked down every building except the school and one house.

Between 23 and 26 of the roughly 70 people who lived there died, by different counts. Scientists of the Geological Survey found that Chenega lost a larger share of its people than any other town. The survivors outran the wave and climbed to high ground.

Rescuers took the survivors to Cordova. Officials of the federal Bureau of Indian Affairs later moved them for good to the village of Tatitlek. In 1971 members of Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. Under it, Alaska Natives received 44 million acres and $962.5 million, held by companies that Alaska Natives own. Former Chenega residents formed one of them, the Chenega Corporation, and won the right to choose 76,093 acres of land. People began living in a new village, Chenega Bay, in 1984.

President Lyndon Johnson declared all of Alaska a disaster area the day after the earthquake. Army engineers of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers led the work of rebuilding roads, clearing wreckage and laying out new town sites, at a cost of 110 million dollars. The people of Valdez moved their town to higher ground four miles to the west. In 1967 federal scientists of the Coast and Geodetic Survey opened a tsunami warning center at Palmer, Alaska.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Floods of 1972: Buffalo Creek and Rapid City" -->
The Pittston Company's coal waste dam above Buffalo Creek, West Virginia, broke at about 8 a.m. on February 26, 1972, and the flood killed 125 people. The Buffalo Mining Company, part of Pittston, owned and ran three dams made of coal waste on the creek's Middle Fork.

The third dam sat on a layer of soft coal mud instead of solid rock. Neither Pittston nor the mine's earlier owner had sent the dams' plans to the state for approval. Four days before the flood, a federal mine inspector called the dam "satisfactory."

The dam gave way and let out 132 million gallons of water, coal waste and mud. The flood ran down the narrow valley through 16 coal towns. It killed whole families, injured about 1,000 people, and destroyed 507 houses and 44 mobile homes. About 4,000 of the 5,000 people who lived along the creek lost their homes.

**"An act of God."** Pittston officials blamed three days of rain and called the flood "an act of God," meaning a natural event that no person could have prevented. Members of the governor's commission that investigated wrote that "the Pittston Company, through its officials, has shown flagrant disregard for the safety of residents of Buffalo Creek," meaning that its officials had openly ignored the residents' safety.

Members of a separate commission formed by citizens said the company was guilty of murdering at least 124 people. The records show no Pittston official charged with a crime.

Pittston paid 13.5 million dollars in 1974 to settle a lawsuit by survivors and family members. One count gives 645 of them, and another gives 625. That came to about 13,000 dollars each after the lawyers were paid. Governor Moore settled the state's own lawsuit in 1977 for 1 million dollars. Members of Congress and West Virginia lawmakers passed new laws on how such dams must be built and kept up.

**Rapid City.** Less than four months later, on June 9 and 10, 1972, about 15 inches of rain fell on part of the Black Hills of South Dakota. Rapid Creek flooded Rapid City, where people had built houses and businesses on the creek's flood plain, the low land beside a river that the river covers when it floods. The flood killed 238 people. Afterward, new zoning rules set what could be built where, and most of the flood plain was rebuilt as parks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="FEMA and Three Mile Island, 1979" -->
President Jimmy Carter put much of the national government's disaster work under one new office in 1979, the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA. Before then, officials of more than 100 separate federal offices could each claim a part in the response to a single disaster. On April 1, 1979, Carter signed two executive orders, written orders from the president, that started FEMA. In July he signed another order that moved disaster relief into it.

Members of Congress passed the Stafford Act in 1988. It set the rules for when a president may declare a disaster, so that federal money and help can be sent.

**Three Mile Island.** Four days before FEMA started, the worst accident in the history of the country's nuclear power plants began at Three Mile Island, near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania. A nuclear reactor makes heat from uranium fuel in its core, and water must keep flowing around the core to cool it.

At 4 a.m. on March 28, 1979, a small fault in the cooling system of the Unit 2 reactor set off a chain of failures. A relief valve stuck open and let cooling water escape. The operators had no instrument that showed them the valve was open. They thought the system held too much water, so they cut the flow of new water. The core overheated, and at least 45 percent of it melted.

The operators at the plant, officials of Metropolitan Edison, the power company that ran it, and state and federal officials gave the public unclear and conflicting reports. Station manager Gary Miller declared an emergency at the plant. Federal nuclear officials had no power to order people to leave.

On March 30, Governor Richard Thornburgh advised pregnant women and small children within five miles to leave. He told people within ten miles to stay indoors. About 140,000 people left on their own. In at least 12 studies since 1981, scientists have found no deaths and no health harm that can be measured from the radiation that escaped. A commission of 12 members, led by John G. Kemeny, reported on the accident on October 31, 1979.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mount St. Helens, 1980, and the Hyatt Regency walkways, 1981" -->
Mount St. Helens, a volcano in Washington State, blew out its north side at 8:32 a.m. on May 18, 1980, and killed about 57 people. An earthquake loosened the side of the mountain, and the whole weakened north face slid away. Hot gas and shattered rock blasted north toward Spirit Lake. The blast turned hundreds of square miles into wasteland. Most of the people who died were suffocated, and a few died of burns.

Among the dead were Harry R. Truman, who owned a lodge at Spirit Lake, the photographers Reid Blackburn and Robert Landsburg, and David Johnston, a volcano scientist of the U.S. Geological Survey. Johnston was on duty at a lookout post about six miles north of the mountain.

**The walkways.** Two walkways fell onto a crowd in the lobby of the Hyatt Regency Hotel in Kansas City, Missouri, on the evening of Friday, July 17, 1981. Engineering mistakes caused the fall. About 1,600 people had come to a dance in the lobby. The two walkways hung over the lobby. They killed 114 people and injured 216.

Engineers at Jack D. Gillum and Associates, the firm of the engineer Jack D. Gillum, designed the walkways. Their first design was already too weak. It could hold only 60 percent of the weight that Kansas City's building code required.

Then the makers of the steel parts, at a company called Havens Steel, proposed a change in how the walkways hung. The engineer Daniel M. Duncan accepted the change in a phone call without doing the math to check it. The change doubled the load on the walkway beams on the fourth floor. Investigators found that the walkways would have failed under one third of the weight they held that night.

Members of a Missouri state board found the engineers to blame for gross negligence, which means a serious failure to take the care their job required. They also found them guilty of misconduct and unprofessional conduct, meaning they had broken the rules of their profession. Gillum's company lost its engineering licenses in Missouri, Kansas and Texas. The engineers were cleared of the crimes they were first charged with, and no one was convicted of a crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Air Florida Flight 90, January 13, 1982" -->
Air Florida Flight 90 took off from Washington National Airport on the snowy afternoon of January 13, 1982, with snow and ice on its wings. It crashed into the Potomac River. The captain, Larry Wheaton, 34, and the first officer, Roger Pettit, 31, had not turned on the engines' ice protection. The jet hit the 14th Street Bridge, struck seven cars with people in them, and fell through the ice of the river. Of the 79 people on the plane, 74 died. Four drivers on the bridge died too.

**In the water.** Six people held on to the wreck in water of about 34 degrees. The rescuers who reached the riverbank had no equipment that could reach them. Roger Olian, the boss of the sheet-metal workers at St. Elizabeths Hospital, was the first to jump into the water to try. A U.S. Park Police helicopter arrived at about 4:20 p.m. and pulled most of the survivors out.

One passenger, Arland D. Williams Jr., 46, was a bank examiner, a person who checks that banks follow the rules. He was strapped into the wreckage and could not free himself. Twice he passed the rescue line to others. When the helicopter crew came back for him, the wreckage had rolled and he was under the water. The coroner, the official who finds out how people died, found that he was the only passenger who drowned. The bridge span was later renamed for him.

The Carnegie Hero Fund, which gives medals for bravery, says that its staff could not firmly establish who the sixth person in the water was. The coroner and the renamed bridge name Williams.

A passenger, Priscilla Tirado, was badly hurt and blinded by jet fuel, and she was too weak to grab the line when the helicopter came back to her. Lenny Skutnik, an assistant at the Congressional Budget Office, took off his coat and boots, swam out in short sleeves and pulled her to shore. "Somebody had to go into the water," he told *Time* magazine. News reporters recorded the rescue and broadcast it live.

**The cause.** Investigators of the National Transportation Safety Board, the federal office that looks into crashes, found that the pilots caused the crash. The investigators blamed the pilots' failure to use the engines' ice protection and the pilots' decision to take off with snow or ice on the wings. The captain did not listen when the first officer told him the engine readings looked wrong. He did not stop the takeoff while there was still room on the runway. Both pilots died in the crash.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="melvin-windsor" name="Melvin Windsor" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Melvin Windsor

> **Who:** A U.S. Park Police officer who stood on a helicopter's skid over the frozen Potomac River to pull a survivor out.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., January 13, 1982.

**Patricia Felch had been in the frozen river for 30 minutes.**

Melvin E. "Gene" Windsor, 41, pulled the last survivor of Air Florida Flight 90 out of the Potomac. He did it standing on one of the helicopter's skids, the long metal bars it lands on, with no safety line. He had joined the Park Police in 1971, and by 1982 he was a rescue technician.

He flew with the chief pilot, Donald W. Usher, 31. Earlier that day Usher had decided that no routine flights would go up. At about 4:09 p.m. a radio call came in. They took off at 4:16 and reached the river at about 4:20. Usher steered by watching the highway below through the windows in the helicopter's floor.

The people holding on to the wreck were in water up to their shoulders and necks. Windsor dropped life jackets and lines to them while Usher flew so low that the skids went under the water.

**The last one out.** The last survivor out of the water was Patricia Felch. She had been in the river for 30 minutes. Windsor unbuckled his seat belt and stepped out onto the right skid, which was under the water. He grabbed her with one arm and held the back of her head with the other.

Of the passenger who twice passed the line to others, Windsor told *Time*: "In a mass casualty, you'll find people like him, but I've never seen one with that commitment." A mass casualty is an event in which many people are hurt or killed at once. Windsor, Usher, Skutnik and Olian each received the Carnegie Medal for bravery. Windsor served 22 years with the Park Police.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="melvin-windsor" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricane Hugo and the Loma Prieta earthquake, 1989" -->
Hurricane Hugo hit two U.S. territories before it reached the mainland in September 1989. On St. Croix in the U.S. Virgin Islands, it damaged more than 90 percent of the buildings and killed three people. In Puerto Rico it killed eight people and left nearly 28,000 without homes. On September 22 it came ashore on Sullivan's Island, South Carolina. The storm surge reached 20.2 feet near McClellanville, and 13 people died in South Carolina.

**Loma Prieta.** An earthquake of magnitude 6.9 struck about 10 miles northeast of Santa Cruz, California, at 5:04 p.m. on October 17, 1989, and killed 63 people. A World Series baseball game was about to begin at Candlestick Park in San Francisco.

In Oakland, the upper deck of the Cypress Street Viaduct fell onto the lower deck, and 42 people were crushed to death in their cars. The viaduct was a two-level section of the Nimitz Freeway, a highway raised above the ground. A section of the San Francisco-Oakland Bay Bridge also fell, and a 23-year-old woman, Anamafi Moala, died there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why help was slow after Hurricane Andrew, 1992" -->
Hurricane Andrew struck Florida on August 24, 1992, and destroyed more than 63,500 houses. Help from the national government was slow to come. Andrew came ashore at Homestead with winds as high as 165 miles an hour. It damaged more than 124,000 other houses and wrecked 99 percent of the mobile homes in Homestead. It killed 44 people in Florida. Fifteen died in the storm itself and 29 from its effects afterward. In all, the storm left 65 people dead.

**"Where in the hell is the cavalry?"** Three days after the storm, Dade County's emergency management director, Kate Hale, went on national television. "Where in the hell is the cavalry on this one?" she asked. "They keep saying we're going to get supplies. For God's sake, where are they?" The cavalry were soldiers on horseback, and she meant the rescuers and supplies that had not come.

Many people criticized FEMA for the slow start. Some critics blamed Florida's governor, Lawton Chiles, instead. FEMA officials believed they could not act until he sent a formal request for federal help. By that account, he waited five days to send it.

Engineers who surveyed the damaged roofs found that concrete roof tiles had been glued to felt paper and shingles stapled on the wrong way. A single building code for the whole state of Florida was set up in 1998, and it took effect in 2002.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Northridge, 1994, and the Chicago heat wave, 1995" -->
The Northridge earthquake, magnitude 6.7, struck Los Angeles at 4:30 a.m. on January 17, 1994. The official count of the dead is 57, and other counts run from 60 to 72. Sixteen people died when the Northridge Meadows apartment building collapsed. Buildings with parking on the bottom floor held up poorly in the shaking.

**The heat wave.** A heat wave in Chicago in mid-July 1995 killed 739 more people in one week than usually die in a week. Most of the dead were poor, old people. Many had no air conditioning, or had it and could not afford to run it. Many kept their windows shut and would not sleep outside, because they were afraid of crime. Black residents were more likely to die than white residents.

City officials did not issue a heat emergency warning until the last day of the heat wave. The records do not say who made that choice. Few people used the city's five cooling centers.

The morgue run by the medical examiner, Edmund Donoghue, a doctor who examines the dead to find out how they died, could hold 300 bodies, and it filled. Refrigerated trucks were parked outside to hold the rest.

Donoghue said later that his office was not allowed to hold press conferences, and that Mayor Richard M. Daley was not told until the weekend. A later study counted 692 deaths above normal over the whole summer, from June 21 to August 10. That is fewer than the worst week's 739, because some of the people the heat killed would have died later that summer anyway, the study found. In August, 41 victims whose bodies no one claimed were buried together in one grave in Homewood, Illinois.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 20 minutes**

Ethel Freeman, 91, died in her wheelchair outside the New Orleans Convention Center in September 2005, waiting for a bus that police had said would come.

In the disasters after 2000, many of the dead were old or sick, like her, and many died in the days and months after the storm or the fire, waiting for help, for electricity or for a warning. Hurricane Katrina killed 1,392 people in 2005, by the National Hurricane Center's count as corrected in 2023. Most of them died in Louisiana, many of them in New Orleans, where floodwalls broke. Investigators found that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers was responsible for those floodwalls.

After Hurricane Maria struck Puerto Rico in 2017, officials of the island's government counted 64 dead. In 2018 they raised the official count to 2,975, after a study of deaths in the months after the storm. Power lines owned by utility companies started deadly fires in California in 2018 and 2025 and in Hawaii in 2023. In late September 2026, members of Congress were still debating whether to keep FEMA.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="September 11, 2001, and the illnesses afterward" -->
Three hundred forty-three members of the New York City Fire Department died in the attacks on the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001, and by 2026 more of its members had died from breathing the dust of the ruins.

That morning 19 hijackers from the group al-Qaeda took over four passenger jets by force. They flew two into the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City and one into the Pentagon, near Washington, D.C. On the fourth plane, United Airlines Flight 93, passengers and crew fought back, and it crashed into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania.

The attacks killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 attackers. Seventy-one police and other law officers also died in New York.

**The dust.** For months afterward, firefighters, police officers, construction workers and volunteers dug through the ruins. They breathed dust of crushed concrete, glass fibers, asbestos, lead and mercury. Asbestos is a mineral whose tiny fibers lodge in the lungs and can cause cancer years later. Fires in the ruins burned for three months.

Many of the workers later got cancer or lung disease. In September 2026 leaders of the Fire Department reported that 453 more of its members had died of illnesses tied to the dust. That is more than the 343 it lost on the day of the attacks.

In December 2010 members of Congress passed the James Zadroga 9/11 Health and Compensation Act. President Barack Obama signed it on January 2, 2011. It set up the World Trade Center Health Program, which gives medical checkups and treatment to responders and survivors with illnesses tied to the attacks. Members of Congress renewed it in December 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricane Katrina, August 29, 2005" -->
Most of the people Hurricane Katrina killed died in Louisiana, where New Orleans flooded after its floodwalls broke. On August 28, 2005, Mayor Ray Nagin ordered the first mandatory evacuation in the city's history, meaning everyone was ordered to leave. Governor Kathleen Blanco opened all lanes of the highways to traffic leaving the city, and about one million people left. Between 100,000 and 150,000 people stayed in the city. City officials opened the Superdome stadium as a "refuge of last resort," a place to go when there was nowhere else.

Katrina came ashore on August 29. The levees and floodwalls broke in more than 50 places, and water covered 80 percent of the city. In the Lower Ninth Ward, a neighborhood where most homeowners were Black, the water was more than 10 feet deep.

**Who built the floodwalls.** Members of Congress had put the Corps of Engineers in charge of the city's flood protection in a law of 1965. Investigators found that the Corps was responsible for the floodwalls that broke, and that the flood protection system the national government built had engineering flaws. Judges of a federal appeals court later ruled that, under a law of 1928, the Corps could not be made to pay for the damage.

Scientists at the National Hurricane Center lowered their count of the dead from 1,833 to 1,392 in 2023, after new studies. Other studies found that most of the dead in New Orleans were old people who lived near the broken levees, in the Lower Ninth Ward and Lakeview neighborhoods.

**Waiting.** Coast Guard crews rescued more than 33,500 of the roughly 60,000 people stranded in the city. People waited for days at the Convention Center, which had no food, water or medical help. Early news reports of mass crime at the Superdome later turned out to be false or exaggerated.

Federal officials did not begin to act in earnest to help the people at the Convention Center until September 2. Michael Brown, the head of FEMA, lost charge of the relief work to Vice Admiral Thad Allen and resigned on September 12.

**Guns on the bridges.** Some police officers turned their guns on survivors. On September 1, police from the city of Gretna and from the Crescent City Connection bridge police, with deputies of the Jefferson Parish sheriff, blocked the bridge over the Mississippi River. They turned back people walking out of New Orleans at gunpoint. Gretna's police chief, Arthur Lawson, made that decision with the sheriff's office and the bridge police.

On September 4, New Orleans police officers shot six unarmed Black people on the Danziger Bridge. They killed James Brissette, 17, and Ronald Madison, 40, a man with a mental disability. Madison was shot seven times, five times in the back. Among the officers were Kenneth Bowen, Robert Gisevius, Anthony Villavaso and Robert Faulcon. Police officers then tried to cover up the killings. In 2016 five former officers pleaded guilty and were sentenced to between 3 and 12 years in prison.

**Memorial Medical Center.** Patients, visitors and workers were trapped in Memorial Medical Center, a New Orleans hospital, during the flood. After the storm, 45 bodies were found there. In July 2006, the office of Charles Foti, Louisiana's attorney general and top lawyer, finished an investigation of nearly a year. A judge then ordered the arrest of a doctor, Anna Pou, and two nurses, Lori Budo and Cheri Landry, for murder, which Louisiana law called second degree murder.

They were accused of giving deadly doses of morphine and midazolam to four patients: Emmett Everett Sr., 61, Hollis Alford, 66, Ireatha Watson, 89, and Rose Savoie, 90. Morphine is a strong painkiller, and midazolam is a drug that makes people calm and sleepy. In large doses, both slow and then stop a person's breathing. In 2007 a grand jury refused to charge any of the three. The state later paid Pou's legal bills of more than 450,000 dollars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="herbert-freeman-jr" name="Herbert Freeman Jr." movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Herbert Freeman Jr.

> **Who:** A New Orleans man who took his 91-year-old mother, Ethel Freeman, out of their flooded home by boat, then stayed beside her body outside the Convention Center after she died waiting for help.
> **When and where:** New Orleans, August 31 to early September 2005.

**He borrowed a friend's boat to get his mother out.**

Herbert Freeman Jr. rescued his mother from their flooded house, and she died the next day outside the New Orleans Convention Center, where police had sent them to wait for a bus. Ethel Mayo Freeman was 91. She had a pacemaker for her heart and a feeding tube, and she used a wheelchair. She was often sick, and she decided to stay home when Katrina came.

The floodwater filled her house several feet deep. On August 31, Herbert, 58, borrowed a friend's boat. He put his mother in her wheelchair, lifted her into the boat and took her to dry ground. New Orleans police officers there sent them to the Convention Center. Herbert told officers his mother needed a doctor. They told him a bus would come.

**At the Convention Center.** The Convention Center had no food, no water, no medical help and no way out. "She was calling out for a doctor or a nurse, but there was nothing there," Herbert said. Ethel Freeman died at the Convention Center on September 1. Her body stayed outside for days, slumped in the chair and covered with a poncho. Pictures of it were shown on national television.

Herbert watched over his mother's body for nearly four days, until he was ordered onto a bus. The NBC News report of his story does not say who gave the order. Before he left, he put a note with his name and phone number in her pocket. It took him seven weeks to find her body, at a morgue in St. Gabriel, Louisiana. Her funeral was held in New Orleans in November 2005.

He and the families of two other people who died sued the national government. In 2009 federal appeals judges ruled that, under the Stafford Act, the government could not be sued over decisions like these in a disaster, and they threw out the case.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="herbert-freeman-jr" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many died after Hurricane Maria, 2017?" -->
Hurricane Maria left all 3.4 million people in Puerto Rico without electricity on September 20, 2017, and thousands died in the months that followed. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, and its people are U.S. citizens. Maria struck two weeks after Hurricane Irma crossed the region. It caused the worst blackout in U.S. history. Some customers did not get their power back until August 2018, 11 months later.

After Irma, FEMA officials had shipped many of the supplies they kept in Puerto Rico to the U.S. Virgin Islands. After Maria, they admitted to failures in moving supplies, too few staff and poor communication.

The mayor of San Juan, Carmen Yulín Cruz, spoke of the national government's relief work on September 29, 2017: "We are dying and you are killing us with the inefficiency." On October 3, President Donald Trump compared the count of dead at that time, 16, with "a real catastrophe like Katrina."

**How many died.** Officials of Governor Ricardo Rosselló's government counted 64 dead for months. Researchers found that many more people than usual died in the months after the storm. People who needed dialysis, a treatment that cleans the blood of people whose kidneys have failed, could not reach emergency care for long stretches of time.

Researchers at George Washington University estimated between 2,658 and 3,290 extra deaths from September 2017 to February 2018. On August 28, 2018, Rosselló's officials accepted their study and raised the official count to 2,975. A Harvard University study in 2018 estimated 4,645 extra deaths from September to December 2017, with a very wide range, from 793 to 8,498.

Trump wrote on Twitter on September 13, 2018, that "3000 people did not die" and blamed Democrats for the number. The official count of 2,975, based on the George Washington University study, has stood since August 28, 2018.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jesse-vazquez" name="Jesse Vazquez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jesse Vazquez

> **Who:** A retired veteran in Bayamón, Puerto Rico, who lived for months without electricity after Hurricane Maria with his 89-year-old mother, Mercedes Mercado.
> **When and where:** Calle Alameda, Bayamón, Puerto Rico, September to December 2017.

**His children flew in from New York with a generator.**

Jesse Vazquez and his mother, Mercedes Mercado, went 96 days without electricity after Hurricane Maria. Maria cut the power to their house on Calle Alameda in Bayamón on September 20, 2017. For weeks the family ran electric fans and charged their phones from the battery of Jesse's car.

A small generator cost about 600 dollars on sale, more than Jesse could afford. In October his son, also named Jesse, and his daughter, Megan, flew in from New York to bring him a generator. "It's basically cheaper to buy a flight, get a generator and bring it down here," his son said. Running it five hours a day took about 80 dollars of gasoline a month.

Exhaust from a neighbor's generator blew into the family's living room. Megan said they needed a detector for carbon monoxide, a poison gas in engine exhaust that has no smell.

By Christmas the house had been without power for 96 days. Jesse and his mother used the generator to run the refrigerator, and fans at night so they could sleep. A FEMA inspector told Jesse he could not get help to fix his damaged roof. "It's kind of humiliating," he said. With his son's help he bought four solar panels. He and his mother decided to stay in Puerto Rico.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jesse-vazquez" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**By 2018:** Puerto Rico's officials had raised the count of Maria's dead from 64 to 2,975, and federal judges had thrown out the lawsuit that Herbert Freeman Jr. and two other Katrina families brought against the national government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Floods: Harvey, 2017, Helene, 2024, and the Texas Hill Country, 2025" -->
Hurricane Harvey struck Texas in late August 2017 and dropped more rain than any other hurricane or tropical storm in U.S. records. Scientists at the National Hurricane Center recorded 60.58 inches near Nederland, Texas. Rescuers made more than 17,000 rescues. Their report records at least 68 people killed by the storm's direct effects in Texas and about 35 more by causes such as electric shock, car crashes and being cut off from medical care.

Hurricane Helene came ashore in Florida, near Perry, late on September 26, 2024. The next day its rain fell on North Carolina and Tennessee. Busick, North Carolina, got 30.78 inches of rain, and the rain set off more than 2,000 landslides. Scientists at the National Hurricane Center count at least 250 dead in the United States, 107 of them in North Carolina.

**The plastics factory.** In Erwin, Tennessee, six people died at the Impact Plastics factory as the Nolichucky River rose on September 27. The flood swept 11 people away, and five were rescued. A worker who survived and the families of the dead spoke out. They said the workers were not told they could leave until floodwater had covered the road to the plant and the power had gone out. Five employees and a contractor who cleaned the offices were killed.

Inspectors of Tennessee's workplace safety agency found in April 2025 that the company had "exercised reasonable diligence," meaning it had taken reasonable care, and that the deaths were not work-related. They issued no citations, which are official notices that a company broke a safety rule. District Attorney General Steven Finney filed no charges against the company's president, Gerald O'Connor, in July 2025, after a review by the Tennessee Bureau of Investigation.

The investigators said they found no evidence that workers were told they could not leave or that they would be fired for leaving, and that the workers had a little more than an hour to get out. The family of Johnny Peterson, one of the dead, sued the company.

**Camp Mystic.** As much as 20 inches of rain fell on the Texas Hill Country early on July 4, 2025, and the Guadalupe River rose about 26 feet in 45 minutes. The flood killed 136 people, 117 of them in Kerr County, by counts reported in July 2026. At Camp Mystic, a Christian summer camp for girls, 25 campers, two counselors, Chloe Childress and Katherine Ferruzzo, and the camp's owner and director, Dick Eastland, died.

The first flash flood warning for Kerr County went out at 1:14 a.m. At 4:03 a.m. a flash flood emergency, a more urgent alert, told people in the towns of Hunt and Ingram to "seek higher ground now!"

Kerr County had no warning system built just for floods. In 2016 the county sheriff had asked for one, with sirens. County Commissioner H. A. "Buster" Baldwin said, "I think this whole thing is a little extravagant for Kerr County, with sirens and such."

After appeals from the camp, FEMA officials took 15 of its buildings on the Guadalupe River off the federal map of land likely to flood in 2013. They took 15 buildings at a newer camp site off the map in 2019 and 2020, the Associated Press reported.

Texas lawmakers passed new camp safety laws in September 2025, at an extra meeting called a special session, among them the Heaven's 27 Camp Safety Act. On June 18, 2026, members of the Texas Senate and House investigating committees released a report. The lawmakers found that the camp had no written emergency plans, which state rules required. They also found that the camp's leaders did not move the campers out in time even though they had chances to.

By July 2026 Kerr County had six new sirens. In the week before July 18, when floods came to the Hill Country again, officials sounded three of them to warn people, an official of the Upper Guadalupe River Authority said.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fires started by power lines: Paradise, 2018, Lahaina, 2023, and Los Angeles, 2025" -->
A single metal hook broke on a power line tower owned by Pacific Gas and Electric, called PG&E, on the morning of November 8, 2018. The tower was part of a line built in the early 1900s, and it was poorly maintained. The line started a fire. The Camp Fire burned through the town of Paradise, California. It killed 85 people and destroyed more than 18,000 buildings. On June 16, 2020, the company pleaded guilty in court to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter, the crime of killing people through carelessness.

**Lahaina.** A wildfire destroyed the town of Lahaina on the island of Maui, Hawaii, on August 8, 2023, and killed 102 people. That is the most people killed in a U.S. wildfire since 1918. High winds knocked down power lines of Hawaiian Electric, and the lines started a fire that morning. Maui firefighters declared that fire under control and left. Less than an hour later, embers from it started the fire again.

Firefighters lost water pressure in the hydrants. Many people died in their cars. Some jumped into the ocean to escape the flames.

The fire destroyed a town that had been the capital of the Kingdom of Hawaii for 35 years. It burned the homes of many Native Hawaiian families who could afford to live in Lahaina only because they had inherited houses that were already paid for.

Maui has more than 80 outdoor warning sirens, and none of them sounded. Herman Andaya, the head of the Maui County Emergency Management Agency, said the sirens would have sent people toward the mountains and into the fire. He resigned the next day.

In an October 2024 report, federal fire investigators and the Maui Fire Department called the fire accidental, meaning no one set it on purpose. They found that the power lines brought down by the wind had started it, but they did not hold any person or company responsible.

Under a settlement of the survivors' lawsuits, Hawaiian Electric, Maui County and others agreed to pay 4 billion dollars. As of August 2026, none of that money had been paid, because of a court fight over the lawyers' fees, the *Honolulu Star-Advertiser* reported.

**Los Angeles.** Fires pushed by winds of up to 100 miles an hour burned parts of Los Angeles in January 2025. They killed at least 31 people and destroyed more than 18,000 homes and other buildings. The Eaton Fire in Altadena killed 19 people. In August 2026 Los Angeles County and state fire investigators reported that it started from electric sparks on an unused Southern California Edison tower.

Seventeen of the 19 died in west Altadena, a neighborhood with a long history as a Black community. Reviewers from the firm Citygate Associates found in May 2026 that county officials' orders to leave reached west Altadena hours after the fire turned toward it. The records do not name the officials who made those decisions. The dead had an average age of 77. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, opened an investigation into whether race, age or disability affected how officials warned people.

The Palisades Fire killed 12 people. In October 2025 Jonathan Rinderknecht, 29, was arrested and charged with starting it. Prosecutors say he set a small fire on January 1, and that strong winds rekindled it, or started it burning again, on January 7 as the Palisades Fire. Firefighters had thought the January 1 fire was out. A Los Angeles Fire Department battalion commander ordered crews to leave that site, although they reported that the ground was still burning slowly.

His first trial ended in June 2026 when the jury could not agree: ten jurors voted not guilty and two voted guilty. Prosecutors charged him again with arson, the crime of setting a fire on purpose, and he pleaded not guilty. As of October 26, 2026, his second trial had not begun, and he had not been convicted of any crime.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cold, a collapse and FEMA's future: 2021 to 2026" -->
A winter storm struck Texas in February 2021, and more than 4.5 million homes and businesses lost power, some of them for days. Officials of the Texas Department of State Health Services counted 246 deaths from the storm. Almost two-thirds of the dead died of hypothermia, which means the body gets so cold that it stops working. Some estimates run as high as 702 deaths. Cristian Pavón Pineda, 11, died of carbon monoxide poisoning in his family's mobile home in Conroe, Texas. The home had no power.

**Surfside.** Part of Champlain Towers South, a 12-story apartment building in Surfside, Florida, fell down at 1:22 a.m. on June 24, 2021, and killed 98 people. For years, water had seeped into the concrete and steel supports of the parking garage under the pool deck, and the steel had rusted.

Engineers of the firm Morabito Consultants reported the damage in 2018. They warned that if the waterproofing was not replaced soon, the damage to the concrete would spread fast. Each family owned its own apartment, and the owners together had a board. On April 9, 2021, Jean Wodnicki, the president of that board, wrote to the residents that "the concrete deterioration is accelerating," meaning the concrete was breaking down faster. A 15-million-dollar repair had been approved, but the main work had not started.

**FEMA's future.** President Trump called for getting rid of FEMA. On February 17, 2025, officials of the Trump administration fired more than 200 FEMA workers. On March 24, 2025, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem said she would move to eliminate FEMA. The acting head of FEMA, Cameron Hamilton, told members of Congress: "I do not believe it is in the best interest of the American people to eliminate the Federal Emergency Management Agency." He was removed from his job the next day.

More than 180 current and former FEMA workers signed a public statement of protest in August 2025 that they called the "Katrina Declaration." On May 7, 2026, a review council the president had set up approved a report with ten recommendations. On September 15, 2026, members of a House committee voted for one bill to change FEMA, the Post-Disaster Protection Act. In late September 2026, FEMA still existed, and members of Congress were still debating its future.

**The three questions, answered**
- What rules did Americans make after disasters, and did they work? After many of these disasters someone wrote a rule: Boston's fire wardens in 1711, brick walls in Boston and New Orleans, steamboat inspections, a Weather Bureau in 1890, 36 factory laws after the Triangle fire, a smell in natural gas after New London, and one building code for Florida after Andrew. Some went into use fast: the smell was in natural gas within weeks. Some failed: the 1838 steamboat law had no one to enforce it, a New York law said factory doors must stay unlocked but witnesses said the Triangle owners kept one locked, the *Eastland*'s added lifeboats may have made it more dangerous, and New Orleans's floodwalls broke in 2005. Some are still being tested, such as Kerr County's new flood sirens and FEMA, whose future members of Congress were still debating in late September 2026.
- Was anyone punished when people's choices killed others? Rarely. No one was punished for the *Sultana*, the Johnstown club won in court, a jury found the Triangle owners not guilty, and no Pittston official was charged after Buffalo Creek. Barnett Welansky went to prison for the Cocoanut Grove fire, and PG&E pleaded guilty to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter after the Camp Fire.
- Did everyone get the same help after a disaster? No. Officials paid out London's money for Charleston in 1740 by the size of each loss, so most of it went to rich merchants. Armed men forced Black workers to handle Galveston's dead in 1900, and camp leaders forced Black people to work in the 1927 flood. In 2005 police turned back people walking out of New Orleans at gunpoint, and in 2025 orders to leave reached west Altadena hours late.

**What changed:** Over ten eras, help after a disaster moved from neighbors and churches to public money and a national office, and warnings moved from church bells to the telegraph and sirens. After 2000, FEMA's help was slow at Katrina, its officials admitted failures after Maria, and none of Maui's sirens sounded in 2023.

**Five dates to keep**
- January 26, 1700: The Cascadia fault breaks, and its tsunami reaches Japan the next day.
- May 31, 1889: The South Fork Dam breaks, and 2,208 people die in Johnstown.
- September 8, 1900: A hurricane floods Galveston, the deadliest natural disaster in the country's history.
- March 25, 1911: The Triangle fire kills 146 workers in New York.
- August 29, 2005: Hurricane Katrina comes ashore, and New Orleans floods when its floodwalls break.

**Not known:** No one knows how many people died in the Galveston hurricane of 1900, and the estimates run from 6,000 to 12,000.

**Where to go:** Clara Barton's house in Glen Echo, Maryland, built partly from the lumber of the Red Cross buildings at Johnstown, is now the Clara Barton National Historic Site.
