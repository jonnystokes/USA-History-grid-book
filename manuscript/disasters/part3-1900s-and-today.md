## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, many people died in disasters because of choices other people made. In New York the owners of a blouse factory kept an exit door locked. Near Los Angeles the city's chief engineer and his assistant judged a leaking dam safe, and in Texas members of a school board piped waste gas into a school to save money. After four of these disasters, lawmakers wrote new safety rules for factories, ships, nightclubs and gas lines. The deadliest disaster came first. The Galveston hurricane of September 1900 killed between 6,000 and 12,000 people, more than any other natural disaster in the country's history. After three of these disasters, officials and local leaders helped white survivors first and made Black survivors do the hardest work. In Galveston, armed militia members forced Black men to bury the dead. In the 1927 Mississippi flood, the local leaders who ran many relief camps gave supplies to white people first and forced Black people to work. After the 1928 hurricane in Florida, officials saved the coffins for white victims, and burned the bodies of Black victims in piles or threw them into mass graves.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Galveston, September 8, 1900" -->
A hurricane flooded Galveston, Texas, on September 8, 1900, and killed more people than any other natural disaster in the country's history. Galveston was a port city of about 38,000 people on an island in the Gulf of Mexico. At about 6:30 that evening, a storm wave put the city under as much as 15 feet of water. The highest water mark was 15.7 feet.

Between 6,000 and 8,000 people died in the city. Estimates for the whole island and the nearby coast run from 10,000 to 12,000. In early October the *Galveston News* printed a list that named 4,263 of the dead. The storm destroyed a third of the city. Counts of the homes destroyed run from 2,636 to about 3,600.

Searchers found about 70 bodies a day during the first month. The last body was found on February 10, 1901. Galveston was put under martial law, which means soldiers or armed guards take over from the police and courts. A volunteer militia, a group of armed citizens, patrolled the streets against looters, people who steal from damaged buildings. Some accounts say that men accused of looting were shot, but the records do not give their names or a count.

Too few volunteers came forward to handle the dead, so armed men rounded up other men at gunpoint or bayonet point to do it. The writer Elizabeth Blasius found that members of the volunteer militia did this, and that the men they forced were Black workers. About one fifth of the people of Galveston were Black. Even before the storm, Black Galvestonians were not allowed to drink at the public fountains or to attend the public schools. At first, workers loaded bodies onto barges, tied heavy rocks to them and dropped them into the Gulf. Days later, some of the bodies floated back to shore. After that, workers burned the dead in large fires. The fires burned into November.

Clara Barton, 78, arrived with Red Cross workers on September 17, nine days after the storm. Mayor Walter C. Jones led a Central Relief Committee that included the banker John Sealy. The committee paid for 483 new houses and gave partial help toward 1,114 more. Afterward, workers built a seawall that stood 17 feet above the low-tide line. Its first section ran three and a half miles. Workers then raised the ground under the city by as much as 17 feet. They lifted about 2,000 buildings on jacks first. St. Patrick's Church, which weighed 3,000 tons, went up five feet.

### Isaac Cline

> **Who:** The chief of the U.S. Weather Bureau office in Galveston, whose pregnant wife died in the 1900 hurricane.
> **When and where:** Galveston, Texas, September 8, 1900.

Isaac Cline was Galveston's chief weather forecaster, and nine years before the storm he had written that a hurricane could never do serious harm to the city. He was born near Madisonville, Tennessee, in 1861 and took charge of the Galveston office in 1889. His brother Joseph joined him there in 1892.

In 1891 Cline wrote an article for the *Galveston Daily News*. In it he called the thought of a hurricane seriously harming Galveston "a crazy idea." Many people in the city had asked for a seawall. His article helped stop it from being built.

On September 8, 1900, water began flooding the low parts of the city. Around noon, Cline raised the hurricane warning flags without waiting for approval from the Weather Bureau's main office in Washington. Weather Bureau rules required that approval.

Cline later wrote that he drove a horse-drawn cart along the beach and warned people to leave. No eyewitness ever reported seeing him do it. The writer Erik Larson argued that Cline warned no one before his office sent out its official warning.

Cline's wife, Cora, was pregnant with their fourth child, and she died in the storm. Cline nearly drowned. He saved his youngest daughter, Esther, who was 6. Joseph saved the two older girls, Allie May, 12, and Rosemary, 11.

In 1901 Cline moved to New Orleans. Between 1900 and 1924 he charted the paths of 16 storms. He retired in 1935 and died in 1955, at 93.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="isaac-cline" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="San Francisco, April 18, 1906" -->
Fire did most of the damage after the San Francisco earthquake of April 18, 1906. The earthquake struck at 5:12 in the morning. Scientists measure an earthquake's strength with a number called its magnitude. This one had a magnitude of about 7.9 and shook the city for about 42 seconds. It broke gas pipes, and within three days more than 30 fires had spread across the city. Between 80 and 95 percent of the damage came from the fires.

Firefighters tried to blow up buildings with dynamite to make empty gaps the fire could not cross. They had no training with dynamite, and some of their blasts started new fires. The city's fire chief, Dennis T. Sullivan, had been hurt in the earthquake itself, when a building next door fell onto the fire station where he lived. He died four days later. General Frederick Funston sent in Army soldiers. Some building owners set fire to their own damaged buildings, because their insurance paid for fire damage and not for earthquake damage.

More than 80 percent of the city was destroyed. Between 227,000 and 300,000 people lost their homes, out of about 410,000. The first counts of the dead ran from 375 to more than 500. Those counts left out hundreds of deaths in Chinatown that no one recorded. In 2005 the members of the city's Board of Supervisors voted to accept more than 3,000 as the official count. They voted for a resolution written by the novelist James Dalessandro and the city historian Gladys Hansen.

On the day of the earthquake, Mayor Eugene Schmitz ordered soldiers and police "to kill any and all persons found engaged in Looting." The records do not say how many people they killed. People also accused soldiers themselves of looting.

About 15,000 Chinese people lived in Chinatown, and they lost nearly everything. Even before the earthquake, some city officials had wanted to move the Chinese out of Chinatown to Hunters Point. After it, they tried to use the ruins to take Chinatown's land for business buildings. The Chinese were the only group that officials kept apart in the camps for people who had lost their homes. Army officers moved the Chinese survivors to camps farther and farther from Chinatown. Most Chinese survivors left for Oakland, and only about 400 stayed in the city. The officials' plan to move Chinatown failed. Chinese residents rebuilt Chinatown.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hugh-kwong-liang" name="Hugh Kwong Liang" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hugh Kwong Liang

> **Who:** A 15-year-old in San Francisco's Chinatown who lived through the 1906 earthquake and fire.
> **When and where:** San Francisco, April 1906.

Hugh Kwong Liang was 15 when the earthquake woke him in Chinatown. He is one of the few Chinese survivors whose own account of the earthquake has lasted to today. He remembered seeing "pieces of plaster falling down like water." He ran out into Washington Street, and the building across from his home fell down.

As the fire spread, city officials who were directing the people fleeing told him and his family to go to the open ground of the Presidio. The Presidio was an Army post by the Golden Gate, the entrance to San Francisco Bay. He remembered the moment he left: "I turned away from my dear old Chinatown for the last time."

At the Presidio, Army officers kept the Chinese survivors apart from everyone else. They moved them from camp to camp. The last camp stood in a cold, windy corner of the Presidio near Fort Point.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hugh-kwong-liang" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Triangle fire, March 25, 1911" -->
The owners of the Triangle Waist Company in New York City kept one of its exit doors locked, and on March 25, 1911, a fire there killed 146 workers. The company made women's blouses on the top three floors of the ten-story Asch Building. Most of its workers were young immigrant women, and many were teenagers. The youngest who died, Kate Leone and Rosaria Maltese, were 14.

The fire started on the eighth floor just before the 4:45 p.m. quitting time. Workers ran down the Greene Street stairs, crowded into the elevators, and climbed onto the one fire escape, which collapsed. On the ninth floor, the door to the Washington Place stairs would not open. Other workers testified that the owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, kept it locked so workers could not leave with stolen blouses. Harris later admitted that all the blouses ever stolen were worth less than 25 dollars.

The firefighters' ladders reached only to the seventh floor. Workers trapped on the ninth floor could not get past the fire, and many jumped from the windows. A crowd in the street watched 62 people jump or fall to their deaths. Joseph Flecher, who worked in the company office, watched from the roof and saw his workers "hit the sidewalk spread out and still." On the tenth floor, workers climbed to the roof. A law professor at New York University, Frank Sommer, and his students found ladders that painters had left and set them across to the school's roof. Workers climbed over them to safety.

Blanck and Harris were put on trial for manslaughter, the crime of killing a person without meaning to, through carelessness. Judge Crain told the jury it had to find that the owners knew the door was locked. On December 27, 1911, after less than two hours, the jury found them not guilty. One juror, Victor Steinman, said he believed the door was locked, "but we couldn't find them guilty unless we believed they knew the door was locked." On March 11, 1914, the families in 23 lawsuits against the building's owner settled. They got an average of 75 dollars for each life.

New York lawmakers set up a nine-member Factory Investigating Commission. From 1911 to 1914 they passed 36 new laws to change the state's rules for workplaces. A state law already in force at the time of the fire had required factory doors to open outward where possible and to stay unlocked during working hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kate-alterman" name="Kate Alterman" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kate Alterman

> **Who:** A worker who escaped the Triangle fire and told the jury how another worker, Margaret Schwartz, died at the locked door.
> **When and where:** New York City, March 25, 1911, and the trial in December 1911.

Kate Alterman saw another worker, Margaret Schwartz, catch fire at the locked Washington Place door, and she told the jury what she saw. Margaret died in the fire. Kate worked on the ninth floor. She had lived in Philadelphia for nine years, and she had been in New York and worked at the Triangle factory for four months.

When the fire reached the ninth floor, the two women ran to the Washington Place door. The manager's brother, whose last name was Bernstein, tried it and could not open it. Margaret tried next. Then Kate pulled the handle "in and out, all ways," and it still would not open.

Kate saw Margaret kneel down by the door. She saw the hem of Margaret's dress and the ends of her loose hair start to burn. Margaret screamed, "Open the door! Fire! I am lost, there is fire!"

Kate turned her coat inside out and put it over her head with the fur against her face. She covered her head with a bunch of dresses. Then she ran out through the flames. "The whole door was a flame right to the roof," she said.

At the trial the prosecutor, Charles Bostwick, used her account to show that the locked door caused Margaret Schwartz's death. The owners' lawyer, Max Steuer, made her tell the story again and again. She used almost the same words each time. In this way Steuer made her story seem less believable without ever saying she was lying. She said she told it the same way "because he asked me the very same story over and over."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kate-alterman" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ships: the Titanic, 1912, and the Eastland, 1915" -->
The *Titanic* carried lifeboats for only some of the people on board, and United States senators blamed British officials for that. The *Titanic* was a British passenger ship on its way to New York. It hit an iceberg in the North Atlantic and sank on April 15, 1912. More than 1,500 passengers and crew died. The ship *Carpathia* picked up about 710 survivors.

The ship had too few lifeboats for everyone, but it met the rules of the British Board of Trade, the government office in charge of British ships. Senator William Alden Smith of Michigan led a Senate investigation. It began in New York on April 19, four days after the sinking. The senators found that Captain Edward Smith's "indifference to danger" was one of the direct causes of the deaths. They blamed the Board of Trade's officials for the missing lifeboats. They called for ships to slow down near ice, to carry enough lifeboats for all on board, and to keep a radio operator on duty day and night. In March 1915 members of Congress passed the Seamen's Act, pushed by Senator Robert La Follette of Wisconsin. It required American ships to carry more lifeboats.

On July 24, 1915, the Great Lakes passenger ship *Eastland* rolled over while it was tied to a dock in the Chicago River. It was taking workers of the Western Electric Company and their families to a company picnic in Indiana, and it had already filled to its limit of 2,500 passengers. The ship had tipped badly before, including in 1906. Under the new law, its owners had added a full set of lifeboats so it could carry more people. That added weight may have made the ship more dangerous. At 7:28 a.m. it rolled onto its side and settled on the river bottom, only 20 feet down. In all, 844 people died. Many of the dead were immigrants, and 220 of them were Czech.

A deckhand, Peter Boyle, drowned while saving people. A nurse, Helen Repa, led much of the rescue work on shore. A grand jury is a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence for a trial. An Illinois grand jury charged the president of the steamship company and three other company officers with manslaughter. It charged the captain and the engineer with criminal carelessness. The men were outside Illinois, and a federal judge refused to send them there for trial. The judge found "barely a scintilla of proof," meaning almost no evidence, partly because the four company officers had not been on the ship. None of the men was ever tried in Illinois.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Mississippi flood of 1927" -->
About 637,000 people had to leave their homes in the Mississippi River flood of 1927, and in the relief camps local leaders gave supplies to white people first and forced Black people to work. In the spring the river broke through its levees in at least 145 places. A levee is a wall of earth built along a riverbank to hold the water in. Water covered 27,000 square miles, as deep as 30 feet. About 500 people died.

On April 29 a group of New Orleans bankers arranged to set off about 30 tons of dynamite on the levee at Caernarvon, Louisiana, below their city. They wanted to let the river out there to keep it from badly damaging New Orleans. The water flooded St. Bernard Parish and part of Plaquemines Parish. A parish is what Louisiana calls a county. Blowing up the levee turned out to be unnecessary. The New Orleans businessmen never paid the people whose homes and land they flooded.

About 325,000 people lived in relief camps, and 69 percent of them were Black. Near Greenville, Mississippi, more than 13,000 people escaped to the top of a levee that had held. Many of them were left there for days without food or clean water. Local leaders from the countryside ran many of the camps. They gave supplies and boats to white people first, and Black people got what was left. A Black person got supplies only by giving the name of a white employer. Black people in the camps were forced to work and were not allowed to leave. The records do not name the camp leaders who gave these orders. A white police officer shot and killed a Black man who refused to unload a relief boat at gunpoint. The records do not give the name of the man who was killed. Near Helena, Arkansas, a plantation overseer, the man who bossed the workers, tried to force a Black man, Owen Flemming, to rescue the plantation owner's mules. Flemming killed the overseer. Then he was lynched, which means he was killed by a mob without a trial. The records do not name the men who killed him.

Herbert Hoover, the Secretary of Commerce, led the national government's relief work. A group of Black leaders headed by Robert Russa Moton studied the camps and reported what was happening in them. Hoover asked that their reports be kept out of the newspapers, and they were. After the flood, members of Congress passed the Flood Control Act of 1928.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two disasters of 1928: the St. Francis Dam and Lake Okeechobee" -->
The St. Francis Dam in northern Los Angeles County broke just before midnight on March 12, 1928, the same day its builder had inspected a new leak and judged it safe. William Mulholland was the chief engineer of the city's Bureau of Water Works and Supply. His workers had built the dam from 1924 to 1926. Leaks and cracks showed up in 1927 and 1928. That morning the dam keeper, Tony Harnischfeger, found a new leak. Mulholland and his assistant, Harvey Van Norman, looked at it and decided it was not dangerous.

The dam gave way at about 11:57 that night. Harnischfeger and his family were probably among the first people killed. The flood ran 54 miles to the Pacific Ocean and killed at least 431 people. Among them were 84 workers of the Southern California Edison company at a construction camp beside the river. At the investigation afterward, the *Express* newspaper reported that Mulholland said, "the only ones I envy are those who are dead." The *Los Angeles Times* printed his words as "on an occasion like this I envy the dead."

On April 12, 1928, a coroner's jury, a group of citizens who decide how people died, ruled on the collapse. The jurors found an error in engineering judgment about the rock under the dam, and they put that blame on Mulholland's bureau and its chief engineer. They found a second error, in the rules meant to keep the public safe. They put that blame on three groups: the city's water and power commissioners, the lawmakers of the city and the state, and the public. The jurors wrote that a great dam should never be left "to the sole judgment of one man, no matter how eminent," meaning no matter how famous and respected. They found no crime and recommended that no one be charged. Mulholland's career as chief engineer ended after the dam broke.

Six months later, early on September 17, 1928, a hurricane came ashore near West Palm Beach, Florida. It had already killed 312 people in Puerto Rico. The storm pushed the water of Lake Okeechobee over the low dike at the lake's south end. A dike is a bank of earth built to keep water back. Floods more than 20 feet deep covered the farm towns of Belle Glade, Canal Point, Chosen, Pahokee and South Bay.

At least 2,500 people died in the United States. For many years the official count was the Red Cross estimate of 1,836. About three out of four of the dead were migrant farm workers, people who moved from farm to farm to pick crops. Most of them were Black. Black workers did most of the cleanup after the storm. Officials saved the few coffins they had for the bodies of white people. White victims were buried with a service, in a shared grave at Woodlawn Cemetery in West Palm Beach. The bodies of Black victims were burned in piles or thrown into mass graves. One mass grave at Port Mayaca holds 1,600 bodies. The records do not name the officials who made these decisions.

Robert Hazard of West Palm Beach started a group to win recognition for the Black victims buried in the city's mass grave. The city bought the grave site back in 2000. In 2003 a state historical marker was put up there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black Sunday, April 14, 1935" -->
On April 14, 1935, a wall of blowing dirt swept across the southern Great Plains, with winds of up to 60 miles an hour. It reached Beaver, Oklahoma, at about 4 p.m. and Boise City, Oklahoma, at about 5:15 p.m. For a short time the sky was so black that people could not see their own hands in front of their faces. The day became known as Black Sunday. An Associated Press reporter, Robert Geiger, and a photographer, Harry Eisenhard, were caught in the storm six miles from Boise City. The next day Geiger wrote a news story that began, "Residents of the southwestern dust bowl marked up another black duster today." A duster was a dust storm. His story is generally accepted as the first use of the words "dust bowl."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New London school explosion, March 18, 1937" -->
Gas that members of a school board had piped in to save money blew up a school in New London, Texas, on March 18, 1937, and killed about 298 people, most of them children. Early that year, board members canceled the school's contract for natural gas. They had plumbers connect the school to a pipe of leftover waste gas from a nearby company, the Parade Gasoline Company. Natural gas that has not been treated has no smell and no color. Gas leaked from the new connection and built up in the closed crawlspace, the low space under the floor, which ran the whole 253-foot length of the building's front. Students had been complaining of headaches for some time, but few people paid attention.

About 500 students and 40 teachers were in the building that afternoon. At 3:17 p.m. Lemmie R. Butler, who taught shop, turned on an electric sander. A spark from its switch probably set off the gas. The *Handbook of Texas*, published by the Texas State Historical Association, records about 298 dead. Other counts run from 294 to more than 400, and the exact number is not known.

More than 70 lawsuits were filed. Judge Robert T. Brown dismissed the ones that came to trial, for lack of evidence, and no one was ever found responsible. The school superintendent, W. C. Shaw, whose son was killed, resigned. Texas lawmakers gave the state's Railroad Commission the power to require a chemical with a strong smell in natural gas, so that people can smell a leak. Within weeks of the explosion, that chemical was being mixed into the gas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hindenburg, 1937, and the Cocoanut Grove, 1942" -->
On May 6, 1937, the German passenger airship *Hindenburg* caught fire as it came in to land at the Naval Air Station in Lakehurst, New Jersey. An airship is a giant aircraft held up by a gas lighter than air, with engines to push it and a cabin for passengers. The *Hindenburg* was filled with hydrogen, a gas that burns very easily. Of the 97 people on board, 35 died. A man working on the ground, Allen Hagaman, died too. Cameramen from four newsreel companies were filming the landing and filmed the fire. Newsreels were short news films shown in movie theaters. None of them caught the moment the fire started. The radio reporter Herbert Morrison recorded what he saw as it happened. What caused the first spark has never been settled. Passenger travel by giant airship ended after the fire.

On November 28, 1942, at about 10:15 p.m., fire spread through the Cocoanut Grove nightclub in Boston and killed 492 people, many of them trapped behind doors that were locked, hidden or blocked. More than 1,000 people were inside a club that was allowed to hold 460. The owner, Barnett Welansky, had locked some exits, hidden others behind curtains, and bricked one up. The main entrance was a single revolving door. Bodies piled up on both sides of it and jammed it until it broke. Other doors opened inward, so people could not open them against the crush of the crowd. Fire officials later testified that if the doors had swung outward, at least 300 lives could have been saved. How the fire started is not known.

Welansky was found guilty of manslaughter and spent nearly four years in prison. Lawmakers then passed new laws that banned decorations that burn easily. The laws required emergency exits to stay unlocked, and a revolving door could no longer be a building's only exit.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Explosions at the docks: Port Chicago, 1944, and Texas City, 1947" -->
On July 17, 1944, two ships being loaded with ammunition blew up at the Port Chicago Naval Magazine in California, a Navy base for storing explosives. The blast killed 320 men, and 202 of them were Black sailors. Navy officers had given Black sailors the job of loading the ships without enough training or equipment.

On the morning of April 16, 1947, a fire broke out in the cargo of the French ship *Grandcamp* at the docks of Texas City, Texas. The ship carried about 2,300 tons of ammonium nitrate, a chemical used as fertilizer that can explode. The ship's captain ordered his crew not to use water on the fire, so that the cargo would not be ruined. The crew closed the hatches and pumped in steam. The unusual smoke drew a crowd of about 500 people to the shore to watch, many of them children.

At 9:12 a.m. the ship exploded. Flying pieces of metal set fire to oil refineries and chemical tanks along the waterfront. The Monsanto chemical plant beside the ship took the worst of the blast, and seven broken oil tanks at the Republic Refinery caught fire. Another ship, the *High Flyer*, which carried 961 tons of ammonium nitrate, blew up at 1:10 the next morning. At least 581 people died. The first explosion killed 26 of the town's volunteer firefighters, all but one member of the fire department, and destroyed all of the city's firefighting equipment. The anchor monument in Texas City lists 576 people known to have died. Sixty-three of them were buried without names.

More than 8,000 people sued the United States government. They used a 1946 law that let people sue the government for harm caused by the carelessness of its workers. In 1953 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled against them, 4 to 3. In 1955 members of Congress voted to pay the claims anyway. The government paid 1,394 awards, worth nearly 17 million dollars in all.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1950 and 2000 forecasters began to warn people a day or more before a hurricane, and the national government put its disaster help under one office. Choices made by owners and engineers still killed hundreds of people. Weather Bureau forecasters began giving hurricanes human names in 1953 and warned the Louisiana coast a day before Hurricane Audrey struck in 1957. In 1979 President Jimmy Carter put much of the government's disaster work under the Federal Emergency Management Agency, called FEMA. In 1972 a coal company's waste dam in West Virginia broke and killed 125 people. In 1981 two hotel walkways in Kansas City fell and killed 114, after an engineer approved a design change without checking the math. News reporters broadcast the rescue after a plane crash in Washington, D.C., live as it happened. After Hurricane Andrew in 1992, Florida lawmakers wrote one building code, a set of rules for how strong buildings must be, for the whole state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricanes with names: Audrey, 1957, and Camille, 1969" -->
Forecasters warned the Louisiana coast a day before Hurricane Audrey struck in 1957, but the storm sped up, arrived sooner than forecasters or residents expected, and killed at least 416 people. By then, hurricanes had names. In 1953 forecasters of the U.S. Weather Bureau began giving human names to storms. In 1956 Weather Bureau officials opened a hurricane center in Miami.

At 10 a.m. on June 26, 1957, forecasters warned the whole Louisiana coast that a hurricane was coming. About 75,000 people left. Audrey came ashore the next day near Cameron, about 24 hours after the warning. It moved faster as it neared land, which surprised both forecasters and the people who lived there. Its storm surge rose as high as 12 feet. A storm surge is the wall of seawater that a hurricane's winds push onto land. The water did damage as far as 25 miles inland. Audrey killed at least 416 people in the United States, most of them in Cameron Parish. The final count may never be known.

Late on August 17, 1969, Hurricane Camille came ashore near Bay St. Louis, Mississippi, with winds of 175 miles an hour. The water rose 24.6 feet at Pass Christian, Mississippi. Camille killed 172 people in Mississippi and 9 in Louisiana. As the storm moved north, its rain caused floods in Virginia that killed more than 153 people there. At least 343 people died in all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Alaska earthquake, March 27, 1964" -->
The strongest earthquake ever recorded in North America struck southern Alaska at 5:36 p.m. on March 27, 1964, and most of the people it killed drowned in the giant waves it caused. The earthquake had a magnitude of 9.2 and lasted four minutes and 38 seconds. The shaking and underwater landslides sent tsunamis into the coast. A tsunami is a series of giant waves set off by an earthquake or a landslide under the sea. Scientists of the U.S. Geological Survey count 131 dead: 122 killed by the tsunamis and 9 by the shaking. Other counts reach 139. At Valdez, 32 people died when the town's harbor and docks collapsed. Tsunamis also reached Oregon and California. In Crescent City, California, one killed 10 people.

Chenega was a village of Chugach Alutiiq people, also called Sugpiaq, on an island in Prince William Sound. Their people had lived there since before Russians came to Alaska. About four minutes after the shaking began, a wave 27 feet high struck the village. It knocked down every building except the school and one house. Between 23 and 26 of the roughly 70 people who lived there died, by different counts. Scientists of the Geological Survey found that Chenega lost a larger share of its people than any other town. The survivors outran the wave and climbed to high ground.

The survivors were taken to Cordova. Officials of the federal Bureau of Indian Affairs later moved them for good to the village of Tatitlek. In 1971 members of Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. Under it, Alaska Natives received 44 million acres and $962.5 million, held by companies that Alaska Natives own. Former Chenega residents formed one of them, the Chenega Corporation, and won the right to choose 76,093 acres of land. People began living in a new village, Chenega Bay, in 1984.

President Lyndon Johnson declared all of Alaska a disaster area the day after the earthquake. Army engineers of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers led the work of rebuilding roads, clearing wreckage and laying out new town sites, at a cost of 110 million dollars. The people of Valdez moved their town to higher ground four miles to the west. In 1967 federal scientists of the Coast and Geodetic Survey opened a tsunami warning center at Palmer, Alaska.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Floods of 1972: Buffalo Creek and Rapid City" -->
The Pittston Company's coal waste dam above Buffalo Creek, West Virginia, broke at about 8 a.m. on February 26, 1972, and the flood killed 125 people. The Buffalo Mining Company, part of Pittston, owned and ran three dams made of coal waste on the creek's Middle Fork. The third dam sat on a layer of soft coal mud instead of solid rock. Neither Pittston nor the mine's earlier owner had sent the dams' plans to the state for approval. Four days before the flood, a federal mine inspector called the dam "satisfactory."

The dam gave way and let out 132 million gallons of water, coal waste and mud. The flood ran down the narrow valley through 16 coal towns. It killed whole families, injured about 1,000 people, and destroyed 507 houses and 44 mobile homes. About 4,000 of the 5,000 people who lived along the creek lost their homes.

Pittston officials blamed three days of rain and called the flood "an act of God," meaning a natural event that no person could have prevented. Members of the governor's commission that investigated wrote that "the Pittston Company, through its officials, has shown flagrant disregard for the safety of residents of Buffalo Creek." Flagrant disregard means ignoring something openly and on purpose. Members of a separate commission formed by citizens said the company was guilty of murdering at least 124 people. The records show no Pittston official charged with a crime.

In 1974 Pittston paid 13.5 million dollars to settle a lawsuit by survivors and family members. One count gives 645 of them, and another gives 625. That came to about 13,000 dollars each after the lawyers were paid. Governor Moore settled the state's own lawsuit in 1977 for 1 million dollars. Members of Congress and West Virginia lawmakers passed new laws on how such dams must be built and kept up.

In June 1972, four months later, about 15 inches of rain fell on part of the Black Hills of South Dakota on June 9 and 10. Rapid Creek flooded Rapid City, where people had built houses and businesses on the creek's flood plain. A flood plain is the low land beside a river that the river covers when it floods. The flood killed 238 people. Afterward, new zoning rules set what could be built where, and most of the flood plain was rebuilt as parks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="FEMA and Three Mile Island, 1979" -->
In 1979 President Jimmy Carter put much of the national government's disaster work under one new office, the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA. Before then, officials of more than 100 separate federal offices could each claim a part in the response to a single disaster. On April 1, 1979, Carter signed two executive orders, written orders from the president, that started FEMA. In July he signed another order that moved disaster relief into it. In 1988 members of Congress passed the Stafford Act. It set the rules for when a president may declare a disaster, so that federal money and help can be sent.

Four days before FEMA started, the worst accident in the history of the country's nuclear power plants began at Three Mile Island, near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania. A nuclear reactor makes heat from uranium fuel in its core, and water must keep flowing around the core to cool it. At 4 a.m. on March 28, 1979, a small fault in the cooling system of the Unit 2 reactor set off a chain of failures. A relief valve stuck open and let cooling water escape. The operators had no instrument that showed them the valve was open. They thought the system held too much water, so they cut the flow of new water. The core overheated, and at least 45 percent of it melted.

The operators at the plant, the power company Metropolitan Edison, and state and federal officials gave the public unclear and conflicting reports. Station manager Gary Miller declared an emergency at the plant. Federal nuclear officials had no power to order people to leave. On March 30, Governor Richard Thornburgh advised pregnant women and small children within five miles to leave. He told people within ten miles to stay indoors. About 140,000 people left on their own. In at least 12 studies since 1981, scientists have found no deaths and no health harm that can be measured from the radiation that escaped. A commission of 12 members, led by John G. Kemeny, reported on the accident on October 31, 1979.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mount St. Helens, 1980, and the Hyatt Regency walkways, 1981" -->
Mount St. Helens, a volcano in Washington State, blew out its north side at 8:32 a.m. on May 18, 1980, and killed about 57 people. An earthquake loosened the side of the mountain, and the whole weakened north face slid away. Hot gas and shattered rock blasted north toward Spirit Lake. The blast turned hundreds of square miles into wasteland. Most of the people who died were suffocated, and a few died of burns. Among the dead were Harry R. Truman, who owned a lodge at Spirit Lake, the photographers Reid Blackburn and Robert Landsburg, and David Johnston, a volcano scientist of the U.S. Geological Survey. Johnston was on duty at a lookout post about six miles north of the mountain.

On the evening of Friday, July 17, 1981, two walkways fell onto a crowd in the lobby of the Hyatt Regency Hotel in Kansas City, Missouri, because of engineering mistakes. About 1,600 people had come to a dance in the lobby. The two walkways hung over the lobby. They killed 114 people and injured 216.

The engineering firm of Jack D. Gillum and Associates designed the walkways. Its first design was already too weak. It could hold only 60 percent of the weight that Kansas City's building code required. Then Havens Steel, the company making the steel parts, proposed a change in how the walkways hung. The engineer Daniel M. Duncan accepted the change in a phone call without doing the math to check it. The change doubled the load on the walkway beams on the fourth floor. Investigators found that the walkways would have failed under one third of the weight they held that night.

Members of a Missouri state board found the engineers to blame for gross negligence, which means a serious failure to take the care their job required. They also found misconduct and unprofessional conduct. Gillum's company lost its engineering licenses in Missouri, Kansas and Texas. The engineers were cleared of the crimes they were first charged with, and no one was convicted of a crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Air Florida Flight 90, January 13, 1982" -->
On the snowy afternoon of January 13, 1982, Air Florida Flight 90 took off from Washington National Airport with snow and ice on its wings and crashed into the Potomac River. The captain, Larry Wheaton, 34, and the first officer, Roger Pettit, 31, had not turned on the engines' ice protection. The jet hit the 14th Street Bridge, struck seven cars with people in them, and fell through the ice of the river. Of the 79 people on the plane, 74 died. Four drivers on the bridge died too.

Six people held on to the wreck in water of about 34 degrees. The rescuers who reached the riverbank had no equipment that could reach them. Roger Olian, a sheet-metal foreman at St. Elizabeths Hospital, was the first to jump into the water to try. A U.S. Park Police helicopter arrived at about 4:20 p.m. and pulled most of the survivors out. One passenger, Arland D. Williams Jr., 46, a bank examiner, caught the rescue line and passed it to others. When the helicopter came back for him, he had gone under. The coroner found that he was the only passenger who drowned. The bridge span was later renamed for him. The Carnegie Hero Fund, which gives medals for bravery, says the sixth man's identity was never firmly established, while the coroner and the renamed bridge name Williams.

A passenger, Priscilla Tirado, was badly hurt and blinded by jet fuel, and she was too weak to grab the line when the helicopter came back to her. Lenny Skutnik, an assistant at the Congressional Budget Office, took off his coat and boots, swam out in short sleeves and pulled her to shore. "Somebody had to go into the water," he told *Time* magazine. News reporters recorded the rescue and broadcast it live.

Investigators of the National Transportation Safety Board, the federal office that looks into crashes, found that the pilots caused the crash. They blamed their failure to use the engines' ice protection and their decision to take off with snow or ice on the wings. The captain did not listen when the first officer told him the engine readings looked wrong. He did not stop the takeoff while there was still room on the runway. Both pilots died in the crash.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="melvin-windsor" name="Melvin Windsor" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Melvin Windsor

> **Who:** A U.S. Park Police officer who stood on a helicopter's skid over the frozen Potomac River to pull a survivor out.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., January 13, 1982.

Melvin E. "Gene" Windsor, 41, pulled the last survivor of Air Florida Flight 90 out of the Potomac while standing on the skid of a flying helicopter with no safety line. A skid is one of the long metal bars a helicopter lands on. Windsor was born in Washington, D.C., in 1940 and grew up in Rockville, Maryland. He had managed a carpet store, among other jobs, before he joined the Park Police in 1971. By 1982 he was a rescue technician.

He flew with the chief pilot, Donald W. Usher, 31. Because of the storm, Usher had ordered earlier that day that no routine flights would go up. At about 4:09 p.m. a radio call reported a possible plane crash. They took off at 4:16 and reached the river at about 4:20. Usher steered by watching the highway below through the windows in the helicopter's floor.

Six people were holding on to the wreck, in water up to their shoulders and necks. Windsor dropped life jackets and lines to them while Usher flew so low that the skids went under the water.

The last survivor out of the water was Patricia Felch. She had been in the river for 30 minutes. Windsor unbuckled his seat belt and stepped out onto the right skid, which was under the water. He grabbed her with one arm and held the back of her head with the other.

They went back for the man who had passed the line to others, and could not find him. "In a mass casualty, you'll find people like him, but I've never seen one with that commitment," Windsor told *Time*. Windsor, Usher, Skutnik and Olian each received the Carnegie Medal for bravery. Windsor served 22 years with the Park Police. He died on August 24, 2014, at 74.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="melvin-windsor" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1989: Hurricane Hugo and the Loma Prieta earthquake" -->
Hurricane Hugo hit two U.S. territories before it reached the mainland in September 1989. On St. Croix in the U.S. Virgin Islands, it damaged more than 90 percent of the buildings and killed three people. In Puerto Rico it killed eight people and left nearly 28,000 without homes. On September 22 it came ashore on Sullivan's Island, South Carolina. The storm surge reached 20.2 feet near McClellanville, and 13 people died in South Carolina.

At 5:04 p.m. on October 17, 1989, an earthquake of magnitude 6.9 struck about 10 miles northeast of Santa Cruz, California, and killed 63 people. A World Series baseball game was about to begin at Candlestick Park in San Francisco. In Oakland, the upper deck of the Cypress Street Viaduct fell onto the lower deck, and 42 people were crushed to death in their cars. The viaduct was a two-level section of the Nimitz Freeway, a highway raised above the ground. A section of the San Francisco-Oakland Bay Bridge also fell, and a 23-year-old woman, Anamafi Moala, died there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricane Andrew, August 24, 1992" -->
More than 63,500 houses were destroyed when Hurricane Andrew struck Florida on August 24, 1992, and help from the national government was slow to come. Andrew came ashore at Homestead with winds as high as 165 miles an hour. It damaged more than 124,000 other houses and wrecked 99 percent of the mobile homes in Homestead. It killed 44 people in Florida. Fifteen died in the storm itself and 29 from its effects afterward. In all, the storm left 65 people dead.

Three days after the storm, Dade County's emergency management director, Kate Hale, went on national television. "Where in the hell is the cavalry on this one?" she asked. "They keep saying we're going to get supplies. For God's sake, where are they?" The cavalry were soldiers on horseback, and she meant the rescuers and supplies that had not come. Many people criticized FEMA for the slow start. FEMA officials believed they could not act until Governor Lawton Chiles sent a formal request for federal help. He waited five days to send it.

Engineers who surveyed the damaged roofs found that concrete roof tiles had been glued to felt paper and shingles stapled on the wrong way. A single building code for the whole state of Florida was set up in 1998, and it took effect in 2002.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Northridge, 1994, and the Chicago heat wave, 1995" -->
At 4:30 a.m. on January 17, 1994, the Northridge earthquake, magnitude 6.7, struck Los Angeles. The official count of the dead is 57, and other counts run from 60 to 72. Sixteen people died when the Northridge Meadows apartment building collapsed. Buildings with parking on the bottom floor held up poorly in the shaking.

A heat wave in Chicago in mid-July 1995 killed 739 more people in one week than usually die in a week. Most of the dead were poor, old people. Many had no air conditioning, or had it and could not afford to run it. Many kept their windows shut and would not sleep outside, because they were afraid of crime. Black residents were more likely to die than white residents.

City officials did not issue a heat emergency warning until the last day of the heat wave. The records do not say who made that choice. Few people used the city's five cooling centers. A medical examiner is a doctor who examines the dead to find out how they died. The morgue run by the medical examiner, Edmund Donoghue, could hold 300 bodies, and it filled. Refrigerated trucks were parked outside to hold the rest. Donoghue said later that his office was not allowed to hold press conferences, and that Mayor Richard M. Daley was not told until the weekend. A later study counted 692 deaths above normal over the summer. In August, 41 victims whose bodies no one claimed were buried together in one grave in Homewood, Illinois.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="disasters" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In three disasters after 2000, Hurricane Katrina, Hurricane Maria and the Eaton Fire, many of the dead were old, sick or disabled and could not get out or get care on their own. Hurricane Katrina killed 1,392 people in 2005, by the National Hurricane Center's count as corrected in 2023. Most of them died in Louisiana, many of them in New Orleans, where floodwalls broke. Investigators found that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers was responsible for those floodwalls. After Hurricane Maria struck Puerto Rico in 2017, officials of the island's government counted 64 dead. In 2018 they raised the official count to 2,975, after a study of deaths in the months after the storm. Power lines owned by utility companies started deadly fires in California in 2018 and 2025 and in Hawaii in 2023.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="September 11, 2001, and the illnesses afterward" -->
On September 11, 2001, 343 members of the New York City Fire Department died in the attacks on the World Trade Center, and by 2026 more of its members had died from breathing the dust of the ruins. That morning 19 men of the terrorist group al-Qaeda took over four passenger jets by force. They flew two into the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City and one into the Pentagon, near Washington, D.C. On the fourth plane, United Airlines Flight 93, passengers and crew fought back, and it crashed into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. The attacks killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 attackers. Seventy-one police and other law officers also died in New York.

For months afterward, firefighters, police officers, construction workers and volunteers dug through the ruins. They breathed dust of crushed concrete, glass fibers, asbestos, lead and mercury. Asbestos is a mineral whose tiny fibers lodge in the lungs and can cause cancer years later. Fires in the ruins burned for three months. Many of the workers later got cancer or lung disease. In September 2026 leaders of the Fire Department reported that 453 more of its members had died of illnesses tied to the dust. That is more than the 343 it lost on the day of the attacks.

In December 2010 members of Congress passed the James Zadroga 9/11 Health and Compensation Act. President Barack Obama signed it on January 2, 2011. It set up the World Trade Center Health Program, which pays for medical care for sick responders and survivors. Members of Congress renewed it in December 2015.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricane Katrina, August 29, 2005" -->
Most of the people Hurricane Katrina killed died in Louisiana, where New Orleans flooded after its floodwalls broke. On August 28, 2005, Mayor Ray Nagin ordered the first mandatory evacuation in the city's history, meaning everyone was ordered to leave. Governor Kathleen Blanco opened all lanes of the highways to traffic leaving the city, and about one million people left. Between 100,000 and 150,000 people stayed in the city. City officials opened the Superdome stadium as a "refuge of last resort," a place to go when there was nowhere else.

Katrina came ashore on August 29. Its storm surge broke the levees and floodwalls in more than 50 places, and water covered 80 percent of the city. In the Lower Ninth Ward, a neighborhood where most homeowners were Black, the water was more than 10 feet deep. Members of Congress had put the Corps of Engineers in charge of the city's flood protection in a law of 1965. Investigators found that the Corps was responsible for the floodwalls that broke, and that the flood protection system the national government built had engineering flaws. Judges of a federal appeals court later ruled that, under a law of 1928, the Corps could not be made to pay for the damage.

In 2023 scientists at the National Hurricane Center lowered their count of the dead from 1,833 to 1,392, after new studies. Other studies found that most of the dead in New Orleans were old people who lived near the broken levees, in the Lower Ninth Ward and Lakeview neighborhoods. Coast Guard crews rescued more than 33,500 of the roughly 60,000 people stranded in the city. People waited for days at the Convention Center, which had no food, water or medical help. Early news reports of mass crime at the Superdome later turned out to be false or exaggerated. Federal officials did not begin to act in earnest to help the people at the Convention Center until September 2. Michael Brown, the head of FEMA, lost charge of the relief work to Vice Admiral Thad Allen and resigned on September 12.

Some police officers turned their guns on survivors. Police from the city of Gretna and from the Crescent City Connection bridge police, with deputies of the Jefferson Parish sheriff, blocked the bridge over the Mississippi River. They turned back people walking out of New Orleans at gunpoint. On September 4, New Orleans police officers shot six unarmed Black people on the Danziger Bridge. They killed James Brissette, 17, and Ronald Madison, 40, a man with a mental disability. Madison was shot seven times, five times in the back. Among the officers were Kenneth Bowen, Robert Gisevius, Anthony Villavaso and Robert Faulcon. Police officers then tried to cover up the killings. In 2016 five former officers pleaded guilty and were sentenced to between 3 and 12 years in prison.

At Memorial Medical Center, 45 bodies were found after the storm. In July 2006, Louisiana Attorney General Charles Foti's office finished an investigation of nearly a year. A judge then ordered the arrest of a doctor, Anna Pou, and two nurses, Lori Budo and Cheri Landry, for second degree murder. They were accused of giving deadly doses of morphine and midazolam to four patients: Emmett Everett Sr., 61, Hollis Alford, 66, Ireatha Watson, 89, and Rose Savoie, 90. Morphine is a strong painkiller, and midazolam is a drug that makes people calm and sleepy. In large doses, both slow and then stop a person's breathing. In 2007 a grand jury refused to charge any of the three. The state later paid Pou's legal bills of more than 450,000 dollars.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="herbert-freeman-jr" name="Herbert Freeman Jr." movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Herbert Freeman Jr.

> **Who:** A New Orleans man who took his 91-year-old mother, Ethel Freeman, out of their flooded home by boat, then stayed beside her body outside the Convention Center after she died waiting for help.
> **When and where:** New Orleans, August 31 to early September 2005.

Herbert Freeman Jr. rescued his mother from their flooded house, and she died the next day outside the New Orleans Convention Center, where police had sent them to wait for a bus. Ethel Mayo Freeman was 91. She had a pacemaker for her heart and a feeding tube, and she used a wheelchair. She was often sick, and she decided to stay home when Katrina came.

The floodwater filled her house several feet deep. On August 31, Herbert, 58, borrowed a friend's boat. He put his mother in her wheelchair, lifted her into the boat and took her to dry ground. New Orleans police officers there sent them to the Convention Center. Herbert told officers his mother needed a doctor. They told him a bus would come.

The Convention Center had no food, no water, no medical help and no way out. "She was calling out for a doctor or a nurse, but there was nothing there," Herbert said. Ethel Freeman died in her wheelchair on September 1. Her body stayed outside for days, slumped in the chair and covered with a poncho. Pictures of it were shown on national television.

Herbert watched over his mother's body for nearly four days, until he was ordered onto a bus. Before he left, he put a note with his name and phone number in her pocket. It took him seven weeks to find her body, at a morgue in St. Gabriel, Louisiana. Her funeral was held in New Orleans in November 2005.

He and the families of two other people who died sued the national government. In 2009 federal appeals judges ruled that, under the Stafford Act, the government could not be sued over decisions like these in a disaster, and they threw out the case.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="herbert-freeman-jr" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricane Maria in Puerto Rico, September 20, 2017" -->
Hurricane Maria left all 3.4 million people in Puerto Rico without electricity on September 20, 2017, and thousands died in the months that followed. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, and its people are U.S. citizens. Maria struck two weeks after Hurricane Irma crossed the region. It caused the worst blackout in U.S. history. Some customers did not get their power back until August 2018, 11 months later.

After Irma, FEMA officials had shipped many of the supplies they kept in Puerto Rico to the U.S. Virgin Islands. After Maria, they admitted to failures in moving supplies, too few staff and poor communication. On September 29, 2017, the mayor of San Juan, Carmen Yulín Cruz, said of the national government's relief work: "We are dying and you are killing us with the inefficiency." On October 3, President Donald Trump compared the count of dead at that time, 16, with "a real catastrophe like Katrina."

The number of dead became a public fight. Officials of Governor Ricardo Rosselló's government counted 64 dead for months. Researchers found that many more people than usual died in the months after the storm. People who needed dialysis could not reach emergency care for long stretches of time. Dialysis is a treatment that cleans the blood of people whose kidneys have failed. In 2018 researchers at Harvard University estimated 4,645 extra deaths from September to December 2017. Their range was very wide, from 793 to 8,498. Researchers at George Washington University estimated between 2,658 and 3,290 extra deaths from September 2017 to February 2018. On August 28, 2018, Rosselló's officials accepted their study and raised the official count to 2,975. On September 13, 2018, Trump wrote on Twitter that "3000 people did not die" and blamed Democrats for the number. He gave no evidence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jesse-vazquez" name="Jesse Vazquez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jesse Vazquez

> **Who:** A retired veteran in Bayamón, Puerto Rico, who lived for months without electricity after Hurricane Maria with his 89-year-old mother, Mercedes Mercado.
> **When and where:** Calle Alameda, Bayamón, Puerto Rico, September to December 2017.

Jesse Vazquez and his mother, Mercedes Mercado, went 96 days without electricity after Hurricane Maria. Maria cut the power to their house on Calle Alameda in Bayamón on September 20, 2017. For weeks the family ran electric fans and charged their phones from the battery of Jesse's car.

A small generator cost about 600 dollars on sale, more than Jesse could afford. In October his son, also named Jesse, and his daughter, Megan, flew in from New York to bring him a generator. "It's basically cheaper to buy a flight, get a generator and bring it down here," his son said. Running it five hours a day took about 80 dollars of gasoline a month. Exhaust from a neighbor's generator blew into the family's living room. Megan said they needed a carbon monoxide detector. Carbon monoxide is a poison gas in engine exhaust that has no smell.

By Christmas the house had been without power for 96 days. Jesse and his mother used the generator to run the refrigerator, and fans at night so they could sleep. A FEMA inspector told Jesse he could not get help to fix his damaged roof. "It's kind of humiliating," he said. With his son's help he bought four solar panels. He and his mother decided to stay in Puerto Rico.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jesse-vazquez" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Floods: Harvey, 2017, Helene, 2024, and the Texas Hill Country, 2025" -->
Hurricane Harvey struck Texas in late August 2017 and dropped more rain than any other hurricane or tropical storm in U.S. records. Scientists at the National Hurricane Center recorded 60.58 inches near Nederland, Texas. Rescuers pulled more than 17,000 people from the water. Their report records at least 68 people killed by the storm's direct effects in Texas and about 35 more by causes such as electric shock, car crashes and being cut off from medical care.

Hurricane Helene came ashore in Florida, near Perry, late on September 26, 2024. The next day its rain fell on North Carolina and Tennessee. Busick, North Carolina, got 30.78 inches of rain, and the rain set off more than 2,000 landslides. Scientists at the National Hurricane Center count at least 250 dead in the United States, 107 of them in North Carolina.

In Erwin, Tennessee, six people died at the Impact Plastics factory as the Nolichucky River rose on September 27. A worker who survived and the families of the dead spoke out. They said the workers were not told they could leave until floodwater had covered the road to the plant and the power had gone out. Five employees and a contractor who cleaned the offices were killed. In April 2025 inspectors of Tennessee's workplace safety agency found that the deaths were not work-related. They issued no citations, which are official notices that a company broke a safety rule. In July 2025, after a review by the Tennessee Bureau of Investigation, District Attorney General Steven Finney filed no charges against the company's president, Gerald O'Connor. The investigators said they found no evidence that workers were told they would be fired for leaving, and that the workers had a little more than an hour to get out. The family of Johnny Peterson, one of the dead, sued the company.

Early on July 4, 2025, more than 20 inches of rain fell on the Texas Hill Country, and the Guadalupe River rose about 26 feet in 45 minutes. The flood killed 136 people, 117 of them in Kerr County, by counts reported in July 2026. At Camp Mystic, a Christian summer camp for girls, 25 campers, two counselors, Chloe Childress and Katherine Ferruzzo, and the camp's owner and director, Dick Eastland, died. National Weather Service forecasters sent their first flash flood warning for Kerr County at 1:14 a.m. At 4:03 a.m. they sent a flash flood emergency, a more urgent alert, for the towns of Hunt and Ingram.

Kerr County had no flood warning system. In 2016 the county sheriff had asked for one, with sirens. County Commissioner H. A. "Buster" Baldwin said, "I think this whole thing is a little extravagant for Kerr County, with sirens and such." After appeals from the camp, several camp buildings were taken off the map of land likely to flood. On June 18, 2026, members of the Texas Senate and House investigating committees released a report. The lawmakers found that the camp had no written emergency plans, which state rules required, and that the camp's leaders did not move the campers out in time even though they had chances to. In September 2025, at an extra meeting called a special session, Texas lawmakers had passed new camp safety laws, among them the Heaven's 27 Camp Safety Act. In July 2026, three of Kerr County's six new sirens sounded during another flood, an official of the Upper Guadalupe River Authority said.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fires started by power lines: Paradise, 2018, Lahaina, 2023, and Los Angeles, 2025" -->
On the morning of November 8, 2018, a single metal hook broke on a poorly maintained power line tower owned by Pacific Gas and Electric, called PG&E. The line started a fire. The tower was part of a line built in the early 1900s. The Camp Fire burned through the town of Paradise, California. It killed 85 people and destroyed more than 18,000 buildings. On June 16, 2020, the company pleaded guilty in court to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter, the crime of killing people through carelessness.

On August 8, 2023, a wildfire destroyed the town of Lahaina on the island of Maui, Hawaii, and killed 102 people. That is the most people killed in a U.S. wildfire since 1918. High winds knocked down power lines of Hawaiian Electric, and the lines started a fire that morning. Maui firefighters declared that fire under control and left. Less than an hour later, embers from it started the fire again. Firefighters lost water pressure in the hydrants. Many people died in their cars. Some jumped into the ocean to escape the flames.

Maui has more than 80 outdoor warning sirens, and none of them sounded. Herman Andaya, the head of the Maui County Emergency Management Agency, said the sirens would have sent people toward the mountains and into the fire. He resigned the next day. In an October 2024 report, federal fire investigators and the Maui Fire Department called the fire accidental and blamed no person or company. Lahaina had been the capital of the Kingdom of Hawaii for 35 years. Many Native Hawaiian families could afford to live there only because they had inherited homes that were already paid for. Under a settlement of the survivors' lawsuits, Hawaiian Electric, Maui County and others agreed to pay 4 billion dollars. As of August 2026, none of that money had been paid, because of a court fight over the lawyers' fees, the *Honolulu Star-Advertiser* reported.

In January 2025, fires pushed by winds of up to 100 miles an hour burned parts of Los Angeles. They killed at least 31 people and destroyed more than 18,000 homes and other buildings. The Eaton Fire in Altadena killed 19 people. In August 2026 Los Angeles County and state fire investigators reported that it started from electric sparks on an unused Southern California Edison tower. Seventeen of the 19 died in west Altadena, a neighborhood with a long history as a Black community. Reviewers from the firm Citygate Associates found in May 2026 that county officials' orders to leave reached west Altadena hours after the fire turned toward it. The records do not name the officials who made those decisions. The dead had an average age of 77. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, opened an investigation into whether race, age or disability affected how officials warned people.

The Palisades Fire killed 12 people. In October 2025 Jonathan Rinderknecht, 29, was arrested and charged with starting it. Prosecutors say he set a small fire on January 1, and that strong winds brought that fire back to life on January 7 as the Palisades Fire. Firefighters had thought the January 1 fire was out. A Los Angeles Fire Department battalion commander ordered crews to leave that site, although they reported that the ground was still burning slowly. Rinderknecht's federal trial ended on June 26, 2026, when the jury could not agree. Ten jurors voted not guilty and two voted guilty. Prosecutors then charged him again with two counts of arson and dropped a third charge. On August 19, 2026, he pleaded not guilty. His second trial was set to begin on November 2, 2026. As of October 2, 2026, he had not been convicted of any crime.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

In February 2021 a winter storm struck Texas, and more than 4.5 million homes and businesses lost power, some of them for days. Officials of the Texas Department of State Health Services counted 246 deaths from the storm. Almost two-thirds of the dead died of hypothermia, which means the body gets so cold that it stops working. Some estimates run as high as 702 deaths. Cristian Pavón Pineda, 11, died of carbon monoxide poisoning in his family's mobile home in Conroe, which had no power.

At 1:22 a.m. on June 24, 2021, part of Champlain Towers South, a 12-story building in Surfside, Florida, where each family owned its own apartment, fell down and killed 98 people. For years, water had seeped into the concrete and steel supports of the parking garage under the pool deck, and the steel had rusted. In 2018 engineers of the firm Morabito Consultants reported the damage. They warned that if the waterproofing was not replaced soon, the damage to the concrete would spread fast. In April 2021 the damage was reported to be "much worse." A 15-million-dollar repair had been approved, but the main work had not started.

President Trump called for getting rid of FEMA. On February 17, 2025, officials of the Trump administration fired more than 200 FEMA workers. On March 24, 2025, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem said she would move to eliminate FEMA. The acting head of FEMA, Cameron Hamilton, told members of Congress: "I do not believe it is in the best interest of the American people to eliminate the Federal Emergency Management Agency." He was removed from his job the next day. In August 2025 more than 180 current and former FEMA workers signed a public statement of protest they called the "Katrina Declaration." On May 7, 2026, a review council the president had set up approved a report with ten recommendations. On September 15, 2026, members of a House committee voted for one bill to change FEMA, the Post-Disaster Protection Act. In late September 2026, FEMA still existed, and members of Congress were still debating its future.
