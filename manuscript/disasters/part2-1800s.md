## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Steamboats became a new cause of sudden death between 1800 and 1850. Steamboat boilers exploded on rivers and at sea, and steamboats caught fire on the Great Lakes. In 1838 members of Congress passed the first federal law to inspect steamboats. Earthquakes, a city fire and a tornado also killed hundreds of people. After the New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812, members of Congress voted to give land to people whose farms the quakes had ruined.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New Madrid earthquakes, 1811 and 1812" -->
Three great earthquakes shook the middle Mississippi Valley in the winter of 1811 to 1812. The first struck northeast Arkansas at about 2:15 in the morning on December 16, 1811. The others came on January 23 and February 7, 1812. Scientists at the U.S. Geological Survey estimate their size at about magnitude 7.3 to 7.5. The seismologist Susan Hough, a scientist who studies earthquakes, has argued that they were closer to magnitude 7.0. The quakes are named for New Madrid, Missouri, the largest town on the Mississippi River between St. Louis and Natchez.

The shaking threw down log cabins and chimneys as far away as Cincinnati and St. Louis. The first quake woke people in New York City, Washington, D.C., and Charleston, South Carolina. Many books state that the shaking rang church bells in Boston. Boston's newspapers of the time never reported it.

On the Mississippi River, high banks and whole islands fell into the water. Huge waves sank boats or threw them onto the shore. For a short time the river ran backward. Land sank near the St. Francis River in Arkansas. In Tennessee, sinking ground dammed Reelfoot Creek and made Reelfoot Lake, about 18 miles long. Aftershocks went on into 1816.

Reports of the time counted fewer than 100 deaths on land. No one counted the dead on the river or in the Native towns of the region. Historians who study the quakes believe the real number of deaths was far higher. Cherokee families who had already moved west from their homelands farmed along the St. Francis River. The earthquakes ruined their land. They left their farms and cattle and moved west again, where the Osage people living there resisted them.

In 1814 Missouri's territorial governor, William Clark, and two other leaders asked Congress for help. In February 1815 members of Congress passed a law that let owners of ruined land claim the same amount of public land somewhere else, up to 640 acres. It was one of the country's earliest disaster relief laws. Speculators, people who buy things cheaply to sell them later for a profit, learned of the law first. In the spring of 1815 they hurried to New Madrid. They bought the ruined land at low prices from owners who had not yet heard of the law. Most Missourians came to believe that the speculators got most of the benefit. The records do not identify them by name.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire in the cold: New York, 1835" -->
On the night of December 16, 1835, a gas pipe burst in a warehouse in lower Manhattan, and a coal stove lit the gas. The temperature that night fell as low as 17 degrees below zero. The rivers were frozen. Firefighters cut holes in the ice to reach water, and the water froze again in their hoses. They had fought two large fires two days before, and the city's water tanks were nearly empty.

The fire burned 17 blocks and between 530 and 700 buildings in New York's business district. Two people died. At about 3 in the morning, U.S. Marines and sailors brought gunpowder from the Brooklyn Navy Yard. They blew up buildings in the fire's path so that it had nothing left to burn. The damage came to about 20 million dollars. Twenty-three of New York's 26 fire insurance companies went out of business. An investigation blamed no one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steamboat boilers explode, 1838" -->
A steamboat's boiler is a large metal tank where a fire turns water into steam to drive the engine. A boiler can burst if the steam pressure grows too high. It can also burst if the water inside runs low and someone then lets cold water in on the hot metal.

On April 25, 1838, all four boilers of the steamboat *Moselle* burst at once as it pulled away from a landing just east of Cincinnati. The captain, Isaac Perin, had kept the boilers at full steam during the stop, to impress people watching from shore. Many witnesses said he was racing another boat, the *Ben Franklin*. The blast threw Perin onto the riverbank and killed him. More than 280 people were on board. A committee of Cincinnati citizens met the next day and counted about 150 dead. Later accounts give 160.

The steamship *Pulaski* ran between Savannah, Georgia, and Baltimore, Maryland. On June 14, 1838, it left Charleston, South Carolina, for Baltimore. At about 11 that night, about 40 miles off the coast of North Carolina, its starboard boiler exploded. Starboard is a ship's right side. The ship broke in two and sank in about 45 minutes. About 128 people died, and 59 were saved.

An inquiry at the end of 1838 found that the engineers had run the boilers wrongly. An account written with the help of two survivors identifies the cause. The second engineer let the water boil away in the boiler and then let a full load of cold water in on the hot copper. The account does not include his name.

Enslaved people traveled on the *Pulaski* as servants of white passengers. The passenger list includes some of them by first name only, among them Jenny, Priscilla and Sallie Middleton. Others appear only as "servant" after a family's name. No one counted how many enslaved people died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Rebecca Lamar

> **Who:** A survivor of the *Pulaski* explosion who spent five days on a floating piece of the wreck and later wrote down what happened.
> **When and where:** Off the coast of North Carolina, June 14 to 19, 1838.

Rebecca Lamar survived five days on a floating piece of the *Pulaski* and later wrote down what happened there. She was traveling north with her brother, the Savannah banker Gazaway Bugg Lamar. With them were his wife, Jane, their six children and two nieces. Her brother owned shares in the ship.

The explosion woke her in her cabin. Her bed had jammed the door, so she began to climb out through the small window above it. Her brother put her and a niece into a small boat. A moment later something struck her, and she was in the sea. She caught hold of a piece of wood and pulled herself onto a floating section of the ship. Her niece Martha and her nephew Charles reached her there. A stranger brought her nephew Thomas to her.

The people on that section of the wreck had almost no food or water. They saved the wine and peppermint for the children. Rebecca Lamar tore strips from her dress into squares. She wet them and laid them on the children's heads during the day, to protect them from the sun and from thirst. On the second day six men who could swim took the best small boat to row for help. Her brother was one of them. They reached the beach 40 miles above Wilmington, North Carolina.

People on the wreck died one by one. A man named Mr. Brown drank sea water. In a storm a woman named Mrs. Woart died, and a wave carried off her body and her husband, the Reverend Woart. Two men became delirious, which means confused and out of their minds, and walked into the sea. A man named Mr. Smith set off on a raft he had built and was never heard from again. On the Monday night her nephew Thomas died in her lap.

On the following Tuesday the schooner *Henry Cameron*, under Captain Eli Davis, found them. A schooner is a sailing ship with two or more masts. The *Pulaski*'s sailing master, Captain Pearson, was already on board, rescued from another piece of the wreck. That group numbered 23 people in all. He asked Davis three times to search for more survivors. The third search found Rebecca Lamar's group.

Her brother's wife, five of their six children and a niece died. Her brother and his son Charles, age 14, lived. In 1858 Charles Lamar led a group of investors who paid for the ship *Wanderer* to bring captive Africans to Georgia. Federal law had banned bringing enslaved people into the country since 1808. The *Wanderer*'s captain, William Corrie, bought 487 captive Africans at the Congo River. About 409 of them lived through the voyage, and the rest died on the way. Six trials were held in 1859 and 1860, and no one was convicted.

Rebecca Lamar later married Hugh McLeod. She wrote her account of the wreck without planning to publish it. The *Georgia Historical Quarterly* later printed it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-lamar" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first steamboat safety law, 1838" -->
On July 7, 1838, members of Congress passed a law "to provide better security of the lives of passengers" on steamboats. It was the first federal law that required steamboats to be inspected. Under it, federal judges named engineers as inspectors. The inspectors checked each boat's hull and boilers and gave the owner certificates. Owners paid 5 dollars for each inspection, repeated every 6 or 12 months. No federal officer enforced the law. Only a person who sued the owner in court could make an owner obey it. Boiler explosions, fires and crashes went on.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A tornado at Natchez, 1840" -->
At about 1 in the afternoon on May 7, 1840, a tornado moved up the Mississippi River. A tornado is a spinning column of wind that reaches down from a storm cloud to the ground. It struck Natchez, Mississippi, and Vidalia, Louisiana, across the river. At the Natchez landing it threw 116 of the 120 flatboats into the river. Flatboats were large, flat-bottomed boats that carried farm goods and people downriver. Their crews and passengers drowned.

At least 317 people died, 269 of them on the river and 48 on land. The courthouse at Vidalia fell and killed Judge Keeton. Many bodies were never identified. By that count it is the second deadliest tornado in the country's history.

The count of 317 leaves out the enslaved people killed on plantations in Louisiana. P. M. Lapice reported that on his plantation below Vidalia the tornado blew down all the houses of the enslaved workers. Four or five people were killed outright, and about twenty were badly hurt. A Natchez newspaper, the *Mississippi Free Trader*, reported hundreds of enslaved people killed on plantations up to 20 miles away. No one made an exact count.

After the storm the planter Stephen Duncan paid to turn the Tremont House hotel into a hospital. Doctors and several thousand dollars came from New Orleans. Slaveholders in the area sent large groups of enslaved men to clear the ruins and pull out the bodies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The steamboat Erie burns, 1841" -->
On the evening of August 9, 1841, the steamboat *Erie* left Buffalo, New York, for Chicago with about 340 people on board. The boat had just been painted and varnished. A group of six painters, hired by William G. Miller of Buffalo, had left jugs of paint, turpentine and varnish on the deck. They left them near the pipe that carried hot air from the boiler. That spot on the deck had caught fire three times before.

At about 8 p.m. the turpentine exploded. Fire spread over the freshly painted boat in minutes. Two of the lifeboats turned over. The third carried the captain, Thomas Jefferson Titus, and four others. At 10 p.m. the crew of another steamboat, the *DeWitt Clinton*, arrived and pulled survivors from the lake. Fewer than 100 people were saved. The first counts put the dead at about 170. Later counts found as many as 254. All six painters died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The country's deadliest shipwreck, its deadliest wildfire and its deadliest dam failure all happened between 1850 and 1900. Each one killed more than 1,000 people. After the *Sultana* sank and the Johnstown dam broke, survivors and investigators blamed named men, and no court punished any of them. In the same years Americans built new groups whose job was to warn people or help them. Army soldiers began sending out storm warnings in 1870, and Clara Barton founded the American Red Cross in 1881.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A stronger steamboat law, 1852" -->
Steamboat explosions and fires went on after the law of 1838. From 1847 to 1852, boiler explosions caused a long run of disasters. On August 30, 1852, members of Congress passed a stronger law, the Steamboat Act. Under it, officials of the Treasury Department enforced the rules through supervising inspectors. Under the old law, only a lawsuit could make an owner obey.

Inspectors had to test every boiler by filling it with water under pressure, to see whether it leaked or burst before it was used with steam. Owners had to fit each boiler with a safety valve, a part that lets steam out before the pressure gets too high. Pilots and engineers had to earn a license from the inspectors. The law left out freight boats, ferries and tugboats. A law of 1871 covered all steam vessels and their crews.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sultana, 1865" -->
The worst shipwreck in the country's history happened on the Mississippi River in April 1865, just as the Civil War was ending. The steamboat *Sultana* was built to carry 376 passengers. At Vicksburg, Mississippi, Union Army officers crowded about 2,000 Union soldiers onto it. Most of the soldiers had just been freed from Confederate prison camps at Cahaba, Alabama, and Andersonville, Georgia.

Army officials paid boat owners for each soldier they carried home. They paid 2 dollars and 75 cents for each enlisted soldier, meaning one who was not an officer, and 8 dollars for each officer. Captain Reuben Hatch was the Army's chief quartermaster, or supply officer, at Vicksburg. He offered the *Sultana*'s captain, J. Cass Mason, a full load of about 1,000 soldiers in return for a kickback, a secret payment for the favor. One of the boat's boilers was leaking. Mason had it patched instead of making the longer repairs a full fix would take. Captain George Williams, the officer in charge of loading, put every man at the camp on board. He believed there were fewer than 1,500.

At about 2 in the morning on April 27, 1865, about seven miles north of Memphis, Tennessee, the boilers exploded. The boat caught fire. Investigators blamed low water in the boilers while the overloaded boat leaned to one side, along with the patched boiler. Men who were thrown into the river floated past Memphis in the dark. Crews of other steamboats and of U.S. Navy ships pulled survivors from the water. On the Arkansas shore, the Fogelman family tied logs into a raft and took 25 soldiers off the burning boat. Franklin Hardin Barton, a Confederate soldier of the 23rd Arkansas Cavalry, was among the rescuers. The *Sultana* burned and sank at about 7 a.m.

Counts of the dead run from about 1,100 to 1,547, and most of the dead were soldiers. General William Hoffman investigated for the Army and counted 1,238 in May 1865. Officials of the Customs Service, who kept records of ships, counted 1,547. The researcher Jerry Potter estimates about 1,800. About 200 of the men pulled out alive later died of their burns. Mason and most of the boat's officers died.

Captain Frederic Speed of the Army was tried and found guilty of overcrowding the boat. The Army's top lawyer, the Judge Advocate General, overturned the verdict. He found that Speed had spent the whole day at the camp and had not put a single soldier on board. Hatch quit the Army and refused three orders to come and testify. No one was punished.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire on October 8, 1871: Chicago and Peshtigo" -->
On the evening of October 8, 1871, two fires began that together killed well over a thousand people. The smaller one burned the city of Chicago. The larger one burned the forests and towns of northeastern Wisconsin and Michigan's Upper Peninsula.

The Chicago fire started at about 8:30 p.m. in or near a small barn behind the home of Catherine O'Leary's family. The cause is not known. The city was built mostly of wood, and a strong wind blew. The fire burned until October 10. It destroyed about 17,500 buildings over more than 3 square miles and left 90,000 to 100,000 people without homes. Searchers found 120 bodies. The county coroner, the official who looks into deaths, said that an exact count was impossible. Estimates run to about 300 dead.

A newspaper reporter, Michael Ahern, wrote that Catherine O'Leary's cow had kicked over a lantern. Many people believed it. She was a poor Irish Catholic immigrant, and many Americans then distrusted Irish Catholics. In 1893 Ahern admitted that he had made the story up. In 1997 the members of the Chicago City Council voted to clear her name. The historian Richard Bales has suggested that a man named Daniel Sullivan started the fire.

Mayor Roswell B. Mason put the city under martial law, rule by the army, and placed General Philip Sheridan in charge from October 11 to 24. Donors in New York City gave $450,000 and donors in St. Louis $300,000. Money came from London too. Mayor Mason put a private charity, the Chicago Relief and Aid Society, in charge of the relief.

The fire in Wisconsin began with people who were clearing forest for farms and railroads. They set small fires to burn away the brush. After a long drought, a strong wind joined several of those fires into one. It burned about 1.2 million acres. It destroyed Peshtigo, Wisconsin, a town of about 1,700 people, and 16 other towns. People jumped into the Peshtigo River, into wells and into ponds to escape the flames. Some drowned, and others died of cold in the water.

The fire burned the town's records, so no one knows exactly how many people died. Estimates run from 1,200 to 2,400. In 1873 a report to the Wisconsin Legislature listed 1,182 names of people who died or went missing. More than 350 bodies were buried in one grave, because no one was left alive who could name them. It is the deadliest wildfire in the country's recorded history. Peshtigo stood on forest land that the Menominee Nation had given up to the United States in the Treaty of the Cedars in 1836. That treaty opened about 4 million acres to logging and white settlement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Red Cross, 1881, and an earthquake in Charleston, 1886" -->
Clara Barton and a group of supporters founded the American Red Cross in Washington, D.C., on May 21, 1881. On September 5, 1881, forest fires burned more than a million acres in the Thumb, the part of Michigan shaped like a thumb. The fires killed 282 people and left thousands without homes. Red Cross workers sent money, clothes and furniture to about 14,000 people in need there. It was the Red Cross's first disaster relief.

At about 9:50 on the night of August 31, 1886, an earthquake of about magnitude 7 shook Charleston, South Carolina. It killed 60 people and damaged about 2,000 buildings. Most of the damage, 81 percent of it, was to brick buildings. Wooden buildings suffered much less.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Storm warnings and the blizzards of 1888" -->
On February 9, 1870, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law that set up a national weather service. Soldiers of the Army's Signal Service gathered weather reports by telegraph. They sent out warnings of storms on the Great Lakes and along the seacoasts.

On January 12, 1888, a blizzard struck the northern Great Plains without warning. A blizzard is a snowstorm with very strong wind and bitter cold. The morning had been warm, near 50 degrees, and many children walked to school without coats. About 235 people died in Dakota Territory, Nebraska, Minnesota and Iowa, by the most used count. Some estimates run as high as 1,000. The storm is remembered as the Schoolchildren's Blizzard.

Near Ord, Nebraska, the wind tore the door and the roof off the sod schoolhouse of Minnie Freeman, a teacher of 19. She tied her students together with twine and carried the youngest in her arms. She led them to a farmhouse about half a mile away, and all of them lived. In Plainview, Nebraska, the teacher Loie May Royce tried to lead three students to her home, less than 90 yards from the school. They lost their way in the snow. The three children froze to death, and Royce's arms and legs were frostbitten. In Turner County, in present-day South Dakota, five boys were found dead four days after the storm. They were John Albrecht, Peter Graber, and Henry, John and Elias Kaufman.

In March 1888 another blizzard struck the Northeast and killed about 400 people. Forecasters of the Signal Service had predicted fair weather. The two storms led members of Congress to pass a law on October 1, 1890, that moved weather forecasting from the Army to a new civilian Weather Bureau in the Department of Agriculture.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Johnstown Flood, May 31, 1889" -->
The dam that broke above Johnstown, Pennsylvania, belonged to a private club of wealthy Pittsburgh men, and the changes they made had weakened it. The South Fork Dam was 69 feet high and held back a lake 14 miles up the valley from Johnstown. Henry Clay Frick led a group of Pittsburgh businessmen who bought the lake for the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. The club had more than fifty members, most of them steel, coal and railroad owners.

Workers the club hired lowered the top of the dam to make room for a road for carriages. They put a screen across the spillway, the channel that lets extra water out, to keep the club's fish in the lake. The pipes at the bottom of the dam, which could let water out, were gone, and club members never replaced them. The club's members had no way to lower the lake in an emergency.

After days of heavy rain, the dam broke in the afternoon of May 31, 1889. Earlier that day the club's president, Elias Unger, had twice sent a man named Parke on horseback to a telegraph office to warn Johnstown. The warnings were never passed to the town's leaders, because there had been many false alarms before.

The water rushed down the valley carrying houses, trees and railroad cars. The railroad engineer John Hess drove his engine backward toward East Conemaugh with the whistle blowing the whole way, and many people who heard it reached high ground. At the Stone Bridge in Johnstown, the wreckage piled up against the bridge and caught fire. At least 80 people died there, and the fire burned for three days.

The count of the dead was 2,209. Eleven years later one man counted as dead, Leroy Temple, turned up alive, which made it 2,208. Ninety-nine whole families died. Of the dead, 396 were children, and 777 were never identified. The flood left 124 women and 198 men widowed and 98 children orphaned.

In 1891 a committee of the American Society of Civil Engineers reported that the dam would have failed even if the club had kept it as first built. The group's president in 1890, William Shinn, had been a business partner of Andrew Carnegie, and the committee held its report back for two years. The authors of a study published in 2016 found that the club's changes cut the amount of water the dam could safely let out by half.

Survivors sued the club. Two of its members, the lawyers Philander Knox and James Hay Reed, defended it in court. They argued that the flood was an "act of God," a natural event that no person could be blamed for. The survivors lost, and no one ever paid them for their losses. Frick and about half of the club's members gave money to help the survivors. Andrew Carnegie built the town a new library.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Victor Heiser

> **Who:** A 16-year-old who rode the Johnstown Flood on a barn roof, lost both his parents, and later became a doctor.
> **When and where:** Johnstown, Pennsylvania, May 31, 1889.

Victor Heiser was 16 when the Johnstown Flood destroyed his home and killed his parents. As the river rose that afternoon, his father sent him to the barn to untie the horses. He heard what he called a "dreadful roar." He looked back and saw his parents at a window of the house. His father waved at him to climb onto the barn roof.

From the roof he saw his house "crushed like an eggshell." He noted the time: 4:20 p.m. The barn floated away with him on its roof. When it was about to hit a neighbor's house, he jumped onto that house. It began to fall in, and he fell back onto the barn roof. The water carried him down to the Stone Bridge.

He spent the night in a house with 19 other people. The next day he went looking for his parents. He tried to help people trapped in the burning wreckage at the bridge. For two weeks he looked at the bodies carried to the morgues, the places where bodies are kept until they are named. He found the body of his mother, Mathilde Heiser. He was never able to identify his father, George.

Heiser later studied at Jefferson Medical College and became a doctor. He worked in many countries against diseases such as leprosy, cholera and malaria. In 1936 he told his story in a book, *An American Doctor's Odyssey*. He was born on February 5, 1873, and died on February 27, 1972.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="victor-heiser" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Sea Islands hurricane, 1893" -->
On August 27, 1893, a hurricane came ashore near Savannah, Georgia. It drove the sea over the low Sea Islands of Georgia and South Carolina. Between 1,000 and 2,000 people died, most of them drowned. Most people on the islands were Black, and most of them owned their land. On St. Helena Island about 6,000 Black people lived. Most of their houses stood less than 2 feet above the ground.

Red Cross workers arrived on October 1, more than a month after the storm, and ran a relief effort for 10 months. The historian Caroline Grego and others found that South Carolina politicians and newspaper writers pushed Red Cross leaders to send supplies to white farmers instead of to Black islanders. The records do not identify those politicians or writers by name. The Democratic Party leaders who ran the state government worked against the efforts of Black islanders to earn a steady living again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Clara Barton

> **Who:** The founder of the American Red Cross, who led its relief work after fires, floods and hurricanes.
> **When and where:** Michigan, 1881. Johnstown, Pennsylvania, 1889. The South Carolina Sea Islands, 1893. Galveston, Texas, 1900.

Clara Barton founded the American Red Cross in 1881, when she was 59, and led its relief work after disasters for more than 20 years. Its first disaster relief went to the people burned out by the Michigan forest fires that September.

She reached Johnstown on June 5, 1889, five days after the flood. She was 67. About 50 Red Cross workers came with her from Washington, D.C., and they stayed five months. Her workers built "Red Cross hotels," large wooden buildings where families who had lost their homes could sleep and eat for free. Red Cross workers gave out supplies worth $211,000 and helped about 25,000 people.

A separate Red Cross group from Philadelphia ran a hospital in Johnstown. Barton quarreled with its leaders, and within a few days the two groups would have nothing to do with each other.

In 1893 she led relief for about 30,000 people left homeless by the Sea Islands hurricane, most of them Black. Her last relief work came after the Galveston hurricane of September 1900. She resigned as president of the Red Cross on May 14, 1904, after growing criticism.

Her house in Glen Echo, Maryland, was built partly from lumber of the Red Cross buildings at Johnstown. She lived there from 1897 to 1912.
