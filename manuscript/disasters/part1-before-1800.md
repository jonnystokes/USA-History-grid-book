## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Volcanoes, earthquakes and droughts that lasted for decades struck this land long before anyone wrote them down. The people who lived through them belonged to Native nations. What is known about these disasters comes from three kinds of evidence: layers of ash in the ground, the growth rings of old trees, and accounts that the nations kept and told to each new generation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two volcanoes" -->
Mount Mazama is a volcano in the Cascade Range of southern Oregon, about 60 miles north of the California line. About 7,700 years ago it erupted, and its top fell in. The eruption lasted only a few days. Scientists estimate that it was more than forty times as powerful as the eruption of Mount St. Helens in 1980. Its ash fell as far away as what is now central Alberta, in Canada.

Rain and melted snow filled the hole that the collapse left behind. The water became Crater Lake. It is 1,943 feet deep, the deepest lake in the United States. In the Klamath language its name is *giiwas*.

People had lived in the region for thousands of years before the eruption. In 1938 an archaeologist named Luther Cressman dug in Fort Rock Cave, in Lake County, Oregon. An archaeologist studies the past from the things people left behind. Under a layer of ash from Mount Mazama, Cressman found dozens of sandals woven from sagebrush bark. Radiocarbon dating is a test that measures how much of one kind of carbon is left in something that was once alive. It later showed that the sandals were made between about 10,500 and 9,100 years ago. The families who wore them lived there long before the mountain erupted.

The Klamath Tribes say that they have lived in the Klamath Basin for 13,000 years. They tell the eruption as a battle between Llao, chief of the Below World, and Skell, chief of the Above World. In their account, Llao attacked the people with fire after a chief's daughter refused him. Skell defended the people, and two holy men gave up their lives. Skell drove Llao back into Mount Mazama, the mountain fell in, and water filled the basin.

Around the year 1085, Sunset Crater erupted near what is now Flagstaff, Arizona. Scientists fixed the date from chemical changes in the growth rings of trees that were alive then. Ash fell on land 12 to 15 miles away and buried people's homes. Crops cannot grow in ash deeper than about 6 to 8 inches. The Sinagua farmers who lived closest to the volcano were ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples. They left their fields and moved farther away. Where the ash lay thin, it worked as a mulch, a covering that keeps soil from drying out, and crops there grew better. No record counts any dead. The people of the area told stories of the eruption, and those stories are still told today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Droughts that lasted decades" -->
A drought is a long stretch of time with too little rain for crops. A tree adds one ring of new wood each year, and in a dry year the ring is thin. Scientists read those rings in very old trees and wooden beams to find out which years were dry.

The rings show that the Colorado Plateau, in the dry Southwest, had too little rain from about 1130 to 1180. From 1140 to 1162 there were 23 dry years in a row. Susan Ryan, a researcher at the Crow Canyon Archaeological Center in Colorado, calls it the greatest drought recorded for North America. The Colorado River carried less water in the middle 1100s than at any other time in the past 1,200 years. At Chaco Canyon, in present-day New Mexico, builders had stopped putting up large stone buildings called great houses by about 1130. In the decades after that, the people of the Chaco towns moved away.

A second drought, called the Great Drought, lasted from 1276 to 1299. Ryan found it less severe than the drought of the 1100s. By about 1300 the Pueblo people of the Mesa Verde region, in present-day southwestern Colorado, had moved south. They went to the Rio Grande valley, to Zuni and Acoma, and to the Hopi mesas, where rain came more reliably.

Archaeologists at Crow Canyon count the drought as one cause of that move among several. They also name social, political and economic causes, which means changes in how the communities lived together, led themselves and made their living. They think the drought caused food shortages in a region crowded with people, and that the shortages led to fighting.

Around 1280, attackers killed at least 41 people at Castle Rock Pueblo and at least 8 at Sand Canyon Pueblo. Both places are in the Mesa Verde region. Kristin Kuckelman and two other scientists studied the skeletons. They found that the attackers had cut the bodies apart and broken, crushed and burned the bones, during or after the fighting. They also found signs that people probably ate human flesh there. No evidence shows who the attackers were.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A fault under the northwest coast" -->
The outer layer of the earth is broken into huge slabs called plates. Off the coast of present-day Washington, Oregon and northern California, one plate slides under another. The line where they meet is a fault, a crack in the earth's crust where the rock on each side can move. This one is called the Cascadia Subduction Zone. Subduction means one plate sliding down under another.

When the rock along a fault moves suddenly, the ground shakes. Scientists have found signs of 19 great earthquakes on this fault in the last 10,000 years. The most recent one struck in January 1700.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish expeditions and colonies of the 1500s left the first written records of disasters in this land. Hurricanes sank their ships. A hurricane is a very large storm that forms over warm ocean water, with winds that spin in a circle and heavy rain. Droughts ruined the corn that colonists planned to get from Native towns. The same droughts struck the Native nations too. What is known about them comes from tree rings and from letters that Spaniards wrote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A hurricane ends a colony, 1559" -->
A hurricane in September 1559 sank most of the ships of the largest Spanish colony yet sent to the Southeast, along with nearly all of its food. The colonists had landed on August 15, 1559, at Pensacola Bay in present-day Florida. The Native name of the bay was Ochuse. They came from Veracruz, in Mexico, on 11 ships.

About 1,500 people made the voyage. They included an army of 500 soldiers and about 200 Aztec warriors and craftsmen from Mexico City. Servants, enslaved Africans, and some wives and children came too. For five weeks they unloaded people and tools and began building a town on a high bluff. They left most of their food on board the ships, because they thought it was safer there than on land.

On the night of September 19 to 20, 1559, a hurricane came into the bay. It sank six ships and pushed a seventh onto the land. Only three ships were left. Nearly all the food for 1,500 people went down with the ships. The people who drowned were mostly the sailors and passengers still on board. No record gives their number.

After the storm the colonists' main problem was food. One ship came from Havana, in Cuba, with supplies soon after the hurricane. Relief ships from Mexico came after that, one every five to seven months. The colonists sent about 200 men inland to look for towns with food to spare. The men found a large town called Nanipacana, on the Alabama River in present-day central Alabama. Its people left before the Spaniards arrived. As they went, they destroyed the food they had stored.

Many colonists grew sick and died from lack of food. Others were killed in fights with Native people. The records do not state which Native people fought them. The colonists shipped many survivors back to Mexico. By September 1560, 362 people were left at the bay, and another 200 were far inland. Most of the rest sailed away in April 1561. Later that year two small ships of that fleet sank in another storm off Cape Hatteras, with everyone aboard. The last Spanish soldiers left the bay in August 1561. In February 1562 the viceroy, the Spanish king's governor in Mexico, gave up the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tristan-de-luna-disasters" name="Tristán de Luna y Arellano" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tristán de Luna y Arellano

> **Who:** A Spanish noble and soldier who led the colony at Pensacola Bay when the hurricane of 1559 destroyed its ships and food.
> **When and where:** Pensacola Bay, Florida, and the Alabama River, 1559 to 1561.

Tristán de Luna y Arellano led the colony at Pensacola Bay, the largest and best-paid-for attempt by Spain up to that time to settle the Southeast. He was born to a noble family in Borobia, in Spain. Years before, he had marched with the Spanish explorer Coronado. The colony he led landed in August 1559. The hurricane of September 19 to 20 sank or wrecked seven of his ships with the food still on board.

From February to June 1560, Luna moved most of his people inland to the town of Nanipacana. In April he sent 200 men under Mateo del Sauz farther north, to Coosa, a chiefdom in present-day northwestern Georgia. A chiefdom is a group of towns under one head chief. The men were looking for food. They stayed there until November.

In late summer 1560, Luna ordered 100 soldiers and 50 servants to march to Coosa on short rations, meaning with less food than they needed. His whole army refused, and the march never took place.

In early April 1561, Ángel de Villafañe arrived at the bay with a small fleet to replace Luna. He dismissed Luna and ordered him back to Spain. Villafañe took most of the roughly 150 settlers still at the bay to Havana. Luna died in New Spain, the Spanish colony in Mexico, on September 16, 1573.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tristan-de-luna-disasters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The droughts behind the Lost Colony" -->
Tree rings from old bald cypress trees on the border of Virginia and North Carolina show two long droughts in the 1500s. A team of scientists led by David Stahle published the record in 1998. The first drought lasted from 1562 to 1571 and was worst from 1565 to 1569. In September 1570 a Spanish priest in the Chesapeake Bay region, Father Juan Batista de Segura, wrote about it. He wrote that the region had gone six years with too little corn and wild fruit, with hunger, death and dry soil.

From 1587 to 1589 came the driest three years in the whole 800-year record. English colonists on Roanoke Island, in present-day North Carolina, disappeared during those years. They are known as the Lost Colony. The Stahle team counts the drought as one of several causes of the colony's end. An earlier English colony on Roanoke had come in wetter years, 1585 and 1586. Only 4 of its 108 people died.

The Croatan people were allies of the English colonists. In 1587 their crops were poor. The same year, the Algonquian people of the town of Dasemunkepeuc killed George Howe, an adviser to the English governor, John White. They killed him in revenge for the killing of their leader, Wingina. Men under Ralph Lane, leader of the earlier colony, had attacked Wingina in June 1586. After Howe's death the people of Dasemunkepeuc left their town.

Before daylight on August 8, 1587, White led his soldiers in an attack on Dasemunkepeuc. The people they found there were Croatan, who had come to the empty town looking for food. White's men killed one of them and wounded many before they saw their mistake. The Stahle team writes that the shooting happened partly because the Croatan needed food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine burns and floods" -->
St. Augustine, in present-day Florida, was burned, raided or flooded again and again in its first 35 years. Spanish soldiers and colonists founded it in 1565 at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. In 1566 Timucua warriors shot flaming arrows at the fort to drive the Spaniards out. On April 1, 1566, the fort was half burned, and all its supplies were lost. The records do not state whether the arrows started that fire.

The colonists moved the town across the bay to Anastasia Island that year. In 1570 soldiers there rose against their officers and destroyed the fort. By 1572 the sea was washing away the rebuilt fort. In 1577 Native warriors attacked and burned many houses. In 1586 the English sea captain Francis Drake and his men burned the whole town and its fort.

In March 1599 a fire swept through the town, which was built of wood with roofs of thatch, meaning dried plants. In September of the same year a hurricane flooded the buildings that the townspeople had not yet repaired.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonies on the Atlantic coast were small in the 1600s. One dry summer or one storm could kill a large share of the people in them. Colonists built their towns mostly of wood, so a fire in one house could spread to the next. Governors, ministers and ordinary colonists wrote down the storms, fires and earthquakes they lived through. Their letters and journals are the first detailed accounts of disasters in this land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown: drought, a hurricane and the Starving Time" -->
English colonists landed at Jamestown, in present-day Virginia, in April 1607. They built their fort inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan people. Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom, a group of many towns and chiefs under one head chief. Its head chief was Powhatan.

Tree rings show that the colonists arrived during the driest seven years in 770 years, from 1606 to 1612. Corn crops failed for the Powhatan people as well as for the English. The colonists' drinking water came from the lower James River, where fresh water mixes with salt water from the sea. In the dry years the water was salty and foul, and colonists fell sick from it. Of the 104 colonists who landed in 1607, only 38 were alive a year later. Malnutrition, which means the body does not get enough food, was a leading cause of death.

On June 2, 1609, the leaders of the Virginia Company of London, the business that ran the colony, sent nine ships and about 600 people to strengthen it. It was the largest fleet England had yet sent west. On July 24, 1609, a hurricane struck the fleet about seven days from land. It drove the lead ship, the *Sea Venture*, away from the others. The ship began to leak so badly that one passenger wrote, "we almost drowned within."

The *Sea Venture* reached Bermuda, an island in the Atlantic. All 150 people on board got ashore, among them the colony's new governor, Sir Thomas Gates. Before the ship sank, the crew saved many of the supplies. The castaways stayed on Bermuda for ten months and built two small ships, the *Patience* and the *Deliverance*. The playwright William Shakespeare drew partly on their story for his play *The Tempest*. The other eight ships reached Jamestown in late August 1609. Many passengers were sick, and the crews had thrown much of the food overboard in the storm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Starving Time, 1609 to 1610" -->
In the winter of 1609 to 1610, most of the people at Jamestown starved to death. That winter is called the Starving Time. The colonists had not farmed, hunted or fished to feed themselves. They lived on supplies from England and on food they bargained for from the Powhatan people, or took from them by force. The drought had hit the Powhatan towns too, and they had little food to spare.

The fighting grew worse in the autumn of 1609. The colonist Francis West cut off the heads of two warriors of the Patawomeck people while he was trading with them. In November 1609 warriors ambushed a party of colonists led by John Ratcliffe at Orapax. They tortured Ratcliffe to death. That same month Powhatan ordered his people to surround Jamestown and cut off its food.

Counts of the colonists differ. The historian Martha McCartney counts 240 people at Jamestown at the start of the winter. Other counts record about 500 in the colony. By May 1610 about 60 were alive. Some survivors dug up and ate the bodies of dead colonists and of Native people the colonists had killed.

The *Sea Venture* castaways reached Jamestown in their two small ships on May 24, 1610. Gates decided to give up the colony and sail home with the survivors. On the river they met Thomas West, Baron De La Warr, who was bringing a year's supply of food, and everyone went back to Jamestown. Between 1607 and 1625, the company's leaders sent about 6,000 people to Jamestown. About 4,800 of them died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Colonial Hurricane, August 1635" -->
A hurricane struck New England on August 15, 1635. The English then used an older calendar that ran ten days behind the one used today, so by today's calendar the date was August 25. William Bradford, the governor of Plymouth Colony, wrote that no one living there, "either English or Indeans, ever saw" a storm like it. *Indeans* is his spelling of Indians.

South of Plymouth the sea rose more than 20 feet above its normal height. Bradford wrote that many Native people climbed into trees to escape the water. No one counted the Native people who drowned. The wind blew down houses and, in Bradford's words, "many hundered thowsands of trees." The worst of the storm lasted five or six hours.

The same storm wrecked the *Angel Gabriel*, a ship full of English settlers, at Pemaquid, in present-day Maine. Three or four passengers and one sailor died. Off Cape Ann, in Massachusetts, it wrecked a small boat carrying two families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anthony-thacher" name="Anthony Thacher" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anthony Thacher

> **Who:** An English colonist who survived the wreck of a small boat in the hurricane of 1635 and described it in a letter to his brother Peter.
> **When and where:** Off Cape Ann, Massachusetts Bay Colony, August 11 to 15, 1635.

Anthony Thacher and his wife were the only two of 23 people who lived through the wreck of their boat off Cape Ann. Days later he wrote down what happened in a letter to his brother Peter.

On August 11, 1635, Thacher and his family left Ipswich, in Massachusetts, for the town of Marblehead. With them went his cousin John Avery, a minister, and Avery's family. The people of Marblehead had asked Avery to be their minister, and they sent a pinnace, a small sailing ship, to carry the two families. Thacher's letter lists 23 people on board: 11 in Avery's family, 7 in his own, a man named William Elliot, and four sailors.

At about 10 on the night of August 14, the wind split the pinnace's old sails. Before daylight the hurricane struck. The anchor dragged along the bottom, and the storm drove the boat onto the rocks of a small island. A wave threw Thacher onto a rock with his oldest daughter, Mary. His cousin Avery and Avery's oldest son joined them there. The next wave broke the boat to pieces. It carried his wife toward the shore on a piece of the deck. All the others still on board drowned. Thacher's letter identifies his children who died: Mary, his daughter Edith, her baby brother Peter, whom she was holding in her arms, and his son William.

Another wave swept all four off the rock. He was in the water for at least a quarter of an hour before he crawled ashore. On the island he and his wife found a knapsack with a steel, a flint and dry gunpowder. They also found a drowned goat, two cheeses, some butter, a hat and his son William's coat. He struck a fire and they cooked some of the goat. They had only salty water to drink. He found the body of Avery's oldest daughter on the shore. She is buried on the island.

On the following Monday a passing boat picked them up. Thacher named the island "Thatchers Woe" and the rock "Avery's Fall." On September 3, 1635, the Massachusetts General Court, the colony's lawmakers, voted him 40 marks from the treasury toward his losses. A mark was an English unit of money. In March 1637 they gave him the island. It is still called Thacher Island.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anthony-thacher" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An earthquake, 1638" -->
An earthquake shook New England on about June 1, 1638, by the old calendar. Bradford wrote that people heard it before they felt it, as a rumbling like far-off thunder. Dishes rattled and fell from shelves. People outdoors could not stand without holding on to posts. Native people felt it inland, and ships along the coast shook.

Scientists today measure an earthquake's strength with a number called its magnitude. Each step up the scale, such as from 6 to 7, means far stronger shaking. Scientists at Boston College's Weston Observatory estimate the 1638 earthquake at about magnitude 6.5. They think it was centered in central New Hampshire, but the few reports of the time make that uncertain. People felt the aftershocks, smaller quakes that follow a large one, for 20 days. No deaths were recorded.

Bradford wrote that the shaking looked like a sign that God was displeased. He added that for years afterward the summers were too cold and wet for the corn to ripen. He left the cause, in his words, "to naturallists to judge," meaning people who study nature.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston burns, and the town buys a fire engine" -->
Boston's first large fire, on January 14, 1653, destroyed many buildings and killed three children. After it, town officials hired the owner of a water engine, a pump that sprays water, to bring it to fires. They bought six long ladders, four strong iron hooks and fire buckets. They required every property owner to keep a ladder and a swab pole, a long pole with a mop on the end.

On November 27, 1676, a fire in Boston's North End destroyed 45 buildings. Among them were several warehouses, the North Meeting House, and the home of its minister, Increase Mather. In 1678 town officials brought a fire engine from England and hired men to run it. A Boston Public Library guide describes them as the first paid fire department in the colonies. Officials also made a law that roofs be slate or tile and walls be brick. Large fires burned parts of Boston again in 1679, 1682 and 1691.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Fires burned large parts of Boston and Charleston between 1700 and 1750, and townspeople began to organize to fight them. In 1722 about two-thirds of Boston's nearly 3,000 houses were built of wood. A fire that started in one building could spread across a neighborhood in a night. Towns named fire wardens to take charge at fires, and neighbors formed fire companies. On the coasts, an earthquake struck the Pacific Northwest in 1700, and a hurricane wrecked a Spanish fleet off Florida in 1715. In New York, officials blamed a series of fires in 1741 on enslaved people and put at least 30 people to death.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cascadia earthquake, January 1700" -->
At about 9 at night on January 26, 1700, the Cascadia fault broke along about 620 miles of the Pacific coast. The break ran from Vancouver Island, in present-day Canada, to northern California. Scientists at the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network call it one of the largest earthquakes known anywhere in the world. They put its size at about magnitude 9.

The coast dropped when the fault broke. Salt water flooded forests of red cedar trees that had grown above the reach of the tides. The salt water killed the trees. Their dead trunks, called ghost forests, still stand along rivers and bays in Washington and Oregon.

The earthquake also set off a tsunami, a series of huge waves that can cross a whole ocean. The waves reached Japan the next day. At Miyako the water destroyed 13 houses, and a fire afterward burned 20 more. Near Nakaminato the waves drove a ship carrying rice onto rocks, and two sailors died. The Japanese records describe the waves but no shaking. In 1996 scientists used those Japanese records to work out the day and the hour of the earthquake. Tree rings from the ghost forests confirmed the year.

No one counted the dead on the American coast. The Native nations there had no written records then. In their accounts, whole villages were lost. The Makah, in present-day Washington, tell of a great earthquake at night. The Quileute tell of a flood that carried canoes far inland. Archaeologists have found Native villages on the coasts of Washington and Oregon that sank, flooded and were left empty after 1700.

In 1933 Annie Miner Peterson, a Coos woman from Oregon, told the language scholar Melville Jacobs what her grandfather had seen. He had seen an old woman who survived the "raised water." When she was a girl, the water had left her hanging high in a tree. She tied her pack line to a branch to climb down, and she fell. The fall broke her back, and she had a humped back for the rest of her life. Scientists who studied the account date the flood she described to between 1690 and 1805.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A hurricane sinks the Spanish treasure fleet, 1715" -->
Early on July 31, 1715, a hurricane drove eleven Spanish treasure ships onto the reefs of Florida's east coast, and at least 1,000 people died. The fleet of twelve ships had left Havana, in Cuba, on July 24, bound for Spain. The eleven Spanish ships carried silver and gold from Spain's colonies in the Americas. They were wrecked between present-day Fort Pierce and Vero Beach. The twelfth ship, the French *Grifon*, escaped, because its captain had sailed farther from shore.

About 2,500 people were on board the fleet. Scientists at NOAA, the US government's ocean and weather agency, and the 1715 Fleet Society, a group that studies the wrecks, count about 1,000 dead. Spanish records from Cuba state about 1,500. The fleet's commander, Juan Esteban de Ubilla, was among the dead.

The survivors camped on the beach in the land of the Ais people. The Ais lived along the coast from Cape Canaveral to the Indian River. On August 6, Admiral Francisco Salmón sent the pilot Nicolás de India and 18 men in an open boat to Havana for help. They reached it 11 days later. Ships from Havana and from St. Augustine brought food, soldiers and tools for salvage, which means bringing up goods from a wreck. By late October 1715 the salvagers had raised more than 5 million pieces of eight, the silver coins of Spain.

English ship captains came for the silver too. One of them, a Captain Barrett, brought an enslaved man named Ned Grant to the wrecks to work as a diver. In the winter of 1715 to 1716, the English pirates Henry Jennings and John Wills attacked the Spanish salvage camp and carried off part of the silver.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston burns, 1711" -->
On the evening of October 2, 1711, a Scottish woman named Mary Morse was working with oakum in a small house off Cornhill in Boston. Oakum is old rope fiber, picked apart and used to seal the seams of ships. A flame nearby set it on fire. By 2 in the morning the fire had burned about 100 buildings. Among them were the Town House, where the colony's government met, and the town's oldest meeting house. The fire left 110 families without homes.

Four sailors climbed the meeting house steeple to save its bell. The roof fell in, and they died. Other people died when they went too close to the fire. Still others died in the gunpowder blasts that people fighting the fire set off to blow up houses in the fire's path, so it would have nothing to burn. The full count of the dead is not known.

Members of Boston's churches raised 700 pounds for the families who lost their homes. A few months after the fire, 12 men were appointed as Boston's first board of fire wardens, to take charge at fires. Bostonians rebuilt the Town House and the meeting house in brick. The new Town House is known today as the Old State House.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston: a hurricane in 1700 and a fire in 1740" -->
On September 3, 1700, a hurricane struck Charleston, South Carolina. It knocked down thousands of trees and dozens of buildings. The Scottish ship *Rising Sun* sank at anchor outside the harbor. It carried people from a failed Scottish colony at Darien, in Panama. About 100 people on board drowned, including the captain.

On November 18, 1740, at 2 in the afternoon, a fire broke out in Charleston. A strong wind from the northwest carried burning pieces across the town. In less than a day the fire destroyed more than 300 houses and shops and several wharves. The officers and crews of British navy ships in the harbor pulled down and blew up houses to stop it. The records that survive do not say whether anyone died.

Lieutenant Governor William Bull ordered every townsman to fight the fire, "by themselves and slaves." Townsmen were to send the people they enslaved to fight it as well. He called out the militia, the colony's armed citizens, to stop people from stealing from the ruins.

Help after the fire went mostly to the rich. The king's government in London promised 20,000 pounds for the people "most affected." Officials paid it out by the size of each person's losses. Most of it went to wealthy merchants, whose losses were largest. Poorer people depended on gifts and loans from the vestry, the governing board of St. Philip's Church. Mary Bedon, an innkeeper with three children, and Catharine Joor, a shopkeeper, both lost their businesses in the fire. Both opened again in rented houses. By February 1741 both needed aid from the St. Philip's vestry to get through the winter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York, 1741: fires and blame" -->
In March and April 1741, 13 fires broke out in lower Manhattan. The first, in March, burned the governor's house and its chapel inside Fort George. Sources record its date as March 8 or March 18. New York officials said that enslaved Black people and poor white people had plotted together to burn the city. A witness named Mary Burton testified that the fires were such a plot.

Three judges, James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse, tried about 200 people. Officials burned 13 Black men alive at the stake, which means they tied each man to a post and set a fire around him. They hanged 17 Black men and 4 white people, two men and two women. Sources record between 30 and 34 people put to death in all. Officials shipped 70 to 84 more people to the Caribbean islands to be sold into slavery there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An earthquake in New England, 1727" -->
At about 10:40 on the night of October 29, 1727, an earthquake shook New England. By today's calendar the date was November 10. Scientists at the Weston Observatory estimate it at about magnitude 5.6. It was strongest at Newbury, Massachusetts. There it knocked down stone walls and the tops of almost all the chimneys. Some solid ground turned to mud, and some marshes dried up. People felt it from Maine to the Delaware River, and smaller shocks went on for months. The records that survive show no one badly hurt.

In Boston the minister Cotton Mather heard "a horrid rumbling like the noise of many coaches." New England's ministers preached that God had sent the earthquake to show his anger at sins such as swearing and failing to keep the Sabbath, the day of rest. Towns such as Medford, Massachusetts, held a day of fasting, when people went without food and prayed. John Barnard, a minister at Marblehead, came to a different view. He decided that the surface of the earth had moved because of shocks deep underground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first fire companies" -->
In Boston, groups of neighbors formed mutual fire societies. Each member promised to help save the houses of the other members, and only theirs. On December 7, 1736, Benjamin Franklin and about 25 other men in Philadelphia signed the articles, or rules, of the Union Fire Company. Each member kept six leather buckets for water and two linen bags for carrying goods out of burning houses. Each promised to come to every fire in the town, not only to the houses of members.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="disasters" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, people who lost their homes in a disaster began to get help from far away. After fires in Boston and Charleston, colonial lawmakers voted public money for the families burned out, and other colonies sent gifts. In 1752 Philadelphians started a company to insure houses against fire. During the Revolutionary War, hurricanes sank Spanish warships and troop ships on the Gulf Coast, and a fire burned a large part of New York while British troops held it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston under water, 1752" -->
On the morning of September 15, 1752, a hurricane pushed the sea more than 10 feet above the normal high-water mark at Charleston, South Carolina. The water covered the whole town. People fled to the upper floors of their houses. Before 11 o'clock nearly every ship in the harbor had been thrown onto land. Only one, the British warship *Hornet*, rode out the storm.

About 500 buildings were destroyed. No one kept an exact count of the dead. Many people drowned, and others died when houses fell apart around them. A second storm on September 30 ruined crops and killed farm animals. Officials then limited how much corn, peas and rice could be shipped out of the colony, to keep food prices down.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire insurance, 1752" -->
Fire insurance means that many owners each pay a small amount into a shared fund, and the fund pays any owner whose house burns. In 1752 Benjamin Franklin and other men in Philadelphia organized the Philadelphia Contributionship, a fire insurance company. Its inspectors looked at each house before insuring it. They set the price by how likely the house was to burn. Insured houses carried a sign showing four clasped hands.

The company's directors required owners to remove the trees from their land, because trees got in the way of firefighters. Owners who wanted to keep their trees started a rival company, the Mutual Assurance Company. People called it the Green Tree.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cape Ann earthquake, 1755" -->
At about 4:30 in the morning on November 18, 1755, an earthquake shook New England. Its center was in the ocean about 25 miles off Cape Ann, Massachusetts. Scientists estimate it at about magnitude 6.2. In Boston the shaking lasted more than a minute. It threw down about 100 chimneys and damaged as many as 1,500 more. Some church steeples were left leaning. People felt it from Nova Scotia, in present-day Canada, to South Carolina. No deaths were recorded in the Boston area.

Seventeen days earlier, a large earthquake had struck Lisbon, in Portugal. New England ministers held prayer services, and officials named days of fasting. Writers in New England published at least 27 sermons, poems and accounts about earthquakes after the one at Cape Ann. A lightning rod is a metal rod on a building that carries lightning into the ground. The Boston minister Thomas Prince wrote that Boston had more lightning rods than any other New England town. He argued that the rods had drawn electricity into the ground and made the shaking worse. John Winthrop, a professor at Harvard College, answered him in lectures. He said that heat and gases deep in the earth had caused the earthquake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston burns, and other colonies send help, 1760" -->
At about 2 in the morning on March 20, 1760, a fire started in a house on Cornhill in Boston. No one ever found the cause. The fire burned 349 buildings, 174 of them homes. It left 220 families, more than 1,000 people, without homes. No one was killed. A Boston Public Library guide describes it as the worst fire in the American colonies up to that time.

The Massachusetts legislature, the colony's elected lawmakers, voted 3,000 pounds for the families who lost their homes. Lawmakers in Pennsylvania and New York voted to send money too. People in Nova Scotia raised money for Boston, and people as far away as London sent gifts. The Massachusetts lawmakers then passed a law that any new building more than 7 feet high had to be made of brick or slate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fires in wartime: New York, 1776, and Charleston, 1778" -->
British troops under General William Howe took New York City in September 1776, during the Revolutionary War. On the night of September 20, a fire broke out near the southern tip of Manhattan. Dry weather and a strong wind spread it north and west. By morning it had burned between 400 and 1,000 buildings. That was between one tenth and one quarter of the city. Trinity Church burned.

British commanders blamed rebel arsonists, people who set fires on purpose. British soldiers and sailors killed a few men they suspected of setting the fire. They killed them on the spot, without a trial. The records do not give the men's names or an exact number. British officers questioned more than 200 suspects and charged none of them.

Historians still disagree about how the fire started. The historian Barnet Schecter found that no accusation of arson holds up. The historian Benjamin Carp argued in 2023 that rebels most likely set the fire on purpose. Gene Procknow, who reviewed Carp's book, wrote that the evidence does not prove it beyond a reasonable doubt.

In Charleston a fire began in a bakery at 4 in the morning on January 15, 1778. By noon it had destroyed more than 250 houses and killed six people, both Black and white. People who had lost their homes got beds and food in the city's public buildings, paid for with public money. The next day South Carolina's General Assembly, the state's lawmakers, voted 20,000 pounds to help them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricanes and the war on the Gulf Coast, 1779 and 1780" -->
In 1779 Spain joined the war against Britain. Bernardo de Gálvez, the Spanish governor of Louisiana, gathered ships at New Orleans to attack the British fort at Baton Rouge, on the Mississippi River. On August 18, 1779, a hurricane sank the ships in less than three hours. Gálvez had to put off his attack.

On October 20, 1780, another hurricane struck a Spanish fleet of 64 ships under Admiral José Solano. The fleet was carrying Gálvez and 4,000 soldiers from Havana to attack the British town of Pensacola, in Florida. About 2,000 of the soldiers died, and the attack was called off.

A different storm that same month crossed the islands of the Caribbean, among them Puerto Rico. It is called the Great Hurricane of 1780. About 22,000 people died on the islands from October 10 to 16. Scientists at NOAA count 1780 as the deadliest Atlantic hurricane season on record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New Orleans burns twice, 1788 and 1794" -->
New Orleans was a Spanish town in 1788. On March 21 of that year, a fire started at about 1:30 in the afternoon in the house of Vicente José Núñez, the army treasurer. That day was Good Friday, the Christian holy day before Easter. Church bells were the town's fire alarm, but the priests refused to let the bells ring on Good Friday. The fire burned 856 of the town's 1,100 buildings. Governor Esteban Miró set up tents for the people who lost their homes.

On December 8, 1794, another fire burned 212 more buildings. Spanish officials then required new buildings to be built with thick brick walls. Builders rebuilt the town in brick, with courtyards and iron balconies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston, 1796" -->
On the afternoon of June 13, 1796, a fire started in Lodge Alley in Charleston and burned until nearly dawn. It burned the buildings on more than 250 lots and burned out at least 300 families. A committee of church leaders and town leaders handed out the money that other townspeople gave, according to what each family needed. The fire reached the roof of St. Philip's Church. An enslaved man named Will climbed up and saved it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="will-st-philips-church" name="Will" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Will

> **Who:** An enslaved man in Charleston who put out the fire on the roof of St. Philip's Church, and whom the church's leaders then bought and freed.
> **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, June to September 1796.

Will put out the fire on the bell tower of St. Philip's Church during the Charleston fire of June 13 to 14, 1796. He climbed to the very top of the tower and tore off the burning wooden shingles of its roof. In 1854 the writer Charles Fraser wrote that he did it "at the risk of his life."

Will was enslaved by Charles Lining. Lining was a member of the vestry, the board that ran St. Philip's Church. On August 14, 1796, two months after the fire, the vestry voted to reward Will by making him free.

The next month the vestry paid Charles Lining $707.14 for Will. Then the vestry freed him with a deed of manumission. Manumission means freeing an enslaved person by a legal paper.

The vestry's records include only his first name. A later history of the church describes the man who saved it as a Black boatman.
