## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 27 of 37 · Disasters and Rescue · 10 eras · about 130 minutes**

**Fires, floods, storms and earthquakes strike suddenly, but people's choices decided how many died and who got help, and most safety rules came only after the deaths.**

The South Fork Dam stood 69 feet high, 14 miles up the valley from Johnstown, Pennsylvania. It belonged to a club of rich Pittsburgh men. Their workers lowered its top by as much as 3 feet for a carriage road, and screened the channel that let extra water out, to keep the club's fish in. On May 31, 1889, after the heaviest rain ever recorded there, the dam broke and the flood killed 2,208 people. In court, two club members who were lawyers called it an "act of God," and the survivors lost.

This chapter follows sudden disasters in order, from a volcano in Oregon about 7,700 years ago to a flood in the Texas Hill Country in 2025. Each era has three things to look for. The first is the choice someone made before the disaster: a town built of wood, a locked door, a warning nobody passed on. The second is who got help afterward, and who was made to do the work or left to wait. The third is the rule that came after, from Boston's first fire wardens in 1711 to the smell put into natural gas in 1937.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- What rules did Americans make after disasters, and did they work?
- Was anyone punished when people's choices killed others?
- Did everyone get the same help after a disaster?

**Five minutes?** Read "The Triangle fire, March 25, 1911" in 1900 to 1950.
**Can skip:** "Fire insurance, 1752", "Hurricanes and the war on the Gulf Coast, 1779 and 1780", "Black Sunday, April 14, 1935". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 5 minutes**

Sandals woven from sagebrush bark lay under the ash of an Oregon volcano for thousands of years, and they show that people lived through this land's disasters long before anyone wrote one down. Those people belonged to Native nations. When ash buried their fields or the rain stopped for decades, many of them moved away. What is known comes from layers of ash, from the growth rings of old trees, and from the accounts the nations told to each new generation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two volcanoes" -->
Mount Mazama is a volcano in the Cascade Range of southern Oregon, about 60 miles north of the California line. About 7,700 years ago it erupted, and its top fell in. The eruption lasted only a few days. Scientists estimate that it was more than forty times as powerful as the eruption of Mount St. Helens in 1980. Its ash fell as far away as what is now central Alberta, in Canada.

Over several hundred years, rain and snow filled much of the hole that the collapse left behind. The water became Crater Lake. It is 1,943 feet deep, the deepest lake in the United States. In the Klamath language its name is *giiwas*.

**The sandals.** People had lived in the region for about 1,500 years or more before the eruption. In 1938 Luther Cressman, an archaeologist who studied the past from the things people left behind, dug in Fort Rock Cave, in Lake County, Oregon. Under a layer of ash from Mount Mazama, he found dozens of sandals woven from sagebrush bark.

Radiocarbon dating, a test that measures how much of one kind of carbon is left in something that was once alive, later showed that the sandals were more than 9,000 years old. Sandals of the same kind from half a dozen sites date from between about 10,500 and 9,100 years ago. The families who wore them lived there long before the mountain erupted.

The Klamath Tribes say that they have lived in the Klamath Basin for 13,000 years. They tell the eruption as a battle between Llao, chief of the Below World, and Skell, chief of the Above World. In their account, Llao attacked the people with fire after a chief's daughter refused him. Skell defended the people, and two holy men gave up their lives. Skell drove Llao back into Mount Mazama, the mountain fell in, and water filled the basin.

**Sunset Crater.** A second volcano, Sunset Crater, near what is now Flagstaff, Arizona, erupted around the year 1085. Scientists fixed the date from chemical changes in the growth rings of trees that were alive then. Ash fell on land 12 to 15 miles away and buried people's homes. Crops cannot grow in ash deeper than about 6 to 8 inches.

The Sinagua farmers who lived closest to the volcano were ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples. National Park Service scientists think they had to leave their fields because of the eruption and move farther away. Where the ash lay thin, it worked as a mulch, a covering that keeps soil from drying out, and crops there grew better. No record counts any dead. The people of the area told stories of the eruption, and those stories are still told today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How do tree rings show a drought?" -->
A tree adds one ring of new wood each year, and in a year of drought, with too little rain for crops, the ring is thin. Scientists read those rings in very old trees and wooden beams to find out which years were dry.

The rings show that the Colorado Plateau, in the dry Southwest, had too little rain from about 1130 to 1180. From 1140 to 1162 there were 23 dry years in a row. Susan Ryan, a researcher at the Crow Canyon Archaeological Center in Colorado, calls it the greatest drought recorded for North America. The Colorado River carried less water in the middle 1100s than at any other time in the past 1,200 years.

At Chaco Canyon, in present-day New Mexico, builders had stopped putting up the large stone buildings called great houses by about 1130. In the decades after that, the people of the Chaco towns moved away.

**The Great Drought.** A second drought, called the Great Drought, lasted from 1276 to 1299. Ryan found it less severe than the drought of the 1100s. By about 1300 the Pueblo people of the Mesa Verde region, in present-day southwestern Colorado, had moved south. They went to the Rio Grande valley, to Zuni and Acoma, and to the Hopi mesas, where rain came more reliably.

Archaeologists at Crow Canyon count the drought as one cause of that move among several. Other causes on their list are changes in how the communities got along, how they were ruled and how they made a living. In their view, the drought probably caused food shortages in a region crowded with people, and the hard times that followed may have led to quarrels and fighting.

Attackers killed at least 41 people at Castle Rock Pueblo and at least 8 at Sand Canyon Pueblo around 1280. Both places are in the Mesa Verde region. Kristin Kuckelman and two other scientists studied the skeletons. They found that someone had cut the bodies apart and broken, crushed and burned the bones, during or after the fighting. They also found signs that people probably ate human flesh there. No evidence shows who the attackers were, or who cut up the dead.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A fault under the northwest coast" -->
The outer layer of the earth is broken into huge slabs called plates. Off the coast of present-day Washington, Oregon and northern California, one plate slides under another. The line where they meet is a fault, a crack in the earth's crust where the rock on each side can move. Scientists call this one the Cascadia Subduction Zone, from subduction, one plate sliding down under another.

When the rock along a fault moves suddenly, the ground shakes. Scientists have found signs of 19 great earthquakes on this fault in the last 10,000 years. The most recent one struck in January 1700.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 6 minutes**

A hurricane in Pensacola Bay sank six Spanish ships in 1559 with the food for 1,500 colonists still on board. Spanish and English colonists of the 1500s wrote down the disasters they lived through, and this storm is one of them.

Two kinds of disaster hit the colonists hardest. Hurricanes, huge storms that form over warm ocean water with winds that spin in a circle, sank their ships and flooded their towns. Droughts left the colonists and their Native neighbors short of corn, and hungry colonists went looking for food in Native towns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A hurricane wrecks a colony, 1559" -->
A hurricane in September 1559 sank most of the ships of the largest Spanish colony yet sent to the Southeast. Nearly all of the colony's food went down with them. The colonists had landed on August 15, 1559, at Pensacola Bay in present-day Florida. The Native name of the bay was Ochuse. They came from Veracruz, in Mexico, on 11 ships, towing a 12th, smaller ship behind them.

About 1,500 people made the voyage. They included an army of 500 soldiers and about 200 Aztec warriors and craftsmen from Mexico City. Servants, enslaved Africans, and some wives and children came too. For five weeks they unloaded people and tools and began building a town on high, flat ground above the bay. They left the food for all 1,500 people on board the ships, because they thought it was safer there than on land.

**The storm.** One of the 11 ships, a galleon, had already sailed back to Mexico in late August or early September. On the night of September 19 to 20, 1559, a hurricane came into the bay. It sank six ships and pushed a seventh a short way onto the land. Three of the 11 ships came through the storm, and the small towed ship later carried supplies on relief voyages. The people who drowned were mostly the sailors and passengers still on board. No record gives their number.

After the storm the colonists did not have enough to eat. One ship came from Havana, in Cuba, with supplies soon after the hurricane. Relief ships from Mexico came after that, one every five to seven months. The colonists sent about 200 men inland to look for towns with food to spare. The men found a large town called Nanipacana, on the Alabama River in present-day central Alabama. Its people left before the Spaniards arrived. As they went, they destroyed the food they had stored.

Many colonists grew sick and died from lack of food. Native people attacked and killed others, and the records do not state which Native people they were. The colonists shipped many survivors back to Mexico. By September 1560, 362 people were left at the bay, and another 200 were far inland.

A new commander, Ángel de Villafañe, arrived in April 1561. He took most of the roughly 150 settlers still at the bay to Havana. Two small ships of his fleet later sank in another storm off Cape Hatteras, with everyone aboard. The last Spanish soldiers left the bay in August 1561. In February 1562 the viceroy, the Spanish king's governor in Mexico, gave up the colony.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tristan-de-luna-disasters" name="Tristán de Luna y Arellano" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tristán de Luna y Arellano

> **Who:** A Spanish noble and soldier who led the colony at Pensacola Bay when the hurricane of 1559 destroyed its ships and food.
> **When and where:** Pensacola Bay, Florida, and the Alabama River, 1559 to 1561.

**Luna ordered his hungry soldiers to march, and every one refused.**

Tristán de Luna y Arellano led the colony at Pensacola Bay. It was the largest Spanish attempt up to that time to settle the Southeast, and the one with the most money behind it. Years before, Luna had marched with the Spanish explorer Coronado.

**Looking for food.** From February to June 1560, Luna moved most of his people inland to Nanipacana, the town the first search party had found. In April he sent a second group of 200 men, under Mateo del Sauz, farther north to look for food. They went to Coosa, a chiefdom, a group of towns under one head chief, in present-day northwestern Georgia. They stayed there until November.

**The refusal.** Luna ordered 100 soldiers and 50 servants to march to Coosa in late summer 1560, on short rations, meaning with less food than they needed. All of his soldiers refused, and the march never took place.

When Ángel de Villafañe arrived at the bay with a small fleet in early April 1561, he dismissed Luna and ordered him back to Spain. Luna died in New Spain, the Spanish colony in Mexico, on September 16, 1573.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tristan-de-luna-disasters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The droughts behind the Lost Colony" -->
Tree rings from old bald cypress trees on the border of Virginia and North Carolina show two long droughts in the 1500s. A team of scientists led by David Stahle published the record in 1998. The first drought lasted from 1562 to 1571 and was worst from 1565 to 1569.

A Spanish priest in the Chesapeake Bay region, Father Juan Batista de Segura, wrote about it in September 1570. He wrote that the region had gone six years with too little corn and wild fruit, with hunger, death and dry soil.

**The driest years.** From 1587 to 1589 came the driest three years in the whole 800-year record. English colonists on Roanoke Island, in present-day North Carolina, disappeared during those years. They are known as the Lost Colony. The Stahle team counts the drought as one of several causes of the colony's end. An earlier English colony on Roanoke had come in wetter years, 1585 and 1586. Only 4 of its 108 people died.

The Croatan people were allies of the English colonists, and in 1587 their crops were poor. That same year the people of the town of Dasemunkepeuc killed George Howe, an adviser to the English governor, John White. They spoke an Algonquian language, one of a large family of Native languages along the coast.

They killed Howe in revenge. Men under Ralph Lane, leader of the earlier colony, had attacked and killed their leader, Wingina, in June 1586. After Howe's death the people of Dasemunkepeuc left their town.

**The wrong people.** John White led his soldiers in an attack on Dasemunkepeuc before daylight on August 8, 1587. The people they found there were Croatan, who had come to the empty town looking for food. White's men killed one of them and wounded many before they saw their mistake. The Stahle team links the killing to the drought, because hunger had brought the Croatan to the empty town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine burns and floods" -->
St. Augustine, in present-day Florida, was burned, raided or flooded again and again in its first 35 years. Spanish soldiers and colonists founded it in 1565 at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. The Timucua were tired of the Spaniards living in their town. In 1566 Timucua warriors shot flaming arrows at the fort. On April 1, 1566, the fort was half burned, and all its supplies were lost. The records do not state whether the arrows started that fire.

**Thirty-five hard years.** The colonists moved the town across the bay to Anastasia Island that year. In 1570 soldiers there rose against their officers and destroyed the fort. The sea was washing away the rebuilt fort by 1572. Native people attacked the town in 1577 and burned many houses. Then, in 1586, the English privateer Francis Drake, a ship captain who raided enemy ships and towns with his own government's permission, burned the whole town and its fort with his men.

A fire swept through the town in March 1599. It was built of wood, with roofs of thatch, meaning dried plants. In September of the same year a hurricane flooded the buildings that the townspeople had not yet repaired.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 8 minutes**

Of the 104 English colonists who landed at Jamestown in 1607, only 38 were alive a year later. The English colonies of the 1600s were that small, and one drought or one storm could kill a large share of the people in them.

Colonists built their towns mostly of wood, so a fire in one house could spread to the next. After two big fires, Boston's leaders bought a fire engine from England and ordered new buildings made of brick, with roofs of slate or tile. Governors and ordinary colonists wrote down what they lived through, in letters and journals full of detail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown: drought and a hurricane" -->
English colonists landed at Jamestown, in present-day Virginia, in April 1607. They built their fort inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan people. Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom, a group of many towns and chiefs under one head chief. Its head chief was Powhatan.

Chapter 26, Health, Disease, and Medicine, tells how salty river water, sickness and hunger killed most of the first colonists. Tree rings show that the colonists arrived during the driest seven years in 770 years, from 1606 to 1612. The drought hit the Powhatan towns as well as the English.

**The relief fleet.** The leaders of the Virginia Company of London, the business that ran the colony, sent nine ships and about 600 people to Jamestown on June 2, 1609, with farm animals and enough food to last a year. It was the largest English fleet yet to sail west.

A hurricane struck the fleet on July 24, 1609, about seven days from land. It drove the lead ship, the *Sea Venture*, away from the others. The ship began to leak so badly that one passenger wrote, "we almost drowned within."

The *Sea Venture* reached Bermuda, an island in the Atlantic. All 150 people on board got ashore, among them the colony's new governor, Sir Thomas Gates. Before the ship sank, the crew saved many of the supplies. The castaways stayed on Bermuda for ten months and built two small ships, the *Patience* and the *Deliverance*. The playwright William Shakespeare drew partly on their story for his play *The Tempest*.

The other eight ships reached Jamestown in late August 1609. Many passengers were sick, and the crews had thrown many of their supplies overboard in the storm.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Jamestown starved, 1609 to 1610" -->
Most of the people at Jamestown starved to death in the winter of 1609 to 1610, the winter called the Starving Time. The colonists had not farmed, hunted or fished to feed themselves. They lived on supplies from England and on food they bargained for from the Powhatan people, or often stole from them. In the drought the Powhatan towns had little food to spare.

The killing began on both sides in the autumn of 1609. The colonist Francis West cut off the heads of two warriors of the Patawomeck people while he was trading with them. In November 1609 warriors ambushed a party of colonists led by John Ratcliffe at Orapax. They tortured Ratcliffe to death. That same month Powhatan ordered his people to surround Jamestown and cut off its food.

**How many died.** About 500 colonists were in Virginia when the winter began. The historian Martha McCartney counts 240 of them in the fort at Jamestown. By May 1610 only about 60 of those 240 were alive. Some survivors dug up and ate the bodies of dead colonists and of Native people the colonists had killed.

The *Sea Venture* castaways reached Jamestown in their two small ships on May 24, 1610. Gates decided to give up the colony and sail home with the survivors. On the river they met Thomas West, Lord De La Warr, an English nobleman. He was bringing a year's supply of food, and everyone went back to Jamestown. Between 1607 and 1625, the company's leaders sent about 6,000 people to Jamestown. About 4,800 of them died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Colonial Hurricane, August 1635" -->
A hurricane struck New England on August 15, 1635. The English then used an older calendar that ran ten days behind the one used today. By today's calendar the date was August 25. William Bradford, the longtime governor of Plymouth Colony, wrote that no one living there, "either English or Indeans, ever saw" a storm like it (*Indeans* is his spelling of Indians).

South of Plymouth the sea rose more than 20 feet above its normal height. Bradford wrote that many Native people climbed into trees to escape the water. No record gives the number of Native people who drowned. The wind blew down houses and, in Bradford's words, "many hundered thowsands of trees." The worst of the storm lasted five or six hours.

The same storm wrecked the *Angel Gabriel*, a ship full of English settlers, at Pemaquid, in present-day Maine. Three or four passengers and one sailor died. Off Cape Ann, in Massachusetts, it wrecked a small boat carrying two families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anthony-thacher" name="Anthony Thacher" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Anthony Thacher

> **Who:** An English colonist who survived the wreck of a small boat in the hurricane of 1635 and described it in a letter to his brother Peter.
> **When and where:** Off Cape Ann, Massachusetts Bay Colony, August 11 to 15, 1635.

**Of 23 people on the boat, only he and his wife lived.**

Anthony Thacher wrote down what happened in a letter to his brother Peter, a few days after the wreck. On August 11, 1635, Thacher and his wife, Elizabeth, and their family left Ipswich, in Massachusetts, for the town of Marblehead. With them went his cousin John Avery, a minister, and Avery's family. The people of Marblehead had asked Avery to be their minister, and they sent a pinnace, a small sailing ship, to carry the two families.

Thacher's letter lists 23 people on board: 11 in Avery's family, 7 in his own, a man named William Elliot, and four sailors.

**On the rock.** At about 10 on the night of August 14, the wind split the pinnace's old sails. Before daylight the hurricane struck. The anchor dragged along the bottom, and the storm drove the boat onto the rocks of a small island. A wave threw Thacher onto a rock with his oldest daughter, Mary. His cousin Avery and Avery's oldest son joined them there.

The next wave broke the boat to pieces. It carried his wife toward the shore on a piece of the deck. All the others still on board drowned. Thacher's letter names four of his children who died. They were his oldest daughter Mary, his son William, his daughter Edith, and baby Peter, whom Edith was holding in her arms.

Another wave swept the four on the rock into the sea: Thacher, Mary, Avery and Avery's son. Thacher was in the water for at least a quarter of an hour before he crawled ashore.

**On the island.** He and his wife found a knapsack with a flint, a piece of steel for striking sparks from it, and dry gunpowder. They also found a drowned goat, two cheeses, some butter, a hat and his son William's coat. He struck a fire and they cooked some of the goat. They had only salty water to drink. He found the body of Avery's oldest daughter on the shore. She is buried on the island.

On the following Monday a passing boat picked them up. Thacher named the island "Thatchers Woe" and the rock "Avery's Fall." On September 3, 1635, the colony's lawmakers, meeting as the Massachusetts General Court, voted him 40 marks, an English unit of money, from the treasury toward his losses. In March 1637 they gave him the island. It is still called Thacher Island.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anthony-thacher" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An earthquake, 1638" -->
An earthquake shook New England on about June 1, 1638, by the old calendar. Bradford wrote that people heard it before they felt it. He compared the sound to faraway thunder. Dishes rattled and fell from shelves. People outdoors could not stand without holding on to posts. Native people felt it inland, and ships along the coast shook.

**How strong.** Scientists at Boston College's Weston Observatory estimate the 1638 earthquake at about magnitude 6.5, on the scale scientists use for an earthquake's strength. Each step up the scale, such as from 6 to 7, means the ground moves about ten times as far, and the quake lets out about 32 times as much energy.

The scientists think it was centered in central New Hampshire, but the few reports of the time make that uncertain. People felt the aftershocks, smaller quakes that follow a large one, for 20 days. No deaths were recorded.

Bradford wrote that the shaking looked like a sign that God was displeased. He added that for years afterward the summers were not warm enough for the corn to ripen well. He left the cause, in his words, "to naturallists to judge," meaning people who study nature.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston burns, and the town buys a fire engine" -->
A fire in Boston on January 14, 1653, destroyed many buildings and killed three children. After it, town officials hired the owner of a water engine, a pump that sprays water, to bring it to fires. They bought six long ladders, four strong iron hooks and fire buckets. They required every property owner to keep a ladder and a swab pole, a long pole with a mop on the end for putting out sparks on roofs.

A fire in Boston's North End destroyed 45 buildings on November 27, 1676. Among them were several warehouses, the North Meeting House, a church, and the home of its minister, Increase Mather.

**The first paid firefighters.** Town officials brought a fire engine from England in 1678 and hired men to run it. A Boston Public Library guide calls them the first paid fire department in what is now the United States. Officials also made a law that roofs be slate or tile and walls be brick. Large fires burned parts of Boston again in 1679, 1682 and 1691.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 10 minutes**

Every member of Philadelphia's Union Fire Company kept six leather buckets ready for the next fire. In these fifty years townspeople began to organize against the fires that kept burning their wooden towns. In 1722 about two-thirds of Boston's nearly 3,000 houses were built of wood. Boston got its first fire wardens after a fire in 1711, and neighbors in Boston and Philadelphia formed fire companies.

When a fire came, the help and the blame did not fall evenly. After Charleston burned in 1740, most of the money from London went to rich merchants. After a string of fires in New York in 1741, officials blamed enslaved Black people and poor white people and put at least 30 people to death. On the coasts, an earthquake in 1700 sent a tsunami across the Pacific to Japan, and a hurricane in 1715 wrecked a Spanish treasure fleet off Florida.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How Japan's records dated the Cascadia earthquake, 1700" -->
The Cascadia fault broke along about 620 miles of the Pacific coast at about 9 at night on January 26, 1700. The break ran from Vancouver Island, in present-day Canada, to northern California. Scientists at the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network call it one of the largest earthquakes known anywhere in the world. They put its size at about magnitude 9.

The coast dropped when the fault broke. Salt water flooded forests of cedar trees that had grown above the reach of the tides. The salt water killed the trees. Their dead trunks, called ghost forests, still stand along rivers and bays in Washington and Oregon.

**The waves in Japan.** The earthquake also set off a tsunami, a series of huge waves that can cross a whole ocean. The waves reached Japan the next day. At Miyako the water destroyed 13 houses, and a fire afterward burned 20 more. Near Nakaminato the waves drove a ship carrying rice onto rocks, and two sailors died.

The Japanese records describe the waves but no shaking. In 1996 scientists used those Japanese records to work out the day and the hour of the earthquake. Tree rings from the ghost forests confirmed the year.

No one counted the dead on the American coast. The Native nations there had no written records then. In their accounts, whole villages were lost. The Makah, in present-day Washington, tell of a great earthquake at night. The Quileute tell of a flood that carried canoes far inland. Archaeologists have found Native villages on the coasts of Washington and Oregon that sank, flooded and were left empty after 1700.

**The raised water.** Annie Miner Peterson, a Coos woman from Oregon, told what her grandfather had seen. Melville Jacobs, a scholar who was writing down Coos stories, recorded her words in 1933. Her grandfather had seen an old woman who survived the "raised water."

When the old woman was a girl, the water had left her hanging high in a tree. She tied her pack line, the rope she used to carry loads, to a branch to climb down, and she fell. The fall broke her back, and she had a humped back for the rest of her life. Scientists who studied the account date the flood she described to between 1690 and 1805.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A hurricane sinks the Spanish treasure fleet, 1715" -->
A hurricane drove eleven Spanish treasure ships onto the reefs of Florida's east coast early on July 31, 1715. At least 1,000 people died. The fleet of twelve ships had left Havana, in Cuba, a few days before, bound for Spain. The 1715 Fleet Society gives the day it sailed as July 24, and a NOAA writer gives July 27.

The eleven Spanish ships carried silver and gold from Spain's colonies in the Americas. They were wrecked along the coast from present-day Fort Pierce to Wabasso, north of Vero Beach. The twelfth ship, the French *Grifon*, escaped, because its captain had sailed farther from shore.

**The count.** About 2,500 people were on board the fleet. Scientists at NOAA, the US government's ocean and weather agency, count about 1,000 dead. The 1715 Fleet Society, a group that studies the wrecks, says well over 1,000. Spanish records from Cuba state about 1,500. The fleet's commander, Juan Esteban de Ubilla, was among the dead.

The survivors camped on the beach in the land of the Ais people. The Ais lived along the coast from Cape Canaveral to the Indian River. On August 6, Admiral Francisco Salmón sent the pilot Nicolás de India and 18 men in a small boat to Havana for help. They reached it 11 days later.

Ships from Havana and from St. Augustine brought emergency supplies, officials, soldiers and tools for salvage, which means bringing up goods from a wreck. By late October 1715 the salvagers had raised more than 5 million pieces of eight, the silver coins of Spain.

A man the records call Captain Barrett came for the silver too. Ned Grant was an enslaved man, and his enslaver hired him out to Barrett. Barrett took Grant to the wrecks to work as a diver.

**The pirates.** The pirates Henry Jennings and John Wills attacked the Spanish salvage camp in the winter of 1715 to 1716. The 1715 Fleet Society dates the raid to early January 1716 and says the pirates took about 120,000 pieces of eight. Other accounts put the raid in December 1715, name the pirate Charles Vane beside Jennings, and give the haul as about 87,500 pounds in English money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston's first fire wardens, 1711" -->
Boston's fire of 1711 began in a building of rented rooms off Cornhill on the evening of October 2. A Scottish woman named Mary Morse was working there with oakum, old rope fiber picked apart and used to seal the seams of ships. A flame nearby set it on fire.

By 2 in the morning the fire had burned about 100 buildings. Among them were the Town House, where the colony's government met, and the town's first meeting house, a building used as a church. The fire left 110 families without homes.

Four sailors climbed the meeting house steeple to save its bell. The roof fell in, and they died. Other people died when they went too close to the fire. Still others died in gunpowder blasts, because people fighting the fire blew up houses in its path so that it would have nothing to burn. The full count of the dead is not known.

**After the fire.** Members of Boston's churches raised 700 pounds, in English money, for the families who lost their homes. A few months after the fire, 12 men became Boston's first board of fire wardens. Each was in charge of the firefighting tools in one part of the town. Bostonians rebuilt the Town House and the meeting house in brick. The new Town House is known today as the Old State House.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston: a hurricane in 1700 and a fire in 1740" -->
A hurricane struck Charleston, South Carolina, on September 3, 1700. It knocked down thousands of trees and dozens of buildings. The Scottish ship *Rising Sun* sank at anchor. It carried people from a failed Scottish colony at Darien, in Panama. About 100 people on board drowned, including the captain.

A fire broke out in Charleston at 2 in the afternoon on November 18, 1740. A strong wind from the northwest carried burning pieces across the town. In less than a day the fire destroyed more than 300 houses and shops and several docks. The officers and crews of British navy ships in the harbor pulled down and blew up houses to stop it. The records that survive do not say whether anyone died.

Lieutenant Governor William Bull ordered every townsman to fight the fire, "by themselves and slaves." Townsmen were to send the people they enslaved to fight it as well. He called out the militia, the colony's armed citizens, to stop people from stealing from the ruins.

**Who got the money.** Most of the help from London after the fire went to the rich. Officials in London promised 20,000 pounds, in the king's name, for the people "most affected." They paid it out by the size of each person's losses, so most of it went to wealthy merchants, whose losses were large. Poorer people depended on gifts and loans from the vestry, the governing board of St. Philip's Church.

Mary Bedon, an innkeeper with three children, and Catharine Joor, a shopkeeper, lost their businesses in the fire and opened again in rented houses. By February 1741 the two women needed aid from the St. Philip's vestry to get through the winter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New York, 1741: fires and blame" -->
Thirteen fires broke out in lower Manhattan in March and April 1741. The first, in March, burned the governor's house and its chapel inside Fort George. Accounts give its date as March 8 or March 18. New York officials claimed that enslaved Black people and poor white people had plotted together to burn the city. A witness named Mary Burton told the court that the fires were such a plot.

Three judges, James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse, tried about 200 people. Officials burned 13 Black men alive at the stake, which means they tied each man to a post and set a fire around him. They hanged 17 Black men and 4 white people, two men and two women. Counts of the people put to death range from 30 to 34. Officials shipped 70 to 84 more people to the Caribbean islands to be sold into slavery there.

Historians disagree about whether any plot existed. Judge Horsmanden had offered Mary Burton, a young servant bound to a tavern keeper, an end to her years of service and a reward in money. Other enslaved witnesses spoke after they were promised their lives. Two of the men burned at the stake, Quack and Cuffee, had no lawyers at their trial. Chapter 24, Slavery and Freedom, tells the trials in full.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An earthquake in New England, 1727" -->
An earthquake shook New England at about 10:40 on the night of October 29, 1727. By today's calendar the date was November 10. Scientists at the Weston Observatory estimate it at about magnitude 5.6. At Newbury, Massachusetts, it knocked down many stone walls and the tops of almost all the chimneys.

Some solid ground turned to mud, and some marshes dried up. People felt it from Maine to the Delaware River, and smaller shocks went on for months. The records that survive show no one badly hurt.

**What the ministers said.** In Boston the minister Cotton Mather heard "a horrid rumbling like the noise of many coaches." Ministers in New England preached that God had sent the earthquake to show his anger. Writers blamed it on sins such as swearing and failing to keep the Sabbath, the day of rest. Towns such as Medford, Massachusetts, held a day of fasting, when people went without food and prayed.

John Barnard, a minister at Marblehead, came to a different view. He decided that the surface of the earth had moved because of shocks deep underground.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who came when a house caught fire?" -->
In Boston, groups of neighbors formed mutual fire societies. Members promised to help save one another's houses, and only those. On December 7, 1736, Benjamin Franklin and about 25 other men in Philadelphia signed the articles, or rules, of the Union Fire Company. Every Union member kept six leather buckets for water and two linen bags for carrying goods out of burning houses. They promised to come to every fire in the town, not only to the houses of members.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 8 minutes**

Massachusetts lawmakers voted 3,000 pounds in 1760 for the Boston families whose homes had burned, and lawmakers in Pennsylvania and New York sent money too. Help after a disaster was starting to come from public money that lawmakers voted, and not only from churches and neighbors.

Philadelphians started a company to insure houses against fire in 1752. After big fires, officials in Boston and New Orleans ordered new buildings made of brick. During the Revolutionary War, hurricanes sank Spanish warships and troop ships on the Gulf Coast, and a fire burned a large part of New York while British troops held it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston under water, 1752" -->
A hurricane pushed the sea more than 10 feet above the normal high-water mark at Charleston, South Carolina, on the morning of September 15, 1752. The water covered the whole town. People fled to the upper floors of their houses. Before 11 o'clock nearly every ship in the harbor had been thrown onto land. Only one, the British warship *Hornet*, rode out the storm.

About 500 buildings were destroyed. No one kept an exact count of the dead. Many people drowned, and others died when houses fell apart around them. A second storm on September 30 ruined crops and killed farm animals. Officials then limited how much corn, peas and rice could be shipped out of the colony, to keep food prices down.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire insurance, 1752" -->
Benjamin Franklin organized the Philadelphia Contributionship, a fire insurance company, in Philadelphia in 1752. Under fire insurance, many owners each pay a small amount into a shared fund, and the company that keeps the fund pays any owner whose house burns. The Contributionship's inspectors looked at each house before insuring it. They set the price by how likely the house was to burn. Insured houses carried a sign showing four hands.

Owners who insured with the Contributionship had to remove the trees from their land, because trees got in the way of firefighters. A rival company, the Mutual Assurance Company, let its customers keep their trees. People called it the Green Tree.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cape Ann earthquake, 1755" -->
An earthquake centered in the ocean about 25 miles off Cape Ann, Massachusetts, shook New England at about 4:30 in the morning on November 18, 1755. Scientists estimate it at about magnitude 6.2. In Boston the shaking lasted more than a minute. It threw down about 100 chimneys and damaged as many as 1,500 more. Some church steeples were left leaning. People felt it from Nova Scotia, in present-day Canada, to South Carolina. No deaths were recorded in the Boston area.

**The argument.** After the quake, a Boston minister and a Harvard professor argued in public over what had caused it. Seventeen days before the Cape Ann quake, a large earthquake had struck Lisbon, in Portugal. New England ministers held prayer services, and officials named days of fasting. Writers in New England published at least 27 sermons (talks given in church), poems and accounts about earthquakes after the one at Cape Ann.

The Boston minister Thomas Prince wrote that Boston had more lightning rods, metal rods on buildings that carry lightning into the ground, than any other New England town. He argued that the rods had drawn electricity into the ground and made the shaking worse. John Winthrop, a professor at Harvard College, answered him in lectures. He said that heat and gases deep in the earth had caused the earthquake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boston burns, and other colonies send help, 1760" -->
A fire started in a house on Cornhill in Boston at about 2 in the morning on March 20, 1760. No one ever found the cause. The fire burned 349 buildings, 174 of them homes. It left 220 families, more than 1,000 people, without homes. No one was killed. A Boston Public Library guide describes it as the worst fire in the American colonies up to that time.

Lawmakers in Massachusetts voted 3,000 pounds for the families who lost their homes. Lawmakers in Pennsylvania and New York voted to send money too. People in Nova Scotia raised money for Boston, and people as far away as London sent gifts. Afterward Massachusetts lawmakers passed a law that any building more than 7 feet high had to be made of brick or slate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fires in wartime: New York, 1776, and Charleston, 1778" -->
British troops under General William Howe took New York City in September 1776, during the Revolutionary War. On the night of September 20, a fire broke out near the southern tip of Manhattan. Accounts differ on the exact spot. John Joseph Henry, an American prisoner on a British ship, said it began in the Fighting Cocks Tavern, near Whitehall Slip.

Dry weather and strong winds spread the fire north and west. By morning it had burned between 400 and 1,000 buildings. That was between one tenth and one quarter of the city. Trinity Church burned.

**Killed without a trial.** British commanders blamed rebel arsonists, people who set fires on purpose. British soldiers and sailors killed a few men they suspected of setting the fire. They killed them on the spot, without a trial. The records do not give the men's names or an exact number. British officers questioned more than 200 suspects and charged none of them.

Historians still disagree about how the fire started. The historian Barnet Schecter found that no accusation of arson holds up. The historian Benjamin Carp argued in 2023 that rebels most likely set the fire on purpose. Gene Procknow, who reviewed Carp's book, wrote that Carp's evidence is not strong enough to prove it for certain.

**Charleston, 1778.** In Charleston, people found a fire in a bakery at 4 in the morning on January 15, 1778. By noon it had destroyed more than 250 houses and killed six people, both Black and white. People who had lost their homes got beds and food in the city's public buildings, paid for with public money. The next day South Carolina's lawmakers, meeting as the General Assembly, voted 20,000 pounds to help them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hurricanes and the war on the Gulf Coast, 1779 and 1780" -->
Spain's leaders joined the war against Britain on June 21, 1779. That year Bernardo de Gálvez, the Spanish governor of Louisiana, was getting ships ready to attack the British fort at Baton Rouge. A hurricane destroyed the ships in less than three hours. Gálvez had to put off his attack for ten days.

Another hurricane struck a Spanish fleet of 64 ships under Admiral José Solano on October 20, 1780. The fleet was carrying Gálvez and 4,000 soldiers from Havana to attack the British town of Pensacola, in Florida. An unknown number of the ships sank. A NOAA writer counts half of the soldiers, about 2,000, as lost. The NOAA account does not say how many of them drowned. The attack on Pensacola did not go ahead that year.

A different storm that same month crossed the islands of the Caribbean. It is called the Great Hurricane of 1780. About 22,000 people died in the Lesser Antilles, the chain of small islands at the eastern edge of the Caribbean, from October 10 to 16. The storm also struck Puerto Rico. Scientists at NOAA count 1780 as the deadliest Atlantic hurricane season on record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why New Orleans was rebuilt in brick" -->
New Orleans was a Spanish town in 1788. On March 21 of that year, a fire started at about 1:30 in the afternoon in the house of Vicente José Núñez, the army treasurer. That day was Good Friday, the Christian holy day before Easter. Church bells were the town's fire alarm, but the priests refused to let the bells ring on Good Friday. The fire burned 856 of the town's 1,100 buildings. Governor Esteban Miró set up tents for the people who lost their homes.

Another fire burned 212 more buildings on December 8, 1794. Spanish officials then required new buildings to be built with thick brick walls. Builders rebuilt the town in brick, with courtyards and iron balconies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charleston, 1796" -->
A fire started in Lodge Alley in Charleston on the afternoon of June 13, 1796, and burned until nearly dawn. It burned the buildings on more than 250 lots and left at least 300 families without homes. A committee of church leaders and town leaders handed out the money that other townspeople gave, according to what each family needed. The fire reached the roof of St. Philip's Church. An enslaved man named Will climbed up and saved it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="will-st-philips-church" name="Will" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Will

> **Who:** An enslaved man in Charleston who put out the fire on the roof of St. Philip's Church, and whom the church's leaders then bought and freed.
> **When and where:** Charleston, South Carolina, June to September 1796.

**The church's leaders bought him for $707.14, then set him free.**

Will put out the fire on the bell tower of St. Philip's Church during the Charleston fire of June 13 to 14, 1796. He climbed to the very top of the tower and tore off the burning wooden shingles of its roof. In 1854 the writer Charles Fraser wrote that he did it "at the risk of his life."

Will was enslaved by Charles Lining. Lining was a member of the vestry, the board that ran St. Philip's Church. On August 14, 1796, two months after the fire, the vestry's members voted to reward Will by making him free.

The next month they bought Will from Charles Lining for $707.14. Then they freed him with a deed of manumission, a legal paper that freed an enslaved person.

The vestry's records include only his first name. A later history of the church describes the man who saved it as a Black boatman.
