## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, Americans began to keep food fresh and sell it to strangers far away. Merchants sold food sealed in tin cans, and they shipped ice cut from frozen ponds. In the Deep South, planters spread cotton fields over land taken from Native nations. On the prairie of the Midwest, farmers used a new steel plow to turn thick grassland into fields of wheat and corn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Food in cans, and ice from ponds" -->
Food spoils when it sits too long. In 1809 a Frenchman named Nicolas Appert proved that food sealed in a container and then heated would keep. The method is called canning.

Americans soon took up canning. William Underwood opened a cannery in Boston in 1820 or 1821. In New York, Thomas Kensett canned seafood, fruit and vegetables. In 1825 he received an American patent for tin cans. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the sole right to make and sell an invention for a set number of years. Food sealed in a can could be sold far from the farm where it grew, to people the grower never met.

Other merchants sold ice. Workers cut blocks of ice from frozen ponds in New England in winter, including Walden Pond in Massachusetts. Merchants shipped the ice to cities. People used it to keep food, fresh fish and drinks cold. Families kept ice in a box called an icebox, which became a common piece of kitchen furniture.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederic-tudor" name="Frederic Tudor" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederic Tudor

> **Who:** A Boston merchant, called the Ice King, who built a business shipping New England pond ice to warm places around the world.
> **When and where:** Boston. First cargo of ice to Martinique in 1806. Ice to India in 1833. Died 1864.

Frederic Tudor built a business selling New England pond ice in hot countries far away. His first cargo of ice left Boston in 1806, bound for the Caribbean island of Martinique. A Boston newspaper, the *Boston Gazette*, printed: "No joke. A vessel has cleared at the Custom House for Martinique with a cargo of ice. We hope this will not prove a slippery speculation." A speculation is a risky business deal.

Much of the ice melted during the three-week voyage. Tudor sold what was left. He lost about $4,500 on the trip, though some accounts say $4,000. He kept at the business for years. In 1812 and 1813 he spent time in debtors' prison, a jail for people who could not pay what they owed.

On May 12, 1833, his ship, the *Tuscany*, sailed from Boston for Calcutta, in India. It carried 180 tons of ice cut the winter before. The ship reached India in September, about four months later. About 100 tons of the ice were still frozen. About 80 tons had melted on the way.

Tudor died in Boston on February 6, 1864.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederic-tudor" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton fields on Native farms" -->
Across the Deep South, planters grew cotton in place of food. Many planters bought corn and pork from Midwestern farms to feed the enslaved people who worked their fields.

The new cotton fields were the farms and hunting grounds of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations. In 1830 President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, federal troops forced these nations off their land and moved them west. On the Cherokee removal of 1838 and 1839, called the Trail of Tears, soldiers forced more than 16,000 people from their homes. More than 4,000 of them died.

Enslaved people did the work in the cotton fields. They worked in groups called gangs, from sunup to sundown, six days a week.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Breaking the prairie" -->
Farmers moving onto the prairie of Illinois found thick, sticky sod. Sod is the top layer of soil, held together by the tangled roots of grass. Prairie sod stuck to iron plows and stopped them.

John Deere was a blacksmith in Grand Detour, Illinois. In 1837 he made a plow of polished steel. The sod slid off its smooth blade instead of sticking, so the plow could cut through the prairie. By 1846 Deere was selling nearly a thousand plows a year.

Six years earlier, in 1831, Cyrus McCormick had shown his reaper at Steele's Tavern, Virginia. A reaper is a machine that cuts grain. Before it, farmers cut wheat by hand, with tools such as the sickle, a curved blade. A reaper cut grain many times faster than a person with hand tools, so a family could plant far more wheat. Within one generation, farmers had turned the grassland of Illinois and the lands west of it into fields of wheat and corn.

Native nations had lived on this land. In 1832 US soldiers and militia fought the Sauk and Meskwaki in the Black Hawk War. In 1833, at a treaty council in Chicago, Potawatomi leaders sold the last of their land in northern Illinois to the United States. Under the treaty, the Potawatomi received land in Iowa and had to leave. Deere made his steel plow four years later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The potato blight" -->
In 1843 a potato disease called late blight broke out near the ports of Philadelphia and New York. It is caused by a water mold, a tiny living thing that destroys potato plants. The blight spread through potato fields in the eastern United States. American farmers lost potato crops, but they had other food to eat.

By 1845 the blight had reached Europe. In Ireland, poor families depended on potatoes and had little other food. The blight destroyed their crop. During the Great Famine in Ireland, from 1845 to 1852, about 1 million people died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1900, growing, packing and shipping food were businesses that reached across the whole country. Under the Homestead Act, families claimed millions of acres of free farmland that had been taken from Native nations. Cowhands drove millions of cattle north from Texas, and meatpackers in Chicago shipped their beef east in cold railroad cars. Starting in 1867, farmers formed groups of their own to buy supplies together and to speak up against the rates railroad companies charged them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead farms on taken land" -->
The Homestead Act became law on May 20, 1862. Under it, a person could claim 160 acres of public land for free. To keep the land, the person had to live on it and farm it for five years. Women could claim land. After 1868 Black Americans could claim land too. Most Native people could not.

Over the years the law was in force, people filed about 4 million claims. About 1.6 million of them finished the five years and received a patent, the paper that made the land theirs. Together they received about 270 million acres, close to one tenth of all the land in the United States. About half of the people who filed claims never finished the five years.

All of this land had belonged to Native nations. US officials and soldiers had taken it by treaty, by purchase and by war. In 1889 officials opened land in Oklahoma that had been taken from Native nations, and settlers raced in to claim it. Under the Dawes Act of 1887, federal officials divided reservation land into plots for Native families. Much of the land that officials called surplus, meaning left over, went to white farmers. Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887. By 1934 they held about 48 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="oblinger-family" name="Uriah and Mattie Oblinger" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Uriah and Mattie Oblinger

> **Who:** A homesteading family whose letters describe the food and work of a Nebraska farm claim.
> **When and where:** Fillmore County, Nebraska. Uriah filed his claim in 1872. Mattie and their baby, Ella, arrived in May 1873. The family letters run from 1862 to 1911.

The letters of Uriah and Mattie Oblinger describe what one homestead family in Nebraska grew and ate in the 1870s. The Library of Congress and the Nebraska State Historical Society keep 318 of the family's letters, written from 1862 to 1911. Uriah and Mattie married on March 25, 1869.

In the fall of 1872, Uriah went ahead from Indiana to Nebraska. He filed a claim about eleven miles west of the town of Geneva, in Fillmore County. He broke the prairie sod and built a house out of blocks of sod. On December 1, 1872, he wrote about the other young families starting out around him: "we will all be poor here together." The Otoe-Missouria and the Pawnee had given up most of their land in this part of Nebraska in treaties of 1854 and 1857. Homesteaders began filing claims in the county in 1866.

Mattie and their baby daughter, Ella, joined Uriah in May 1873. On June 16, 1873, Mattie wrote home about the garden and the crops. She spelled words her own way. "Uriah has 23 acres of sod corn planted it looks real well," she wrote. "We have a nice lot of Squashes and Cucumbers & Mellons & Beans comeing on." She was growing tomato plants too. She bought milk and butter from a neighbor, Mrs. Furgison, and paid twelve cents a pound for the butter. "I like the sod house the best," she wrote. She also wrote that "every lick we strike is for our selves and not half for some one else."

Over the next years the family lived through blizzards, drought, swarms of grasshoppers and low prices for their crops. In February 1880 Mattie died giving birth, and her newborn son died with her. She was 36. She left three daughters, Ella, Stella and Maggie. Uriah moved the family to Minnesota that year. The family later lived in Nebraska, Kansas and Missouri, and came back to Nebraska in 1894.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="oblinger-family" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sharecropping: growing a crop someone else sold" -->
After the Civil War ended slavery, most freedpeople had no land of their own. Many became sharecroppers. A landowner gave a sharecropping family food, seed, tools and a cabin on credit. This was called the furnish. The family usually worked about twenty acres.

At harvest the landowner sold the crop, took out the cost of the furnish plus interest, and paid the family its share. The share was usually half of the crop's value or less. The landowner held a lien on the crop, which meant the landowner had a legal claim to it until the debt was paid. The landowner also kept the account books. Most sharecropping families stayed in debt to the landowner year after year. They grew food and cotton but did not control the money from selling it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison killed, the cattle driven north" -->
About 30 million bison lived on the Great Plains in the early 1800s. By 1900 fewer than 1,000 were left. One count in 1884 found 325. Hunters killed bison to sell their hides. Railroad building, disease and drought also shrank the herds. US Army officers encouraged the killing, because the herds were the food supply of the Plains nations. Sitting Bull's people were starving after the bison were gone, and they surrendered at Fort Buford in July 1881.

Cattle took the place of the bison on the grasslands. Between 1866 and 1884, cowhands drove about 5.2 million Texas cattle north to railroad towns. In 1867 Joseph McCoy built pens and a market for cattle at Abilene, Kansas, beside the railroad. Herds came up to it along the Chisholm Trail. In Texas, as many as one cowhand in four was Black. Many others were Mexican or Mexican American.

The long drives ended once ranchers fenced the open range with barbed wire. Joseph Glidden received a patent for barbed wire on November 24, 1874. Quarantine lines, which kept cattle from some areas to stop the spread of disease, also closed the trails.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-goodnight" name="Charles Goodnight" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Goodnight

> **Who:** A Texas cattleman who invented the chuckwagon, the kitchen on wheels that fed cowhands on cattle drives.
> **When and where:** Texas, 1866.

Charles Goodnight invented the chuckwagon in 1866. A cattle drive crossed open grassland for months, and its crew had to eat every day. Goodnight took an old Army wagon and built a pantry box onto the back. The side of the box was hinged. It dropped down flat to make a table where the cook could work.

The chuckwagon carried the food for the trail, such as beans, bacon, biscuits and coffee. On a drive, the cook ranked second only to the trail boss. The characters of the story *Lonesome Dove* are made up, but some are partly based on Goodnight.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-goodnight" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beef from the Plains to a city plate" -->
The Union Stock Yards opened in Chicago on December 25, 1865. Stock yards are pens where cattle, hogs and sheep are held until they are sold. The Chicago yards became the largest market for farm animals in the world.

The meatpacker Gustavus Swift shipped beef east from Chicago already butchered, instead of shipping live animals. In 1878 an engineer named Andrew Chase designed a railroad car for Swift that was cooled with ice. Beef packed in it stayed cold all the way to the cities of the East. A steer could now be raised in Texas, killed in Chicago and eaten in New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farmers join together" -->
In 1867 Oliver Hudson Kelley founded the Patrons of Husbandry, better known as the Grange. Kelley was a Minnesota farmer and a clerk in the federal Department of Agriculture. The Grange was the first large farmers' group that reached across the whole country. Members met in Grange halls. They bought supplies together to get lower prices. They also spoke up together against the rates the railroads charged to carry their crops.

In 1887 members of Congress passed the Hatch Act, named for Representative William Hatch of Missouri. Hatch led the House committee on farming. Under the act, each state's land-grant college received $15,000 a year at first to run a farm experiment station. A land-grant college is a state college started with federal land. At these stations, scientists studied soil, seed and farm animals and sent their results to farmers. There are stations today in all 50 states.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
