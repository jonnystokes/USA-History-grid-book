## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 15 of 37 · Food and Farming · 10 eras · about 75 minutes**

**American food began with crops that Native farmers bred, and since then much of it has been grown on taken land by people who were enslaved or paid little.**

On Cape Cod in November 1620, men from the *Mayflower* found baskets of corn buried in the ground. Nauset farmers had buried the corn to plant the next spring. The Englishmen dug it up and named the spot Cornhill. They wrote that they would "satisfy them for their corn," meaning they would pay the owners later. It was the first corn the Plymouth colonists ate. Native farmers had spent thousands of years turning a wild grass into that corn.

This chapter follows American food from those fields to the drive-in and the lab. Watch two things as you go. The first is whose land and whose work the food came from: Native farmers, enslaved Africans in the rice and cotton fields, families who farmed for a share of the crop, Mexican farmworkers, and today's crop pickers. The second is how far the food travels. When the country began, about nine of every ten working people were farmers. Then came tin cans, pond ice, cold railroad cars, frozen dinners and fast food. In 2022 the country had fewer than 2 million farms for the first time since before the Civil War.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did Native farmers turn a wild grass into corn?
- Who did the hardest farm work, and what did they get for it?
- How did Americans come to eat food grown far away by people they never met?

**Five minutes?** Read "Who got the cotton money?" in 1900 to 1950.
**Can skip:** "Farming began in the East too", "Why turn corn into whiskey?", "The potato blight", "Organic farming rules become law". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 5 minutes**

Native farmers turned a wild grass into corn, and by 1500 corn fed whole towns. Farming here was already thousands of years old. People in each region also ate what grew or lived best around them: bison on the Plains, salmon in the Northwest, wild rice around the Great Lakes.

Europeans later carried corn, beans and squash from these fields across the ocean. Corn became an everyday food in Europe, Africa and Asia.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How corn was made" -->
Native farmers made corn out of a wild grass called teosinte. A teosinte seed head holds only a few hard kernels. Farmers began changing it about 9,000 years ago in the Balsas River valley of southern Mexico. Each year they took seed only from the plants they chose and planted it again, so the crop changed a little at a time. After thousands of years of this breeding, the grass had become corn.

Scientists have confirmed the date and the place in two ways. Ancient corn dug from the ground shows it, and so do the genes of corn and teosinte, the tiny instructions inside every living thing that parents pass on to their young.

Corn is so changed from the wild grass that it cannot reseed itself. It grows only where people plant it. Native farmers grew it a little farther north at a time, over thousands of years, until it reached what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Three Sisters" -->
Native farmers planted corn, beans and squash together in the same field, and each crop helped the other two grow. The three crops are called the Three Sisters.

The corn grew tall, and the bean vines climbed the corn stalks. Bean roots hold tiny living things called bacteria. These bacteria take nitrogen, a gas in the air, and put it into the soil. Plants need nitrogen to grow, so the beans fed the soil that all three plants shared. The squash spread wide leaves over the ground, and their shade kept water in the soil and blocked the sunlight that weeds needed.

Corn and beans eaten in the same meal also feed people better than either food does alone. Together they give the body a fuller mix of protein, the part of food the body uses to build muscle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farming began in the East too" -->
Native people in the eastern part of North America started farming on their own, long before corn reached them. They took wild plants and bred them into crops. About 5,000 years ago they had tamed a kind of squash.

They also grew six plants for their seeds: sunflower, goosefoot, sumpweed, maygrass, little barley and erect knotweed. Sunflower and sumpweed seeds are rich in oil. The other four have starchy seeds. Maygrass and little barley are grasses whose grain can be ground into flour, and people cooked and ate goosefoot's small seeds.

Scientists call this group of crops the Eastern Agricultural Complex. Eastern North America is one of the places in the world where people started farming on their own, without learning it from anyone else.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Foods by region" -->
Cahokia was a Native town near where St. Louis is today. Around the year 1100, between about 10,000 and 20,000 people lived there, and corn farming fed them.

People in other regions lived mainly on other foods. On the Great Plains, hunters killed bison. By a widely used estimate, about 30 million bison still lived there in the early 1800s. Along the Northwest coast and its rivers, salmon was the main food. People on the coasts gathered shellfish. In the Northeast, people collected sap from maple trees.

Around the Great Lakes, people harvested manoomin, a wild rice that grows in shallow water, from canoes. In Ojibwe, the word means "the good berry." The Ojibwe are one of the Anishinaabe peoples, a family of related nations that also includes the Odawa and the Potawatomi. In an Anishinaabe prophecy, a teaching about what was to come, the people were led to the place where they would find "the food that grows on water." Manoomin is still a central food of Ojibwe life.

No farmer from before 1500 is known by name. Because corn cannot reseed itself, every generation of Native farmers had to save the seed and plant it again. Seed keepers in Native nations still grow these crops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

European ships began carrying crops and farm animals across the Atlantic in both directions in the 1500s. Corn, potatoes and turkeys went east to Europe, and wheat, cattle and pigs came west. Africans, most of them carried across in chains by slave traders, brought crops of their own. The historian Alfred W. Crosby named this movement the Columbian Exchange, in his 1972 book of that name. Native people here still grew and ate their own foods, and in Florida a Spanish army lived on the corn it took from them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What crossed the ocean" -->
Europeans carried American crops and animals home to Europe. These included corn, potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, peanuts, peppers, vanilla, turkeys and cacao, the bean that chocolate is made from. Corn and potatoes later became main foods across Europe, Africa and Asia.

Europeans brought wheat, rice, sugarcane, citrus fruit (the family of fruit that includes oranges and lemons), and honeybees to the Americas on their ships. They also brought cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and chickens. Columbus brought horses on his second voyage, in 1493. Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565, and began a colony in New Mexico in 1598. In both places they planted wheat and citrus and raised cattle.

The word exchange can sound like a friendly trade, but Europeans took American crops home from lands they were invading. Much of what arrived was grown to feed the colonists. Before long, planters were forcing enslaved people to grow sugarcane on plantations.

Slave traders carried most of the Africans who came to the Americas across the ocean in chains. Those Africans brought their own crops and the skill to grow them, among them okra, watermelon, yams, sorghum, black-eyed peas and African rice.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="De Soto's army and Apalachee corn" -->
The Spanish soldier Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay, in Florida, in May 1539, with an army of more than 600 people. He carried no food supplies except a small herd of pigs. Men who marched with him wrote accounts of the trip, and those accounts say the herd began with about 13 pigs. By 1542 it had grown to about 700. The army rarely killed the pigs for food, and pigs that escaped ran wild across the Southeast.

De Soto's army moved from town to town and took the corn that Native villagers had stored, and the soldiers ate it. Without that corn, the villagers were often at risk of starving. De Soto also seized dozens of Native men, women and children. His soldiers forced them to carry the army's equipment and supplies and to do the work of the camp.

**The captives.** Rodrigo Ranjel, de Soto's secretary, kept a diary of the march. An account written from it states that the Spaniards took the men "to have more slaves and servants." Some of the captives died and others ran away, so the soldiers seized more. The same account records that the soldiers wanted the women "for their lewdness and lust," which means they wanted them for sex. The women were prisoners and could not refuse. The soldiers raped them, forcing them to have sex.

De Soto's army seized Anhaica, the main town of the Apalachee, in October 1539. Tallahassee, Florida, stands there today. The army lived in the town until March 1540 and took its stored food, which was enough to feed 600 men and 220 horses for five months.

Archaeologists, scientists who study the past by digging up what people left behind, have found burned corncobs at the site. The Apalachee fought back with quick raids and ambushes, and they went after the Spaniards' horses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 6 minutes**

Native corn kept the first English colonists at Jamestown and Plymouth alive in the 1600s. The colonists bought some of that corn and grew some of it with Native teaching, and they took some by force. In Virginia, planters then grew tobacco in place of food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown: hunger, and corn the English took by force" -->
The colonists at Jamestown, in Virginia, planned to live partly on food they traded from the Powhatan people and partly on supply ships from England. In the autumn of 1609, Powhatan leaders stopped trading food with them, and Powhatan warriors surrounded the fort. The winter of 1609 to 1610 is called the Starving Time. About 500 colonists were alive when it began. By spring, about 61 were still alive.

The colonists ate their horses, dogs and rats, and they ate leather. Some ate the bodies of people who had died. Archaeologists found the bones of a girl whom researchers call Jane. The bones show that colonists ate her body after she died. Scientists at the Smithsonian announced the finding in 2013.

**Percy's raid.** English soldiers also took corn by force and destroyed Native fields. On August 10, 1610, George Percy led English soldiers against the Paspahegh town near Jamestown. They burned the houses, cut down the cornfields and took corn. Percy wrote that they killed 15 or 16 people. Other accounts put the number of villagers killed as high as 75.

The soldiers captured the wife and two children of Wowinchopunck, the Paspahegh weroance, or leader. When Percy hesitated, his men complained. Percy then agreed to kill the children. His soldiers threw the children overboard from a boat and shot them in the head in the water.

That night at Jamestown, Captain Davis told Percy that Lord De La Warr, the colony's governor, wanted the children's mother burned alive. Captain Davis and two soldiers took her into the woods and killed her with a sword. Percy wrote that he did not believe the order really came from the governor.

**Ten years of raids.** Powhatan warriors attacked the English settlements in March 1622. After that, colonial soldiers marched out almost every summer for ten years to attack Powhatan towns and cornfields. At a peace meeting in 1623, colonists poisoned the Powhatans' wine, shot them, and then destroyed their cornfields.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why Virginia planters grew tobacco instead of food" -->
Tobacco sold for so much money that many Virginia planters grew it in place of food crops. John Rolfe grew the colony's first successful tobacco crop in 1612, from a sweet kind of tobacco.

Members of Virginia's assembly, the colony's lawmaking body, passed laws again and again to make planters grow food. Under a tobacco law of 1629 and 1630, a family could grow 2,000 tobacco plants for each family member and no more. The lawmakers set the limit partly so that families would still plant food crops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth: corn dug up, then corn planted" -->
The first corn the Plymouth colonists ate was corn they took. In November 1620, men from the *Mayflower* went exploring on Cape Cod. There they found baskets of corn that the local people had buried in the ground. The owners were saving it as seed for the next spring.

The Englishmen dug up about ten bushels of the corn (a bushel holds about 8 gallons) and a bag of beans. They wrote that they planned to "satisfy them for their corn," which meant to pay the owners later. They named the place Cornhill.

The same group of men dug up graves nearby. They opened bundles that held the bones of a man and a small child. They wrote that they "brought sundry of the prettiest things away," meaning they took several of the objects buried with the dead.

The owners of the corn were Nauset people. In the summer of 1621, the colonists met one of them and promised to pay him. They offered to bring the owners as much corn as they had taken. Later in 1621 the colonist Edward Winslow wrote that the owners had been given "full content," meaning they were fully satisfied. His letter does not say what they were given.

The colonists built their town on the land of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. An epidemic, a disease that spreads quickly through many people, had killed most of the people of Patuxet between 1616 and 1619. One reason the colonists gave for settling there was the "good corn-ground ready to our hands." The Patuxet people had cleared those fields.

The colonists planted about 20 acres of corn in the spring of 1621. The corn did well, and their first harvest, that autumn, fed them through their second winter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="squanto-food-farming" name="Squanto (Tisquantum)" movie="Squanto: A Warrior's Tale (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Squanto (Tisquantum)

> **Who:** A Patuxet man who taught the Plymouth colonists to plant corn, after an English captain had kidnapped him.
> **When and where:** Patuxet, where the colonists built Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts. Kidnapped 1614, home 1619, taught the colonists in the spring of 1621, died 1622.
> **Movie:** *Squanto: A Warrior's Tale* (1994), a feature film that mixes his real story with invented events.

**He taught the colonists to bury fish with their corn.**

Squanto, also called Tisquantum, showed the Plymouth colonists how to plant corn in the spring of 1621. He also interpreted between the colonists and the Wampanoag, which means he put each side's words into the other's language.

An English captain named Thomas Hunt kidnapped Squanto in 1614 and took him to Spain to be sold as a slave. Squanto reached England and learned English there. In 1619 he came home with an English expedition and found his town of Patuxet empty. An epidemic had killed most of its people.

The colonist Edward Winslow described the planting in a letter of December 1621. Following "the manner of the Indians," he wrote, "we manured our ground with herrings, or rather shads," two kinds of fish. The colonists buried the fish in the soil with the corn seed to feed the crops, and Squanto taught them to do it.

Scholars have argued for decades about where Squanto learned the method. In 1975 Lynn Ceci, an anthropologist (a scientist who studies how people live), argued in the journal *Science* that burying fish with corn was a European method. She found little evidence that many Native farmers used fish this way, and she argued that Squanto probably learned it during his years among Europeans. Other scholars defended the older account, the one Winslow gave.

**Known:** Squanto showed the colonists how to bury fish with their corn.
**Not known:** Where he learned the method, from Native farmers or among Europeans, is still not settled.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="squanto-food-farming" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the colonists ate" -->
The English colonists learned corn dishes from Native cooks, and Americans went on eating them for centuries. One was hominy, which is dried corn cooked in water. For samp, cooks pounded corn into coarse meal and boiled it into a mush, and the name comes from the Narragansett word *nasàump*. Johnnycakes were flat cakes baked from cornmeal. Along with their corn, the colonists ate beans, and once their hogs had multiplied, they ate pork and drank cider made from apples.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 6 minutes**

Planters in the Carolina Lowcountry, the flat, wet land along the coast of South Carolina, became some of the richest people in the colonies by forcing enslaved Africans to grow rice. The enslaved Africans built the rice fields and did the work in them. Planters paid more for captives from West Africa who already knew how to grow rice.

Farther north, farmers in Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey grew more wheat than they could eat and shipped flour to other places. New Hampshire's town histories say that settlers there planted the first potato patches in North America that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carolina rice and African skill" -->
English settlers first brought a small amount of rice to Charles Town, now Charleston, in 1670. No record shows that anything grew from it. By July 1699, samples of Carolina rice were on sale in London to test whether buyers wanted it. The first crops grew on dry, high ground. By the 1720s, planters had moved their rice into freshwater swamps inland, and they had enslaved workers build dams to hold the water in ponds.

**The rice boom.** Carolina planters shipped out about 269,000 pounds of rice a year from 1698 to 1702. Forty years later, from 1738 to 1742, they shipped out more than 30 million pounds a year, more than a hundred times as much. One kind of Carolina rice later became known as Carolina Gold.

Planters put out advertisements asking for captives from the part of West Africa known as the Rice Coast. That coast included Senegambia, the land along the Senegal and Gambia rivers, and Sierra Leone. Planters paid more for people from there because those people knew how to grow rice.

**African methods.** Records from Carolina fields show African methods at work. Enslaved workers built earth banks around fields beside the tidal rivers so they could flood and drain them. They sowed seed by pressing it into the mud with the heel and toe. They separated the grain from its husks by tossing it in coiled baskets called fanner baskets.

**The argument.** Scholars disagree about how much of Carolina's rice farming came from African knowledge. Most of them now hold a middle view: planters built the rice business with their money and by force, and they also depended on African skill. In her 2001 book *Black Rice*, the geographer Judith Carney, a scientist who studies the land and how people use it, argued that African knowledge was the base of the whole rice business.

The historians David Eltis, Philip Morgan and David Richardson argued in 2005 that she had made the African share too large. Records from the colony also show that in its early years, more captives came from Angola than from any other place, and Angola is not on the Rice Coast.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the rice grew on" -->
The Kiawah, Etiwan and Stono people lived along the Ashley, Cooper and Stono rivers. They were still there in 1670, when English colonists settled among them at Charleston Harbor.

English colonists captured Kussoe people who fought back in 1671, and Stono people in 1674, and sold them into slavery in the Caribbean. The Kiawah left the Ashley River in the early 1680s and moved to the island now named for them. By then no nation of the Lowcountry had more than fifty bowmen, and the Stono had sixteen hunters. In the spring of 1684, leaders of eight nations signed away their land claims to the English who ran the colony.

English colonists in Carolina made their biggest profits before 1720 from two kinds of goods they shipped out. One was ship supplies, such as tar made from pine trees. The other was Native people, whom they sold as slaves. In 1770 a colonist named William Bull wrote of the Native nations along the coast that "there remains now, except a few Catawbas, nothing of them but their names, within three hundred miles of our sea coast."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The rice workers, unnamed in the records" -->
Planters' records list the enslaved rice workers by first name, age and price. They say almost nothing else about them. No single rice grower from these years can be followed through life in the records.

Planters gave each enslaved worker a set task every day. The usual task was to work a quarter of an acre of land. A worker who finished the task had the rest of the day. Workers labored in direct sun, standing in water or mud, among mosquitoes, horseflies, snakes and alligators.

Enslaved Africans dug the banks and canals of the rice fields. Nic Butler, a historian at the Charleston County Public Library, has written that the earth they moved across the Lowcountry is about as much as all the great pyramids of Egypt put together.

More enslaved people died in the Lowcountry than were born there until the 1760s. Many of the dead were children. Planters kept buying new captives to replace them, and by 1708 more Black people than white people lived in South Carolina.

**The slave ships.** Slave traders rarely landed more than 300 enslaved Africans a year at Charles Town in 1710. By 1720 they landed more than 1,000 a year, and by 1770 more than 3,000. More than four of every ten Africans brought to North America in the slave trade came in through Charleston Harbor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney

> **Who:** A planter's daughter who ran her family's South Carolina plantations as a teenager and made the colony's first successful indigo dye.
> **When and where:** Wappoo plantation, near Charleston, South Carolina. Indigo experiments from 1739, first successful dye in 1744.

**She was about 16 when she took charge of three plantations.**

Eliza Lucas produced South Carolina's first successful dye from indigo, a plant that colors cloth blue, in 1744. Indigo is a farm crop but not a food.

Her father, George Lucas, went back to the island of Antigua, in the Caribbean, in 1739 to serve as its lieutenant governor, the official just below the governor. Eliza was about 16. She took charge of Wappoo plantation and of the 20 enslaved people who worked it. She also oversaw the family's two other plantations, which were much larger.

That same year she began experimenting with indigo seed. Turning the plant into dye was skilled work done by experts. She first worked with an indigo maker from the island of Montserrat. Her best results came with an indigo maker of African descent whom her father hired from the French islands of the Caribbean.

She used her 1744 crop to make seed and gave the seed to other planters. Planters in South Carolina shipped out about 5,000 pounds of indigo in the shipping year 1745 to 1746. By 1748 they shipped out 130,000 pounds. She later married and took the name Pinckney.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wheat, potatoes and everyday meals" -->
Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey were called the breadbasket colonies. Farmers there grew more wheat than their families needed. Merchants shipped the extra wheat as flour out of the ports of Philadelphia and New York.

Settlers known as the Scots-Irish, families of Scottish background who had lived in the north of Ireland, planted potatoes at Londonderry, New Hampshire, in 1719. New Hampshire's histories count their patches as the first lasting potato fields in North America. The potato first grew as a crop in the Andes mountains of South America. People carried it to Europe, and settlers later brought it back across the ocean to North America.

Most colonial families ate corn, pork and bread, and drank cider. Rich families also bought tea, sugar and wine from overseas, and they ate bread made of wheat.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 5 minutes**

About nine of every ten working people in the United States were farmers when the country began, by a widely used estimate from the US Department of Agriculture. The first census, a count of the people, was taken in 1790. It counted about 4 million people.

Thomas Jefferson wrote that farmers were God's chosen people, while people he enslaved farmed his land. In 1796 Amelia Simmons became the first American to write a cookbook for American kitchens, and some of its recipes used American cornmeal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-patten" name="Matthew Patten" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Patten

> **Who:** A New Hampshire farmer, carpenter and judge whose diary records what his family grew, caught and ate.
> **When and where:** Bedford, New Hampshire, on the Merrimack River. His surviving diary runs from 1754 to 1788.

**In ten days of fishing, he sent 354 fish home.**

Matthew Patten kept a diary that shows how one farm family in New Hampshire fed itself. He headed each little booklet of it "A Day Book continued." His entries for the year 1767 record the family's food through the seasons.

Patten was a joiner, a carpenter who does the finer woodwork in a house. He also farmed, worked as a judge, and surveyed land, which means he measured it and marked its borders.

**Winter.** A neighbor, James Gilmore, paid Patten three pecks of corn, about 24 quarts, for surveying his land on January 3, 1767. That same day Patten bought tea and 7 pounds of sugar, and he took corn and rye to a neighbor's mill to be ground into meal and flour.

He butchered the family's hogs on January 30. In February and March, other neighbors paid him in bushels of corn for surveying and for writing deeds, the papers that prove who owns a piece of land.

**Spring.** The family's potatoes had spent the winter buried in a pit. Patten opened it on March 4 and wrote that they were "sound and good." In April and May the family sowed peas, then planted potatoes, corn, barley, and hemp and flax, plants whose fibers are made into rope and cloth. He wrote that "the boys" planted the field behind the barn with corn and potatoes and spread manure from the barn on it.

**Fishing.** Patten spent a May night helping haul a net in the Merrimack River, from dark to sunrise. His share was 60 shad, a fish the family took from the Merrimack each spring. From May 28 to June 6 he fished at Amoskeag Falls and sent 354 fish home.

The name Amoskeag comes from a Pennacook word that means roughly "good fishing place." Long before Bedford was a town, Pennacook people strung large nets across the river at the falls and caught sturgeon, salmon and a fish called the alewife. Bedford lies in Pennacook country.

**Summer.** Patten took salmon weighing from 6 to 19 pounds in June and July and split them with two neighbors. In August he reaped his rye and wrote, "I have 40 Stooks in all this year" (a stook is a bundle of cut grain stood up to dry). Later that month he mowed his meadow and counted 205 piles of hay.

Patten died on August 27, 1795, in one of the town's fields. He had carried dinner out to the men who were mowing.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="matthew-patten" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson's farmers and Monticello's rations" -->
Thomas Jefferson believed that family farms made free citizens. He wrote about the idea in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, first printed in 1785: "Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever he had a chosen people." For about a hundred years afterward, American leaders wrote land laws meant to put land in the hands of family farmers.

People Jefferson enslaved did the farm work at Monticello, his plantation in Virginia. He enslaved more than 600 people during his adult life. In the years Jefferson had tobacco grown at Monticello, enslaved children picked worms off the tobacco plants.

**The rations.** Each week, Jefferson gave each enslaved worker at Monticello a set amount of food, called a ration: a peck of cornmeal, half a pound of pork or pickled beef, and four salted fish. Nursing mothers received extra, and workers got whiskey at harvest. Researchers who studied the Monticello records found that these rations were not enough food for a person to live on. So in their little free time, enslaved people hunted, fished, trapped, kept gardens and raised animals.

Enslaved families also sold chickens and vegetables from their own gardens and flocks to the Jefferson family. Jefferson's granddaughter Anne Cary Randolph wrote down these purchases in the household accounts. Most of the sellers she recorded were enslaved people who worked in the house, many of them members of the Hemings family.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why turn corn into whiskey?" -->
Farmers in the backcountry, the land far from the coast beyond the older farms and towns, could not easily haul wagons of corn over the long roads to buyers. So they turned their extra corn into whiskey, which they could carry to market in jugs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-simmons" name="Amelia Simmons" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Simmons

> **Who:** The author of *American Cookery*, the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens.
> **When and where:** Hartford, Connecticut, 1796.

**On her title page she called herself "an American orphan."**

Amelia Simmons wrote *American Cookery*, the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens. It was printed in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1796. Beyond those words on her title page, nothing about her life is known.

Her book contains the first known printed recipes that used American cornmeal. Among them were johnnycake, "Indian slapjack" and Indian pudding. Colonists called corn "Indian corn" and cornmeal "Indian meal," so the word Indian in these names meant that the dishes were made with cornmeal.

It also contains the first printed recipe that used pearlash, a salt made from wood ashes, to make dough rise. Bakers used pearlash before baking powder existed. The book includes some of the earliest printed uses of the words "cookey" (cookie) and "slaw" (a salad of shredded cabbage), which came into American English from Dutch settlers in America.
