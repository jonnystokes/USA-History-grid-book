## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Farming in North America was thousands of years old by 1500. Native farmers had turned a wild grass into corn, and corn fed whole towns. Each region also ate from the foods that grew or lived best there, such as bison on the Plains and salmon in the Northwest.

Several crops grown in these fields later became everyday food around the world. Among them were corn, beans and squash.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How corn was made" -->
Corn did not grow wild. Native farmers bred it from a grass called teosinte. The seed head of teosinte holds only a few hard kernels. Farmers began changing it about 9,000 years ago in the Balsas River valley of southern Mexico. Scientists have confirmed that date and place with genetics and with finds dug from the ground.

Breeding a plant means choosing which plants to take seed from, year after year, so the crop changes over time. Native farmers bred teosinte for thousands of years, and the result was corn. Corn is so changed from the wild grass that it cannot reseed itself. It grows only where people plant it. Over many centuries, farmers carried corn north into what is now the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Three Sisters" -->
Native farmers planted corn, beans and squash together in the same field, and each plant helped the other two grow. The three crops are called the Three Sisters.

The corn stalk grew tall and gave the bean vine a pole to climb. Bean roots hold tiny living things called bacteria. These bacteria take nitrogen, a gas in the air, and put it into the soil. Plants need nitrogen to grow, so the beans fed the soil that all three plants shared. The squash spread wide leaves over the ground. The shade kept water in the ground and crowded out weeds.

The three crops also fed people well together. Corn and beans eaten in the same meal give the body more complete protein than either food alone. Protein is the part of food the body uses to build muscle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farming began in the East too" -->
Native people in the eastern part of North America started farming on their own, long before corn reached them. They took wild plants and bred them into crops. About 5,000 years ago they had tamed a kind of squash. They also grew sunflower, goosefoot, sumpweed, maygrass, little barley and erect knotweed. Goosefoot and sumpweed are plants with small seeds that people ground or cooked.

Scientists call this group of crops the Eastern Agricultural Complex. Farming began on its own in only a few places in the world. Eastern North America is one of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Foods by region" -->
Cahokia stood near where St. Louis is today. Around the year 1100, between about 10,000 and 20,000 people lived there, and corn farming fed them.

People in other regions lived mainly on other foods. On the Great Plains, hunters killed bison. Scientists estimate that about 30 million bison still lived there in the early 1800s. Along the Northwest coast and its rivers, salmon was the main food. People on the coasts gathered shellfish. In the Northeast, people collected sap from maple trees.

Around the Great Lakes, people harvested manoomin, a wild rice that grows in shallow water. In Ojibwe, the word means "the good berry." People harvest it from canoes. In an Anishinaabe prophecy, the people were led to the place where they would find "the food that grows on water." Manoomin is still a central food of Ojibwe life today.

No farmer from before 1500 is known by name. Farmers taught what they knew about these crops to each new generation, and seed keepers in Native nations still grow them today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s, crops and farm animals began crossing the Atlantic Ocean in both directions. Historians call this movement the Columbian Exchange. The historian Alfred W. Crosby named it in his 1972 book *The Columbian Exchange*. Native people here still grew and ate their own foods through most of the century. The food story of the 1500s is what crossed the ocean and who carried it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What crossed the ocean" -->
Europeans carried American crops and animals home to Europe. These included corn, potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, peanuts, peppers, cacao, vanilla and turkeys. Cacao is the bean that chocolate is made from. Corn and potatoes later became main foods across Europe, Africa and Asia.

Ships brought wheat, rice, sugarcane, citrus fruit and honeybees to the Americas. They also brought cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and chickens. Columbus brought horses on his second voyage, in 1493. Spanish colonists founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565, and began a colony in New Mexico in 1598. In both places they planted wheat and citrus and raised cattle.

The word exchange can sound like a friendly trade. It was not one. Europeans took American crops home from lands they were invading. Much of what arrived was grown to feed the colonists. Before long, planters grew sugarcane on plantations worked by enslaved people. Africans, most of them brought across the ocean in chains, carried their own crops and the skill to grow them. These included okra, watermelon, yams, sorghum, black-eyed peas and African rice. Historians today credit enslaved Africans with bringing these crops to the Americas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="De Soto's army and Apalachee corn" -->
The Spanish soldier Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay, in Florida, in May 1539, with an army of more than 600 people. He carried no food supplies except a small herd of pigs. The chronicles of the expedition say it began with about 13 pigs. By 1542 the herd had grown to about 700. The army rarely killed the pigs for food. Pigs that escaped ran wild across the Southeast.

De Soto's army moved from town to town and ate the corn that villagers had stored. Taking that corn often left the villagers at risk of starving. De Soto also seized dozens of Native men, women and children and forced them to carry the army's equipment and supplies.

From October 1539 to March 1540, de Soto's army held Anhaica, the main town of the Apalachee, where Tallahassee, Florida, stands today. The Spanish found enough stored food there to feed 600 men and 220 horses for five months. Archaeologists digging at the site have found burned corncobs. The Apalachee fought back with quick raids and ambushes. They aimed their arrows at the Spaniards' horses.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The English colonists who came in the 1600s did not know how to grow food in this land. Native corn kept the first colonies at Jamestown and Plymouth alive. Some of that corn was bought or grown with Native teaching, and some was taken by force. In Virginia, planters then grew tobacco, a crop that cannot be eaten, in place of food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jamestown: hunger, and corn taken by force" -->
The colonists at Jamestown, in Virginia, planned to live partly on food they traded from the Powhatan people and partly on supply ships from England. In the autumn of 1609, Powhatan leaders stopped trading food with them and surrounded the fort. The winter of 1609 to 1610 is called the Starving Time. About 500 colonists were alive when it began. By spring, about 61 were still alive.

The colonists ate their horses, dogs and rats, and they ate leather. Some ate the bodies of people who had died. Archaeologists found the bones of a girl whom researchers call Jane. The bones show that colonists ate her body after she died. Scientists at the Smithsonian announced the finding in 2013.

English soldiers also took corn by force and destroyed Native fields. On August 10, 1610, George Percy led English soldiers against the Paspahegh town near Jamestown. They burned the houses, cut down the cornfields and took corn. Percy wrote that they killed more than a dozen warriors. Other accounts put the dead at up to 75 villagers.

The soldiers captured the wife and two children of Wowinchopunck, the Paspahegh weroance, or leader. When Percy hesitated, his men complained. Percy then agreed to put the children to death. His soldiers threw the children overboard from a boat and shot them in the head in the water. The English at Jamestown killed the children's mother that same night.

In March 1622 Powhatan warriors attacked the English settlements. After that, colonial soldiers marched out almost every summer for ten years to attack Powhatan towns and cornfields. In 1623 colonists at a peace meeting poisoned the Powhatans' wine, shot them, and then destroyed their cornfields.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco in place of food" -->
John Rolfe grew Virginia's first successful tobacco crop in 1612. He planted a sweet kind of tobacco. It paid planters so well that many of them grew it in place of food crops.

Members of Virginia's assembly, the colony's lawmaking body, passed laws again and again to make planters grow food. Under a tobacco law of 1629 and 1630, a family could grow 2,000 tobacco plants for each family member and no more. Part of the reason for the limit was to make sure families still planted corn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plymouth: corn dug up, then corn planted" -->
The first corn the Plymouth colonists ate was corn they took. In November 1620, men from the *Mayflower* went exploring on Cape Cod. There they found baskets of corn that the local people had buried in the ground. The owners were saving it as seed for the next spring. The Englishmen dug up about ten bushels of the corn and a bag of beans. A bushel is a measure of about 8 gallons. They wrote that they planned to "satisfy them for their corn," which meant to pay the owners later. They named the place Cornhill.

The same group of men dug up graves nearby. They opened bundles that held the bones of a man and a small child. They wrote that they "brought sundry of the prettiest things away," meaning they took several of the objects buried with the dead.

The owners of the corn were Nauset people. In the summer of 1621, the colonists met one of them and promised to pay him. Later in 1621 the colonist Edward Winslow wrote that the owners had been given "full content," meaning they had been paid in full.

The colonists built their town on the land of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town. An epidemic, a disease that spreads quickly through many people, had killed the people of Patuxet between 1616 and 1619. The colonists listed as one reason for settling there the "good corn-ground ready to our hands." The Patuxet people had cleared those fields.

In the spring of 1621, the colonists planted about 20 acres of corn. The corn did well. Their first harvest, that autumn, fed them through their second winter.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="squanto-food-farming" name="Squanto (Tisquantum)" movie="Squanto: A Warrior's Tale (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Squanto (Tisquantum)

> **Who:** A Patuxet man who taught the Plymouth colonists to plant corn, after an English captain had kidnapped him.
> **When and where:** Patuxet, where the colonists built Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts. Kidnapped 1614, home 1619, taught the colonists in the spring of 1621, died 1622.
> **Movie:** *Squanto: A Warrior's Tale* (1994), a feature film. Most of its story is made up.

Squanto, also called Tisquantum, showed the Plymouth colonists how to plant corn in the spring of 1621. He also interpreted between the colonists and the Wampanoag, which means he put each side's words into the other's language.

In 1614 an English captain named Thomas Hunt kidnapped Squanto and took him to Spain to be sold. Squanto reached England and learned English there. In 1619 he came home with an English expedition. He found that everyone in his town of Patuxet had died in the epidemic.

The colonist Edward Winslow wrote about the corn planting in a letter of December 1621. He wrote that "according to the manner of the Indians, we manured our ground with herrings, or rather shads." To manure ground means to put something into the soil to feed the crops. Herring and shad are kinds of fish. The colonists buried fish in the ground with the corn seed. Squanto taught them to do this.

Historians disagree about where Squanto learned it. In 1975 the anthropologist Lynn Ceci argued in the journal *Science* that burying fish with corn was a European method. She argued that Squanto probably learned it during his years among Europeans. She found little evidence that many Native farmers used fish this way. Other scholars defended the older account, and the question is still argued. Winslow himself called it "the manner of the Indians."

Squanto died in 1622.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="squanto-food-farming" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the colonists ate" -->
The English colonists learned corn dishes from Native cooks. They ground corn and cooked it as hominy, samp and johnnycakes. They also ate beans. Once their hogs had multiplied, they ate pork, and they drank cider made from apples. Americans went on eating these corn dishes for centuries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1700 and 1750, planters in the Carolina Lowcountry grew rice and became some of the richest people in the colonies. The Lowcountry is the flat, wet land along the coast of South Carolina. Enslaved Africans built the rice fields and did the work in them. Many of them brought knowledge of rice growing from West Africa.

Farther north, farmers in Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey grew more wheat than they could eat and shipped flour to other places. In New Hampshire, settlers planted the first potato patches in North America that lasted.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carolina rice and African skill" -->
English settlers first brought a small parcel of rice to Charles Town, now Charleston, in 1670. No record shows that anything grew from it. By July 1699, Carolina planters were sending samples of their rice to London to test whether it would sell. The first crops grew on dry, high ground. By the 1720s, growers had moved their rice into freshwater swamps inland. They built dams to hold the water in ponds.

From 1698 to 1702, Carolina planters shipped out about 269,000 pounds of rice a year. From 1738 to 1742, they shipped out more than 30 million pounds a year. One kind of Carolina rice later became known as Carolina Gold.

Planters put out advertisements asking for captives from the part of West Africa they called the Rice Coast. That coast included Senegambia and Sierra Leone. Planters paid more for people from there because those people knew how to grow rice. Records from Carolina fields show African methods at work. Workers built earth banks around fields beside the tidal rivers so they could flood and drain them. They sowed seed by pressing it into the mud with the heel and toe. They separated the grain from its husks by tossing it in wide, flat, coiled baskets called fanner baskets.

Historians disagree about how much of Carolina's rice farming came from African knowledge. In her 2001 book *Black Rice*, the geographer Judith Carney argued that African knowledge was the base of the whole rice business. In 2005 the historians David Eltis, Philip Morgan and David Richardson argued that she had made the African share too large. Records also show that in the early years, more captives came from Angola than from any other place, and Angola is not on the Rice Coast. Most scholars now hold a middle view. Planters built the rice business with their money and by force, and they also depended on African skill. The African methods in the fields are documented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land the rice grew on" -->
The first rice lands lay along the Ashley, Cooper and Stono rivers. The Kiawah, Etiwan and Stono people lived there. They were still there in 1670, when English colonists settled at Charleston Harbor.

English colonists captured Kussoe people who fought back in 1671, and Stono people in 1674, and sold them into slavery in the Caribbean. The Kiawah left the Ashley River in the early 1680s and moved to the island that carries their name today. In the spring of 1684, leaders of eight nations signed away their land claims to the colony's government. By then no nation of the Lowcountry had more than fifty bowmen. The Stono had sixteen hunters.

Before 1720, some of Carolina's most valuable trade was the sale of Native people as slaves. In 1770 a colonist named William Bull wrote that "there remains now, except a few Catawbas, nothing of them but their names, within three hundred miles of our sea coast."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The rice workers, unnamed in the records" -->
Planters' records list the enslaved rice workers by first name, age and price. They say almost nothing else about them. No single rice grower from these years can be followed through life in the records. What the records do show applies to all of them.

Each worker had to finish a set task every day. The usual task was to work a quarter of an acre of land. A worker who finished the task had the rest of the day. Workers labored in direct sun, standing in water or mud, among mosquitoes, horseflies, snakes and alligators. Enslaved Africans dug the banks and canals of the rice fields by hand. The earth they moved across the Lowcountry is about as much as all the great pyramids of Egypt put together.

Until the 1760s, more enslaved people died in the Lowcountry than were born there. Many of the dead were children. Planters kept buying new captives to replace them. In 1710 slave traders rarely landed more than 300 enslaved Africans a year at Charles Town. By 1720 they landed more than 1,000 a year. By 1770 they landed more than 3,000 a year. More than four of every ten Africans brought to North America in the slave trade came in through Charleston Harbor. By 1708 more Black people than white people lived in South Carolina.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney

> **Who:** A planter's daughter who ran her family's South Carolina plantations as a teenager and grew the colony's first successful crop of indigo dye.
> **When and where:** Wappoo plantation, near Charleston, South Carolina. Indigo experiments from 1739, first successful dye in 1744.

Eliza Lucas produced the first successful indigo dye in South Carolina in 1744. Indigo is a plant, and dye made from it colors cloth blue. It is a farm crop but not a food.

In 1739 her father, George Lucas, went back to the island of Antigua, in the Caribbean, to serve as its lieutenant governor. Eliza was about 16. She took charge of Wappoo plantation and of the 20 enslaved people who worked it. She also oversaw the family's two other plantations, which were much larger.

That same year she began experimenting with indigo seed. Turning the plant into dye takes skill. She first worked with an indigo maker from the island of Montserrat. Her best results came with an indigo maker of African descent whom her father hired from the French islands of the Caribbean. The enslaved workers at Wappoo planted and tended the crop.

She used her 1744 crop to make seed and gave the seed to other planters. Planters in South Carolina shipped out about 5,000 pounds of indigo in 1745 and 1746. By 1748 they shipped out 130,000 pounds. She later married and took the name Pinckney.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wheat, potatoes and everyday meals" -->
Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey were called the breadbasket colonies. Farmers there grew more wheat than their families needed. Merchants shipped the extra wheat as flour out of the ports of Philadelphia and New York.

In 1719 settlers known as the Scots-Irish planted potatoes at Londonderry, New Hampshire. Their patches were the first lasting potato fields in North America. The potato first grew as a crop in the Andes mountains of South America. People carried it to Europe, and settlers later brought it back across the ocean to North America.

Most colonial families ate corn, pork and bread, and drank cider. Rich families also bought tea, sugar and wine from overseas, and they ate bread made of wheat.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Most Americans were farmers when the United States began. The first census, a count of the people, was taken in 1790. It counted about 4 million people. About nine of every ten working people farmed, by a widely used estimate from the US Department of Agriculture.

Thomas Jefferson wrote that farmers were God's chosen people, while people he enslaved farmed his land. In 1796 an American woman printed the first cookbook written for American kitchens. Its recipes used cornmeal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="matthew-patten" name="Matthew Patten" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Matthew Patten

> **Who:** A New Hampshire farmer, carpenter and judge whose diary records what his family grew, caught and ate.
> **When and where:** Bedford, New Hampshire, on the Merrimack River. His surviving diary runs from 1754 to 1788.

Matthew Patten kept a diary that shows how one farm family in New Hampshire fed itself. He headed each little booklet of it "A Day Book continued." His entries for the year 1767 follow the family's food through the seasons.

Patten was born in Ireland on May 19, 1719. He came to the colonies in 1728, when he was nine, and settled at the place later named Bedford in 1738. He was a joiner, a carpenter who does the finer woodwork in a house. He also farmed, surveyed land and worked as a judge.

In January 1767, Patten butchered the family's hogs. Neighbors paid him for surveying their land in bushels of corn. He took corn and rye to a neighbor's mill to be ground into meal and flour. He bought tea and 7 pounds of sugar.

On March 4 he opened the pit where the family's potatoes had been buried for the winter. He wrote that they were "sound and good." In April and May the family sowed peas, then planted potatoes, corn, barley, hemp and flax. Hemp and flax are plants whose fibers are made into rope and cloth. He wrote that "the boys" planted the field behind the barn with corn and potatoes and spread manure from the barn on it.

In late May, Patten helped pull a net across the Merrimack River all night. His share was 60 shad, a fish the family took from the Merrimack each spring. From May 28 to June 6 he fished at Amoskeag Falls and sent 354 fish home. The name Amoskeag comes from a Pennacook word that means roughly "good fishing place." Pennacook people had fished at the falls with large nets long before Bedford was a town. Bedford lies in Pennacook country.

In June and July Patten took salmon weighing from 6 to 19 pounds and split them with two neighbors. In August he reaped his rye and wrote, "I have 40 Stooks in all this year." A stook is a bundle of cut grain stood up to dry. Later that month he mowed his meadow and counted 205 piles of hay.

Patten died on August 27, 1795, in one of the town's fields. He had carried dinner out to the men who were mowing.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="matthew-patten" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson's farmers and Monticello's rations" -->
Many American leaders believed that family farms made free citizens. Thomas Jefferson wrote about the idea in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, first printed in 1785: "Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever he had a chosen people." For about a hundred years afterward, American leaders wrote land laws meant to put land in the hands of family farmers.

People Jefferson enslaved did the farm work at Monticello, his plantation in Virginia. Jefferson owned more than 600 enslaved people during his adult life. When Monticello grew tobacco, enslaved children picked worms off the tobacco plants.

Each week, each enslaved worker at Monticello received a peck of cornmeal, half a pound of pork or pickled beef, and four salted fish. A peck is about 8 quarts. Nursing mothers received extra, and workers got whiskey at harvest. Researchers who studied the Monticello records found that these rations were not enough food for a person to live on. So in their little free time, enslaved people hunted, fished, trapped, kept gardens and raised animals.

Enslaved families also sold chickens and vegetables from their own gardens and flocks to the Jefferson family. Jefferson's granddaughter Anne Cary Randolph wrote down these purchases in the household accounts. Most of the sellers she recorded were enslaved people who worked in the house, many of them members of the Hemings family.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corn into whiskey" -->
The backcountry was the frontier land far from the coast. A wagon of corn could not easily get from there to buyers, but a jug of whiskey could. So backcountry farmers turned their extra corn into whiskey to get it to market.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-simmons" name="Amelia Simmons" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Simmons

> **Who:** The author of *American Cookery*, the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens.
> **When and where:** Hartford, Connecticut, 1796.

Amelia Simmons wrote *American Cookery*, the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens. It was printed in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1796. On the title page she called herself "an American orphan." Nothing else about her life is known.

Her book printed the first known recipes that used American cornmeal. Among them were johnnycake, "Indian slapjack" and Indian pudding. The word Indian in these names meant that they were made with corn.

The book was also the first to print a recipe using pearlash to make dough rise. Pearlash is a salt made from wood ashes. Pearlash was the forerunner of baking powder. The book also contains some of the earliest printed uses of the Dutch American words "cookey" and "slaw."
