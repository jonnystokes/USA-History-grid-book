## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1900 and 1950, federal officials took on new jobs in how Americans got their food. Federal inspectors went into the meatpacking plants in 1906. In the 1930s, federal officials paid farmers to grow less, bought up extra food and gave it to hungry families. During World War II, officials limited how much sugar, meat and coffee each person could buy. In 1946 President Harry Truman signed a law that made school lunch a permanent federal program. On the farms, tractors took the place of horses, and new kinds of seed corn and chicken farming began.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was in the meat: the 1906 food laws" -->
On June 30, 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt signed two food laws on the same day. Under the Pure Food and Drug Act, no one could sell adulterated or mislabeled food and medicine from one state to another. Adulterated food is food that has been made impure, or mixed with something that should not be in it. The federal office that enforced this law later became the Food and Drug Administration, or FDA.

The second law was the Meat Inspection Act. Under it, federal inspectors worked inside the packing plants and checked the meat. Members of Congress passed both laws within a few months after a novel about Chicago's packinghouses came out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="upton-sinclair-food-farming" name="Upton Sinclair" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Upton Sinclair

> **Who:** A novelist whose book about the Chicago meatpacking plants came out months before the first federal food-safety laws.
> **When and where:** Chicago, 1904. His novel *The Jungle* came out as a book in February 1906.

Upton Sinclair wrote *The Jungle* to show how immigrant workers in Chicago's packinghouses lived. In 1904 he spent about seven weeks working undercover in the plants. His novel was printed in parts in 1905 and came out as a book in February 1906.

Readers paid less attention to the workers than to what Sinclair's pages said went into the meat. Within months, Roosevelt signed the two food laws of June 30, 1906. Later that year Sinclair wrote about how readers had taken his book: "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="upton-sinclair-food-farming" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-washington-carver-food-farming" name="George Washington Carver" movie="George Washington Carver: An Uncommon Life (2018)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### George Washington Carver

> **Who:** A scientist who taught poor Southern farmers how to rebuild worn-out cotton land and grow their own food.
> **When and where:** Born enslaved near Diamond, Missouri, about 1864. Taught at Tuskegee Institute, Alabama, from 1896 until his death in 1943.
> **Movie:** *George Washington Carver: An Uncommon Life* (Iowa PBS, 2018), a documentary.

George Washington Carver taught Southern farmers to plant crops that rebuilt their soil. He was born enslaved near Diamond, Missouri, in about 1864. No record gives the exact date. He was the first Black student at Iowa State, and later he taught there. In October 1896 he took charge of farming studies at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, and he stayed until he died in 1943.

Year after year of cotton had worn out much of the South's soil. Carver taught crop rotation, which means changing the crop planted in a field from one year to the next. A farmer planted peanuts, cowpeas or sweet potatoes, which built the soil back up, and then went back to cotton, which wore it down.

Carver wrote 44 short bulletins in plain words and gave them to farmers for free. A bulletin is a small printed booklet. He also sent out the Jesup wagon, a demonstration wagon pulled by a mule, to carry his lessons to farmers who could not come to Tuskegee. His best-known bulletin came out in 1916 and was reprinted for decades. It was called *How to Grow the Peanut and 105 Ways of Preparing It for Human Consumption*. In his laboratory he made hundreds of test products from peanuts and sweet potatoes.

Carver did not invent peanut butter. The Aztecs of Mexico made peanut paste centuries earlier. Marcellus Gilmore Edson received a patent for a peanut paste in Canada in 1884, and John Harvey Kellogg received one in 1895. Carver spent his working life on soil and on teaching poor farmers, Black and white, to grow more of their own food.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-washington-carver-food-farming" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pellagra: a disease from a corn diet" -->
Pellagra is a disease caused by a diet missing a vitamin called niacin. Between 1900 and 1940, about 3 million Americans got pellagra, and about 100,000 died of it. Those hit hardest were poor farmers, sharecroppers and cotton-mill workers in the South. They lived mostly on cornmeal, molasses and dried pork, with few fresh vegetables and little meat or milk. Many mill owners paid workers in scrip, a kind of paper money that could be spent only at the company's own store. Those stores sold mostly cornmeal, dried meat and molasses.

A person with pellagra got skin rashes, diarrhea and dementia, which means confusion and loss of memory. The disease could end in death.

In 1915 Joseph Goldberger, a doctor with the US Public Health Service, tested the diet on prisoners. With the agreement of Mississippi's governor, Earl Brewer, he fed volunteer prisoners at the Rankin State Prison Farm the corn-based meals poor Southerners ate, starting on April 19, 1915. In return, the governor promised the men pardons, meaning they would be let out of prison. Accounts of the results differ. About half of the eleven men got pellagra, and the others showed some signs of it. The men had skin rashes and stomach illness. The men who got pellagra recovered. In 1916 Goldberger injected himself, his wife and other volunteers with blood from pellagra patients, to show that the disease did not spread from person to person.

In 1937 a scientist in Wisconsin found that the missing nutrient was niacin, also called vitamin B3. Under federal and state laws passed during World War II, bakers had to add niacin to bread.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sugar and pineapple from Hawaii" -->
Hawaii was an independent kingdom until 1893. On January 17 of that year, a group of mostly American businessmen moved to overthrow Queen Liliuokalani. The US minister in Hawaii, John L. Stevens, had 162 US sailors and Marines brought ashore from the warship USS *Boston*. The queen gave up her power under protest. In 1897 Native Hawaiians signed 21,269 names on petitions against joining the United States. On July 7, 1898, President William McKinley signed the resolution that annexed Hawaii, which means the United States took it over.

Sugar plantations made up most of the islands' business. In 1901 James Dole started the Hawaiian Pineapple Company. Workers at his company canned pineapple in huge amounts for kitchens on the mainland.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tractors, hybrid corn and chickens" -->
In 1920 American farms had 246,000 tractors and about 26 million horses and mules. Most of the pulling was still done by animals. In 1917 Henry Ford's company began selling a cheap tractor called the Fordson. In 1924 the International Harvester company brought out the Farmall. The Farmall could also cultivate row crops, meaning it could work the soil between rows of growing plants. That had been the last big farm job that still needed horses.

A farm horse ate crops too. Each horse a farmer sold or stopped using freed about five acres that had grown its feed. Farmers could plant those acres with food for people.

In 1926 Henry A. Wallace founded Pioneer Hi-Bred, the first company that sold hybrid seed corn. Hybrid seed comes from crossing two different parent plants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="cecile-steele" name="Cecile Steele" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Cecile Steele

> **Who:** A Delaware farm wife whose chick order, shipped by mistake, started the business of raising chickens for meat.
> **When and where:** Ocean View, Delaware, 1923. She lived from 1900 to 1940.

In 1923 Cecile Long Steele ordered 50 chicks for her farm in Ocean View, Delaware. The hatchery made a mistake and sent her 500. She raised them indoors. At 16 weeks old she sold them for 62 cents a pound.

In 1924 she ordered 1,000 chicks on purpose. By 1926 she was raising 10,000. By 1928 about 500 farms on the Delmarva Peninsula, where Delaware, Maryland and Virginia meet, were raising broilers. A broiler is a young chicken raised for its meat.

Chicken later became the meat Americans eat most. The National Chicken Council, a group that speaks for chicken companies, counts how much meat each American eats in a year. By its count, each American ate about 32 pounds of chicken in 1965. In 2025 each American ate about 103 pounds of chicken, 59 pounds of beef and 49 pounds of pork.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="cecile-steele" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dust, pigs and food stamps in the 1930s" -->
During World War I, wheat sold for high prices. Farmers on the southern Great Plains plowed up the grassland to plant it. A drought began in 1931, and the wheat died. The bare, dry soil blew away in dust storms. The storm of April 14, 1935, is called Black Sunday. Crops failed year after year. Farm families lived on vegetables from their gardens, canned food, food handed out by the government, and jackrabbits.

No one kept an official count of the people the dust killed. In Kansas alone, dozens of people died of dust pneumonia in 1935. Dust pneumonia happened when dust settled deep in a person's lungs. It stopped the cilia, the tiny hairs that sweep the airways clean, from moving. The person had fever, chest pain, coughing and trouble breathing.

Under the Agricultural Adjustment Act of May 1933, federal officials paid farmers to grow less, so that crop prices would rise. In 1933 farmers plowed under about 10 million acres of cotton that was already growing. Federal officials bought about 6 million pigs and had them killed. At the same time, hungry people across the country stood in breadlines, long lines for free food.

Many Americans were angry that food was being destroyed while people went hungry. Federal officials set up the Federal Surplus Relief Corporation to give extra farm food to families on relief, meaning families getting government help. On May 16, 1939, the first Food Stamp Program began in Rochester, New York. Mabel McFiggin was the first buyer. People bought orange stamps that paid for food. With them came free blue stamps, which could buy only foods that federal officials listed as surplus, meaning extra. A court struck down the farm law in 1936, and members of Congress wrote a new one in 1938. Federal payments to farmers based on what they grow have continued ever since.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who got the cotton money" -->
Under the cotton contracts of 1934 and 1935, federal officials paid landowners to take land out of cotton. Sharecroppers, the families who farmed that land for a share of the crop, were supposed to get a part of the payment. The contracts gave them only about 11 percent. A landowner could keep the whole payment by evicting the sharecroppers, meaning forcing them off the land, and hiring day laborers instead. When planters cut their cotton land by 40 or 50 percent, they cut the number of families working it by the same share.

In July 1934, seven Black men and eleven white men met at the Sunnyside School in Poinsett County, Arkansas. They started the Southern Tenant Farmers' Union to fight the evictions. Planters and their allies answered with force. In January 1935 lawmen arrested a white organizer, Ward Rodgers, and jailed him at Marked Tree. At a union meeting in Gilmore, four armed white men broke in. They ordered the Black members to leave and told them they would be lynched, meaning killed by a mob, if they were caught at another union meeting. Many members were beaten and threatened with guns. On March 15, 1935, at Birdsong, about forty armed planters, their riding bosses and lawmen broke up a union crowd. Later that month a mob attacked the home of the union's lawyer, Cornelius Tyree Carpenter.

Some lawyers at the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, led by Jerome Frank, ruled that landowners had to keep the same tenant families. In February 1935 the agency's head, Chester Davis, had Secretary of Agriculture Henry Wallace fire them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ration books and victory gardens" -->
During World War II, federal officials rationed food, which means they limited how much of it each person could buy. Every person got a ration book of stamps, even babies. Sugar was the first food rationed, in May 1942. Coffee followed that November. In March 1943 meat, cheese, fats and canned foods were added. Sugar was also the last food to come off the list, in June 1947.

Each person was allowed 26 pounds of sugar a year, about 8 ounces a week. In 1945 the amount fell as low as 4.5 ounces a week. Every month each person got 64 red points for meat and fats and 48 blue points for processed foods, such as canned goods. A shopper had to hand over the right points, along with money, to buy these foods.

Americans also grew their own food. They planted about 20 million victory gardens. By 1943 and 1944, these gardens grew about 40 percent of the country's fresh vegetables, roughly 9 to 10 million tons. Sources differ on which of the two years was the peak.

Frozen food was already on sale by the war years. Clarence Birdseye's quick-frozen foods first went on sale on March 6, 1930, in 18 stores in Springfield, Massachusetts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="adelaide-wisdom-benjamin" name="Adelaide Wisdom Benjamin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Adelaide Wisdom Benjamin

> **Who:** A girl whose family grew a victory garden and shopped with ration stamps during World War II. She told her story in an oral history for the National WWII Museum in 2013.
> **When and where:** New Orleans, Louisiana, and El Paso, Texas, 1941 to 1945. She was born in New Orleans in 1932.

Adelaide Wisdom grew up in New Orleans. During the war her father tried to join the Army and the Navy but did not meet their requirements. He was accepted by the Marines. After boot camp he was posted to El Paso, Texas, as a Marine recruiter, and the family moved with him.

At the store, the family could buy sugar, butter and some vegetables only with the right ration stamps. Beef and lamb were hard to find. With so little to serve, the family could not have guests over for meals.

The family grew a victory garden in New Orleans and in El Paso. "We grew mirlitons, and string beans and tomatoes in our backyard," she said in 2013. A mirliton is a pale green squash. The crops grew well in New Orleans. They did not grow well in El Paso. She collected scrap metal with her brother, including old coat hangers and bicycle tires. She put 10 cents of her 25-cent weekly allowance into war savings stamps.

When Germany surrendered in 1945, she spent her own money on a chocolate soda, a comic book and bubble gum. She later graduated from Tulane University Law School. With her husband, Eddie Benjamin, she practiced law for 55 years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="adelaide-wisdom-benjamin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farms taken from Japanese Americans" -->
On February 19, 1942, President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. In March and April, General John L. DeWitt ordered about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, both citizens and noncitizens, removed from the West Coast and sent to camps.

Many of these families were farmers. Lawrence Hewes ran the Farm Security Administration in the region, the federal office put in charge of handing their farms to new operators. He said his agents had registered 6,000 Japanese American farms, covering about 200,000 acres. In March 1942 alone, more than 1,000 of those farms, about 50,000 acres, passed to new operators. That same month, leaders of the California Farm Bureau, a farmers' group, said Japanese American farmers grew 40 percent of the state's vegetables. Those included nearly all of its tomatoes, celery, strawberries and peppers. In April 1942 federal officials estimated that the crops on 21 percent of the land might be lost because no one was found to farm it.

Inside the camps, the people held there farmed. About 15,000 people were sent to the camp at Tule Lake, California, alone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Braceros in the fields" -->
In 1942 US officials started the bracero program to bring Mexican workers into the country for farm and railroad work. The workers were called braceros. Over 22 years, workers signed more than 4.5 million contracts. Braceros picked much of the country's fruit and vegetables until the program ended in 1964.

From 1942 to 1949, US employers held back 10 percent of each bracero's pay. They sent the money through two US banks, Wells Fargo and the Union Trust Company of San Francisco, to banks in Mexico. The workers were told they would get it when they went home. Many braceros never got it back. In 2009 a settlement paid former braceros or their heirs a one-time sum of 38,000 pesos, about $3,500.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School lunch" -->
Towns and charities had run school meal programs for decades. In the 1930s, extra food bought by the government went into them. During World War II, many young men called up to serve in the military failed their physical exams because of malnutrition. Malnutrition means a person has not had enough of the right food. On June 4, 1946, President Harry Truman signed the National School Lunch Act. It made federal money for school lunches permanent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950, American farms grew fewer and bigger, and each acre grew more food. Farmers used tractors, hybrid seed, fertilizer and pesticides. Families bought more of their meals ready-made, from fast-food stands and from the freezer case. In the 1980s, after land prices fell and interest rates rose, about 300,000 farmers could not pay their loans. At the same time, some farmers and cooks began to grow food by organic methods and to serve food from nearby farms.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fewer horses, fewer farms, more corn" -->
The 1954 Census of Agriculture was the first count in which American farms had more tractors than horses and mules. Farms had about 4.6 million tractors that year and about 4 million horses and mules. In 1920 there had been about 25 to 26 million horses and mules.

The number of farms fell too. In 1935 the country had 6.8 million farms, the most it ever had. After that the number kept dropping, toward 2 million. The farms that were left were bigger. Many grew only one or two crops, and farmers used more chemicals on them.

By the 1950s nearly all the corn in the country grew from hybrid seed. For about a hundred years, farmers had grown roughly 25 to 30 bushels of corn on an acre. A bushel is a measure that holds about 8 gallons. By the 1970s farmers were growing more than 100 bushels an acre.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fast food and the TV dinner" -->
Richard and Maurice McDonald opened a drive-in restaurant in San Bernardino, California, in 1940. In 1948 they rebuilt it around what they called the Speedee Service System. They cut the menu down to 15-cent hamburgers, french fries and milkshakes. They let go of the carhops, the servers who had carried food out to people's cars. Workers made each part of the meal on a kind of assembly line, and customers got their food in seconds.

In 1953 the Swanson company put a frozen turkey dinner in an aluminum tray on sale and called it a TV dinner. It cost about 98 cents. The company sold more than 10 million in 1954 and 25 million the next year. According to company stories, Swanson had too many turkeys left over after Thanksgiving. Who thought of the TV dinner is disputed. For decades a salesman named Gerry Thomas said the idea was his. The company's owners were the brothers Gilbert and Clarke Swanson. Their relatives, and a Swanson scientist named Betty Cronin, said the brothers came up with it at the company and that Thomas took the credit. Frozen meals had been sold before either claim. In 1945 the Maxson company made frozen "Strato-Plates" to serve on airplanes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ray-kroc" name="Ray Kroc" movie="The Founder (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ray Kroc

> **Who:** A milkshake-machine salesman who became the McDonald brothers' franchise agent and then bought them out.
> **When and where:** Visited San Bernardino, California, in 1954. Opened his first McDonald's in Des Plaines, Illinois, on April 15, 1955. Bought out the brothers in 1961.
> **Movie:** *The Founder* (2016), a drama based on his story and the brothers'.

Ray Kroc sold milkshake machines. The McDonald brothers bought machines from him, and in 1954 he went to see their stand in San Bernardino. He became the brothers' franchise agent. A franchise is a deal in which a business owner lets other people open stores under the owner's name and system, in return for fees.

On April 15, 1955, Kroc opened his own McDonald's in Des Plaines, Illinois. In 1961 he bought the brothers out for $2.7 million. The brothers said Kroc had made a handshake promise to keep paying them royalties, a share of the money from every sale, and never kept it. Kroc also opened a McDonald's near their original stand to take away its customers. The movie *The Founder* is a drama based on these events.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ray-kroc" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="norman-borlaug" name="Norman Borlaug" movie="The Man Who Tried to Feed the World (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Norman Borlaug

> **Who:** An Iowa-born plant scientist whose wheat helped poor countries grow far more grain, in what was called the Green Revolution.
> **When and where:** Mexico from 1944. India and Pakistan in the 1960s. Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.
> **Movie:** *The Man Who Tried to Feed the World* (PBS American Experience, 2020), a documentary.

Norman Borlaug bred wheat that grew much more grain on the same land. Starting in 1944, he worked in Mexico for a program paid for by the Rockefeller Foundation. He crossed thousands of kinds of wheat. The result was a semi-dwarf wheat, with short stems, that gave high yields and resisted plant diseases. By 1956 Mexico grew all the wheat it needed.

In the 1960s, experts predicted famines in India and Pakistan. Farmers there planted Borlaug's wheat, and the famines they predicted did not happen. In 1970 Borlaug won the Nobel Peace Prize. He is the only farm scientist to have won it. People often say his work saved as many as a billion lives. That number is a common estimate, not a count.

Farmers grew his wheat together with chemical fertilizer and irrigation, water brought to the fields by pipes and ditches. Critics say this made farmers in many countries depend on chemicals. They also say farmers stopped planting many old kinds of crops, so fewer kinds were grown.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="norman-borlaug" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poison in the rows" -->
Farmers sprayed their crops with pesticides, chemicals that kill insects and weeds. DDT was in wide use from the mid-1940s. It made the shells of eagles' eggs thin. In September 1962 Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* described what these long-lasting pesticides did to birds, soil, water and people. By 1963 only 417 pairs of bald eagles were nesting in the lower 48 states. Officials at the Environmental Protection Agency banned DDT in 1972.

Farmworkers in steady contact with pesticides had high rates of burns, cancer and children born with birth defects. In the 1980s, children in several towns of California's San Joaquin Valley had cancer at rates "800% above the norm," according to the Walter P. Reuther Library at Wayne State University. Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta and the United Farm Workers, known for the Delano grape strike in California, organized the people who picked the crops.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black farmers and their land" -->
In 1920 Black farmers ran more than 900,000 farms, about 15 percent of all the farms in the United States. Between 1910 and 1997, the amount of farmland Black Americans owned fell by nearly 90 percent. In 2022 five economists published a study that put the value of the land lost from 1920 to 1997 at about $326 billion in today's dollars. By 2007 about 1.5 percent of the country's farms, 32,938 of them, had Black operators.

Black farmers took the US Department of Agriculture, or USDA, to court. The case was called *Pigford v. Glickman*. The farmers showed that from 1983 to 1997, USDA officials had discriminated against Black farmers because of their race and had failed to look into their complaints. Local county committees made the decisions on USDA farm loans. Black farmers said the committees turned down their loans, or made them wait longer for approval than white farmers. The records do not name the committee members. On April 14, 1999, Judge Paul L. Friedman approved a settlement. Under it, 15,645 farmers had their claims approved, out of 22,721 who qualified. Under one part of the settlement, each approved farmer received $50,000, and the farmer's USDA loans were forgiven. The payments and debt relief came to about $1.06 billion. On February 18, 2010, a second settlement of $1.25 billion was announced for farmers who had missed the first deadline.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organic food becomes law" -->
Organic farmers build up their soil with compost, which is rotted plant and animal matter. In 1942 J.I. Rodale began a magazine called *Organic Farming and Gardening* in Emmaus, Pennsylvania. He spent decades urging farmers and gardeners to farm this way. In 1990 members of Congress passed the Organic Foods Production Act. It required national rules for what could be sold as organic. The USDA organic seal, a label for food that meets those rules, came into use on October 21, 2002.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alice-waters" name="Alice Waters" movie="Alice Waters and Her Delicious Revolution (2003)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alice Waters

> **Who:** A cook who built her restaurant around food from nearby farms and started a school garden where children grow and cook their own food.
> **When and where:** Chez Panisse restaurant, Berkeley, California, opened in 1971. The Edible Schoolyard, Berkeley, started in 1995.
> **Movie:** *Alice Waters and Her Delicious Revolution* (PBS American Masters, 2003), a documentary.

In 1971 Alice Waters opened a restaurant called Chez Panisse in Berkeley, California. She cooked with food that was fresh, grown nearby, in season and organically grown. Chez Panisse was one of the first restaurants of the style later called California cuisine. It also helped start the farm-to-table idea, which means buying food straight from local farms.

In 1995 Waters started the Edible Schoolyard at Martin Luther King Jr. Middle School in Berkeley. Students there grow a garden, cook what they grow and eat it, as part of their school day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alice-waters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The farm crisis of the 1980s" -->
In the 1970s, American farmers sold large amounts of grain to other countries. They planted "fencerow to fencerow," every acre up to the fences. In the early 1980s, interest rates on loans rose high and the price of farmland fell. In parts of the Midwest, farmland lost as much as 60 percent of its value between 1981 and the mid-1980s. By 1984 American farmers owed about $215 billion, twice what they had owed in 1978.

During the 1980s, an estimated 300,000 farmers defaulted, meaning they could not pay back their loans. About 300 banks in farm country failed, meaning they ran out of money and closed. On September 22, 1985, Willie Nelson, John Mellencamp and Neil Young held a concert called Farm Aid in Champaign, Illinois. About 80,000 people came, and the concert raised more than $7 million for farm families. Farm Aid concerts still take place.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jordan-family-farm" name="Russel and Mary Jane Jordan" movie="Troublesome Creek: A Midwestern (1995)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Russel and Mary Jane Jordan

> **Who:** An Iowa farm couple who sold their machinery, livestock and household goods at auction to pay their bank. Their daughter filmed it.
> **When and where:** Iowa. The family had owned its farm for 125 years. Their bank was in Atlantic, Iowa.
> **Movie:** *Troublesome Creek: A Midwestern* (1995), a documentary about the family. It won the Grand Jury Prize at the Sundance Film Festival and was nominated for an Academy Award.

Russel and Mary Jane Jordan owned a farm in Iowa that had been in their family for 125 years. A company called Norwest, which owned banks in several states, bought their bank in Atlantic, Iowa. A man from the company's headquarters, Tim Wolf, took over their loans. The bank's officers called in a debt of $70,000 that had built up over the years, meaning they demanded it be paid. Along with their yearly operating loan of $150,000, the loan that paid for each year's farming, the Jordans had about $220,000 to deal with.

The Jordans held an auction. They sold all their machinery, their livestock and even their household goods. Only the land itself was kept. The sale raised about $300,000, more than enough to pay the back debt. Russ and Mary Jane moved to a rented house in Atlantic. Their son Jim took over farming the home place with his own equipment. Later the crop land was rented out to neighbors.

Their daughter, Jeanne Jordan, and her husband, Steven Ascher, made the film about the family.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jordan-family-farm" -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The 2022 Census of Agriculture, the USDA's count of the country's farms, found 1.9 million farms in the United States. That was 7 percent fewer than in 2017. It was the first count below 2 million since before the Civil War. In 1935 there had been 6.8 million. Since 2000, Americans have argued over what goes into their food, who grows and picks it, and who can get enough of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Weight, hunger and food stamps" -->
Doctors at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or CDC, measure obesity, which means a body weight high enough to raise the risk of illness. By their count, 30.5 percent of American adults had obesity in 1999 and 2000. The share rose to 41.9 percent in the years 2017 to March 2020. It was 40.3 percent from August 2021 to August 2023. Type 2 diabetes and heart disease are among the illnesses that doctors link to diet.

Some Americans live far from a place to buy fresh food. USDA officials map low-income areas where at least 500 people, or a third of the people, live more than 1 mile from a supermarket in a city, or more than 10 miles in the country. People call these areas food deserts. By that measure, USDA data for 2019 put about 19 million people, about 6 percent of the population, in them. A broader measure used for 2015 counted 39.4 million. The number depends on which measure is used.

Every year from 1995, USDA economists counted households that did not always have enough food. In 2024, 13.7 percent of households did not. Nearly 48 million people lived in those households, including more than 14 million children. That report came out on December 30, 2025, and it was the last one. In September 2025, USDA officials under Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins announced that they would end the count. They called it "redundant, costly [and] politicized."

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, is the program once called food stamps. On July 4, 2025, President Donald Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Economists at the Congressional Budget Office, which estimates costs for Congress, estimated that the law cuts federal spending on SNAP by $186.7 billion over ten years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farmers' markets and the 2020 pandemic" -->
Farmers' markets, where farmers sell their own crops straight to shoppers, grew in number. USDA counted 1,755 of them in 1994 and 8,771 in 2019. The number grew about 7 percent a year until about 2011, and then it stopped growing much.

In 2020 the disease COVID-19 spread through meat and poultry plants. In April and May 2020, 23 states reported 16,233 cases of COVID-19 among workers in 239 plants, and 86 of those workers died. This count comes from a CDC report of July 2020. Of the sick workers whose race was recorded, 87 percent were members of racial or ethnic minority groups.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Changed seeds and new kinds of meat" -->
In 2025, more than 90 percent of the country's corn, soybean and cotton acres were planted with genetically engineered seed, according to USDA economists. Genetic engineering means scientists change a plant's DNA, the chemical instructions inside every cell, in a laboratory. Most of it was changed so the plants survive sprays that kill weeds.

In 2016 the National Academies of Sciences, a group of leading American scientists, reviewed about 1,000 studies. The scientists wrote that they "could not find persuasive evidence of adverse health effects directly attributable to consumption" of these foods. Many Americans still argued against them. Under federal rules in force since January 2022, foods that contain changed DNA that tests can detect must carry a "bioengineered" label. The first engineered food sold in stores was the Flavr Savr tomato, in 1994.

Companies also began selling new kinds of meat. The Impossible Burger, made from plants, came out in 2016. Beyond Meat also makes meat from plants. In 2019 its shares went on sale to the public for the first time. Their price rose more on the first day than that of any other company's new shares that year. In June 2023 USDA officials gave two companies, UPSIDE Foods and GOOD Meat, the first approval in the United States to sell chicken grown from animal cells instead of from a live bird. People call it lab-grown meat.

Other companies grew vegetables indoors on shelves stacked high, called vertical farms. Investors put large sums into them. In June 2023 AeroFarms, one of the first of these companies, filed for bankruptcy, a court process for a business that cannot pay its debts. The company later reorganized and stayed in business.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who picks the food" -->
USDA economists report that most hired crop workers in the United States were born in other countries. Many come on H-2A visas. A visa is a paper that lets a person from another country enter the United States. An H-2A visa lets a farmworker come for one season of farm work. By the same economists' figures, officials at the US Department of Labor approved about 48,000 H-2A jobs in 2005 and about 372,000 in 2022. In recent years H-2A workers have filled about 15 percent of crop-farm jobs, and more than 90 percent of them come from Mexico. The Labor Department's own count for 2025 was 398,258 jobs.

From 1992 to 2006, 68 crop workers died from heat, according to a CDC report of 2008. Crop workers died of heat at about 20 times the rate of all American workers. One of them was a 56-year-old H-2A worker harvesting tobacco by hand in North Carolina in July 2005. It was his third day on the job, and no one had trained him about heat. His body temperature reached 108 degrees Fahrenheit, and he died of heat stroke, which happens when the body cannot cool itself. The report does not give his name.

Federal law lets children work on farms at younger ages than in other jobs. Outside school hours, a child under 12 may work on a farm owned or run by a parent, or, with a parent's permission, on some small farms. Children of 12 and 13 may work with a parent's permission or on a farm where a parent works. At 14, a child may do farm jobs outside school hours.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Lucas Benitez

> **Who:** A tomato picker who helped start the Coalition of Immokalee Workers and its Fair Food Program.
> **When and where:** Born in Guerrero, Mexico. Moved to Immokalee, Florida, at 17. The coalition began in 1993, and the Fair Food Program began in 2011.
> **Movie:** *Food Chains* (2014), a documentary about the coalition. Benitez appears in it.

Lucas Benitez came to Immokalee, Florida, from the state of Guerrero in Mexico when he was 17. He picked tomatoes in the fields there. In 1993 he helped start the Coalition of Immokalee Workers, a group of farmworkers.

In November 2010 the coalition and Florida's tomato growers announced an agreement, and the Fair Food Program began running in 2011. Under it, large buyers of tomatoes, such as McDonald's and Walmart, agree to pay a small extra amount for the tomatoes and to hold growers to a code of conduct. The code sets a lowest pay rate and requires shade, water and protection from abuse for the pickers. Auditors check that growers follow it. In 2023 Benitez received the University of Michigan's Wallenberg Medal.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lucas-benitez" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black farmers in the new century" -->
The 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 46,738 Black farmers on 32,653 farms, according to a USDA report of June 2024. They made up 1.4 percent of all the country's farmers and worked 5.3 million acres. The number of farms run by Black farmers was 8 percent lower than in 2017.

On July 31, 2024, USDA officials paid about $2 billion to more than 43,000 farmers, ranchers and forest owners who had faced discrimination in USDA farm loans. The NAACP Legal Defense Fund reported the payments that day. The money came from a section of the Inflation Reduction Act, a law passed by Congress in 2022.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first farmers, still farming" -->
Native nations are working to control their own food again. This is called food sovereignty. Tribes run bison herds, seed banks that save the seeds of old crops, and programs that bring back traditional foods. Native chefs and seed keepers are rebuilding the ways their nations cooked and ate. Among the Ojibwe of the Great Lakes, manoomin, or wild rice, is harvested by canoe and is still a central food.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nephi Craig

> **Who:** A White Mountain Apache and Diné (Navajo) chef who cooks Apache foods for his own community.
> **When and where:** White Mountain Apache lands, Arizona, 2010s to today.
> **Movie:** *Gather* (2020), a documentary about Native food sovereignty that features him.

Nephi Craig is a chef who opened a café built around Apache foods. He also founded the Native American Culinary Association, a group for Native cooks. The 2020 documentary *Gather* follows him and others who are bringing back Native foods. Among them are Twila Cassadore, a San Carlos Apache woman who gathers wild foods, and Elsie DuBray, a student scientist from the Cheyenne River Sioux.
