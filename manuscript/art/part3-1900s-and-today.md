## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
About 75,000 people came to see modern European paintings at the Armory Show in New York in 1913. In Harlem in the 1920s and 1930s, Black painters, sculptors, photographers and writers worked side by side. From 1935 to 1943, federal officials paid thousands of artists a weekly wage, and Black artists in New York had to organize before they were given that work. Government photographers recorded hungry farm families in the 1930s. In 1942 one of them photographed Japanese American families as soldiers and officials forced them into camps. Officials kept most of those pictures out of public view.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Armory Show, 1913, and photographs as art" -->
The International Exhibition of Modern Art ran from February 17 to March 15, 1913, in the armory of the 69th Regiment, a unit of soldiers, on Lexington Avenue in New York. An armory is a large building where soldiers train and keep their weapons. People call it the Armory Show. A group of American painters and sculptors organized it. Its catalog listed 1,090 works by 306 artists, and several hundred more were added during the show. About 75,000 people came to see it in New York. By the end of its tour to Chicago and Boston, nearly 300,000 people had seen it.

Marcel Duchamp's painting *Nude Descending a Staircase* drew jokes. The critic Julian Street called it "an explosion in a shingle factory." Theodore Roosevelt said it looked like the Navajo rug in his bathroom. The sculptor Gutzon Borglum, one of the show's early organizers, called it "a staircase descending a nude."

In Chicago, on April 16, 1913, students of the School of the Art Institute held a pretend trial of the painter Henri Matisse. They charged him with "pictorial arson" and "criminal misuse of line." Arson is the crime of setting fire to something on purpose. Then they burned copies they had painted of three of his pictures, among them *Blue Nude*. School officials had told them not to. A reporter for the *Chicago Evening Post* counted two hundred students and more than a thousand people watching.

The photographer Alfred Stieglitz argued that a photograph could be fine art, as a painting was. He published *Camera Work*, a magazine of photographs, from 1903 to 1917. He also ran a gallery at 291 Fifth Avenue in New York from 1905 to 1917, and people called the gallery 291.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Georgia O'Keeffe

> **Who:** A painter whose drawings Alfred Stieglitz showed in New York in 1916, and who later lived and painted in New Mexico.
> **When and where:** Born November 15, 1887, on a dairy farm at Sun Prairie, Wisconsin. Texas, New York, and New Mexico from 1929. Died in Santa Fe on March 6, 1986.
> **Movie:** *Georgia O'Keeffe* (about 1977), a documentary by Perry Miller Adato, made with the New York public television station WNET. It is about her life and work.

In April 1916 Alfred Stieglitz hung ten of Georgia O'Keeffe's drawings in his gallery, 291. She had been born on a dairy farm at Sun Prairie, Wisconsin, on November 15, 1887. In the fall of 1916 she became head of the art department at West Texas State Normal College, in Canyon, Texas, a college that trained teachers. She married Stieglitz in 1924.

From 1929 she spent her summers painting in New Mexico. In late 1945 she bought a house at Abiquiu, New Mexico, from the Catholic Church in Santa Fe. Stieglitz died in 1946. In 1949 she moved to New Mexico for good.

By 1972 an eye disease called macular degeneration had destroyed the center of her sight, and she could see only at the sides. She had stopped painting in oils without help. She died in Santa Fe on March 6, 1986, at the age of 98.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="georgia-okeeffe" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native artists, the boarding schools, and the Studio" -->
Angel De Cora was a Ho-Chunk painter from the Winnebago Agency in Nebraska. She was born on May 3, 1871. As a child she was taken to the boarding school at Hampton, Virginia. A boarding school is a school where children live as well as study. In her own account, a strange white man came to the reservation and asked her, through an interpreter, if she would like to ride on a steam train. When the ride ended she was at Hampton. "[It was] three years later when I returned to my mother," she said. As an adult she taught art at the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, the school Richard Henry Pratt had founded. She died on February 6, 1919.

Zitkala-Ša was a Yankton Dakota writer who lived from 1876 to 1938. In 1900 three of her essays ran in the *Atlantic Monthly* magazine. Two were about her childhood and her boarding school, and the third was about her work as a teacher. Her book *American Indian Stories* came out in 1921.

In 1932 a white teacher named Dorothy Dunn asked Chester Faris, the head of the Santa Fe Indian School, to let her open a painting studio. The school was a federal boarding school in New Mexico. The Studio opened on September 9, 1932. By 1937 it had 170 students. Dunn chose not to teach the students to draw real people and things as the eye sees them. She did not teach them to make objects look near or far, or to mix colors by rule. She taught one style: flat areas of color with heavy outlines, showing ceremonies, dances and old stories. She believed it "was the only authentic painting style for Native American artists to follow." Authentic means real and true to its roots.

Her students disagreed about her. The Chiricahua Apache artist Allan Houser said, "she trained us all the same way... Her style lacked originality and creativity." Geronima Cruz Montoya, of Ohkay Owingeh Pueblo in New Mexico, said Dunn "made us realize how important our own Indian ways were, because we had been made to feel ashamed of them. She gave us something we could be proud of." Dunn left in 1937. Montoya then ran the Studio until it closed in 1962. Other students included Pablita Velarde, the first girl in the art classes, and Harrison Begay, Quincy Tahoma, Oscar Howe, Pop Chalee and Andrew Tsinajinnie. In 1935, while the Studio was running, members of Congress created the Indian Arts and Crafts Board inside the Interior Department. They meant the board to widen the market for Native arts and crafts.

Lottie Queen Stamper, of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina, taught basket weaving for more than 30 years. In 1940 she found a photograph of the Cherokee double-woven baskets taken to London in 1725. A double-woven basket is two baskets woven one inside the other from the same strips. She spent two and a half days working out the pattern from the photograph. Then she wove a copy from 500 splints, thin strips cut for weaving. "This, indeed, was the happiest day of my life," she wrote. "Now it was my duty to teach it to my brightest students." By the middle of the 1900s, people building along the rivers had destroyed many patches of cane. The weaver Eva Wolfe drove 80 miles to Hayesville, North Carolina, to gather river cane.

### Maria Martinez

> **Who:** A potter at San Ildefonso Pueblo, a Tewa town in New Mexico, known for black-on-black pottery.
> **When and where:** Born Maria Poveka Montoya at San Ildefonso, probably in 1887. Died there on July 20, 1980.
> **Movie:** *Maria Martinez: Indian Pottery of San Ildefonso* (1972), a documentary that shows her and her son Popovi Da making pottery at the pueblo.

Maria Martinez and her husband, Julian Martinez, made black-on-black pottery at San Ildefonso Pueblo. San Ildefonso is a Tewa pueblo about 20 miles northwest of Santa Fe. Maria was born there, probably in 1887. Her aunt Nicolasa taught her to work clay.

In 1904 the couple showed how they made pottery at the World's Fair in St. Louis. Between 1907 and 1909, Edgar Lee Hewett asked Maria to make copies of ancient pots dug up at a site where he worked. Hewett was an archaeologist, a scientist who digs up and studies the things people left behind long ago. Maria shaped the pots and Julian painted them. They showed their work again at fairs in San Diego in 1914 and Chicago in 1934.

The black color comes from the fire. While the pots are fired, the potters smother them with dried manure, and the smoke turns the clay black. Potters at Santa Clara Pueblo had made polished black pots since the 1600s, and Maria learned the method from the Tafoya family there. In 1918 Julian finished the first pot with a dull black design on a shining black surface.

From 1923 to 1925 Maria signed the pots "Marie." From 1925 until Julian died in 1943, she signed them "Marie + Julian." Later she signed with her daughter-in-law, Santana, and then with her son, Popovi Da. Popovi Da built a shop at the pueblo and sold the family's work.

In 1932 officials of the Santa Fe Indian School asked Maria to teach there. She said no. "I come and I work and they can watch ... nobody teaches," she said. She died at San Ildefonso on July 20, 1980.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maria-martinez" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Harlem Renaissance" -->
In the 1920s and 1930s, Black painters, photographers and writers worked side by side in Harlem, in New York City. This time is called the Harlem Renaissance. Renaissance is a French word that means rebirth.

James Van Der Zee was born in Lenox, Massachusetts, in 1886. In 1916 he and Gaynella Greenlee opened the Guarantee Photo Studio on West 125th Street in Harlem, where he made portraits of his customers. In 1919 he photographed the victory parade of the 369th Infantry Regiment, an army unit. He also photographed the leader Marcus Garvey. His work became widely known in 1969, when it hung in a show called *Harlem on My Mind* at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Langston Hughes was born in Joplin, Missouri, in 1901. His poem "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" came out in 1920. Negro was a word people then used for Black people. Readers first noticed him through *The Crisis*, a magazine. His first book of poems, *The Weary Blues*, came out in 1926.

Aaron Douglas was a painter from Topeka, Kansas. In 1925 he meant to stop in Harlem on his way to Paris, and he stayed. He designed the cover of the magazine *Fire!!* in 1926 and drew pictures for Alain Locke's book *The New Negro*. In 1934, paid through a federal program, he painted a set of four murals for the 135th Street branch of the New York Public Library. A mural is a painting made on a wall or for a wall. The murals are called *Aspects of Negro Life*. They show Africa, slavery and the years after the Civil War, then the lynching and segregation that followed, and the move north. Segregation means keeping people apart by race. Lynching is the killing of a person by a mob, without any trial. In 1944 Douglas started the art department at Fisk University, in Nashville, and he taught there until 1966.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Augusta Savage

> **Who:** A sculptor and art teacher in Harlem.
> **When and where:** Born Augusta Christine Fells on February 29, 1892, in Green Cove Springs, Florida. Harlem from the 1920s. A farm at Saugerties, New York, from about 1940. Died in New York City on March 27, 1962.
> **Movie:** *Searching for Augusta Savage* (2024), a short documentary by Charlotte Mangin and Sandy Rattley for the PBS series *American Masters*. It is about her life and her lost work.

Augusta Savage taught young artists in Harlem, among them Jacob Lawrence, and she lost her largest sculpture because she had no money to save it. She was born in Green Cove Springs, Florida, in 1892. Her father, Edward Fells, was a Methodist minister. He said her clay figures were sinful. Her own words about him are printed by the public television station WMHT: "My father licked me four or five times a week," and he "almost whipped all the art out of me." Licked here meant beat. Some other printings of her words say "kicked" instead of "licked."

In 1923 she was chosen for a summer art school at Fontainebleau, in France. French officials offered the school to 100 American women, and her place came with a $500 scholarship. When the American committee that chose the students learned that she was Black, its members took her place away. Savage told the *New York World*: "My brother was good enough to be ... [in a] regiment that saw service in France during the war, but it seems his sister is not good enough to be a guest of the country for which he fought." One committee member, the sculptor Hermon MacNeil, had supported her. He invited her to study with him.

In 1934 she opened the Savage Studio of Arts and Crafts in a basement on West 143rd Street. Jacob Lawrence, Norman Lewis and Gwendolyn Knight studied with her. In 1935 she helped start the Harlem Artists Guild. In 1937 she became the first director of the Harlem Community Art Center, which opened on December 27, 1937. By one count, 70,592 children and adults took part in its first sixteen months. Another count gives 1,500 people.

The organizers of the 1939 New York World's Fair hired her to make a sculpture. She was the only African American woman given such a job. She made *Lift Every Voice and Sing*, named after a song by James Weldon Johnson. It was a harp 16 feet tall. Its strings were twelve Black singers, and the arm and hand of God formed its sounding board. The fair's organizers renamed it *The Harp*. She made it of plaster painted to look like black stone. She needed money to cast it in bronze, which means pouring melted metal into a mold of the sculpture. She sold small metal copies as souvenirs, but she never raised enough. When the fair closed in 1940, its temporary buildings and plaster art were bulldozed, and *The Harp* with them. No record names who gave the order. Some of the small copies survive.

In about 1940 she moved to a farm at Saugerties, New York, and mostly left the art world. She died in New York City on March 27, 1962.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="augusta-savage" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zora-neale-hurston" name="Zora Neale Hurston" movie="Zora Neale Hurston: Jump at the Sun (2008)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Zora Neale Hurston

> **Who:** A novelist and folklorist. A folklorist collects the stories, songs and sayings that people pass along by word of mouth.
> **When and where:** Born January 7, 1891, in Notasulga, Alabama. Grew up in Eatonville, Florida. Harlem, Haiti and Jamaica. Died in Fort Pierce, Florida, on January 28, 1960.
> **Movie:** *Zora Neale Hurston: Jump at the Sun* (2008), a documentary by Sam Pollard for the PBS series *American Masters*. It is about her life and writing.

Zora Neale Hurston wrote novels and collected the stories of Black Southerners, and she died poor. She was born in Notasulga, Alabama, in 1891 and grew up in Eatonville, Florida. According to the official Hurston website, Eatonville was the nation's first incorporated Black township. Incorporated means the state recognized it as a town by law, with its own government.

In 1927 she interviewed Kossola, also called Cudjo Lewis. In April 1860 soldiers of Ghezo, the king of Dahomey in West Africa, attacked Kossola's town and took him captive. Captain William Foster bought him and other captives there for Timothy Meaher, a businessman in Mobile, Alabama. In July 1860 Foster smuggled about 110 Africans into Mobile on the ship *Clotilda*, to be enslaved. Bringing enslaved people into the country had been against federal law for 52 years. In Mobile, Meaher's brother James enslaved Kossola. Hurston gathered Black folktales in Florida for her book *Mules and Men* (1935), and stories in Haiti and Jamaica for *Tell My Horse* (1938). Her novel *Their Eyes Were Watching God* came out in 1937. In 1938 and 1939 she worked in Florida for the Federal Writers' Project.

The most she ever earned in royalties from any of her books was $943.75. A royalty is the share of each book's price that goes to the writer. She had a stroke in early 1959. In October 1959 she had to enter the St. Lucie County Welfare Home, a home the county ran for people with no money, and she died there on January 28, 1960. Her neighbors in Fort Pierce collected money for her funeral, but not enough for a headstone.

After she died, a yardman cleaning out her house began burning her papers. Patrick DuVal, a law officer and her friend, put out the fire. The papers went to the University of Florida. She lay in an unmarked grave in the Garden of Heavenly Rest, a cemetery for Black people only, until August 1973. That month the novelist Alice Walker found the spot and placed a marker there.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zora-neale-hurston" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters of the 1930s and 1940s" -->
Grant Wood painted *American Gothic* in 1930. His models were his sister, Nan Wood Graham, and the family dentist, Byron McKeeby. They stand in front of a house in Eldon, Iowa. The painting won a bronze medal and $300 at the Art Institute of Chicago. One judge called it a "comic valentine." Some Iowans who read about it in the *Cedar Rapids Gazette* were angry. They felt it showed them as "pinched, grim-faced, puritanical Bible-thumpers."

Edward Hopper painted *Nighthawks* in 1942, and on May 13 of that year the Art Institute of Chicago bought it for $3,000.

Norman Rockwell's four paintings called the *Four Freedoms* ran in the *Saturday Evening Post* in February and March 1943, one each week. The paintings then went on a tour of 16 cities, where people bought war bonds, loans to the government to pay for World War II. About 1.2 million people saw the paintings, and the tour raised about $132 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Art paid for by the government, 1934 to 1943" -->
The Federal Art Project ran from August 29, 1935, to June 30, 1943, as part of the Works Progress Administration, a federal program that gave people jobs during the Great Depression, the hard years of the 1930s when many people had no work. Holger Cahill directed it. Most of its artists had to show they were poor enough to get government aid. They earned about $23.50 a week, and each had to turn in a finished work within a set number of weeks. According to Britannica, project officials employed more than 5,000 artists at its peak in 1936. Another count puts the total at about 10,000 artists and craft workers. They made about 400,000 paintings, murals, prints and posters. They also opened more than 100 community art centers. When officials cut the budget and sent out notices that ended people's jobs, artists held sit-down strikes, refusing to leave their workplaces.

The project's rules said workers must be hired fairly, whatever their race, but Black artists were routinely refused work. The records do not name the people who turned them away. In 1935 Augusta Savage, Charles Alston, Elba Lightfoot, Louise E. Jefferson, Arthur Schomburg and others started the Harlem Artists Guild, with Aaron Douglas as its president. Alston called it "a pressure group to get more black artists on the federal projects." According to *Black Art in America*, Alston became the first Black supervisor on the project.

In 1936 Alston, Vertis Hayes and Georgette Seabrooke designed murals for Harlem Hospital. According to the Harlem history project Harlem-is, it was the first mural job the WPA gave to African American artists anywhere in the country. The head of the hospital rejected the sketches because of their "Negro subject matter." Members of the Guild and the Artists Union wrote to Mayor Fiorello La Guardia and President Franklin Roosevelt. The murals were then painted as the artists had planned.

Edward Bruce ran a second program, in the Treasury Department, from 1934 to 1943. Its artists painted about 1,400 murals for post offices in more than 1,300 towns and cities. For each new post office, about 1 percent of the building money was set aside to pay for art. Bruce's officials pushed the artists toward American scenes and away from subjects that might start public quarrels. They also kept them away from abstract art, which shows shapes and colors instead of things. In Safford, Arizona, local people who were "still resentful about Apache raids" demanded a different subject for a mural, and the subject was changed. The account of the Safford mural does not say who changed it.

Federal officials also hired writers, for the Federal Writers' Project. From 1936 to 1938 its interviewers in 17 states recorded more than 2,300 first-person accounts by people who had been enslaved. The Library of Congress holds the accounts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jacob-lawrence" name="Jacob Lawrence" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jacob Lawrence

> **Who:** The painter of the *Migration Series*, sixty panels about Black families moving north.
> **When and where:** Born September 7, 1917, in Atlantic City, New Jersey. Philadelphia, Harlem from the age of 13, and Seattle. Died in Seattle on June 9, 2000.

Jacob Lawrence painted all 60 panels of *The Migration Series* in 1940 and 1941, when he was 22 and 23. His parents split up in 1924. From 1927 to 1930 he and his younger brother and sister lived in foster care in Philadelphia, with families paid to raise children not their own, while their mother worked in New York City. At 13 he moved with his mother to Harlem. He took art classes with Charles Alston at the Harlem Art Workshop. Augusta Savage got him a scholarship to the American Artists School and a paid place on the federal art project.

Before *The Migration Series* he painted series of pictures about the lives of Toussaint L'Ouverture in 1938, Frederick Douglass in 1939 and Harriet Tubman in 1940. A grant, a gift of money, from the Julius Rosenwald Fund paid for *The Migration Series*. The panels show the Great Migration, when Black families moved from the South to cities in the North. He wrote a caption for each panel first. Then he painted all 60 at the same time, one color at a time. Each panel is 12 inches by 18 inches.

In November 1941 the editors of *Fortune* magazine printed panels from the series with an essay by Alain Locke. Today the Phillips Collection in Washington holds the odd-numbered panels, and the Museum of Modern Art in New York holds the even-numbered ones.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-lawrence" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photographs of hunger, and photographs of the camps" -->
In the 1930s officials of the federal Resettlement Administration, later called the Farm Security Administration, paid photographers to record poor farm families. In March 1936 Dorothea Lange stopped at a camp of pea pickers at Nipomo, California. Freezing rain had destroyed the crop. As many as 2,500 to 3,500 people were camped there. Lange made seven pictures of a mother and her children. The government caption reads: "Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two."

Lange later wrote, "I did not ask her name or her history." She wrote that the mother said the family had been living on frozen vegetables from the fields and on birds the children killed. The mother had just sold the tires from her car to buy food, Lange wrote. The mother's son, Troy Owens, said later, "There's no way we sold our tires, because we didn't have any to sell." He thought Lange had mixed up two stories. The two accounts do not agree.

The mother was Florence Owens Thompson. She was born on September 1, 1903, in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. Both of her parents said they had Cherokee ancestors. Cherokee Nation records list her father as Cherokee, but her mother told a federal commission that both of her own parents were white. The government had paid for the photograph, so anyone could print it for free, and neither Lange nor Thompson earned money from it.

In 1978, about 42 years after the picture was taken, Emmett Corrigan, a reporter for the *Modesto Bee*, found her living in a mobile home park in Modesto, California, and named her. She told him: "I wish she hadn't taken my picture. I can't get a penny out of it. She didn't ask my name. She said she wouldn't sell the pictures. She said she'd send me a copy. She never did." In 1983, when she was sick, her children asked for help with her medical bills. More than 2,000 letters came with money. "For Mama and us, the photo had always been a bit of [a] curse," Troy Owens said. "After all those letters came in, I think it gave us a sense of pride." She died on September 16, 1983.

On February 19, 1942, President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, an order from the president that has the force of law. Under it, Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, who commanded the Army on the West Coast, ordered Japanese Americans out of their homes, some with only 48 hours' notice. Soldiers and federal officials sent about 122,000 men, women and children to ten camps. They held them there behind wire fences watched by armed guards in towers. Nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens. That year officials of the War Relocation Authority, the agency that ran the camps, hired Lange to photograph the forced removal.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dorothea-lange" name="Dorothea Lange" movie="Dorothea Lange: Grab a Hunk of Lightning (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Dorothea Lange

> **Who:** A photographer paid by the federal government to record poor families in the 1930s and the forced removal of Japanese Americans in 1942.
> **When and where:** Born Dorothea Nutzhorn on May 26, 1895, in Hoboken, New Jersey. California and the West. Died October 11, 1965.
> **Movie:** *Dorothea Lange: Grab a Hunk of Lightning* (2014), a documentary for the PBS series *American Masters*, directed by her granddaughter Dyanna Taylor. It is about her life and work.

Dorothea Lange photographed Japanese American families as soldiers and officials forced them from their homes in 1942, and officials kept most of those photographs from the public. She was born Dorothea Nutzhorn in Hoboken, New Jersey, on May 26, 1895. As a child she had polio, a disease that attacks the nerves, and it left her with a limp.

In 1942 she photographed families getting ready to leave their homes between Fresno and Sacramento. She photographed the Tanforan assembly center at San Bruno, a holding camp at a racetrack, where horse stalls had been turned into rooms for families. She also photographed the Manzanar camp. She made more than 800 photographs. Officials discouraged her from talking with the prisoners. At Manzanar they did not allow her to photograph the wire fences, the watch towers, the armed guards or any sign that people were resisting. On July 30, 1942, officials of the War Relocation Authority let her go, with the note "completion of work."

Army officers judged her pictures critical of the camps and held them until the war ended, according to the National Coalition Against Censorship. The Densho Encyclopedia says officials of the War Relocation Authority kept most of them from public view. Neither account names the officers who gave the order. After the war, the coalition says, the photographs were "quietly placed" in the National Archives. It does not say who placed them there. In 2006 the historians Linda Gordon and Gary Y. Okihiro published a selection of them in a book called *Impounded*.

On November 12, 1943, Lange wrote to the photographer Ansel Adams: "I fear the intolerance and prejudice is constantly growing." She died on October 11, 1965.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dorothea-lange" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A book banned and burned, 1939" -->
John Steinbeck's novel *The Grapes of Wrath* came out on April 14, 1939. It was the best-selling book of 1939, and by February 1940 publishers had printed 430,000 copies. It won the National Book Award and the Pulitzer Prize.

In August 1939 the members of the Kern County Board of Supervisors in California voted 4 to 1 to take the book out of the county's libraries and schools. One supervisor called it a "libel and lie." A libel is a printed lie that harms someone's good name. Bill Camp, who led the Associated Farmers, a group of large landowners, pushed for the ban. He had one of his workers, Clell Pruett, burn a copy in a trash can for a newspaper photographer. Pruett had not read the book. The county librarian, Gretchen Knief, wrote to the supervisors: "Ideas don't die because a book is forbidden reading. If Steinbeck has written the truth, that truth will survive." She did not throw the books away. She offered them to other county libraries. The book went back on the shelves in January 1941.

Laura Ingalls Wilder's Little House books, about her family's life as settlers on the frontier, came out from 1932 to 1943. Decades later, readers criticized how the books show Native and Black people, and in 2018 a librarians' group took her name off its children's book award.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paintings sent abroad, and called back, 1946 and 1947" -->
In 1946 J. LeRoy Davidson, an art historian at the State Department, spent just under $50,000 on 79 modern American paintings by 47 artists, among them Georgia O'Keeffe. He sent them to tour other countries in a show called *Advancing American Art*. One congressman said, "I am just a dumb American who pays taxes for this kind of trash." President Harry Truman also mocked the paintings. He said that if that was art, then he was a member of an African people. The word he used for them was an insult that Europeans used for the Khoekhoe people of southern Africa.

The tour was stopped in early April 1947. By the end of that month, members of Congress had cancelled the tour, cut off the State Department's art program and done away with Davidson's job. The writer Matthew Algeo says Secretary of State George Marshall ended the show in early May and told a House committee that the paintings would be brought home and sold. In June 1948 officials sold the paintings, along with 38 watercolors, by sealed bid. In a sealed bid, buyers send in secret offers and the highest offer wins. Three universities in Alabama, Oklahoma and Georgia bought 81 of the 117 works.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Painters in New York after 1950 made pictures that showed no objects at all, and officers of the CIA secretly paid for shows that sent some of that painting to Europe. In the 1960s other artists painted soup cans and comic strips, and the prices of paintings climbed. A painting that Robert Rauschenberg sold for $900 went for $85,000 at an auction in 1973. Black and Chicano artists painted murals on neighborhood walls and bridge pillars, and women and Black artists protested at museums that left them out. In 1987 volunteers laid 1,920 quilt panels on the National Mall for people who had died of AIDS, a disease that destroys the body's defenses against other illnesses. In 1989 and 1990 members of Congress fought over whether public money should pay for art that some of them called offensive. In 1990 they passed laws to protect Native artists' work and to return Native remains and sacred objects to the nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painting with no objects in it" -->
From 1947 to 1950 Jackson Pollock laid his canvas on the floor and dripped and poured paint onto it. Other people called his way of working drip painting or action painting. His pictures showed no things that a viewer could name. This kind of painting is called Abstract Expressionism. Abstract means it shows no real objects. The historian Frances Stonor Saunders writes that the center of the art world had been moving from Paris to New York since the 1930s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jackson-pollock" name="Jackson Pollock" movie="Pollock (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jackson Pollock

> **Who:** A painter who dripped and poured paint onto canvas laid on the floor.
> **When and where:** Born January 28, 1912, in Cody, Wyoming. New York City, then Springs, on Long Island, New York. Died there on August 11, 1956.
> **Movie:** *Pollock* (2000), a feature film directed by Ed Harris, who also plays Pollock. It is about his life.

Jackson Pollock painted by walking around a canvas on the floor and dripping and pouring paint onto it. He was born in Cody, Wyoming, on January 28, 1912. In 1945 he married the painter Lee Krasner, who was born in New York on October 27, 1908, and died on June 19, 1984. In 1947 he explained his method in a magazine called *Possibilities*: "I prefer to tack the unstretched canvas to the hard wall or the floor. I need the resistance of a hard surface. On the floor I am more at ease. I feel nearer, more a part of the painting, since this way I can walk around it, work from the four sides and literally be in the painting." He wrote that this was like the method of the Native sand painters of the West.

On August 8, 1949, the editors of *Life* magazine put this question in a headline over a story about him: "Jackson Pollock: Is he the greatest living painter in the United States?"

On the night of August 11, 1956, in Springs, New York, Pollock drove his car while drunk. He crashed less than a mile from his home and was killed, and so was his passenger Edith Metzger. A second passenger, Ruth Kligman, survived.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jackson-pollock" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The CIA and the painters" -->
In 1995 the historian Frances Stonor Saunders reported in *The Independent*, a British newspaper, that officers of the CIA had helped promote Abstract Expressionism in other countries. The CIA, the Central Intelligence Agency, is the government's spy agency. In those years the United States and the Soviet Union were rivals in the Cold War. The Cold War was a long contest for power and friends around the world, and it never became open war between the two. The Soviet government's official style of art was called Socialist Realism.

Donald Jameson, a former CIA officer, told Saunders that CIA officers saw a chance in this new painting. He said "Abstract Expressionism was the kind of art that made Socialist Realism look even more stylised and more rigid." The support came "at two or three removes," he said, which means through other groups. The painters did not know. Tom Braden, who led a CIA division from 1950, had worked at the Museum of Modern Art in New York in 1949. "We wanted to unite all the people who were writers, who were musicians, who were artists, to demonstrate that the West and the United States was devoted to freedom of expression," Braden said.

The money went through the Congress for Cultural Freedom, a group set up with CIA money in 1950 and run by a CIA agent. It had offices in 35 countries. It paid for touring shows of American art. One, "The New American Painting," visited every large city in Europe in 1958 and 1959. Staff of the Museum of Modern Art organized most of these shows.

Accounts differ about how much this mattered to the painters or to their fame. The art historian Jennifer McComas writes that the claims hold "some truth." She also writes that some of the writers who made them got facts wrong and chose only the shows that fit their argument. The amount of money spent on the painting shows is not known.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photographs and books" -->
In 1955 the photographer Robert Frank won a Guggenheim Fellowship, a grant of money for artists and writers. He used it to pay for trips across the United States. In the South, a sheriff told him he had "an hour to leave town." His book of photographs, *The Americans*, came out in Paris in 1958 and in New York in 1959. A writer in *Popular Photography* called the pictures "meaningless blur, grain, muddy exposures, drunken horizons and general sloppiness."

In the 1950s and 1960s Black writers won national prizes and had a play on Broadway. Ralph Ellison's novel *Invisible Man* came out in 1952 and won the National Book Award in 1953. James Baldwin published his novel *Go Tell It on the Mountain* in 1953. Lorraine Hansberry's play *A Raisin in the Sun* opened on Broadway on March 11, 1959. Its director, Lloyd Richards, was the first Black director of a Broadway play, and the New York Drama Critics' Circle named it the best play of the year. Maya Angelou's *I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings* came out in February 1969.

On November 14, 1960, Ruby Bridges, who was six, walked into William Frantz Elementary School in New Orleans between four federal officers called deputy US marshals. She and three other six-year-old girls at another school that day were the first Black children in white public schools in Louisiana. A crowd outside screamed at her. In 1963 Norman Rockwell left the *Saturday Evening Post*, whose editors limited what political subjects he could paint, and he painted Ruby Bridges's walk. On the wall behind her he painted a racial slur, the letters "KKK" and a smashed tomato. KKK stands for the Ku Klux Klan, a white group that has threatened, beaten and killed Black people. The editors of *Look* magazine printed the painting, *The Problem We All Live With*, on January 14, 1964.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gordon-parks" name="Gordon Parks" movie="Half Past Autumn: The Life and Works of Gordon Parks (2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gordon Parks

> **Who:** A photographer, the first African American staff photographer at *Life* magazine.
> **When and where:** Born November 30, 1912, in Fort Scott, Kansas. Washington, D.C., from 1942. New York. Died in New York on March 7, 2006.
> **Movie:** *Half Past Autumn: The Life and Works of Gordon Parks* (2000), a documentary about his life.

Gordon Parks was the first African American photographer on the staff of *Life* magazine. He was born in Fort Scott, Kansas, on November 30, 1912, the youngest of 15 children. He went to a segregated elementary school, a school for Black children only. He bought his first camera at a pawnshop, a store that lends money and sells things people leave there and do not come back for.

In 1942 he won a Julius Rosenwald Fellowship and went to Washington, D.C., to work for the Farm Security Administration. Workers in restaurants and shops in the segregated capital refused to serve him. After that he photographed Ella Watson, a Black woman on the cleaning crew of the agency's building. She stands with a broom and a mop in front of an American flag. He titled the picture *American Gothic, Washington, D.C.* His boss, Roy Stryker, said the picture was an attack on America and could get all his photographers fired.

In 1948 Parks published a photo essay, a set of photographs that tell a story, about a young gang leader in Harlem. It won him a staff job at *Life*, and he stayed at the magazine until 1972. He died in New York on March 7, 2006.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gordon-parks" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pop Art, and the price of a painting" -->
In the early 1960s, Pop Art painters made pictures of comic strips, soup cans and famous faces. Roy Lichtenstein painted scenes from comic strips in oil paint. His 1961 painting *Look Mickey* is now in the National Gallery of Art in Washington.

In July 1962 Andy Warhol showed 32 paintings of Campbell's soup cans at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles. Each canvas is 20 inches by 16 inches. The art dealer Irving Blum, who bought and sold art for a living, bought all 32 for $1,000, paid in ten monthly payments of $100. The Museum of Modern Art got them from Blum in 1996. In 1970 one of Warhol's soup-can paintings sold at auction for $60,000. At that time it was the highest auction price for a living American artist.

On October 18, 1973, Robert Scull, who owned a fleet of taxis, sold 50 works from his art collection at an auction in New York for about $2.2 million. Years earlier, Robert Rauschenberg had sold Scull his painting *Thaw* for $900. That night it sold for $85,000. Artists received nothing when their work was sold again. Rauschenberg shoved Scull and told him he had been working hard for Scull to make that profit.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andy-warhol" name="Andy Warhol" movie="Andy Warhol: A Documentary Film (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andy Warhol

> **Who:** A painter who made pictures of soup cans, dollar bills and famous faces. His studio was called the Factory.
> **When and where:** Born Andrew Warhola Jr. on August 6, 1928, in Pittsburgh. New York. Died in New York on February 22, 1987.
> **Movie:** *Andy Warhol: A Documentary Film* (2006), a four-hour documentary by Ric Burns, shown on PBS. It is about his life.

Andy Warhol started out drawing pictures for magazines before he made his paintings of soup cans. He was born Andrew Warhola Jr. in Pittsburgh in 1928. His parents were working-class immigrants, Rusyns from the village of Miková, in what is now northeastern Slovakia. His father worked in a coal mine.

In 1949 he moved to New York. There Tina Fredericks, an editor at *Glamour* magazine, bought a drawing of an orchestra from him for $10. Through the early 1950s his drawings appeared regularly in fashion magazines and in *The New York Times*.

In 1961 a printer, Floriano Vecchi, taught him silkscreen printing. The artist puts a design on a fine screen and pushes ink through it with a rubber blade, so the same picture can be printed again and again. His first silkscreens, made in early 1962, showed one-dollar and two-dollar bills. Muriel Latow, who knew him from the art world, had told him to paint what he liked most, and he answered, "money." One of his best-known early silkscreen paintings is *Marilyn Diptych*, from 1962, a picture in two parts of the actress Marilyn Monroe.

His studio was called the Factory. On June 3, 1968, the writer Valerie Solanas shot Warhol and the critic Mario Amaya at the Factory. Warhol was in the hospital for nearly two months. He died in New York on February 22, 1987, after an operation on his gallbladder.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andy-warhol" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Walls, bridges and museums: art made as protest" -->
In 1967 fourteen artists of the Organization of Black American Culture, among them William Walker, painted the *Wall of Respect* on the side of a closed bar at 43rd Street and Langley Avenue in Chicago. It showed Nat Turner, Harriet Tubman, W. E. B. Du Bois, Marcus Garvey, Malcolm X, Gwendolyn Brooks, Muhammad Ali, Aretha Franklin and others. In the eight years after it, artists painted more than 1,500 murals across the country. A fire damaged the building in 1971. The building was then torn down, and the mural with it. The records do not say who ordered the building torn down.

Barrio Logan is a Mexican American neighborhood of San Diego. Many Mexican Americans call themselves Chicano. In 1963 highway builders cut it in two with Interstate 5. In 1969 they cut it again with the ramps of the bridge to Coronado. City officials approved a park under the bridge that June and then did nothing. On April 22, 1970, a student named Mario Solis saw bulldozers there getting the land ready for a parking lot for a California Highway Patrol station. Residents took over the land.

On July 1, 1970, city officials set aside $21,814.96 for a park of 1.8 acres, called Chicano Park. The artist Salvador Torres had proposed painting the bridge pillars. On March 23, 1973, the artist Victor Ochoa brought 300 brushes, and nearly 300 people painted all weekend. The artists raised the money for the paint themselves.

Faith Ringgold and other artists protested at the Whitney Museum of American Art in New York in 1970, because its show of American art had no Black artists in it. They also demanded that half the artists it showed be women.

Judy Chicago finished *The Dinner Party* in 1979. It is a table with three sides, set with 39 places for 39 women from history and legend, among them Sacajawea, Sojourner Truth, Susan B. Anthony, Virginia Woolf and Georgia O'Keeffe. Each place has a hand-painted plate. Most of the plates show a shape based on the female body.

The critic Hilton Kramer called it "very bad art ... failed art." Representative Robert K. Dornan called it "ceramic 3-D pornography." Pornography means pictures or objects made to show sex. The writer Alice Walker objected that Sojourner Truth, the one Black woman at the table, was shown in a different way from the white women. In 1990 the board of the University of the District of Columbia voted to accept it as a gift. After the *Washington Times* printed stories against it, the plan was dropped. The records do not say who dropped it. *The Dinner Party* is now at the Brooklyn Museum.

Jean-Michel Basquiat and Al Diaz wrote graffiti signed "SAMO" on walls in Manhattan in the late 1970s. Graffiti is writing or drawing on walls without permission. Basquiat later became a painter. He died of a heroin overdose on August 12, 1988, at 27.

On September 15, 1983, New York City Transit Police officers arrested Michael Stewart, a 25-year-old Black artist, at the First Avenue subway station for writing graffiti. While the officers held him, he went into a coma, a state like deep sleep that a person cannot wake from. He died on September 28. The city's medical examiner, the doctor who studies a body to find the cause of death, said his heart had stopped. Others disputed that finding. Six officers were tried for causing his death by carelessness, for assault and for lying under oath. On November 24, 1985, an all-white jury found them not guilty. A lawsuit over his death was later settled for $1.7 million. Basquiat painted *Defacement (The Death of Michael Stewart)* about him.

Keith Haring drew in the early 1980s in white chalk on the black paper that covered empty advertising frames in New York subway stations. He learned he had AIDS in 1988, and in 1989 he started the Keith Haring Foundation. He died on February 16, 1990.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="faith-ringgold" name="Faith Ringgold" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Faith Ringgold

> **Who:** A painter and quilt maker who also wrote and illustrated children's books.
> **When and where:** Born Faith Willi Jones on October 8, 1930, in Harlem. New York City. Died in Englewood, New Jersey, on April 13, 2024.

Faith Ringgold painted stories on quilts, and she was arrested for protesting at a museum that left Black artists out. She was born in Harlem on October 8, 1930. For years she taught art in New York City public schools. In 1967 she painted *The Flag Is Bleeding* and *Die* for a series called *The American People*.

With the critic Lucy Lippard and the artist Poppy Johnson, she started the Ad Hoc Women's Art Committee. They protested at the Whitney Museum and demanded that half the artists in its shows be women. The Whitney's show had no Black artists at all, not even Jacob Lawrence, whose work the museum owned. Police arrested Ringgold on November 13, 1970. That year she and her daughter, Michele Wallace, also started a group called Women Students and Artists for Black Art Liberation.

From the 1980s she painted stories on quilts. *Tar Beach 2* (1990) shows Cassie Louise Lightfoot, an eight-year-old girl who dreams of flying over Harlem. She wrote and illustrated more than a dozen children's books. She died in Englewood, New Jersey, on April 13, 2024.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="faith-ringgold" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Native artists and Native objects" -->
Oscar Howe was a Yanktonai Dakota painter, born on the Crow Creek Sioux Reservation in South Dakota in 1915. He had studied at Dorothy Dunn's Studio in Santa Fe. In 1958 the judges of the Indian art show at the Philbrook Museum in Tulsa, Oklahoma, turned down his painting as "a fine painting, but not Indian." Howe wrote back: "Who ever said, that my paintings are not in the traditional Indian style, has poor knowledge of Indian art indeed. There is much more to Indian Art, than pretty, stylized pictures." He added: "Are we to be herded like a bunch of sheep, with no right for individualism, dictated as the Indian has always been."

When the Studio closed in 1962, the Institute of American Indian Arts opened in Santa Fe. Fritz Scholder, a registered member of the La Jolla Band of Luiseño Indians, taught there from 1964. From 1967 he painted Native people with American flags, beer cans and cats. The paintings started an argument, among Native artists too, that still goes on. The Cherokee painter Roy Boney Jr. said of *Indian with Beer Can* (1969): "While some might find the image offensive or even ugly, it definitely was not a maiden on the river or a warrior on horseback, and I liked that."

T. C. Cannon, a Kiowa and Caddo painter, studied at the Institute. He served in Vietnam in 1967 and 1968 as a paratrooper, a soldier who jumps from planes, and he won two Bronze Stars, medals for brave or useful service in war. He died in 1978 at the age of 31.

In 1981 Maya Lin, a student of 21, won the contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington. The judges knew the 1,421 entries only by number, not by name.

In 1990 members of Congress passed the Indian Arts and Crafts Act. It makes it a crime to sell work as made by a Native artist when it was not. A person who does it for the first time can be fined up to $250,000 or sent to prison for five years, or both. A business can be fined up to $1,000,000.

On November 16, 1990, President George H. W. Bush signed the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, called NAGPRA. Representative Mo Udall had introduced it. Repatriation means sending something back home. The law covers museums and agencies that take federal money. They must return Native human remains, objects buried with the dead, sacred objects and objects that belong to a whole nation. They must return them to descendants and to the Native nations.

Some of those remains and objects had been dug out of graves and mounds. At Craig Mound, a burial mound at Spiro, Oklahoma, six men who called themselves the Pocola Mining Company dug into the graves from 1933 to 1935. Photographs of the dig from 1935, and notes drawn from a 1952 report on the mound, name some of the diggers on the website of a company that sells copies of Spiro objects: the brothers Bill and Hayden Vandagriff, W. Guinn Cooper, John Hobbs and K. A. McKenzie. They destroyed about a third of the mound and sold thousands of objects to collectors around the world. Then, from 1936 to 1941, University of Oklahoma archaeologists led workers paid by a government jobs program, and those workers took the remains of more than 600 people out of the same mound.

In 1992 the artist Fred Wilson rearranged the collection of the Maryland Historical Society in Baltimore for a show called *Mining the Museum*. In a case of fine silver, he placed shackles that had been used to chain enslaved people. He also hung a portrait of Henry Darnall III, a boy from a rich Maryland family, painted in about 1710 by the German-born painter Justus Engelhardt Kühn. An enslaved Black boy stands beside Henry in the picture. Wilson lit the face of the enslaved boy and played a recorded voice asking, "Am I your brother?" In another room he set old chairs facing a whipping post. Several writers reported that the post had been used on enslaved people. In fact it had been used at the Baltimore City jail on men convicted of beating their wives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The AIDS Memorial Quilt, 1987" -->
AIDS is a disease that destroys the body's defenses against other illnesses. In November 1985, Cleve Jones, an activist in San Francisco, learned that more than 1,000 people in his city had died of AIDS. He made the first quilt panel, for his friend Marvin Feldman. In June 1987 he, Mike Smith, Gert McMullin and others started the NAMES Project Foundation.

Each panel is three feet by six feet, about the size of a grave, and each one is made in memory of people who died of AIDS. Many people who died of AIDS had no funeral. Some of their families were ashamed, and workers at many funeral homes and cemeteries refused to handle their bodies.

On October 11, 1987, volunteers laid 1,920 panels on the National Mall in Washington, over an area larger than a football field. They read all 1,920 names aloud. About half a million people came that weekend. By October 1989 the quilt had more than 12,000 panels. In October 1996 the whole quilt covered the Mall for the last time, and about 1.2 million people came to see it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fights over public money for art, 1989 and 1990" -->
The National Endowment for the Arts, or NEA, is a federal agency that gives money to artists and museums. In 1987 the photographer Andres Serrano made a picture of a small plastic crucifix, a model of Jesus on the cross, in a glass of his own urine. It won an award of $15,000, paid for in part with NEA money. In 1989 Senators Al D'Amato and Jesse Helms attacked the award. Some members of Congress called art like this obscene or blasphemous. Obscene means so shocking or indecent that some people want it banned, and blasphemous means insulting to God or religion. Serrano received death threats and hate mail, and he lost grants.

Robert Mapplethorpe was a photographer who died of AIDS on March 9, 1989. That June, Christina Orr-Cahall, director of the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, cancelled a show of his photographs. She feared for the NEA's money. Senator Helms then proposed that no NEA money could pay for "obscene" art. For a time, artists who took NEA grants had to sign a promise not to promote obscenity.

In 1990 the Mapplethorpe show opened at the Contemporary Arts Center in Cincinnati. After a campaign by a group called Citizens for Community Values, law officers ordered 400 visitors out of the museum and videotaped the photographs. Prosecutors charged the museum and its director, Dennis Barrie, with obscenity. It was the first time a museum in the United States was put on trial for art it showed. On October 5, 1990, a jury found them not guilty. More than 80,000 people saw the show in Cincinnati.

In June 1990 the NEA chairman, John Frohnmayer, turned down grants to four performance artists, who put on live shows, because of what their work was about. They were Karen Finley, Tim Miller, John Fleck and Holly Hughes. Panels of experts had already approved the grants. The four artists won in court in 1993. In 1998 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled in favor of the way the NEA chose its grants. After that, NEA officials stopped giving most grants to single artists.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="toni-morrison" name="Toni Morrison" movie="Toni Morrison: The Pieces I Am (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Toni Morrison

> **Who:** A novelist and book editor who won the Nobel Prize in Literature.
> **When and where:** Born Chloe Ardelia Wofford on February 18, 1931, in Lorain, Ohio. Syracuse, then New York City. Died in the Bronx on August 5, 2019.
> **Movie:** *Toni Morrison: The Pieces I Am* (2019), a documentary by Timothy Greenfield-Sanders about her life and work.

Toni Morrison published other Black writers as an editor before she became a famous novelist herself. She was born Chloe Ardelia Wofford in Lorain, Ohio, on February 18, 1931. In about 1965 she began work as an editor for a textbook company owned by Random House, in Syracuse, New York. About two years later she moved to Random House in New York City. There she was the first Black woman senior editor in the fiction department.

As an editor she published the novelist Gayl Jones, whose writing she had discovered, and the writer Toni Cade Bambara. She also published books by the activist Angela Davis and the boxer Muhammad Ali. She published the poet Henry Dumas, whom a transit officer had shot dead in a New York subway station in 1968. The records do not name the officer. In 1974 she and the collector Middleton A. Harris put together *The Black Book*, a collection of old papers, photographs, advertisements and pictures about Black life in America.

Her first novel, *The Bluest Eye*, came out in 1970. While working on *The Black Book*, she found the story of Margaret Garner. On January 28, 1856, Garner and her family escaped from slavery to a place near Cincinnati, Ohio. When slave catchers came for them, Garner killed her two-year-old daughter, Mary, with a knife rather than see the child returned to slavery. That story became the base for Morrison's novel *Beloved* (1987), which won the Pulitzer Prize in 1988. In 1993 she won the Nobel Prize in Literature. She died in the Bronx on August 5, 2019.

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="art" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 2025, 91 out of every 100 adults in the United States owned a smartphone with a camera in it, according to a survey by the Pew Research Center. After 2022, computer programs could make pictures in the style of living artists, and some of those artists sued the companies that made the programs. Under a rule that took effect in January 2024, museums must get a Native nation's consent before they show its ancestors' remains or sacred objects. Two Black artists painted the official portraits of a president and a first lady in 2018. In 2025 one of them cancelled her show at a national museum after officials there questioned one of her paintings. That year officials of President Donald Trump's administration ended many federal arts grants and asked for a review of eight Smithsonian museums.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A camera in every pocket" -->
In a survey taken from February to June 2025, the Pew Research Center found that 98 out of every 100 adults in the United States owned a cellphone. Ninety-one out of every 100 owned a smartphone. In Pew's first survey about smartphones, in 2011, only 35 out of every 100 did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who is in the picture" -->
On February 12, 2018, officials of the National Portrait Gallery in Washington unveiled the official portraits of former President Barack Obama and former First Lady Michelle Obama. Kehinde Wiley painted the former president. Amy Sherald painted the former first lady. According to the gallery's staff, Wiley and Sherald were the first African American artists chosen to paint the gallery's official portraits of a president or a first lady. Wiley painted Obama sitting in front of a wall of leaves and flowers. The chrysanthemums stand for Chicago, the jasmine for Hawaii and the African blue lilies for Kenya, his father's country. Sherald titled her painting *Michelle LaVaughn Robinson Obama*.

The two portraits then toured museums, first the Art Institute of Chicago, and in 2022 Atlanta, Houston, San Francisco and Boston. The Los Angeles County Museum of Art was another stop. At the Art Institute of Chicago, the crowds broke the museum's records for daily attendance again and again, according to the Portrait Gallery.

Kara Walker was born in Stockton, California, on November 26, 1969. She cuts figures out of black paper and sets them up in scenes, as big as a room, about slavery in the South. In 1997, when she was 28, she won a MacArthur Fellowship, a large prize of money given to people for creative work. That same year the artist Betye Saar sent letters to about 200 artists and politicians. Saar objected that Walker's work used racist pictures of Black people. In a 1999 PBS documentary, Saar called the work "revolting and negative" and said it pandered to "the white art establishment." Pandered means gave people what they wanted, to win their favor. Other writers call Walker one of the most prominent and praised Black American artists working today.

From May to July 2014, inside the old Domino Sugar Refinery in Brooklyn before it was torn down, Walker showed a huge white figure coated in sugar. It was a sphinx, a creature with a lion's body and a human head, and its face had the features of a Black woman. Fifteen smaller figures stood around it. She called it *A Subtlety, or the Marvelous Sugar Baby*. Its full title calls it a tribute to "the unpaid and overworked Artisans who have refined our Sweet tastes from the cane fields to the Kitchens of the New World." The unpaid workers in the cane fields were enslaved people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amy-sherald" name="Amy Sherald" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Amy Sherald

> **Who:** The painter of Michelle Obama's official portrait and of Breonna Taylor.
> **When and where:** Born August 30, 1973, in Columbus, Georgia. Baltimore, then New Jersey from 2018.

Amy Sherald waited tables until she was 38 while she kept painting, and then she painted Michelle Obama's official portrait. She was born in Columbus, Georgia, on August 30, 1973. She studied painting at Clark Atlanta University, where she finished in 1997, and at the Maryland Institute College of Art in Baltimore, where she finished in 2004. She taught art at the Baltimore City Detention Center.

When she was 30, doctors found that she had congestive heart failure, which means her heart could not pump blood well enough. On December 18, 2012, when she was 39, she had a heart transplant, an operation that gave her the heart of a person who had died.

In 2016 she won the National Portrait Gallery's portrait contest with a painting called *Miss Everything (Unsuppressed Deliverance)*. She was the first woman and the first African American to win it. Two years later, gallery officials unveiled her portrait of Michelle Obama. In 2018 she moved from Baltimore to New Jersey.

On March 13, 2020, police officers in Louisville, Kentucky, forced their way into Breonna Taylor's apartment with a warrant to search for drugs. Taylor was a Black woman. Her boyfriend fired one shot, the officers fired 32, and a shot by officer Myles Cosgrove killed her. No officer was charged with killing her. One officer, Brett Hankison, whose shots hit no one, was later convicted in federal court of using too much force. For the September 2020 cover of *Vanity Fair*, Sherald painted Taylor's portrait. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture and the Speed Art Museum in Louisville bought the painting together.

In 2024 and 2025 a large show of Sherald's work, *American Sublime*, hung at the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art and at the Whitney Museum of American Art in New York. It was due at the National Portrait Gallery in September 2025. Officials at the gallery, whom the reports do not name, raised "concerns" about one painting, *Trans Forming Liberty*. It shows a transgender woman in the pose of the Statue of Liberty. A transgender person is someone whose sense of being a man or a woman is different from the sex written down at birth. The officials talked about taking the painting out of the show. On July 24, 2025, Sherald cancelled the show. "I cannot in good conscience comply with a culture of censorship, especially when it targets vulnerable communities," she said. In a statement, Smithsonian officials said they had proposed adding a video to explain the painting. The Baltimore Museum of Art showed *American Sublime* instead, from November 2, 2025, to April 5, 2026.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amy-sherald" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Answering the Confederate statues" -->
In February 1861 delegates from seven Southern states that had left the United States met in Montgomery, Alabama. They set up the Confederate States of America, called the Confederacy. Its leaders then fought the United States in the Civil War to keep slavery.

In 2016 a show of the painter Kehinde Wiley's work opened at the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts. While he was there, he saw the Confederate statues on Monument Avenue in Richmond, Virginia. He then made *Rumors of War*, a bronze statue of a young Black man in jeans and sneakers riding a horse. The pose copies the statue of the Confederate general J. E. B. Stuart on Monument Avenue. It stands more than 27 feet tall. It was first shown in Times Square in New York in September 2019. On December 10, 2019, it was unveiled outside the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts in Richmond, where it stays. Museum staff estimated the crowd at more than 5,000 people. Work by Wiley is also in the Freedom Monument Sculpture Park in Montgomery, Alabama, which opened on March 27, 2024.

A bronze statue of the Confederate general Stonewall Jackson, made by Charles Keck in 1921, stood in Charlottesville, Virginia, for 100 years. On August 12, 2017, at a rally in Charlottesville called Unite the Right, James Alex Fields Jr. drove his car into people protesting against the rally. He killed Heather Heyer, who was 32. City workers took the Jackson statue down on July 10, 2021, along with a statue of Robert E. Lee. The members of the City Council voted, all in favor, to give the Jackson statue to The Brick, an art gallery in Los Angeles. Kara Walker cut the bronze apart and joined the pieces into a new shape, which she finished in 2023. She called it *Unmanned Drone*. The Museum of Contemporary Art in Los Angeles bought it in February 2026. In October 2023 members of the Jefferson School African American Heritage Center melted down the Lee statue, in a project called Swords into Plowshares.

From October 23, 2025, to May 3, 2026, the Museum of Contemporary Art and The Brick showed ten statues that had been taken down from public places, most of them Confederate, in a show called *MONUMENTS*. New works by living artists stood beside them. Kara Walker helped choose the works. The oldest statue in the show was an 1887 figure of Roger B. Taney. As chief justice, the head judge of the Supreme Court, he wrote the Dred Scott decision of 1857, which held that Black Americans were not and could never be citizens. Hamza Walker, the director of The Brick, said the works "address the questions of who we want to be as a nation, and who and what is worth remembering, let alone celebrating."

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Street murals after 2020" -->
On May 25, 2020, a Minneapolis police officer, Derek Chauvin, murdered George Floyd by kneeling on his neck for more than nine minutes. In 2021 a jury convicted Chauvin of murder. In the weeks after, people painted words and portraits on walls, on streets and on the plywood boards nailed over shop windows. In early June 2020 a team at the University of St. Thomas in St. Paul, Minnesota, led by the professors Heather Shirey, Todd Lawrence and Paul Lorah, began recording this art, from small stickers to large murals. Some of the painted boards were taken down, stored and later shown in exhibits.

In early June 2020, days after federal officers used tear gas on protesters in Lafayette Square near the White House, Washington's mayor, Muriel Bowser, had the words "Black Lives Matter" painted across two blocks of 16th Street. The yellow letters were 48 feet wide. She renamed the spot Black Lives Matter Plaza. Keyonna Jones, one of the artists who painted it, said, "We are saying it loud. We are here." According to NPR, the whole project cost more than $4 million.

In early March 2025 Representative Andrew Clyde of Georgia introduced a bill to cut federal money to the city unless it removed the words and renamed the spot Liberty Plaza. Bowser had earlier called the mural permanent. Now she announced it would go: "now we can't afford to be distracted by meaningless congressional interference." Workers of the District Department of Transportation began tearing it out on March 10, 2025.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What museums hold, and what goes back" -->
On December 6, 2023, officials of the Interior Department announced new rules for NAGPRA, the 1990 law on returning Native remains and sacred objects. The rules took effect on January 12, 2024. A museum must now get the consent of the Native nation, or of the dead person's descendants, before it shows Native human remains or sacred objects. The same consent is needed before staff study them or let others get at them. These remains and objects had been taken from Native graves and lands in earlier years, by men such as the diggers at Mound City in 1846 and at Spiro in the 1930s.

In January 2024 museum leaders closed or covered Native displays. Sean Decatur, president of the American Museum of Natural History in New York, closed its Eastern Woodlands and Great Plains halls on January 27, 2024, and covered seven more cases. He wrote to his staff that the halls came from "an era when museums such as ours did not respect the values, perspectives, and indeed shared humanity of Indigenous peoples." About 500,000 children visit that museum each year on school or camp trips. In October 2023 museum officials had said that about 26 out of every 100 of the 12,000 human remains the museum held were Native American. Staff at the Field Museum in Chicago, the Cleveland Museum of Art and the Metropolitan Museum of Art covered cases or took objects down. Harvard's Peabody Museum closed most of its floors on January 16, 2024, while its staff asked Native leaders which objects to remove. They took down nearly 40. No one has published a count of every museum that did the same.

According to *The Art Newspaper*, from 1990 to early 2024 museums paid less than $60,000 in all in fines for breaking NAGPRA, and those fines came from 20 museums. Kelly Applegate, of the Mille Lacs Band of Ojibwe, wrote to the Interior Department that museums had used the law's wording "to not give complete information about the entirety of their collections."

The latest federal count comes from the National Park Service, in a report published on May 29, 2026. Since 1990, museums and federal agencies have reported holding the remains of 222,824 Native people. For 142,389 of them, officials have published a notice naming the nation or the family with the right to them. That notice is a legal step, and it does not mean the person's body has gone home. As of September 30, 2025, the remains of 80,435 people had not yet gone through those legal steps. They were held by 381 museums and 104 federal offices. The report states that each year museums find and report remains they had never listed before. As of September 2026, the 2024 rule was still in force.

Some returns fall outside NAGPRA. On October 29, 2025, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston gave the descendants of David Drake, the enslaved potter and poet, ownership of two jars he made in 1857. The museum's officials said Drake "was not permitted to possess the jars and did not receive remuneration for them," which means he was not paid. The jars had been sold for the profit of his enslaver, Lewis J. Miles. The family kept one jar and lent it back to the museum. Museum officials bought the other one back from them. On that second jar, called the Poem Jar, Drake wrote: "I made this Jar = for cash / Though its called Lucre trash." Lucre is an old word for money.

### Jeffrey Gibson

> **Who:** A painter and sculptor, a member of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians and of Cherokee descent.
> **When and where:** Born March 31, 1972, in Colorado Springs, Colorado. Grew up in North Carolina, New Jersey, West Germany and South Korea. Lives near Hudson, New York.

In 2024 Jeffrey Gibson was the only artist shown in the United States Pavilion at the Venice Biennale. The Biennale is an international art show held in Venice, Italy, and each country shows its artists in its own building, called a pavilion. According to SITE Santa Fe, one of the show's sponsors, he was the first Native artist to represent the United States there alone. He is a member of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians and of Cherokee descent. He was born in Colorado Springs, Colorado, on March 31, 1972. His father was a civil engineer for the Defense Department, and the family lived in North Carolina, New Jersey, West Germany and South Korea. Both of his parents had attended boarding schools as children. At the federal boarding schools for Native children, staff had for many years cut the children's hair, taken away their clothes and forbidden them to speak their own languages.

He studied painting at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago and at the Royal College of Art in London. The Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians paid for his study in London. "My chief felt that me going there, being a strong artist, made him stronger," he said, speaking of the chief of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians. His paintings and sculptures use bright color, detailed patterns and written words. According to the Portland Art Museum, he mixes American, Native and queer history with music and popular culture. Queer is a word many gay and lesbian people use for themselves. He won a MacArthur Fellowship in 2019.

His Venice show ran from April 20 to November 24, 2024. He named it *the space in which to place me*, after a poem by the Oglala Lakota poet Layli Long Soldier. The same sponsor says it was also the first United States pavilion organized by a Native curator, Kathleen Ash-Milby of the Portland Art Museum. A curator is the museum worker who chooses the art for a show and plans it. From September 12, 2025, to June 9, 2026, four large sculptures by Gibson, of a deer, a coyote, a squirrel and a hawk, stood on the front of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. On April 9, 2026, the Obama Foundation said it had asked him to make a wall of 17 round prints for the Obama Presidential Center.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prices, and a picture that is only a file" -->
On May 9, 2022, a buyer paid $195 million at Christie's auction house in New York for Andy Warhol's 1964 portrait of Marilyn Monroe, *Shot Sage Blue Marilyn*. The Associated Press reported that no work by an American artist had ever sold for more at auction. It beat the $110.5 million paid in 2017 for a 1982 painting of a skull by Jean-Michel Basquiat. The money went to a charity for children in Zurich. As of September 2026, no American artist's work had sold for more. In May 2026 a painting by Jackson Pollock, *Number 7A, 1948*, sold for $181.2 million, according to the *Robb Report*.

Georgia O'Keeffe's *Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1* sold for $44.4 million in 2014. According to the Associated Press, that was the most ever paid at auction for a work by a woman until November 20, 2025, when a painting by the Mexican painter Frida Kahlo sold for $54.7 million.

On March 11, 2021, a digital picture by Mike Winkelmann, who calls himself Beeple, sold at Christie's for more than $69.3 million, according to Al Jazeera. It is a collage of 5,000 pictures he made, one each day, starting on May 1, 2007. The buyer, Vignesh Sundaresan, a programmer based in Singapore, paid in Ether, a kind of digital money. He got a record on a computer network that names him as the owner of the file, and the right to show it. He did not get the copyright, the legal right to copy the work and sell copies. The buyer and the seller both owned shares in digital tokens tied to other Beeple works, and some people called the sale a publicity stunt.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures made by computer programs" -->
By the summer of 2022, anyone could type a few words into a website and get a picture back, as NPR reported. In January 2023 Kelly McKernan, Sarah Andersen, Karla Ortiz and other artists sued Stability AI, the company behind the program Stable Diffusion, along with Midjourney and the art website DeviantArt. Accounts give the date as January 12 or January 13. In their lawsuit, the artists say the companies built these programs by feeding them huge collections of pictures gathered from the internet. They say the collection behind Stable Diffusion held more than five billion pictures, copied without asking the people who made them. These are the artists' claims, and no court has yet ruled on whether they are true.

On August 12, 2024, Judge William Orrick in San Francisco let the artists' copyright claims go forward. As of September 2026, the case was still before him, and no trial had been held, according to the court's record of the case.

On November 3, 2018, the computer scientist Stephen Thaler asked the US Copyright Office to register a picture made by his computer program, with the program named as the only author. On August 12, 2019, officials refused, because the picture had no human author. They relied in part on an 1884 Supreme Court case. In that case the justices had called a photograph of the writer Oscar Wilde "an original work of art, the product of plaintiff's intellectual invention." Judges on a federal appeals court agreed with the refusal in 2025. On March 2, 2026, the justices of the Supreme Court declined to hear Thaler's appeal. As the law stood in 2026, according to the National Constitution Center, no one could hold the copyright to a picture made by a program with no human author.

### Kelly McKernan

> **Who:** An illustrator and painter, one of three artists who sued the makers of picture-making programs in January 2023.
> **When and where:** Born 1986. Nashville, Tennessee.

Kelly McKernan was one of three artists who sued the companies behind picture-making programs in January 2023. McKernan uses the words they and them. They were born in 1986 and live in Nashville, Tennessee, where they teach at the Nossi College of Art and Design. McKernan is a single parent.

In 2002, as a high school student, McKernan joined DeviantArt, a website where artists share their work. For about 20 years they posted paintings there, "not for money," and built a following. In January 2023 McKernan told NPR: "I almost barely make rent most months. I will spend, you know, 30 hours on a painting, and I won't see any money from that until it sells."

People using picture programs had typed the words "in the style of Kelly McKernan" more than 12,000 times. Some people sold the results on websites such as Redbubble, with McKernan's name attached. "There's more and more images with my name attached to it that I can see my hand in, but it's not my work," McKernan said.

On October 30, 2023, Judge William Orrick dismissed most of the claims in the artists' case and allowed them to rewrite them. Cleary Gottlieb, a law firm that represents Midjourney, wrote a report on the ruling. According to that report, he dismissed McKernan's copyright claims because McKernan had not registered those paintings with the Copyright Office. In 2023 *Time* magazine named McKernan to its list of the 100 most influential people in artificial intelligence. By September 2026 McKernan's name was no longer on the list of artists suing.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Books" -->
Books for sale carry an identifying number called an ISBN, and in the United States a company called Bowker issues them. By Bowker's figures, reported by the publishing writer Jane Friedman, writers who published their own books got these numbers for 53 percent more new books in 2021 than in 2018. People in the United States spent $2.43 billion on audiobooks in 2025, 9 percent more than in 2024, according to a survey for the Audio Publishers Association.

In the mid-2020s, school and library officials removed or restricted thousands of books. Two groups count this, and their numbers differ because they count different things. PEN America, a group of writers that defends free expression, counts each time school officials remove or restrict a book. It counted 10,046 in the 2023-24 school year and 6,870 in 2024-25. The American Library Association counts attempts it hears about to remove books from school, public and college libraries, listed by title. It counted 4,235 different titles in 2025, and it found that 92 out of every 100 of those attempts were started by "pressure groups, government officials and decision makers."

On June 23, 2018, the board of the Association for Library Service to Children took Laura Ingalls Wilder's name off its award for children's books. The award had carried her name since 1954. The board members wrote that her books include "expressions of stereotypical attitudes inconsistent with ALSC's core values." A stereotype is an unfair, fixed idea about a whole group of people. They acted after critics pointed to how her books show Native and Black people, and after a group that included the American Indian Library Association reviewed the award. In the 1935 book *Little House on the Prairie*, Wilder described one place as somewhere "there were no people. Only Indians lived there." Later printings changed that line. Several characters in the Little House books say that "the only good Indian is a dead Indian." The prize is now called the Children's Literature Legacy Award.

In March 2023 the members of the board of the National Audubon Society voted to keep the name of John James Audubon, who painted *The Birds of America* and who had enslaved people. "The name has come to represent not one person, but a broader love of birds and nature," said the board chair, Susan Bell. Three board members resigned. The leaders of the local Audubon groups in Chicago, Seattle, Portland and Washington, D.C., had already decided to drop the name.

On May 2, 2025, White House officials sent Congress a budget for 2026 that would have shut down the National Endowment for the Arts. The same day, NEA staff emailed arts groups across the country that their grants were withdrawn or ended because the projects fell outside the President's priorities. On May 5 about a dozen senior NEA staff members resigned. Members of Congress did not close the agency. They gave it $207 million for 2026, the same amount as in 2024 and 2025, according to the Congressional Research Service. On April 3, 2026, White House officials proposed shutting the NEA down again, for 2027. As of September 2026, members of Congress had not decided.

On March 27, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an order titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." It said the Smithsonian Institution had "come under the influence of a divisive, race-centered ideology." On May 30, Trump announced he had fired Kim Sajet, who had directed the National Portrait Gallery for 12 years. He called her a "highly partisan person." Sajet resigned on June 13, 2025. On August 12, 2025, three White House officials, Lindsey Halligan, Vince Haley and Russell Vought, wrote to the head of the Smithsonian, Lonnie Bunch. They asked for a review of eight Smithsonian museums, among them the American Art Museum and the Portrait Gallery. They told the museums' leaders to begin changing their exhibits within 120 days. On July 24, 2026, Trump signed another order about the Smithsonian, aimed at the National Museum of American History.
