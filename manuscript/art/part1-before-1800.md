## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
All the art made in this land before 1500 was made by the people of Native nations. Much of it was made to be used. Potters in the Southwest painted bowls and jars, and carvers in the Ohio valley shaped stone pipes into birds and animals. In the towns of the Mississippi valley and the Southeast, metalworkers hammered copper into sheets and pressed pictures into it. On the Northwest Coast, people carved bone, horn and stone.

No surviving record names the people who made these things. Many of the objects that do survive were buried with the dead in graves and mounds. In the 1800s and 1900s, diggers took them out of the ground, and many of those diggers sold what they found.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painted pottery in the Southwest" -->
The Ancestral Pueblo people were the ancestors of today's Pueblo nations. Their potters made a kind of pottery now called black-on-white. The white is the color of the clay. The potter rolled clay into a long snake and coiled it around in a spiral to build up the pot. Then the potter scraped the coils together until the sides were smooth.

The black paint came from boiled plants, such as beeweed or tansy mustard. It also came from crushed rock with iron in it, such as hematite. Potters made their brushes from the fibers of the yucca plant. At Mesa Verde, people made this pottery from about the year 600 to about 1300.

In the Mimbres valley of southwestern New Mexico, potters painted bowls from about 1000 to 1130 or 1150. Most of these painted bowls have been found in graves. Archaeologists think people set a bowl over the dead person's face or head. Before they did, someone punched a hole through the bottom of the bowl on purpose. Archaeologists call this a kill hole.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures on the rock" -->
A petroglyph is a picture cut or pecked into rock. A pictograph is a picture painted on rock. On the volcanic cliffs west of Albuquerque, New Mexico, there are about 24,000 to 25,000 petroglyphs. They show animals, people, crosses, the brand marks that ranchers burn into cattle, and other images.

Ancestral Pueblo people chipped most of them into the rock with chisels and other stones between 1300 and the 1680s. The oldest are much older and date from about 2000 BC. For some Native nations the petroglyphs still have religious and ceremonial meaning today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pipes, copper and stone in the Ohio and Mississippi valleys" -->
People of the Hopewell culture lived in the Ohio valley from about 100 BC to about AD 400 or 500. Their carvers made smoking pipes of stone in the shapes of animals. The pipes show birds of prey, which are birds that hunt. They also show ducks and other water birds, songbirds, frogs, toads, meat-eating animals and river otters.

One Ohio mound, Tremper Mound in Scioto County, held a store of more than 500 objects. Among them were 136 pipes. Archaeologists thought most of the pipes were carved from Ohio pipestone. When scientists tested some of them, they found that 16 out of every 100 were made of a stone called catlinite. That stone came from pits in southwestern Minnesota, where people dug it out of the ground.

At Etowah, near Cartersville, Georgia, metalworkers hammered copper into thin sheets between about 1250 and 1325. They pushed the design up from the back of the sheet so it stood out on the front. The best-known plates show a dancing figure that archaeologists call the Birdman, or the falcon dancer.

Near Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, a carver made a figure between 1050 and 1200. The carver used a stone called flint clay, which came from near St. Louis, Missouri. It shows a kneeling woman with a pack on her back. She is using a hoe on the back of a snake that has the head of a cat. It was dug up in 1979 and is called the Birger figurine.

At Spiro, in eastern Oklahoma, people built twelve mounds between 850 and 1450, along with ceremonial grounds and a town. One of the mounds, Craig Mound, held objects of stone, copper, shell, basketry and cloth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carving on the Northwest Coast" -->
On the Northwest Coast, people carved bone, stone and horn at least 5,000 years ago. The oldest Coast Salish basketry comes from an old fishing camp on the Hoko River, on the Olympic Peninsula. It is about 2,500 years old.

Most carving on the coast was done in cedar wood. Wood rots, so very little of it survives from before 1500. One piece that may be that old is a hat woven from twisted strands of cedar bark. It dates from between 1450 and 1620, and it was found at the mouth of Wapato Creek. Carvers on the coast still work in this tradition today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who dug the objects up, and where they went" -->
In 1846 Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis dug into the Mound City mounds, near Chillicothe, Ohio. They took more than 200 stone pipes. Long before the two men came, the pipes had been broken and laid together under the floor of a building used for ceremonies. In 1864 Davis sold his collection for $10,000 to William Blackmore, who ran a museum in Salisbury, England. In 1931 the British Museum in London bought the collection. Fewer than two dozen of the carved animal pipes have been on public display there.

In 1885 John Rogan dug at Etowah for the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington. He took the two best-known Birdman plates from a grave lined with stone. The Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History still holds them.

In 1933 six men who called themselves the Pocola Mining Company paid $50 each. Together they paid $300 to George Evans for the right to dig into Craig Mound at Spiro for two years. Evans needed the money to pay off a loan on his land and to treat his grandson's tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs. From 1933 to 1935 the men dug into the burial mound to find things to sell. They destroyed about a third of the mound. They sold thousands of objects of stone, copper, shell, basketry and cloth to collectors around the world.

People who saw the dig described what the diggers did. Beads, pearls and arrowheads lay spilled across the ground, and feather capes and woven cloth were trampled. The diggers hauled out decorated shells by the wheelbarrow and dumped them near the entrance, where the shells were crushed underfoot. They burned ancient cedar poles as firewood and piled human bones at the edge of their camp, where the bones crumbled to dust.

Forrest Clements, an archaeologist at the University of Oklahoma, tried to stop the dig. In 1935 Oklahoma lawmakers passed a law that required a license to dig at such sites. Clements filed a complaint with the sheriff of LeFlore County. When he left for California, the diggers came back. The historian David La Vere calls Craig Mound by its other name, the Great Temple Mound. In La Vere's words, "In a fit of spite, just to jab their finger in Clements' eye, they packed the central chamber of the Great Temple Mound with kegs of black powder and touched off a mighty explosion." The blast caved in part of the mound and left a large crack in it. In November 1935 the Pocola Mining Company was shut down for good. From 1936 to 1941, University of Oklahoma archaeologists led workers paid by a government jobs program, the Works Progress Administration. The workers took the remains of more than 600 people out of Craig Mound, some whole skeletons and some only parts.

In the Mimbres valley, looters dug up graves to reach the painted bowls. In the 1960s they began using bulldozers and earth movers to get to the bowls faster, and they wiped out many village sites completely. Every Mimbres site has been looted. The records do not name the people who ran the bulldozers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nathan Jackson

> **Who:** A Tlingit carver of totem poles, masks, canoes, carved doors and clan crest panels, of the Sockeye Clan on the Raven side of the Chilkoot Tlingit.
> **When and where:** Born August 29, 1938, in southeastern Alaska. He lives in Ketchikan, Alaska.

Nathan Jackson carves in the Northwest Coast tradition that began thousands of years ago. He is Tlingit, of the Sockeye Clan on the Raven side of the Chilkoot Tlingit. A clan is a large group of related families. He carves totem poles, masks, canoes, carved doors and panels that show clan crests, the animal symbols that belong to a clan. He also works metal and dances.

He learned his people's traditions from Jack David, his clan uncle and grandfather. He served in the Army in Germany until 1959. Later he spent 54 days in a hospital with pneumonia. While he was there he whittled small totem poles. "I told the nurses, 'Those totem poles there, they have a price on them,'" he said later. Published accounts of his life disagree about when this happened. One puts it soon after his Army service. Another puts it in his mid-30s.

In the early 1960s he joined the Chilkat Dancers of Haines, Alaska. Then he studied for about three years at the Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe, New Mexico. After that the carver Duane Pasco taught him to work with an adze, a carving tool with a curved blade set across the end of its handle. Through a program of the Alaska State Council on the Arts, he taught apprentices, younger carvers who learn by working beside a master.

In 1995 he won a National Heritage Fellowship, a US government honor for master artists in old traditions. Officials of the National Endowment for the Arts choose who gets it. He has said that "the traditional way of life is something I identify with."

In 2024, at the age of 86, he carved an eight-foot pole that tells the Tlingit story of Raven and Fog Woman. He began it at the Edwin DeWill Carving Center in Saxman, Alaska. He finished it that autumn at the Arts Council of Princeton, in New Jersey, where it stands in the lobby.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s two European artists drew this land and its people. Each came with a colony that did not last. Jacques Le Moyne drew the French colony in Florida in 1564. John White painted the coast of what is now North Carolina in 1585. In Europe, Theodor de Bry cut copies of both men's pictures into metal plates so that he could print them in books, and he changed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jacques Le Moyne in Florida, 1564 and 1565" -->
In 1564 French colonists set up a colony called Fort Caroline, in a part of Florida that Spanish leaders claimed. They brought along an official artist, Jacques Le Moyne, to record it. Le Moyne made many drawings of the fort, the settlers and the land. He also drew the people who already lived there. They were Timucua people.

In 1565 Spanish soldiers attacked the fort. Le Moyne escaped, but he lost all of his drawings. Back in France, he drew them again from memory. He died in 1588, and his widow sold the pictures to de Bry, who printed them in 1591. De Bry changed the people and the landscapes to make his books sell better. He also made different versions of the pictures for different buyers.

One painting by Le Moyne, titled *Laudonnière and Athore, 1564*, shows the French commander René de Laudonnière with Athore, a Timucua leader. The New York Public Library holds it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-white-roanoke-art" name="John White" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John White

> **Who:** The English artist on the 1585 expedition to Roanoke Island, and later governor of the 1587 colony there.
> **When and where:** Roanoke Island and the coast of what is now North Carolina, 1585 and 1586, and again in 1587. His years of birth and death are not known. He was working from 1577 to 1593.

John White made the first pictures of this land by an English artist. Of all the pictures that Europeans made here on the spot, his are the oldest that survive. Le Moyne drew Florida twenty-one years before White, but Le Moyne's drawings from Florida were lost.

White was probably born in Cornwall, in England. In 1585 he sailed on Sir Richard Grenville's voyage to Roanoke Island as its artist. Walter Raleigh and other investors paid for the expedition. White worked beside the mathematician Thomas Harriot. The coast they recorded was the land of nations that spoke Algonquian languages, a family of related languages. Among them were the Roanoke, the Secotan and the Croatoan. In July 1585 White visited the town of Secotan and painted it.

In about thirteen months he made more than seventy watercolor pictures of Algonquian towns, people, dances, clothing and fishing, and of plants, birds and fish. He drew each outline in black lead, a kind of pencil. Then he filled it in with watercolor and added touches of gold and silver paint.

In 1590 de Bry printed engravings of White's pictures of people in a book with Harriot's report on the colony. An engraving is a picture cut into a metal plate, so that many copies can be printed from it. De Bry changed some of the pictures. In one of White's watercolors, a Secotan girl holds a small doll dressed in English clothes. De Bry's copy also puts a model of the globe and the sky in her hands.

The British Museum in London owns White's original watercolors and keeps them away from light. In the middle of the 1800s, water badly damaged them during a fire at the Sotheby's auction house. Over time, chemical changes have turned the silver paint black.

In 1587 White returned to Roanoke as the governor of a new colony. In August 1587 he led an attack on the town of Dasamonquepeuc, to pay back the killing of a colonist named George Howe. The people in the town turned out to be Croatoan, who were friends of the English, and White's men killed several of them. That same year the colonists talked White into sailing back to England, because they did not have enough supplies to last long. When he got back in August 1590, the colonists were gone. The word "Croatoan" was carved on a tree.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the English colonies of the 1600s, painters made portraits for rich families, carvers cut pictures into gravestones, and girls stitched needlework. The painter of the best-known portraits of the 1670s is not known by name. One gravestone carver whose name is known is Joseph Lamson. In 1640 a printer in Massachusetts produced the first full book printed in the English colonies. In 1650 a book of poems by a woman living in Massachusetts was printed in London.

In New Mexico, a Spanish friar had Acoma people build a church on top of the steep hill where their town stands, and they had to carry every load up to the top. A friar is a member of a Catholic religious order. Other friars, and Spanish soldiers, burned the masks that Pueblo people used in their ceremonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portraits, gravestones and needlework in New England" -->
English colonists built Boston on land of the Massachusett nation, on a peninsula the Massachusett called Mushauwomuk. In the 1670s a painter in Boston painted about ten portraits of people in the town. No record gives the painter's name. Today the painter is called the Freake Painter, after the best-known portrait. An article in *The Magazine Antiques* calls the painter "probably Samuel Clement," but nobody has proved it.

That portrait shows Elizabeth Freake holding her baby daughter, Mary. The Freakes were one of the richest families in Boston. The painter first painted Elizabeth alone, in about 1671. Mary was born on May 6, 1674. X-rays of the painting show that the painter added the baby later. The Worcester Art Museum, in Massachusetts, owns the painting.

Many settlers of New England were Puritans, English Protestants who wanted a plainer and stricter church. Puritans did not want religious symbols such as crosses and cherubs, carvings of baby angels, on their graves. Carvers cut other pictures into Puritan gravestones: skulls with wings, crossed bones and hourglasses. Many stones carry the Latin words "Memento Mori," which mean "remember you will die." Joseph Lamson of Charlestown, Massachusetts, who lived from 1658 to 1722, is one carver whose name is known. He carved winged skulls with eyebrows that hook at the ends, inside borders of fruit, vines and flowers. His sons Nathaniel and Caleb and his grandson Joseph carried on the work.

Girls learned needlework from their mothers. Many stitched samplers, strips of cloth that kept a record of stitches and patterns to copy later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="loara-standish-sampler" name="Loara Standish" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Loara Standish

> **Who:** A daughter of Myles and Barbara Standish of Plymouth Colony, thought to be the maker of the oldest known sampler made in America.
> **When and where:** Duxbury, in Plymouth Colony, in the 1640s or about 1653. She was born after 1627 and died before 1655.

Loara Standish is thought to have stitched the oldest known sampler made in America. It is also the oldest known sampler with a verse. She made it in Duxbury, in Plymouth Colony. English settlers built Plymouth on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town.

Loara was one of the seven children of Myles and Barbara Standish. She was born after 1627 and died before 1655. According to the Pilgrim Hall Museum, she probably made the sampler in her teens. She stitched it in silk thread on linen cloth, with roses, carnations and oak leaves, and a letter S twisted around itself.

She stitched this verse into it: "Loara Standish is my name Lorde guide my hart that I may doe thy will also My hands with such Convenient skill as may Conduce to virtue void of Shame and I will give The glory to thy name." In plain words, she asks God to guide her heart and to give her hands enough skill to do good, and she promises to give God the glory.

In 1844 Lucius Alden gave it to the Pilgrim Hall Museum in Plymouth. It has faded from being hung up for so many years. Experts at the museum date it to about 1653. Experts at the Textile Research Centre in Leiden, in the Netherlands, date it to the 1640s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="loara-standish-sampler" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first books printed and written here" -->
In 1638 the Reverend Jose Glover sailed for Massachusetts with a printing press. He died on the ship. His widow, Elizabeth Glover, set up the press in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She put it in the charge of Stephen Daye, a locksmith.

In 1640 Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes*, now called the *Bay Psalm Book*. The psalms are 150 poems in the Hebrew Bible. A group of thirty elders, men respected in the colony for their learning, had put them into English verse that could be sung. Among them were John Cotton, Richard Mather and John Eliot. Daye is thought to have printed about 1,700 copies. They sold for twenty pence each. A penny was a small English coin, and pence means pennies. Only eleven copies survive today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-bradstreet" name="Anne Bradstreet" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Bradstreet

> **Who:** A poet in the Massachusetts Bay colony, whose book was the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** Born in England in 1612. Came to Massachusetts in 1630. Died in 1672.

Anne Bradstreet wrote the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies. It was printed in London in 1650 as *The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America*. In old Greek stories, nine goddesses called the Muses watched over poetry and the other arts, so the title describes her as a tenth one. The title page identifies the writer only as "a Gentlewoman of those parts." Her full name appears inside the book.

She was born in England in 1612 to Thomas and Dorothy Dudley. In about 1628 she married Simon Bradstreet. In 1630 she and her husband sailed to Massachusetts with her parents. The Bradstreets had eight children.

Her brother-in-law, the Reverend John Woodbridge, took her poems to London. In the book's opening pages, Woodbridge wrote that she had not known he was doing it. Later, in a poem called "The Author to Her Book," Bradstreet called Woodbridge a "friend more loving than true" for taking them.

The biographer Charlotte Gordon studied the poems and the letters between Bradstreet and Woodbridge. She found that Bradstreet knew about the plan and helped with it from the start. One poem in the book is about events of 1649. Woodbridge had sailed in 1648, so someone had to send that poem after him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-bradstreet" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mission churches and burned masks in New Mexico" -->
Acoma Pueblo is a town built on top of a mesa, a hill with steep sides and a flat top. In December 1598 Acoma people killed the Spanish captain Juan de Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men. Accounts differ on the number. In the Acoma people's own account, Zaldívar's soldiers had raped an Acoma woman. Rape means forcing a person into sex. In January 1599 Spanish soldiers sent by Juan de Oñate attacked Acoma, took the town and burned much of it. They killed hundreds of Acoma men, women and children. Estimates of the dead run from about 300 to about 1,500. Oñate sentenced the men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off and to work for the Spanish without pay for twenty years. Whether the feet were really cut off is disputed.

In 1629 a friar named Juan Ramírez arrived at Acoma. He probably began soon after to have Acoma people build a church on the mesa, called San Esteban del Rey. The exact years of the building are not known. The Acoma workers had to carry every load up the mesa. The loads included roof beams 40 feet long, cut in mountains 30 to 40 miles away.

One account says the builders were enslaved. Another says only that they had to do the work. The surviving records do not say whether they were paid. San Esteban del Rey is the oldest European church in New Mexico that still stands.

Pueblo people made masks for their ceremonies. Katsina masks show spirit beings. They were kept in kivas, rooms used for religious ceremonies. From 1656 to 1665, Alonso de Posada, the head of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico, banned Pueblo ceremonies. He ordered friars to go into the kivas and burn the masks. They also burned prayer sticks, which are sticks made for prayers, and carved figures, or took them away. In 1661 Spanish soldiers raided kivas and burned masks as well. No surviving record counts the masks that friars and soldiers burned.

In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested. Three of them were hanged, and one killed himself. The rest were whipped in public and jailed, until Pueblo men came to Santa Fe and forced their release. The records name Treviño as the man who ordered the arrests. They do not name the men who hanged and whipped the prisoners. One of the freed men was Po'pay, of Ohkay Owingeh. On August 10, 1680, Po'pay led the Pueblo Revolt. Pueblo people killed 21 friars and burned the mission churches across the province.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A few painters trained in Europe settled in the colonies between 1700 and 1750. They earned their living painting portraits of merchants, planters and officials, because only families with money could pay for a portrait. Two portraits from these years show slavery. One shows an enslaved boy beside the son of a rich Maryland family. The other shows the Royall family, whose money came from slavery. One painter made portraits of two Lenape leaders two years before William Penn's sons took a large part of Lenape land. A colonial governor also took Native art across the ocean. In 1725 he took Cherokee baskets to London, where they are still kept.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portrait painters in the colonies" -->
Henrietta Johnston drew portraits in Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708. John Smibert came from Scotland and painted in Boston from 1729 or 1730.

Justus Engelhardt Kühn came from Germany. In 1708 he applied at Annapolis, Maryland, to become an English subject, with the rights of a person born English. He painted the children of rich Maryland families until he died in 1717. He was the earliest artist known to work for pay in the Middle Atlantic colonies, the group of colonies that included Maryland.

Gustavus Hesselius was born in Sweden in about 1682 and studied art there. He painted portraits in Philadelphia. He also built church organs, among them one for the church of the Moravians, a Protestant group, in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, in 1746. He died in Philadelphia on May 23, 1755. Robert Feke painted portraits in Newport, Rhode Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henrietta-johnston" name="Henrietta Johnston" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henrietta Johnston

> **Who:** A portrait artist who worked in pastels. She was the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** Born in northern France in about 1674. Worked in Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708 until she died there on March 9, 1729.

Henrietta Johnston was the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies. She was also the first artist in the colonies to use pastels. A pastel is a crayon made from a paste of colored powder and resin, a sticky stuff that comes from trees.

She was born in northern France in about 1674. Her family were Protestants. In 1687 they fled to London. Two years earlier, King Louis XIV of France had taken away Protestants' right to practice their religion. He sent soldiers to live in Protestant homes, and they were allowed to steal from the families and bully them until they became Catholic or left France. In 1694 she married and moved to Ireland, where she learned to draw portraits in pastel. Her first husband died in 1704 and left her with two small children.

Her second husband, Gideon Johnston, was a minister of the Church of England. In 1708 he moved the family to Charleston, where he served as the representative of the bishop of London. The Stono, Kiawah and Etiwan peoples had lived along the rivers at Charleston when English colonists settled there in 1670. The Johnstons were short of money in Charleston, so Henrietta drew portraits of the town's richest families for pay.

Pastels could be bought only in England. In 1711 she sailed to London for supplies. On the voyage home, pirates attacked her ship, and she survived. After Gideon died in 1716, she earned the family's living alone. She died in Charleston on March 9, 1729. About three dozen of her portraits survive.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henrietta-johnston" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-smibert" name="John Smibert" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Smibert

> **Who:** A Scottish portrait painter in Boston, whose shop and studio showed younger painters copies of European art. He also drew the plans for Faneuil Hall.
> **When and where:** Born in Edinburgh, Scotland, in 1688. Newport, Rhode Island, in 1729, then Boston until he died there in 1751.

John Smibert is described as the first painter with formal art training to work in the thirteen colonies. His father was a wool dyer. In 1728 a church leader named George Berkeley asked Smibert to come to America. Berkeley planned to start a college in Bermuda, where Smibert would teach art. The college was never started.

Smibert reached Newport, Rhode Island, with Berkeley's group in 1729. He painted Berkeley and his party in a picture now at Yale University. By 1729 or 1730 he had moved to Boston. There he painted merchants, such as Peter Faneuil, and Judge Samuel Sewall. He married Mary Williams in 1730.

Portraits alone did not earn Smibert enough, so he also kept a shop. In 1734 he began selling paint, other art supplies and prints. Upstairs, in his studio, he hung copies of famous European paintings that he had made in Europe. He also showed casts there, copies made by pouring material into molds. The historian Richard Saunders calls it "America's first art gallery." Younger painters later studied the collection, among them John Singleton Copley, Charles Willson Peale, Gilbert Stuart, John Trumbull and Washington Allston. Some of them were born after Smibert died.

In 1742 Smibert drew the plans for Faneuil Hall in Boston.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-smibert" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two portraits that show slavery" -->
In about 1710, Kühn painted Henry Darnall III, the son of a rich Maryland family, when the boy was about eight. He wears fine clothes and holds a bow. Beside him stands a Black boy with a metal collar fastened tight around his neck. The Black boy was enslaved. No record gives his name, and no record says who put the collar on him. The Maryland Center for History and Culture owns the painting and calls him "Unnamed attendant." One art history website calls the picture the earliest known formal painting of an enslaved African in America.

In 1741 Robert Feke painted Isaac Royall Jr., who was 22, with his wife Elizabeth, their baby daughter Elizabeth, his sister Penelope and his wife's sister-in-law Mary Palmer. Isaac's father, Isaac Royall Sr., had set up a sugar plantation on the island of Antigua. The father got rich by trading in sugar, in rum and in enslaved people. In 1737 the family moved to Medford, Massachusetts, and brought at least 27 enslaved Africans with them. When his father died in 1739, the son inherited his father's huge fortune, at the age of 20. Harvard Law School owns the painting.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two Lenape leaders painted, 1735" -->
English colonists led by William Penn built Philadelphia on Lenape land. In 1735 Gustavus Hesselius painted two Lenape leaders, Lapowinsa and Tishcohan, for John Penn, a son of William Penn. Lapowinsa is shown as a strong man of about 40. A writer on Lenape history called the two men "the only two chiefs of the Lenni Lenape whose portraits have been preserved." The Historical Society of Pennsylvania owns them.

In 1737 Lapowinsa and Tishcohan signed a land deal called the Walking Purchase with John Penn and his brother Thomas Penn. The Lenape leaders Nutimus and Menakihikon also signed it. James Logan, who worked for the Penns, showed the Lenape an unsigned copy of a deed from 1686, a paper selling land. He claimed it gave the Penns as much land as a man could walk in a day and a half. The Penns' men cleared a path ahead of time. On September 19, 1737, three walkers set off at a fast pace, and in a day and a half one of them covered more than 60 miles. The Penns' surveyors, the men who measured and marked out the land, then bent the boundary line to take in even more land. In all, the Penns took more than a thousand square miles of Lenape land, and they forced Nutimus and his people to leave their homes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cherokee baskets taken to London, 1725" -->
Cherokee weavers made baskets from river cane, a tall plant with hollow stems that grows along rivers. They split the cane into thin strips and dyed some of the strips with walnut and pokeberry. Some baskets were double woven. A double-woven basket is two baskets woven one inside the other from the same strips, so both the inside and the outside show a finished pattern.

In 1725 Francis Nicholson, the first governor of South Carolina chosen by the British king, left Charleston for London. He took a set of Cherokee double-woven baskets with him and passed them to Sir Hans Sloane, a collector in London. A note tied to them says, "They will keep anything in them from being wetted by rain." The British Museum in London holds them today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1750 to 1800, American painters made the portraits of the men who led the Revolution. Several of these painters trained in London. In 1773 Phillis Wheatley's *Poems* was printed in London. It was the first book of poems by an African American, and she was still enslaved in Boston when it came out. Enslaved Black artists worked in these same years, and a few records name them. One of them, Prince Demah, painted portraits in Boston. Later he served during the Revolution in the Massachusetts militia, a part-time army of local men. The painter Charles Willson Peale was paid for portraits with two enslaved people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters who went to London" -->
John Singleton Copley was born in Boston on July 3, 1738. In 1766 he sent a painting called *A Boy with a Flying Squirrel* to be shown in London. The London painters Joshua Reynolds and Benjamin West praised it. In 1769 Copley painted Isaac Royall Jr., the rich man from Medford, and his wife. In 1774 Copley left America for England. He painted *Watson and the Shark* there in 1778, and he died in London on September 9, 1815.

Benjamin West was born in Pennsylvania. He settled in England in 1763 and became the official painter of history scenes for King George III. West was known for helping young American painters who came to London to learn.

Patience Wright was born Patience Lovell in 1725. Britannica gives her birthplace as Bordentown, New Jersey, and other accounts give Oyster Bay, on Long Island in New York. She made portraits by shaping wax, and she took her wax figures around on tour to show them. In February 1772 she sailed to England. In London she showed wax figures of actors, politicians and nobles. She died in London on March 23, 1786.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-willson-peale" name="Charles Willson Peale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Willson Peale

> **Who:** A painter of the leaders of the Revolution, who opened a museum in Philadelphia. He was paid for portraits with two enslaved people.
> **When and where:** Born in Chester, Maryland, in April 1741. Worked in Annapolis, then Philadelphia. Died in 1827.

George Washington sat for Charles Willson Peale seven times during his life, so that Peale could paint him. He first trained to make saddles. Then he became interested in art and studied painting in England for three years. Back in Annapolis, Maryland, he joined the Sons of Liberty. The Sons of Liberty were groups of colonists who formed in 1765 to resist a British tax on printed paper, called the Stamp Act.

On May 18, 1772, Peale came to Washington's home at Mount Vernon and stayed two weeks. He painted small portraits of Martha Washington and her children. He also painted George Washington in oil, in the uniform of the Virginia militia.

In 1784 Peale announced a show of 44 portraits of leaders of the Revolution in his home in Philadelphia. Visitors paid to get in. Peale called it the Philadelphia Museum. One account dates the opening of his museum to 1786 instead. Washington paid a yearly fee to support it and gave it two golden pheasants from China. In 1795 Peale's museum was the site of the Columbianum, the first show in America put on by a society of artists.

After Peale died, his family sold the museum's collection of things from nature in 1849. They sold its portraits at auction in 1854.

By 1776 a Maryland plantation owner had given Peale two enslaved people as payment for portraits. Their names were Scarborough and Lucy. The records do not name the planter. Peale freed Scarborough and Lucy in 1786. Their son, Moses Williams, was born in about 1777. In 1780 Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a law to end slavery slowly, over many years. Under that law Moses Williams was not free. Peale kept him enslaved in his household until about 1802, when Moses was about 25 or 27. Peale trained him for work in the museum: cutting silhouettes, which are outline portraits cut from paper, preserving dead animals for display and building exhibits.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-willson-peale" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="prince-demah" name="Prince Demah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Prince Demah

> **Who:** An enslaved portrait painter in Boston, who later lived free and served as a soldier in the Massachusetts militia.
> **When and where:** Baptized in Boston in May 1745. Worked in Boston and London from 1769 to 1778. Died in Boston in March 1778.

Prince Demah painted portraits in Boston while he was enslaved. One of his signed paintings belongs today to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. He and his mother, Daphney, were baptized at Trinity Church in Boston in May 1745.

Henry Barnes was a merchant in Marlborough, Massachusetts. His family already held Daphney in slavery. In 1769 Barnes bought her son, Prince, who was then in his late twenties. Barnes's wife, Christian, wrote to a friend that they planned on "improving his genius in painting." Genius here meant talent. She also wrote that the Boston painter John Singleton Copley would not be willing to teach him.

In October 1770 Barnes took Prince to London. A painter named Pine gave him lessons there. He was probably Robert Edge Pine. Barnes was afraid that Prince would "attempt his freedom," which meant try to escape. He wrote, "I do not let [Prince] converse with any of his own colour here." He meant that he kept Prince from talking with other Black people in London.

In 1773, back in Boston, an advertisement ran at least nine times in the *Boston News-Letter*. It offered the work of a "Negro Artist" who "takes Faces at the lowest Rates." Negro was a word people of that time used for Black people. Taking faces meant painting portraits. That February, Prince painted William Duguid, a Scottish cloth merchant living in Boston. He signed it "Prince Demah Barnes." He also painted, or copied from Copley, portraits of Henry and Christian Barnes.

The Barnes family sided with the king. In late 1775 they left for England, and they left Daphney behind at their Marlborough home. In April 1777 Prince joined an artillery regiment of the Massachusetts militia under the name Prince Demah. The records do not say how he became free. On March 11, 1778, he wrote his will as "Prince Demah, limner," a painter of portraits, and as a "free Negro." He left everything he had to his mother. He died one week later. The record does not give the cause.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="prince-demah" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="phillis-wheatley" name="Phillis Wheatley" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Phillis Wheatley

> **Who:** A poet, the first African American to publish a book of poems. She was enslaved in Boston when the book was printed.
> **When and where:** Brought from Africa to Boston in 1761, at seven or eight years old. Her book was printed in London in 1773. She died in Boston in 1784, at about 31.

Phillis Wheatley wrote the first book of poems by an African American. It came out in London in September 1773, while she was enslaved in Boston. The title page describes her as "Negro Servant to Mr. John Wheatley, of Boston." Servant there stood for enslaved.

She was taken from Africa to Boston in 1761, when she was seven or eight. The records do not name the people who took her. In Boston, John Wheatley bought her. He wrote that she learned English in sixteen months, taught at home and not in any school. In 1770, when she was about seventeen, a Boston printer named Ezekiel Russell printed her poem on the death of George Whitefield, a preacher who had toured the colonies.

The publisher of her book expected readers to doubt that an enslaved African girl could write poems. So he printed a statement at the front of the book, signed by 18 men of Boston. Among them were Governor Thomas Hutchinson, John Hancock, the Reverend John Moorhead and John Wheatley, listed as "her Master." The men wrote that the poems were "written by PHILLIS, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave." Uncultivated meant untaught. Barbarian was an insult for a person thought to be wild and without learning. They wrote that she "has been examined by some of the best Judges, and is thought qualified to write them."

One poem in the book is called "To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works." The Wheatleys freed her by November 1773, soon after the book appeared. In 1778 she married John Peters. She died poor in 1784, at about 31.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="phillis-wheatley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two more Black painters, Scipio Moorhead and Joshua Johnson" -->
Scipio Moorhead was an enslaved Black painter in Boston. The scholars Kwame Anthony Appiah and Henry Louis Gates Jr. write that Wheatley's poem to "S. M." may describe two of his paintings. He was held in slavery by the Reverend John Moorhead of Boston, one of the 18 men who signed the statement in Wheatley's book. Sarah Moorhead probably gave Scipio lessons. Appiah and Gates also write that the engraved portrait of Wheatley at the front of her book was based on his painting of her. No signed painting by Scipio Moorhead survives.

Joshua Johnson was born enslaved in Maryland in 1763. His father was a white man named George Johnson. His mother was a woman held in slavery by William Wheeler Sr. Her name was not written down. In 1782 George Johnson bought his 19-year-old son for £25. He sent him to learn the trade of a blacksmith and signed a manumission, a legal paper that freed him. By 1796 Joshua Johnson was listed in Baltimore as a portrait painter. In a 1798 newspaper advertisement, he called himself a "self-taught genius." He wrote that he had faced "many insuperable obstacles," which means problems he could not get past. Amelia Peck, a curator at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and Paula M. Bagger write that before Prince Demah's work was found, Johnson was thought to be the earliest African American painter of portraits in oil paint.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercy Otis Warren's plays" -->
Mercy Otis Warren was born in Barnstable, Massachusetts, on September 14, 1728. She wrote plays that made fun of the British governor of Massachusetts and attacked his policies. The first, *The Adulateur*, came out in a newspaper, the *Massachusetts Spy*, in 1772. Others followed, among them *Defeat* in 1773, *The Group* in 1775, *The Blockheads* in 1776 and *The Motley Assembly* in 1779. All of them came out without her name. The National Women's History Museum says she likely did this to avoid punishment, and so that readers would take the work of a woman seriously. She died in 1814.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gilbert-stuart" name="Gilbert Stuart" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gilbert Stuart

> **Who:** A portrait painter whose unfinished picture of George Washington is the model for the face on the one-dollar bill.
> **When and where:** Born in Kingstown, Rhode Island, on December 3, 1755. Worked in London and Dublin, then in the United States from 1793. Died in 1828.

The picture of George Washington on the one-dollar bill is an engraving based on a portrait by Gilbert Stuart. Stuart was born in Kingstown, Rhode Island, on December 3, 1755. In 1775 he sailed to London. There Benjamin West hired him and gave him a studio and a place to live.

In 1787 Stuart left London for Dublin to get away from the people he owed money. He arrived in New York in May 1793.

In 1796 Martha Washington ordered portraits of her husband and herself from Stuart. He painted only Washington's head and neck on a brown background, left the rest of the canvas unfinished and kept it. He never gave it to her, although she asked for it again and again after her husband died in 1799. Stuart used the unfinished head as a model and painted more than a hundred copies of it, 130 by one count. He sold them for $100 each. An engraving of the head, flipped left to right, is on the one-dollar bill.

In 1831 the trustees of the Boston Athenaeum, a library in Boston, bought the original for $1,500. The painting has been called the Athenaeum portrait ever since. Since 1980 the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston and the National Portrait Gallery in Washington have owned it together.
