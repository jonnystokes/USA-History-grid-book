## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 32 of 37 · Art · 10 eras · about 150 minutes**

**Who was allowed to make art in America, who got paid for it, and who ended up owning it has always depended on who held power.**

An enslaved potter in South Carolina named Dave cut two lines into a big clay jar in 1857. They use lucre, an old word for money: "I made this Jar = for cash / Though its called Lucre trash." Dave was not paid for that jar. It was sold for the profit of the man who held him, Lewis J. Miles. On October 29, 2025, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston gave Dave's descendants ownership of it and of a second jar he made that year.

This chapter follows the pictures, carvings, pots, photographs and books made in this land, from Ancestral Pueblo bowls to pictures made by computer programs. Keep an eye on the names. The carvers of the Ohio pipes and the Boston painter of the 1670s left none, but the men who dug up, bought and sold such work often did. Then watch the money. Enslaved painters and potters earned it for other men, the mother in a famous 1936 photograph never got a penny, and artists today are suing over computer programs built from their pictures. From the 1800s on, the fights are also about who gets into the art schools, the museums and the shows.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did enslaved artists make art, and who got paid for it?
- Who decided what counted as art, and who got to see it?
- Why are museums giving back things they once took?

**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 11 minutes**

Every pot, carving and copper plate made in this land before 1500 was made by Native people, and no surviving record names the people who made them. Potters in the Southwest painted bowls and jars. Carvers in the Ohio valley shaped stone pipes into birds and animals. In the towns of the Mississippi valley and the Southeast, metalworkers hammered copper into sheets and pressed pictures into it, and on the Northwest Coast people carved bone, horn and stone.

Many of the objects that survive were buried with the dead in graves and mounds. In the 1800s and 1900s, diggers took them out of the ground, and many of those diggers sold what they found.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did Ancestral Pueblo potters paint a pot?" -->
Ancestral Pueblo potters painted black designs over the white of the clay, and the pottery is now called black-on-white. The Ancestral Pueblo people were the ancestors of today's Pueblo nations. A potter rolled clay into a long snake and coiled it around in a spiral to build up the pot. Then the potter scraped the coils together until the sides were smooth.

The black paint came from boiled plants, such as beeweed or tansy mustard, or from crushed rock with iron in it, such as hematite. Potters made their brushes from the fibers of the yucca plant. At Mesa Verde, people made this pottery from about the year 600 to about 1300.

**Bowls with a hole.** In the Mimbres valley of southwestern New Mexico, potters painted bowls from about 1000 to 1130 or 1150. Most of these bowls have been found in graves. Archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study the things people left behind, think people set a bowl over the dead person's face or head. Before they did, someone punched a hole through the bottom of the bowl on purpose. Archaeologists call it a kill hole.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures on the rock" -->
Native artists cut or pecked some pictures into rock, called petroglyphs, and painted others, called pictographs. On the volcanic cliffs west of Albuquerque, New Mexico, there are about 24,000 to 25,000 petroglyphs. They show animals, people, crosses, the brand marks that ranchers burn into cattle, and other images.

Ancestral Pueblo people chipped most of them into the rock with chisels and other stones between 1300 and the 1680s. The oldest are much older and date from about 2000 BC. For some Native nations the petroglyphs still have religious meaning today, and a place in their ceremonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pipes, copper and stone in the Ohio and Mississippi valleys" -->
Carvers of the Hopewell culture made smoking pipes of stone in the shapes of animals. The Hopewell people lived in the Ohio valley from about 100 BC to about AD 400 or 500. Their pipes show birds of prey, the birds that hunt, and ducks and other water birds. They also show songbirds, frogs, toads, meat-eating animals and river otters.

**Stone from far away.** One Ohio mound, Tremper Mound in Scioto County, held a store of more than 500 objects, among them 136 pipes. Archaeologists thought most of the pipes were carved from Ohio pipestone. When scientists tested some of them, 16 out of every 100 turned out to be catlinite. That stone came from quarries, pits where people dug stone, in southwestern Minnesota.

**Copper plates.** Metalworkers at Etowah, near Cartersville, Georgia, hammered copper into thin sheets between about 1250 and 1325. They pushed the design up from the back of the sheet so it stood out on the front. The best-known plates show a dancing figure that archaeologists call the Birdman, or the falcon dancer.

**The Birger figurine.** Near Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, a carver made a figure of flint clay, a stone from near St. Louis, Missouri, between 1050 and 1200. It shows a kneeling woman with a pack on her back. She is using a hoe on the back of a snake that has the head of a cat. It was dug up in 1979.

At Spiro, in eastern Oklahoma, people built twelve mounds between 850 and 1450, along with grounds for ceremonies and a town. One of the mounds, Craig Mound, held objects of stone, copper, shell, basketry and cloth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Carving on the Northwest Coast" -->
On the Northwest Coast, people carved bone, stone and horn at least 5,000 years ago. The oldest Coast Salish basketry comes from an old fishing camp on the Hoko River, on the Olympic Peninsula. It is about 2,500 years old.

Most carving on the coast was done in cedar wood. Wood rots, so very little of it survives from before 1500. One piece that may be that old is a hat woven from twisted strands of cedar bark, found at the mouth of Wapato Creek. It dates from between 1450 and 1620. Carvers on the coast still work in this tradition today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who dug the objects up, and where did they go?" -->
Two men named Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis dug into the Mound City mounds, near Chillicothe, Ohio, in 1846 and took more than 200 stone pipes. Long before they came, the pipes had been broken and laid together under the floor of a building used for ceremonies.

**Mound City to London.** Davis sold his collection in 1864 for $10,000 to William Blackmore, who ran a museum in Salisbury, England. The collection was sold again in 1931, to the British Museum in London. Fewer than two dozen of the carved animal pipes have been on public display there.

**Etowah.** John Rogan dug at Etowah for the Smithsonian, the national museum in Washington, in 1885. He took the two best-known Birdman plates from a grave lined with stone. The Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History still holds them.

**Spiro.** Craig Mound at Spiro was a burial mound. In 1933 six men who called themselves the Pocola Mining Company paid $50 each, $300 in all, to George Evans for the right to dig into it for two years. Evans was the grandfather of the children who owned the land. He needed the money to pay off a loan on the land and to treat his grandson's tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs. A local man, R. W. Wall, handled the company's business.

A company that sells copies of Spiro objects shows photographs of the dig from 1935 on its website, with notes drawn from a 1952 report on the mound. Those photographs and notes name some of the men who dug: the brothers Bill and Hayden Vandagriff, W. Guinn Cooper, John Hobbs and K. A. McKenzie. The company also hired coal miners, who dug tunnels into the mound.

**What the diggers did.** From 1933 to 1935 the diggers took things out of the graves to sell, and they destroyed about a third of the mound. They sold thousands of objects of stone, copper, shell, basketry and cloth to collectors around the world. One pile of objects, found in April 1935, went to a secondhand furniture dealer from Indianapolis, Indiana, for $100.

People who saw the dig described it. Beads, pearls and arrowheads lay spilled across the ground. The diggers trampled feather capes and woven cloth. They hauled out decorated shells by the wheelbarrow and dumped them near the entrance, where the shells were crushed under the diggers' feet. They burned ancient cedar poles as firewood. They piled human bones at the edge of their camp, where the bones crumbled to dust.

**The blast.** Forrest Clements, an archaeologist at the University of Oklahoma, tried to stop the dig. Oklahoma lawmakers passed a law in 1935 that required a license to dig at such sites, and Clements filed a complaint with the sheriff of LeFlore County. When he left for California, the diggers came back.

The historian David La Vere calls Craig Mound by its other name, the Great Temple Mound. In his words, "In a fit of spite, just to jab their finger in Clements' eye," the diggers "packed the central chamber of the Great Temple Mound with kegs of black powder and touched off a mighty explosion." The blast caved in part of the mound and left a large crack in it.

The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History says the Pocola Mining Company was shut down for good in November 1935. It does not say who shut it down.

Then University of Oklahoma archaeologists led workers paid by a government jobs program, the Works Progress Administration, from 1936 to 1941. The workers took the remains of more than 600 people out of Craig Mound, some whole skeletons and some only parts.

**Mimbres.** In the Mimbres valley, looters dug up graves to reach the painted bowls. In the 1960s they began using bulldozers and earth movers to get to the bowls faster, and they wiped out many village sites completely. Every Mimbres site has been looted. The records do not name the people who ran the bulldozers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nathan Jackson

> **Who:** A Tlingit carver of totem poles and masks, still carving in the Northwest Coast tradition in his eighties.
> **When and where:** Born August 29, 1938, in southeastern Alaska. He lives in Ketchikan, Alaska.

**At 86, he carved an eight-foot pole about Raven and Fog Woman.**

Nathan Jackson is Tlingit, of the Sockeye Clan, a large group of related families, on the Raven side of the Chilkoot Tlingit. He carves totem poles, masks, canoes, carved doors and panels that show clan crests, the animal symbols that belong to a clan. He also works metal and dances. He learned his people's traditions from Jack David, his clan uncle and grandfather.

**Totem poles in a hospital.** Jackson served in the Army in Germany until 1959. Later he spent 54 days in a hospital with pneumonia, a serious sickness of the lungs, and while he was there he whittled small totem poles. "I told the nurses, 'Those totem poles there, they have a price on them,'" he said later.

**Not known:** whether that hospital stay came soon after his Army service or in his mid-30s, because published accounts of his life disagree.

**Learning the adze.** He joined the Chilkat Dancers of Haines, Alaska, in the early 1960s. Then he studied for about three years at the Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe, New Mexico. After that the carver Duane Pasco taught him to work with an adze, a carving tool with a curved blade set across the end of its handle. Through a program of the Alaska State Council on the Arts, he taught apprentices, younger carvers who learn by working beside a master.

Officials of the National Endowment for the Arts, a US government agency, gave him an honor called a National Heritage Fellowship in 1995. He has said that "the traditional way of life is something I identify with."

He began the Raven and Fog Woman pole in 2024 at the Edwin DeWill Carving Center in Saxman, Alaska. He finished it that autumn at the Arts Council of Princeton, in New Jersey, where it stands in the lobby.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 4 minutes**

The oldest European pictures of this land and its people were made by two artists who came with colonies that did not last. Jacques Le Moyne drew the French colony in Florida in 1564. John White painted the coast of what is now North Carolina in 1585. In Europe, Theodor de Bry cut copies of both men's pictures into metal plates so that he could print them in books, and he changed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How Le Moyne lost his Florida drawings" -->
Jacques Le Moyne lost every drawing he made in Florida when Spanish soldiers took the French fort there in 1565, so the pictures that survive are ones he drew again from memory. French colonists had set up the colony, called Fort Caroline, in 1564, in a part of Florida that Spanish leaders claimed. They brought Le Moyne along as their official artist, to record it. He drew the fort, the settlers and the land, and the people who already lived there, the Timucua.

**The attack.** Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and 500 Spanish soldiers took the fort in an attack at dawn on September 20, 1565. They killed most of the French settlers they found. Menéndez spared about 50 women and children and shipped them to Havana. Le Moyne escaped, but his drawings did not.

**Drawn again, and changed.** Back in France, Le Moyne drew the pictures again from memory. He died in 1588, and his widow sold them to de Bry, who printed them in 1591. De Bry changed the people and the landscapes to make his books sell better. He also made different versions of the pictures for different buyers.

One painting by Le Moyne, *Laudonnière and Athore, 1564*, shows the French commander René de Laudonnière with Athore, a Timucua leader. The year in the title is the year of the meeting the picture shows. Le Moyne made it after he got back to Europe. The New York Public Library holds it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-white-roanoke-art" name="John White" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John White

> **Who:** The English artist whose Roanoke watercolors are the oldest surviving pictures Europeans made on the spot in this land. Later he was governor of the 1587 colony there.
> **When and where:** Roanoke Island and the coast of what is now North Carolina, 1585 and 1586, and again in 1587. Probably born in Cornwall, England. His years of birth and death are not known. He was working from 1577 to 1593.

**More than seventy watercolors survive from his thirteen months at Roanoke.**

John White made the first pictures of this land by an English artist. Le Moyne drew Florida twenty-one years before him, but Le Moyne's Florida drawings were lost, so of all the pictures Europeans made here on the spot, White's are the oldest that survive.

**The voyage.** White sailed as the artist on Sir Richard Grenville's voyage to Roanoke Island in 1585. Walter Raleigh and other men who put money into the voyage paid for it. White worked beside the mathematician Thomas Harriot. The coast they recorded was the land of nations that spoke Algonquian languages, a family of related languages. Among them were the Roanoke, the Secotan and the Croatoan. In July 1585 White visited the town of Secotan and painted it.

**What he painted.** In about thirteen months he painted Algonquian towns, people, dances, clothing and fishing, and plants, birds and fish. He drew each outline in black lead, a kind of pencil. Then he filled it in with watercolor and added touches of gold and silver paint.

De Bry printed engravings, pictures cut into metal plates so that many copies could be printed, of White's pictures of people in 1590, in a book with Harriot's report on the colony. He changed some of them. In one of White's watercolors, a Secotan girl holds a small doll dressed in English clothes. De Bry's copy also puts a model of the globe and the sky in her hands.

The British Museum in London owns White's original watercolors, and its staff keep them stored away from light. In the middle of the 1800s, water badly damaged them during a fire at the Sotheby's auction house. Over time, chemical changes have turned the silver paint black.

**The governor.** White returned to Roanoke in 1587 as the governor of a new colony. After the colonists landed, one of them, George Howe, was found dead. He had been shot with sixteen arrows and beaten. The Encyclopedia Virginia account of the colony does not name the men who killed him.

In August 1587 White led an attack on the town of Dasamonquepeuc to pay back Howe's killing. He believed its people were Roanoke, enemies of the English. They turned out to be Croatoan, who were friends of the English, and White's men killed a number of them. One account says the dead included a leader named Menatonon, and other accounts name the leader differently.

That same year the colonists talked White into sailing back to England, because they did not have enough supplies to last long. When he got back in August 1590, the colonists were gone. The word "Croatoan" was carved on a tree.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 8 minutes**

Art in the English colonies of the 1600s was made for families with money, for the graves of the dead and for the church. Painters made portraits for rich Boston families, carvers cut pictures into gravestones, and girls stitched needlework. A printer in Massachusetts produced the first full book printed in the English colonies in 1640, and a book of poems by a woman living in Massachusetts came out in London ten years later.

In New Mexico, a Spanish friar, a member of a Catholic religious order, had Acoma people build a church on top of the steep hill where their town stands, and they had to carry every load up to the top. Other friars, and Spanish soldiers, burned the masks that Pueblo people used in their ceremonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portraits, gravestones and needlework in New England" -->
English colonists built Boston on land of the Massachusett nation, on a peninsula the Massachusett called Mushauwomuk. Experts credit about ten portraits of people in Boston, all painted from 1670 to 1674, to one painter. No record gives the painter's name, so today the painter is called the Freake Painter, after the best-known portrait. An article in *The Magazine Antiques* calls the painter "probably Samuel Clement," but nobody has proved it.

**A baby painted in later.** That portrait shows Elizabeth Freake holding her baby daughter, Mary. The Freakes were one of the richest families in Boston. The painter first painted Elizabeth alone, in about 1671. Mary was born on May 6, 1674, and X-rays of the painting show that the baby was painted in afterward. The Worcester Art Museum, in Massachusetts, owns the painting.

**Skulls with wings.** Many settlers of New England were Puritans, English Protestants, Christians outside the Catholic Church, who wanted a plainer and stricter church. Puritans did not want religious symbols such as crosses and cherubs, carvings of baby angels, on their graves. Carvers cut other pictures into Puritan gravestones: skulls with wings, crossed bones and hourglasses. Many stones carry the Latin words "Memento Mori," which mean "remember you will die."

Joseph Lamson of Charlestown, Massachusetts, who lived from 1658 to 1722, is one carver whose name is known. He carved winged skulls with eyebrows that hook at the ends, inside borders of fruit, vines and flowers. His sons Nathaniel and Caleb and his grandson Joseph carried on the work.

Girls learned needlework from their mothers. Many stitched samplers, strips of cloth that kept a record of stitches and patterns to copy later.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="loara-standish-sampler" name="Loara Standish" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Loara Standish

> **Who:** A daughter of Myles and Barbara Standish of Plymouth Colony, thought to be the maker of the oldest known sampler made in America.
> **When and where:** Duxbury, in Plymouth Colony, in the 1640s or about 1653. She was born after 1627 and died before 1655.

**"Loara Standish is my name," she stitched in silk on linen.**

Loara Standish is thought to have stitched the oldest known sampler made in America, and the oldest known sampler with a verse. She made it in Duxbury, in Plymouth Colony. English settlers built Plymouth on the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town.

Loara was one of the seven children of Myles and Barbara Standish. According to the Pilgrim Hall Museum, she probably made the sampler in her teens. She stitched it in silk thread on linen cloth, with roses, carnations and oak leaves, and a letter S twisted around itself.

**The verse.** She stitched this into it: "Loara Standish is my name Lorde guide my hart that I may doe thy will also My hands with such Convenient skill as may Conduce to virtue void of Shame and I will give The glory to thy name." The verse asks God to guide her heart and to give her hands enough skill to do good. She promises to give God the glory.

Lucius Alden gave the sampler to the Pilgrim Hall Museum in Plymouth in 1844. It has faded from being hung up for so many years. Experts at the museum date it to about 1653. Experts at the Textile Research Centre in Leiden, in the Netherlands, date it to the 1640s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="loara-standish-sampler" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first books printed and written here" -->
The Reverend Jose Glover sailed for Massachusetts with a printing press in 1638 and died on the ship. His widow, Elizabeth Glover, set up the press in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She put it in the charge of Stephen Daye, a locksmith.

**The Bay Psalm Book.** Daye printed *The Whole Booke of Psalmes*, now called the *Bay Psalm Book*, in 1640. A group of thirty elders, men respected in the colony for their learning, had put the psalms, 150 poems in the Hebrew Bible, into English verse that could be sung. Among them were John Cotton, Richard Mather and John Eliot. Daye is thought to have printed about 1,700 copies, which sold for twenty pence, or pennies, each. Only eleven copies survive today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="anne-bradstreet" name="Anne Bradstreet" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Anne Bradstreet

> **Who:** A poet in the Massachusetts Bay colony, whose book was the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** Born in England in 1612. Came to Massachusetts in 1630. Died in 1672.

**The title page called her only "a Gentlewoman of those parts."**

Anne Bradstreet wrote the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies. It was printed in London in 1650 as *The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America*. In old Greek stories, nine goddesses called the Muses watched over poetry and the other arts, so the title describes her as a tenth one. Her full name appears inside the book.

She was the daughter of Thomas and Dorothy Dudley. She married Simon Bradstreet in about 1628, sailed to Massachusetts with him and her parents in 1630, and had eight children.

**How the poems got to London.** Her brother-in-law, the Reverend John Woodbridge, took her poems to London. In the book's opening pages, Woodbridge wrote that she had not known he was doing it. Later, in a poem called "The Author to Her Book," Bradstreet called him a "friend more loving than true" for taking them.

**What she knew.** The biographer Charlotte Gordon studied the poems and the letters between Bradstreet and Woodbridge. She found that Bradstreet knew about the plan and helped with it from the start. One poem in the book is about events of 1649. Woodbridge had sailed in 1648, so someone had to send that poem after him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="anne-bradstreet" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mission churches and burned masks in New Mexico" -->
Acoma Pueblo is a town built on top of a mesa, a hill with steep sides and a flat top. Chapter 2 tells in full what happened there in 1598 and 1599. In the Acoma people's own account, the soldiers of the Spanish captain Juan de Zaldívar raped an Acoma woman, which means they forced her into sex. In December 1598 Acoma people killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men.

**The attack on Acoma.** In January 1599 Spanish soldiers sent by Juan de Oñate, the leader of the Spanish colony in New Mexico, took the town and burned much of it. They killed hundreds of Acoma men, women and children, between about 300 and about 1,500 by the estimates. Oñate sentenced the Acoma men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off and to do forced labor, work for others without pay and without the right to leave, for twenty years. Whether the feet were really cut off is disputed.

**The church on the mesa.** A friar named Juan Ramírez arrived at Acoma in 1629. He probably began soon after to have Acoma people build a church on the mesa, called San Esteban del Rey. The Acoma workers had to carry every load up the mesa. The loads included roof beams 40 feet long, cut in mountains 30 to 40 miles away. One account says the builders were enslaved. Another says only that they had to do the work.

**Not known:** the exact years of the building, or whether the workers were paid, which the surviving records do not say.

San Esteban del Rey is the oldest European church in New Mexico that still stands.

**Burned masks.** Pueblo people made masks for their ceremonies. Katsina masks show spirit beings, and they were kept in kivas, rooms used for religious ceremonies. Alonso de Posada, the head of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico, banned Pueblo ceremonies from 1656 to 1665. He ordered friars to go into the kivas and burn the masks. They also burned prayer sticks and carved figures, or took them away. In 1661 Spanish soldiers raided kivas and burned masks as well. No surviving record counts the masks that friars and soldiers burned.

Governor Juan Francisco Treviño had 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested in 1675. Three of them were hanged, and one killed himself. The rest were whipped in public and jailed, until Pueblo men came to Santa Fe and forced their release. Treviño ordered the arrests. Dennis Zotigh's account of these events for the Smithsonian does not name the men who hanged and whipped the prisoners.

One of the freed men was Po'pay, of Ohkay Owingeh. He led the Pueblo Revolt on August 10, 1680, in which Pueblo people killed 21 friars and burned the mission churches across the province.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 7 minutes**

The first trained painters in the colonies earned their living from families with money, and some of that money came from slavery and from Native land. A few painters trained in Europe settled here between 1700 and 1750. They painted merchants, planters and officials, because only families with money could pay for a portrait.

Two portraits from these years show slavery. One shows an enslaved boy in a metal collar beside the son of a rich Maryland family. The other shows the Royall family, whose money came from trading in sugar, rum and people. One painter made portraits of two Lenape leaders two years before William Penn's sons took a large part of Lenape land. A colonial governor took Cherokee baskets to London in 1725, and they are still kept there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Portrait painters in the colonies" -->
Henrietta Johnston drew portraits in Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708. John Smibert came from Scotland and painted in Boston from 1729 or 1730.

Justus Engelhardt Kühn came from Germany. He applied at Annapolis, Maryland, in 1708 to become an English subject, with the rights of a person born English. He painted the children of rich Maryland families until he died in 1717. He was the earliest artist known to work for pay in the Middle Atlantic colonies, the group of colonies that included Maryland.

Gustavus Hesselius was born in Sweden in about 1682 and studied art there. He painted portraits in Philadelphia. He also built church organs, among them one for the church of the Moravians, a Protestant group, in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, in 1746. He died in Philadelphia on May 23, 1755. Robert Feke painted portraits in Newport, Rhode Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henrietta-johnston" name="Henrietta Johnston" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henrietta Johnston

> **Who:** A portrait artist who worked in pastels. She was the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies.
> **When and where:** Born in northern France in about 1674. Worked in Charleston, South Carolina, from 1708 until she died there on March 9, 1729.

**Pastels came only from England, and pirates attacked her voyage home.**

Henrietta Johnston was the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies. She was also the first artist in the colonies to use pastels, crayons made from a paste of colored powder and resin, a sticky stuff that comes from trees.

**Driven out of France.** Her family were French Protestants. Two years before they fled to London in 1687, King Louis XIV of France had taken away Protestants' right to practice their religion. He sent soldiers to live in Protestant homes, and the king's officers let the soldiers loot the homes and ruin the families.

The soldiers tortured some people who would not become Catholic, which means they hurt them on purpose to make them give in. They stayed until the families became Catholic or left France.

Henrietta married in 1694 and moved to Ireland, where she learned to draw portraits in pastel. Her first husband died in 1704 and left her with two small children.

**Charleston.** Her second husband, Gideon Johnston, was a minister of the Church of England. In 1708 he moved the family to Charleston, where he served as the representative of the bishop of London. The Stono, Kiawah and Etiwan peoples had lived along the rivers at Charleston when English colonists settled there in 1670. The Johnstons were short of money, so Henrietta drew portraits of the town's richest families for pay.

Pastels could be bought only in England. She sailed to London for supplies in 1711, and on the voyage home pirates attacked her ship. She survived. After Gideon died in 1716, she earned the family's living alone. About three dozen of her portraits survive.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henrietta-johnston" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-smibert" name="John Smibert" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Smibert

> **Who:** A Scottish portrait painter in Boston, whose shop and studio showed younger painters copies of European art. He also drew the plans for Faneuil Hall.
> **When and where:** Born in Edinburgh, Scotland, in 1688. Newport, Rhode Island, in 1729, then Boston until he died there in 1751.

**Upstairs over his paint shop hung copies of Europe's famous paintings.**

John Smibert was the first painter with formal art training to work in the thirteen colonies. In 1728 a church leader named George Berkeley asked him to come to America. Berkeley planned to start a college in Bermuda, where Smibert would teach art. The college was never started.

Smibert reached Newport, Rhode Island, with Berkeley's group in 1729 and painted Berkeley and his party in a picture now at Yale University. By 1729 or 1730 he had moved to Boston. There he painted merchants, such as Peter Faneuil, and Judge Samuel Sewall. He drew the plans for Faneuil Hall in Boston in 1742.

**The shop and the studio.** Portraits alone did not earn Smibert enough, so he kept a shop. In 1734 he began selling paint, other art supplies and prints. Upstairs, in his studio, he hung copies of famous European paintings that he had made in Europe. He also showed casts there, copies made by pouring material into molds.

The historian Richard Saunders calls it "America's first art gallery." Saunders writes that the collection gave Charles Willson Peale, Gilbert Stuart and John Trumbull much of their early training. A biography of Smibert on Encyclopedia.com names John Singleton Copley and Washington Allston as painters who learned from it. Stuart was born in 1755, four years after Smibert died, so he studied the copies after Smibert's death.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-smibert" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two portraits that show slavery" -->
**The boy in the collar.** Justus Engelhardt Kühn painted Henry Darnall III, the son of a rich Maryland family, in about 1710, when the boy was about eight. Henry wears fine clothes and holds a bow. Beside him stands a Black boy with a metal collar around his neck. The Black boy was enslaved.

His name was not recorded, and neither was the name of whoever put the collar on him. The Maryland Center for History and Culture owns the painting and calls him "Unnamed attendant." One art history website calls the picture the earliest known formal painting of an enslaved African in America.

**The Royall family.** Robert Feke painted Isaac Royall Jr. in 1741, when he was 22, with his wife Elizabeth, their baby daughter Elizabeth, his sister Penelope and his wife's sister-in-law Mary Palmer. Harvard Law School owns the painting.

Isaac's father, Isaac Royall Sr., had set up a sugar plantation on the island of Antigua. The father got rich by trading in sugar, in rum and in enslaved people. In 1737 the family moved to Medford, Massachusetts, and brought at least 27 enslaved Africans with them. When the father died in 1739, the son got his very large fortune, at the age of 20.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two Lenape leaders painted, 1735" -->
Gustavus Hesselius painted two Lenape leaders, Lapowinsa and Tishcohan, in 1735, for John Penn, a son of William Penn. English colonists led by William Penn had built Philadelphia on Lenape land. Lapowinsa is shown as a strong man of about 40. They are the only two Lenape chiefs whose portraits have survived. The Historical Society of Pennsylvania owns them.

**The Walking Purchase.** Two years later Lapowinsa and Tishcohan signed a land deal called the Walking Purchase with John Penn and his brother Thomas Penn. The Lenape leaders Nutimus and Menakihikon also signed it. James Logan, who worked for the Penns, showed the Lenape an unsigned copy of a deed from 1686, a paper selling land. He claimed it gave the Penns as much land as a man could walk in a day and a half.

**The walk.** The Penns' men cleared a path ahead of time. On September 19, 1737, three walkers set off at a fast pace, and in a day and a half one of them covered more than 60 miles. The Penns' surveyors, the men who measured and marked out the land, then bent the boundary line to take in even more. In all, the Penns took more than a thousand square miles of Lenape land, and they forced Nutimus and his people to leave their homes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cherokee baskets taken to London, 1725" -->
Cherokee weavers made baskets from river cane, a tall plant with hollow stems that grows along rivers. They split the cane into thin strips and dyed some of the strips with walnut and pokeberry. Some baskets were double woven, two baskets woven one inside the other from the same strips, so both the inside and the outside show a finished pattern.

Francis Nicholson, the first governor of South Carolina chosen by the British king, left Charleston for London in 1725. He took a set of Cherokee double-woven baskets with him and passed them to Sir Hans Sloane, a collector in London. A note tied to them says, "They will keep anything in them from being wetted by rain." The British Museum in London holds them today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 11 minutes**

The men who led the Revolution had their portraits painted by Americans, several of them trained in London, and in the same cities enslaved Black artists painted and wrote poems, though only a few records name them. In Boston, Prince Demah painted portraits while he was enslaved, and later he served during the Revolution in the Massachusetts militia, a part-time army of local men.

Phillis Wheatley's *Poems*, the first book of poems by an African American, came out in London in 1773 while she was still enslaved in Boston. The painter Charles Willson Peale was paid for portraits with two enslaved people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters who went to London" -->
John Singleton Copley, a Boston painter born on July 3, 1738, sent a painting called *A Boy with a Flying Squirrel* to be shown in London in 1766. The London painters Joshua Reynolds and Benjamin West praised it. In 1769 Copley painted Isaac Royall Jr., the rich man from Medford, and his wife. Copley left America for England in 1774. He painted *Watson and the Shark* there in 1778, and he died in London on September 9, 1815.

The Pennsylvania painter Benjamin West settled in England in 1763 and became the king's official painter of history scenes. West was known for helping young American painters who came to London to learn.

**Faces in wax.** Patience Wright, born Patience Lovell in 1725, made portraits by shaping wax. Britannica gives her birthplace as Bordentown, New Jersey. The American Battlefield Trust says she was born at Oyster Bay, on Long Island in New York, and that her family moved to Bordentown when she was four. She took her wax figures around on tour to show them. She sailed to England in February 1772, and in London she showed wax figures of actors, politicians and nobles. She died in London on March 23, 1786.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-willson-peale" name="Charles Willson Peale" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Charles Willson Peale

> **Who:** A painter of the leaders of the Revolution, who opened a museum in Philadelphia and held a young man in slavery to work in it.
> **When and where:** Born in Chester, Maryland, in April 1741. Worked in Annapolis, then Philadelphia. Died in 1827.

**A planter paid him for portraits with two people.**

Charles Willson Peale painted the leaders of the Revolution, and George Washington sat for him seven times. Peale first trained to make saddles. Then he studied painting in England for three years. Back in Annapolis, Maryland, he joined the Sons of Liberty, groups of colonists who formed in 1765 to resist a British tax on printed paper, called the Stamp Act.

**Mount Vernon.** Peale came to Washington's home at Mount Vernon on May 18, 1772, and stayed two weeks. He painted small portraits of Martha Washington and her children. He also painted George Washington in oil, in the uniform of the Virginia militia.

**The museum.** Peale announced a show of 44 portraits of leaders of the Revolution in his home in Philadelphia in 1784. Visitors paid to get in, and Peale called it the Philadelphia Museum. One account dates the opening of his museum to 1786 instead.

Washington paid a yearly fee to support it and gave it two golden pheasants from China. In 1795 the museum was the site of the Columbianum, the first show in America put on by a society of artists. After Peale died, his family sold the museum's collection of things from nature in 1849 and its portraits at auction in 1854.

**Paid in people.** By 1776 a Maryland plantation owner had given Peale two enslaved people, Scarborough and Lucy, as payment for portraits. The account of this payment does not give the planter's name. Peale freed Scarborough and Lucy in 1786.

Their son, Moses Williams, was born in about 1777. Pennsylvania lawmakers passed a law in 1780 to end slavery slowly, over many years, and under that law Moses Williams was not free. Peale kept him enslaved in his household until about 1802, when Moses was about 25 or 27. Peale trained him for work in the museum: cutting silhouettes, which are outline portraits cut from paper, preserving dead animals for display and building exhibits.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-willson-peale" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="prince-demah" name="Prince Demah" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Prince Demah

> **Who:** An enslaved portrait painter in Boston, who later lived free and served as a soldier in the Massachusetts militia.
> **When and where:** Baptized in Boston in May 1745. Worked in Boston and London from 1769 to 1778. Died in Boston in March 1778.

**He signed his portrait of a Boston merchant "Prince Demah Barnes."**

Prince Demah painted portraits in Boston while he was enslaved. One of his signed paintings belongs today to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. He and his mother, Daphney, were baptized at Trinity Church in Boston in May 1745.

**Bought for his talent.** Henry Barnes was a merchant in Marlborough, Massachusetts, whose family already held Daphney in slavery. In 1769 Barnes bought her son, Prince, who was then in his late twenties. Barnes's wife, Christian, wrote to a friend that they planned on "improving his genius in painting," genius meaning talent. She also wrote that the Boston painter John Singleton Copley would not be willing to teach him.

**London.** Barnes took Prince to London in October 1770, and a painter named Pine gave him lessons there. He was probably Robert Edge Pine. Barnes was afraid that Prince would "attempt his freedom," which meant try to escape. He wrote, "I do not let [Prince] converse with any of his own colour here." He meant that he kept Prince from talking with other Black people in London.

**The advertisement.** Back in Boston, an advertisement ran at least nine times in the *Boston News-Letter* in 1773. It offered the work of a "Negro Artist," using the word of that time for a Black person, who "takes Faces at the lowest Rates," which meant painted portraits cheaply. That February, Prince painted William Duguid, a Scottish cloth merchant living in Boston, and signed it "Prince Demah Barnes." He also painted, or copied from Copley, portraits of Henry and Christian Barnes.

**Free.** The Barnes family sided with the king. In late 1775 they left for England, and they left Daphney behind at their Marlborough home.

Prince joined a unit of cannon soldiers in the Massachusetts militia in April 1777, under the name Prince Demah. The records do not say how he became free. On March 11, 1778, he wrote his will as "Prince Demah, limner," a painter of portraits, and as a "free Negro." He left everything he had to his mother. He died one week later. The record does not give the cause.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="prince-demah" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="phillis-wheatley" name="Phillis Wheatley" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Phillis Wheatley

> **Who:** A poet, the first African American to publish a book of poems. She was enslaved in Boston when the book was printed.
> **When and where:** Kidnapped in West Africa as a child and landed in Boston in 1761, at seven or eight years old. Her book was printed in London in 1773. She died in Boston in 1784, at about 31.

**Eighteen men signed a statement at the front that she wrote it.**

Phillis Wheatley wrote the first book of poems by an African American. It came out in London in September 1773, while she was enslaved in Boston. The title page describes her as "Negro Servant to Mr. John Wheatley, of Boston." Servant there stood for enslaved.

**Named for a ship.** She was kidnapped in West Africa as a child. In 1761, when she was seven or eight, she landed in Boston on a slave ship named the *Phillis*, and her first name came from the ship. John Wheatley's letter in her book says only that she "was brought from Africa to America," and it does not name the men who kidnapped her. In Boston, John Wheatley bought her. He wrote that she learned English in sixteen months, taught at home and not in any school.

When she was about seventeen, in 1770, a Boston printer named Ezekiel Russell printed her poem on the death of George Whitefield, a preacher who had toured the colonies.

**The statement.** The publisher of her book expected readers to doubt that an enslaved African girl could write poems. So he printed a statement at the front of the book, signed by 18 men of Boston. Among them were Governor Thomas Hutchinson, John Hancock, the Reverend John Moorhead and John Wheatley, listed as "her Master."

The men wrote that the poems were "written by PHILLIS, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave" (uncultivated meant untaught, and barbarian was an insult for a person thought to be wild and without learning). They wrote that she "has been examined by some of the best Judges, and is thought qualified to write them."

One poem in the book is called "To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works." The Wheatleys freed her by November 1773, soon after the book appeared. She married John Peters in 1778. She died poor in 1784, at about 31.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="phillis-wheatley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two more Black painters, Scipio Moorhead and Joshua Johnson" -->
Scipio Moorhead was an enslaved Black painter in Boston. The scholars Kwame Anthony Appiah and Henry Louis Gates Jr. write that Wheatley's poem to "S. M." may describe two of his paintings. He was held in slavery by the Reverend John Moorhead of Boston, one of the 18 men who signed the statement in Wheatley's book. Sarah Moorhead probably gave Scipio lessons.

Appiah and Gates also write that the engraved portrait of Wheatley at the front of her book was based on his painting of her. No signed painting by Scipio Moorhead survives.

**Joshua Johnson.** Joshua Johnson was born enslaved in Maryland in 1763. His father was a white man named George Johnson. His mother was a woman held in slavery by William Wheeler Sr., and her name was not written down. George Johnson bought his 19-year-old son for £25 in 1782. He sent him to learn the trade of a blacksmith and signed a manumission, a legal paper that freed him.

Joshua Johnson was listed in Baltimore as a portrait painter by 1796. In a 1798 newspaper advertisement he called himself a "self-taught genius." He wrote that he had faced "many insuperable obstacles," which means problems he could not get past. Amelia Peck of the Metropolitan Museum of Art and Paula M. Bagger write that before Prince Demah's work was found, Johnson was thought to be the earliest African American painter of portraits in oil paint.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mercy Otis Warren's plays" -->
Mercy Otis Warren wrote plays that made fun of the British governor of Massachusetts and attacked his policies. She was born in Barnstable, Massachusetts, on September 14, 1728. The first play, *The Adulateur*, came out in a newspaper, the *Massachusetts Spy*, in 1772. Others followed, among them *Defeat* in 1773, *The Group* in 1775, *The Blockheads* in 1776 and *The Motley Assembly* in 1779.

All of them came out without her name. The National Women's History Museum says she likely did this to avoid punishment, and so that readers would take the work of a woman seriously. She died in 1814.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gilbert-stuart" name="Gilbert Stuart" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gilbert Stuart

> **Who:** A portrait painter whose unfinished picture of George Washington is the model for the face on the one-dollar bill.
> **When and where:** Born in Kingstown, Rhode Island, on December 3, 1755. Worked in London and Dublin, then in the United States from 1793. Died in 1828.

**He never delivered Washington's portrait, and he sold copies for $100.**

The picture of George Washington on the one-dollar bill is an engraving based on a portrait by Gilbert Stuart. Stuart sailed to London in 1775. There Benjamin West hired him and gave him a studio and a place to live. In 1787 Stuart left London for Dublin to get away from the people he owed money. He arrived in New York in May 1793.

**The unfinished head.** Martha Washington ordered portraits of her husband and herself from Stuart in 1796. He painted only Washington's head and neck on a brown background, left the rest of the canvas unfinished and kept it. He never gave it to her, although she asked for it again and again after her husband died in 1799.

Stuart used the unfinished head as a model and painted more than a hundred copies of it, 130 by one count. He sold them for $100 each. An engraving of the head, flipped left to right, is on the one-dollar bill.

The people in charge of the Boston Athenaeum, a library in Boston, bought the original for $1,500 in 1831, and the painting has been called the Athenaeum portrait ever since. Since 1980 the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston and the National Portrait Gallery in Washington have owned it together.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gilbert-stuart" -->
