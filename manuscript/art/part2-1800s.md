## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From about 1825, a group of American painters known as the Hudson River School made landscapes, pictures of the land and sky. Buyers in the cities paid for pictures of the Hudson River valley and the Catskill Mountains. In the same years, federal officials, the officials of the national government, forced Native nations off their land. The painter George Catlin travelled west and painted the people of about fifty nations. Enslaved artists worked too. In South Carolina an enslaved potter named Dave cut his own verses into his jars, starting in 1834, and later signed them "Dave." That was the year the state's lawmakers made it a crime for enslaved people to learn to read and write. News of photography reached the United States in 1839. By the early 1850s, a small photograph in New York cost as little as 25 cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black artists in Philadelphia and Baltimore" -->
Moses Williams was born in about 1777, enslaved in the household of the painter Charles Willson Peale. A Maryland planter had given his parents, Scarborough and Lucy, to Peale as payment for portraits. Peale held Moses until about 1802. Then Williams cut silhouettes at Peale's museum in Philadelphia. A silhouette is a portrait that shows only the outline of a face from the side. Williams used a machine called a physiognotrace. It traced the outline of the face of the person being pictured onto paper, through a set of connected rods. Peale's own children learned painting from their father. Karl Cole's account of Williams's life for Davis Art says Williams was not given the chance to paint. The Philadelphia Museum of Art holds silhouettes he cut of the Peale family between 1803 and 1805.

Joshua Johnson was born into slavery in Maryland in 1763. In 1782 his white father, George Johnson, bought him and freed him. From 1796 to 1824 the Baltimore city directories, books that listed the town's people and their trades, listed him as a portrait painter.

In 1805 Peale, the sculptor William Rush and other artists and business leaders founded the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in Philadelphia. The academy's leaders very seldom let Black students in. Robert Douglass Jr., a Black painter of Philadelphia who lived from 1809 to 1887, was one of the few. He trained at the academy and studied under the painter Thomas Sully. In 1834 he painted the portraits of William Lloyd Garrison and James Forten, two men who worked to end slavery. An article by the Library Company of Philadelphia describes him as Philadelphia's first African American photographer.

In 1844 the academy's leaders gave women artists the room of statues to draw in, on three mornings a week. In 1860 they let women take classes on the body's bones and muscles and draw from copies of ancient statues. In life classes, students draw from a model without clothes. The leaders opened life classes to women in 1868, with women as the models. Men became the models for these classes in 1874.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters on the road" -->
Many portrait painters of these years had taught themselves. They travelled from town to town and charged somewhat less than painters with training. A story told later says these painters painted bodies ahead of time and added the customer's face when they arrived. The unfinished portraits that survive show the opposite. They have the face done and the body not yet painted, so the painters began with the head.

Ammi Phillips travelled and painted portraits in Connecticut, Massachusetts and New York from the middle of the 1810s until the early 1860s. He was born in Colebrook, Connecticut, on April 24, 1788. For years nobody knew who had painted many of his pictures, and collectors called the unknown painter the "Border Limner." Limner is an old word for a painter of portraits. The collectors Barbara and Larry Holdridge matched those paintings to Phillips. In the mid-1830s, in Dutchess County, New York, he painted *Girl in Red Dress with Cat and Dog*. In 1985 it was bought for the American Folk Art Museum for one million dollars. Phillips died on July 11, 1865.

Edward Hicks, born in Pennsylvania on April 4, 1780, became an apprentice to coach makers at 13. An apprentice is a young worker who learns a trade by working for masters of it. From 1812 he was a minister of the Quakers, a Christian group. He painted 62 versions of *The Peaceable Kingdom*, based on a passage in the book of Isaiah in the Bible. Many versions show William Penn meeting Native people. They stand for the Lenape, on whose land Penn founded his colony. In his own lifetime, Hicks was better known as a minister than as a painter. He died on August 23, 1849.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-whiting-stock" name="Joseph Whiting Stock" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Whiting Stock

> **Who:** A travelling portrait painter who could not walk. He kept a journal of the paintings he made and what he was paid for them.
> **When and where:** Born in Springfield, Massachusetts, on January 30, 1815. Painted in Massachusetts, Rhode Island and New York from 1832. Died in 1855, at the age of forty.

Joseph Whiting Stock painted more than 900 pictures, although he could not walk. He was the third of twelve children in a Springfield, Massachusetts, family. When he was eleven, an oxcart turned over and fell on him. He was paralyzed from the waist down, which means he could not move or feel his legs. He could not leave his bed until he was nineteen.

A doctor suggested that he take up art. He began lessons at seventeen, and his teacher was a painter named Franklin White. He tried a portrait of his sister Eliza. "I attempted my sister Eliza's portrait and made so good a likeness as to induce my friends to encourage me by their patronage," he wrote in his journal. Patronage meant paying him for pictures.

In 1834 a doctor named James Swan hired Stock to make drawings of the human body. Swan also built him a wheelchair. With it, Stock could paint large pictures and be lifted onto trains to travel for work. His first painting trip was a month in Wilbraham, Massachusetts, in 1836. He later spent two years in Warren, Rhode Island, and New Bedford, Massachusetts, and several years in Orange County, New York. He lived with local families while he worked, and sometimes he paid for his room and meals with paintings.

His journal lists more than 900 paintings and what he was paid for them. About a hundred of those paintings are known today. He died of tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs, in 1855, at forty. The Springfield Museums hold his journal.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-whiting-stock" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Writers" -->
Mercy Otis Warren, who had written plays against the British governor of Massachusetts in the 1770s, published her history of the American Revolution in three volumes in 1805. Washington Irving set his 1819 story "Rip Van Winkle" in the Catskill Mountains of New York, the same mountains the Hudson River School painted a few years later.

James Fenimore Cooper's novel *The Last of the Mohicans* came out in Philadelphia on February 4, 1826. The Mohican people named in its title were living in New York and Wisconsin that year. Agents from New York and officials of the War Department were making treaties, written agreements between governments, under which the Mohican, by then called the Stockbridge, and the Munsee had to move from New York to Wisconsin.

Ralph Waldo Emerson's long essay *Nature* appeared in 1836. Edgar Allan Poe's poem "The Raven" was first printed in a New York newspaper, the *Evening Mirror*, on January 29, 1845. Other papers printed it at least ten more times in the next few weeks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hudson River School, and whose land it painted" -->
The Hudson River School was a group of American landscape painters. They worked from about 1825 to 1870. The name comes from their shared aims more than from one place, but many of them painted the Catskill Mountains west of the Hudson River. Thomas Cole, Thomas Doughty and Asher B. Durand were the first leaders.

In 1826 Cole made his home in the village of Catskill, New York. That land is the homeland of the Muh-he-conneok, the People of the Waters That Are Never Still. Today they are called the Stockbridge-Munsee Band of Mohican Indians. A marker at Cole's house states that they were forced from there.

The Mohican had lost the Hudson valley long before Cole came. Through the 1600s and 1700s, settlers pushed them out. Most moved to Stockbridge, Massachusetts, a mission town, which means a town built around a church where ministers taught Christianity. From 1764 white settlers poured into Stockbridge and took thousands of acres of Mohican land. They squatted on it, which means they settled on it without any right, and they bought it in ways the law did not allow. They also took land to pay off Mohican debts. By 1783 the Mohican had lost all their land in the town. In 1785 a group of them founded New Stockbridge in upstate New York, on land of the Oneida nation.

After an 1822 treaty, Stockbridge people in New York began moving to Wisconsin. By 1831, 225 Stockbridge people and 100 Munsee people had moved there. Agents from New York and commissioners from the War Department arranged the treaties for these moves. From 1832 to 1834, federal officials made them move again, to the east shore of Lake Winnebago. Their reservation, land set aside for them by treaty, is in northeastern Wisconsin today.

While Cole was selling paintings of the Catskills, federal officials were moving Native nations west. On May 28, 1830, President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, the president could make treaties in which Native nations gave up their lands east of the Mississippi River for lands in the west. In 1838 and 1839, federal officials and soldiers forced more than 16,000 Cherokee people from their homeland to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. At least 4,000 of them died of cold, hunger and disease along the way, by the usual count, and other counts are higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-cole" name="Thomas Cole" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Cole

> **Who:** A painter of landscapes and one of the first leaders of the Hudson River School.
> **When and where:** Born February 1, 1801, in Bolton-le-Moors, Lancashire, England. Came to America in 1818. Lived in Catskill, New York, from 1826. Died there on February 11, 1848.

Thomas Cole was one of the first leaders of the Hudson River School. He was born in England in 1801 and came to America with his family in 1818. They lived first in Philadelphia and then in Steubenville, Ohio. A travelling portrait painter named Stein gave him lessons. Then he studied for two years at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts.

In 1825 Cole put some landscapes in the window of a shop in New York City. The painters John Trumbull and Asher B. Durand saw them and bought them. The next year Cole moved to Catskill. He made pencil sketches outdoors. In winter he painted in his studio. He also spent years painting in Europe, mostly in Italy, from 1829 to 1832 and again from 1841 to 1842.

In 1836 he painted *The Oxbow*, a view from Mount Holyoke, near Northampton, Massachusetts. It shows the valley of the Connecticut River, not the Hudson, and Cole joined two views from the mountain into one picture. On the far hill, the bare strips where trees were cut seem to spell the Hebrew letters for "Noah." The art historian Matthew Baigell was the first to notice them. Cole made the painting for a show in New York in April 1836. His patron, Luman Reed, had suggested it. A patron is a person who pays an artist to make work. In March 1836 Cole wrote to Reed, "Fancy pictures seldom sell & they generally take more time than views so I have determined to paint one of the latter." By "fancy pictures" he meant pictures from his imagination, and by "views" he meant real places. That same year, 1836, he finished *The Course of Empire*, a set of five paintings. The Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York has owned *The Oxbow* since 1908.

Cole died in Catskill on February 11, 1848. Durand painted a picture called *Kindred Spirits* in his memory. Cole's house and studio in Catskill are now the Thomas Cole National Historic Site.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-cole" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="George Catlin and the people he painted" -->
George Catlin was a lawyer who became a painter. He lived from 1796 to 1872. In 1830 he went to St. Louis to meet William Clark, the federal official in charge of Indian affairs there. From St. Louis, Catlin made five trips west between 1830 and 1836. In 1832 he travelled more than 2,000 miles up the Missouri River. He wrote, "If my life be spared, nothing shall stop me from visiting every nation of Indians on the Continent of North America." By one count he visited 48 nations, and by another, fifty.

In 1832, at a Mandan village in what is now North Dakota, Catlin painted Four Bears, a Mandan leader. His Mandan name was Máh-to-tóh-pa. Catlin called him "the first and most popular man in the nation." Four Bears was a painter too. He painted his own battles on buffalo robes. Catlin got one of those robes in 1832, and it is now at the Smithsonian. A German prince, Maximilian of Wied, travelled up the Missouri with the painter Karl Bodmer in 1833 and 1834. He took another Four Bears robe to Germany. It is in the Linden Museum in Stuttgart.

Catlin sometimes got objects from the people he painted, as proof that his pictures were accurate. One was a Sioux cradle. In 1836 he went to the place in southwestern Minnesota where people dug red stone for pipes. Sioux elders protested strongly, and he went anyway.

Smallpox was a disease caused by a virus. It spread when people were close together, face to face. It caused a fever and a rash that turned into scabs, and about 3 of every 10 people who caught it died. On May 5, 1832, President Jackson signed the Indian Vaccination Act, a law to give Native people vaccinations, shots that protect a person from smallpox. Secretary of War Lewis Cass ran the program through federal Indian agents. The Mandan, the Hidatsa and the Arikara of the upper Missouri were kept out of it. The records do not name the officials who left them out.

On June 18, 1837, the American Fur Company's steamboat *St. Peters* reached Fort Clark, next to the Mandan villages. The fort's trader, Francis Chardon, learned from the captain, Bernard Pratte Jr., that people on board had smallpox. The crew unloaded goods and loaded furs in less than a day, and the boat went on up the river. The boat had brought smallpox to the Mandan villages. About 2,000 Mandan lived there that spring. Smallpox killed most of them, and by October 138 were alive.

Four Bears caught smallpox and died on July 30, 1837. Chardon wrote down Four Bears's last speech: "I have loved the Whites ... and how have they repaid it! With ingratitude!" Ingratitude means not being thankful.

The Seminole leader Osceola sat for Catlin in January 1838 as a dying prisoner. On October 21, 1837, near St. Augustine, Florida, General Thomas Jesup had ordered soldiers to seize Osceola while he was under a white flag of truce. A white flag of truce is a sign that the two sides have stopped fighting to talk. Osceola was held at Fort Marion in St. Augustine and then at Fort Moultrie in South Carolina. There he was very sick with malaria, a disease spread by mosquitoes that brings fever and chills, and with other illnesses. Three painters, Catlin, W. M. Laning and Robert John Curtis, talked him into letting them paint him anyway. Osceola died a prisoner on January 30, 1838. The army doctor, Frederick Weedon, cut off his head and then buried the body. Weedon kept the head and showed it in his drugstore.

On September 23, 1837, Catlin opened his paintings to the public at Clinton Hall in New York, at 50 cents a visit. He called the show his Indian Gallery. He tried to sell it to the United States government, and members of Congress did not buy it. In November 1839 he sailed for England with the gallery, a tepee of buffalo hide and two live bears. He showed the gallery in London, Brussels and Paris. Groups of Native people who were touring Europe with promoters joined him there, 21 Ojibwe and 14 Iowa. Catlin talked them into acting out hunts, dances and even scalpings for the crowds. Scalping means cutting the skin and hair from the top of an enemy's head. In Paris in 1845, the Ojibwe group caught smallpox. Two of them died. The account that records their deaths does not name them.

In 1852 Catlin ran out of money and was put in a London prison for debt. Members of Congress still would not buy the gallery, even after he lowered his price from $65,000 to $25,000. Joseph Harrison, a Pennsylvania railroad owner, paid Catlin's debts and got the gallery for $20,000. He stored it in his boiler factory in Philadelphia. In 1879 Harrison's widow gave the paintings to the Smithsonian.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Audubon's birds, and the people he sold" -->
John James Audubon's *The Birds of America* was printed in England from 1827 to 1838. It has 435 pictures of birds, each bird shown at its real size. The historian Gregory Nobles records that the Audubons held nine enslaved people in their household in Henderson, Kentucky, in the 1810s. By the end of that decade, short of money, they sold them. In early 1819 Audubon took two enslaved men down the Mississippi River to New Orleans on a small boat, and there he offered the boat and the two men for sale. The family bought more enslaved people in the 1820s and sold them in 1830, when Audubon moved to England to oversee the printing of the birds. In 1834 Audubon wrote to his wife about the freeing of the enslaved people in Britain's colonies in the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean. He wrote that the British government had "acted imprudently and too precipitously," which means without enough care and too fast. The surviving record counts the people Audubon held and sold. It does not give their names.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Dave (David Drake)

> **Who:** An enslaved potter and poet who cut his name and his own verses into large jars of hard-fired clay.
> **When and where:** Born about 1800 in South Carolina. Worked in the potteries of the Edgefield District from about 1818 to 1864. Listed in the 1870 census as "David Drake, Turner." Believed to have died in the 1870s.

Dave was an enslaved potter who wrote his own verses on the jars he made. He is the only Black potter in Edgefield before the Civil War known to have signed his own work. He was born into slavery around 1800. In the Edgefield District of South Carolina, white factory owners made their potteries grow by using the unpaid work of enslaved men, women and children. At least 76 enslaved people worked at the district's 12 potteries.

The first record of Dave is dated June 13, 1818. It describes "a boy about 17 years old country born," which meant born in America and not brought from Africa. His enslaver, Harvey Drake, mortgaged him to a man named Eldrid Simkins. That meant Drake used Dave to guarantee a loan: if Drake did not pay the money back, Simkins could take Dave. At least four men held Dave in slavery during his life. After Harvey Drake died in 1832, his brother Reuben Drake held him until 1835. Then the Reverend John Landrum held him. When Landrum died in 1846, all 18 of the people he enslaved were put up for sale. The records do not name who sold them. Franklin Landrum held Dave for a time. In 1849 Lewis Miles, who owned the Stony Bluff pottery, bought him.

In 1834 South Carolina's lawmakers made it a crime for enslaved people to learn to read and write. That same year, Dave cut words into a jar: "put every bit all between / surely this jar will hold 14." How he learned to read is not known for sure. He may have learned while he worked at a newspaper, the *Edgefield Hive*, printed by Harvey Drake's business partner Abner Landrum. On July 31, 1840, he wrote on a jar: "Dave belongs to Mr. Miles / wher the oven bakes and the pot biles." So by his own words, Lewis Miles held him in 1840, nine years before the 1849 sale. Accounts of his life do not agree on when Miles first held him or how.

Dave shaped the bottom of each jar on a potter's wheel. Then he built up the top by hand with thick coils of clay. Two of his jars at the Charleston Museum each hold more than 40 gallons.

His family was sold away from him. The records do not say who sold them or where they were taken. One of his legs was amputated, which means it was cut off. How he lost it is not known. One account says an enslaver beat him for writing. Another says he was hurt in a train accident. Neither account has been proved.

He wrote three verses on his pots in 1857, eight in 1858 and seven in 1859. On August 16, 1857, he wrote: "I wonder where is all my relation / Friendship to all, and every nation." In 1862 he wrote: "I made this Jar all of cross, If you dont repent, you will be, lost."

A 2006 survey by Arthur F. Goldberg and James Witkowski counted 169 pots made by Dave or credited to him. More than a hundred, dated from 1849 to 1864, are signed "Dave." His verses and marks appear on 50 of them. Sometimes he added the names of other enslaved potters beside his own: Mark, Abram and Baddler. His last dated pot is from 1864. Dave was free by the end of the Civil War in 1865. The Smithsonian American Art Museum credits his freedom to the Emancipation Proclamation, President Abraham Lincoln's order freeing enslaved people in the states at war with the United States. He took the last name Drake, probably from Harvey Drake, the first man who had held him. The census of 1870, the government's count of everyone in the country, lists him as "David Drake," and his work as "turner," a person who shapes pots on a wheel. His name is not in the census of 1880. He is believed to have died in the 1870s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dave-drake" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photography arrives, 1839" -->
In France, the artist Louis Daguerre worked out a way of making pictures with light. News of his process reached the United States in late August and September 1839. In October or November 1839, Robert Cornelius of Philadelphia took a picture of himself. It is the oldest American portrait photograph that survives.

These first photographs are called daguerreotypes. Each one was made directly on a copper plate coated with silver. It was a single picture, and it could not be copied.

A history of New York's photographers, published by the Bard Graduate Center, says the first American studio that sold portrait photographs opened in New York in 1840. By 1850 New York had 71 daguerreotype studios. By 1853 it had more of them than all of England. Mathew Brady opened his first studio at 205 Broadway in 1844. Cheaper studios on Broadway and on the Bowery made portraits for working people. In the early 1850s, a very small daguerreotype, about 1 5/8 inches by 1 3/8 inches, cost 25 cents. A cheap portrait from a "picture factory," a shop that turned out many portraits fast, cost 50 cents. A medium-sized portrait at Brady's studio cost $2.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A writer and speaker who escaped slavery. He wrote the story of his own life and sat for 160 known photographs.
> **When and where:** Born in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. Escaped slavery on September 3, 1838. Lived in Massachusetts, then Rochester, New York, then Washington. Died in 1895.
> **Movie:** *Becoming Frederick Douglass* (2022), a documentary by Stanley Nelson, shown on PBS. It is about his life.

By the count of the historians John Stauffer, Zoe Trodd and Celeste-Marie Bernier, Frederick Douglass sat for more photographs than any other American of the 1800s. He was born into slavery in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. On September 3, 1838, he escaped by train through Baltimore, dressed in a sailor's clothes. Anna Murray, a free Black woman, helped him, and he married her.

On May 1, 1845, his book came out from the Anti-Slavery Office in Boston. It was called *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave. Written by Himself*. By one count it sold 4,500 copies in four months. By another count it sold 5,000. By 1860 almost 30,000 copies had sold.

In 1849 Douglass wrote about pictures of Black people drawn by white artists. He wrote that "negroes can never have impartial portraits at the hands of white artists." Negroes was a word of that time for Black people, and impartial means fair. "It seems to us next to impossible for white men to take likenesses of black men, without most grossly exaggerating their distinctive features," he wrote.

Douglass sat for photographs again and again. The historians John Stauffer, Zoe Trodd and Celeste-Marie Bernier counted 160 photographs of him. They counted 126 of Abraham Lincoln. Douglass said that "the great cheapness and universality of pictures must exert a powerful, though silent, influence upon the ideas and sentiment of present and future generations." Universality means that pictures were everywhere.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Photographers of the second half of the 1800s showed Americans the bodies of soldiers killed in the Civil War, and the valleys and mountains of the far West. Books got cheap: the first ten-cent novel of 1860 sold more than 65,000 copies in a few months. *Uncle Tom's Cabin* sold 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year. Several Black artists of these years did their work far from home. The sculptor Edmonia Lewis said she was "practically driven to Rome" by prejudice against her color, and the painter Henry Ossawa Tanner made his career in Paris. At an army fort in Florida, Plains men held as prisoners without trial drew their lives in account books. In New York, the Metropolitan Museum of Art opened in 1872.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Writers and cheap books" -->
Nathaniel Hawthorne's novel *The Scarlet Letter* came out on March 16, 1850. Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel *Uncle Tom's Cabin* ran in parts in *The National Era*, a newspaper against slavery, from 1851. In 1852 it came out as a book in two volumes. It sold 10,000 copies in the United States in its first week and 300,000 in its first year. In Britain it sold 1.5 million copies in one year.

Herman Melville's *Moby-Dick* came out in London on October 18, 1851, in 500 copies. It came out in New York on November 14, 1851, in a first printing of 2,915 copies. On July 4, 1855, Walt Whitman paid to print his own book of poems, *Leaves of Grass*. The first edition held twelve poems. Whitman kept adding to it and changing it until he died in 1892.

In 1860 the brothers Erastus and Irwin Beadle began selling novels of about 100 pages for ten cents each. People called them dime novels, because a dime is ten cents. The first was Ann S. Stephens's *Malaeska, the Indian Wife of the White Hunter*, dated June 9, 1860. It sold more than 65,000 copies in its first few months. The Beadles printed 321 of these books in the series.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Robert S. Duncanson in Cincinnati" -->
Robert S. Duncanson was a free Black painter who had taught himself to paint. He was born in about 1821 in Fayette, New York, the son of a free Black tradesman. In 1840 he moved to Cincinnati, Ohio, where about 3,000 Black people lived. In about 1850 or 1851, Nicholas Longworth hired him to paint eight landscapes on the walls of the entrance hall of his house, Belmont. Each is about nine feet high. The house is now the Taft Museum of Art. In 1853 Duncanson painted *Uncle Tom and Little Eva*, from Stowe's novel. It is at the Detroit Institute of Arts. During the Civil War he left the United States. He showed his work in Montreal, Canada, in 1864 and in Dublin, Ireland, in 1865. He died in Detroit on December 21, 1872.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photographs of the Civil War dead" -->
Alexander Gardner, born in Paisley, Scotland, in 1821, worked for the New York photographer Mathew Brady from 1856. In 1858 Brady put him in charge of his studio in Washington. In September 1862 Gardner and his team photographed the dead soldiers on the battlefield at Antietam, in Maryland. The American Battlefield Trust estimates that 22,717 soldiers were killed, wounded or missing in that battle.

In October 1862 Brady showed the photographs at his gallery in New York, under a sign that read "The Dead of Antietam." On October 20, 1862, a writer for *The New York Times* reported that crowds of people kept climbing the stairs to see them. "If he has not brought bodies and laid them in our door-yards and along the streets, he has done something very like it," the writer said. Door-yards are the yards in front of houses. The article gave Brady the credit.

Brady put the words "Photographed by Brady" on pictures his employees took. In about November 1862 Gardner stopped working for Brady, probably in part because of this. In May 1863 Gardner and his brother James opened their own studio in Washington and hired many of Brady's former workers. In 1866 Gardner published a book of 100 of the war's photographs in two volumes. Under each picture he printed the name of the man who took it, among them Timothy H. O'Sullivan and James F. Gibson. The book did not sell well.

On July 6, 1863, after the Battle of Gettysburg, Gardner's team photographed a dead soldier among the rocks at a place called Devil's Den. Gardner later called him a Confederate, a soldier of the Southern states that fought the United States to keep slavery. He titled the picture *Home of a Rebel Sharpshooter*. A sharpshooter is a soldier who shoots at a distance with a rifle. In 1975 the historian William A. Frassanito showed that the same body appears in six photographs. The photographers had carried the body from the south edge of Devil's Den to a stone wall, and the rifle beside it was probably put there for the picture. In 1998 the artist James Groves argued that the body was carried the other way, out of the wall and to the first spot. Both men agree that the photographers moved the body. The soldier's name is not known.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="winslow-homer" name="Winslow Homer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Winslow Homer

> **Who:** A painter who drew the Civil War for a magazine and later painted the sea.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston on February 24, 1836. Drew the Civil War for *Harper's Weekly*. Lived in England from 1881 and in Prouts Neck, Maine, from 1883. Died there on September 29, 1910.

Winslow Homer drew the Civil War at the front for *Harper's Weekly*, a magazine. He drew everyday life in the soldiers' camps. He was born in Boston on February 24, 1836. He began as an apprentice at a lithography company. Lithography is a way of printing pictures from drawings made on stone. Then he drew pictures for magazines, among them *Ballou's Pictorial* and *Harper's Weekly*.

He began painting in watercolor in 1873. In 1881 he went to live in Cullercoats, a town in England. In 1883 he moved to Prouts Neck, on the coast of Maine, and the sea became his main subject. He painted people at sea, in pictures such as *The Life Line* and *Fog Warning*. He died at Prouts Neck on September 29, 1910.

### Edmonia Lewis

> **Who:** A sculptor of Mississauga Ojibwe and African American descent, who carved in marble in Rome.
> **When and where:** Born about July 4, 1844, in Greenbush, New York. Oberlin, Ohio, from 1859. Boston. Rome from 1865 or 1866. Died in London on September 17, 1907.

Edmonia Lewis carved statues in marble in Rome. Before that, at college in Ohio, a group of white men beat her, officials charged her with poisoning two classmates, and college officials kept her from graduating. She was born in Greenbush, New York, in about 1844. On official papers she gave her birth year as 1842, 1844 and 1854 at different times. Her mother, Catherine Mike Lewis, was of Mississauga Ojibwe and African American descent. Edmonia's name at birth was Wildfire. By the age of nine she had lost both parents. As a girl she and her aunts sold Ojibwe baskets, moccasins and embroidered blouses to tourists at Niagara Falls. Her brother Samuel paid for her schooling with money he made in the gold rush.

From 1859 she studied at Oberlin, in Ohio. In the winter of 1862, she gave spiced wine to two white students she lived with, Maria Miles and Christina Ennes, before a sleigh ride. They fell sick. Doctors said they had been poisoned with cantharides, a poison made from blister beetles. Both young women got well.

One night soon after, Lewis was walking home alone. A group of white men dragged her into an open field, beat her badly and left her for dead. The men who did it were never identified. A few days later, officials arrested her for poisoning the two students. She was hurt so badly she could not walk, so her trial was put off. John Mercer Langston, the first Black lawyer in Ohio, defended her. The court dropped the charges, because no one had tested what was in the young women's stomachs, so there was no proof of poison.

About a year later she was accused of stealing art supplies. The record does not name who accused her. That case was dropped too. But Oberlin officials did not let her sign up for her last term, so she could not graduate. In 2022, more than a hundred years after her death, the leaders of Oberlin College gave her a degree.

She went to Boston and made a bust of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw. A bust is a sculpture of a person's head and shoulders. Shaw had led the 54th Massachusetts, a regiment of Black soldiers, in the Civil War. Lewis sold 100 plaster copies of the bust for $15 each, and the money paid her way to Rome. Her passport of 1865 says she was "sent by subscription to Italy having displayed great talents as a sculptor." Sent by subscription means that a group of people paid for the trip. In Rome she carved her work in marble.

In 1878 she told *The New York Times*: "I was practically driven to Rome in order to obtain the opportunities for art culture, and to find a social atmosphere where I was not constantly reminded of my color. The land of liberty had no room for a colored sculptor." Colored was a word of that time for Black people.

Her marble statue *The Death of Cleopatra* was shown at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. In 1878 the critic William J. Clark called the way it showed death "ghastly" and "absolutely repellant," which means disgusting. Later a gambler named "Blind John" Condon bought it from a saloon in Chicago. He used it to mark the grave of a racehorse named Cleopatra. The Smithsonian American Art Museum's record of the statue states that it was thought lost for almost a century. It was later moved to a construction yard in Cicero, Illinois. The scholars Marilyn Richardson and Dorothy Porter Wesley told a Smithsonian curator, George Gurney, about it. In 1994 the Historical Society of Forest Park, Illinois, gave it to the Smithsonian American Art Museum. Lewis died in London on September 17, 1907.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures of the West, and whose land they showed" -->
In July 1861 Carleton Watkins carried a very large camera into Yosemite Valley, in California. It took pictures on glass plates 18 inches by 22 inches. He came back with 30 of these large plates and 100 smaller negatives. His photographs were shown at a gallery in New York in 1862. They helped persuade members of Congress to protect the valley. On June 30, 1864, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant.

Yosemite Valley was the home of the Ahwahneechee people. In 1851 California's governor, John McDougal, raised a force of about 200 armed volunteers on horseback, called the Mariposa Battalion. Under Major James D. Savage, they invaded the valley in March 1851. According to the National Park Service, the battalion's men burned villages and food supplies, one after another, and forced men, women and children away from their homes. They marched Tenaya, the leader of the Ahwahneechee, and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River. The National Park Service counts that by 1910, more than 90 out of every 100 of the Ahwahneechee who first lived there were dead or missing.

In 1871 the photographer William Henry Jackson and the painter Thomas Moran went to Yellowstone with the Hayden survey, a government expedition. Jackson took the first known photographs of the Yellowstone falls. When President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law on March 1, 1872, Yellowstone became a national park. That year members of Congress paid Moran $10,000 for his painting *The Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone* and hung it in the Capitol. It shows four small figures in front, and one of them is a Native man.

The Tukudika, a band of Mountain Shoshone, had lived in the Yellowstone area for thousands of years. According to the National Park Service, they were forced onto reservations, land set aside for Native nations. The Park Service account does not say who first forced them. In the fall of 1879, the park's superintendent, Philetus W. Norris, found one of their camps. He asked the government agent at Fort Washakie for help. The agent sent a party of Shoshone men to take the Tukudika to the Wind River Reservation.

Timothy O'Sullivan, who had photographed the war, later photographed for the Wheeler survey, an army survey of the West, from 1872 to 1879. The army's orders for that survey included a duty to "discover the numbers, habits, and disposition of Indians." Disposition here meant whether they were likely to be friendly or to fight.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Museums, and who could get in" -->
On April 13, 1870, New York's lawmakers approved the founding of the Metropolitan Museum of Art. It opened on February 20, 1872, on Fifth Avenue, and moved to Central Park in 1880. Many of its founders were Protestants, Christians who belonged to churches outside the Catholic church, and they refused to open the museum on Sundays. Sunday was the one day off that most working New Yorkers had. City officials demanded Sunday opening. In 1885 officials of the city's parks department threatened to cut the museum's money if it stayed closed on Sundays. The trustees, the people in charge of the museum, agreed in the end, although they did not want to. The museum first opened on a Sunday on May 31, 1891, after nearly twenty years of argument.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Marion: drawings by Plains prisoners" -->
Plains artists had long painted their deeds on buffalo hides. About 30 million buffalo lived on the Plains in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. Hunters killed them for their hides, and army officers encouraged the killing, because the herds fed the Plains nations. Disease and dry years also cut the herds, and so did the railroads built across the Plains. With the buffalo gone, Plains artists drew on paper instead, often in ledgers, lined account books meant for keeping records of money. This is now called ledger art. Prisoners held at an army fort in Florida made many ledger drawings.

In 1874 and 1875 US Army soldiers fought Comanche, Kiowa, Cheyenne and other Plains people on the southern Plains, in what is called the Red River War. After it, army officers charged 72 Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche and Caddo people with war crimes, acts in war that break the rules of war, or with being "ringleaders," the leaders of the fighting. General Philip Sheridan ordered them sent "to an eastern fort to be held without trial for an undefined time." They were 71 men and one woman, named Mochi. A girl of about nine, Black Horse's daughter, went with them. The trip from Fort Sill, in Indian Territory, took 26 days by wagon, train, steamship and oxcart. The poet Sidney Lanier saw them arrive "in leg-irons," chains locked around their ankles.

They were held at Fort Marion, in St. Augustine, Florida, from 1875 to April 1878. Spanish builders had begun the fort in 1672 as the Castillo de San Marcos. Lieutenant Richard Henry Pratt, who was in charge of them, took off their chains. Then he cut the prisoners' hair, put them in army uniforms, made them march in drills, taught them English and sent them out to work for others as laborers. Army officers kept them in an old fort without proper living quarters. In the Florida summer heat, many prisoners fell sick, and many were deeply sad and homesick. The writer Judith Lee Wilmot says the sickness, the sadness, the heat and the poor quarters together raised the number of prisoners who died. In June 1875 Mah-mante, a Kiowa leader, asked, "only give us our women and children." President Grant agreed, and then changed his mind. In 1877 Making Medicine, a Cheyenne man, wrote for the young prisoners: "We have lived in this old place two years. It is old and we are young. We want Washington to give us our wives and children, our fathers and mothers. We are willing to learn and want to work."

Pratt gave the prisoners pencils, ink, crayons, watercolor paint and paper. Twenty-six of them made drawings. They were younger Cheyenne, Arapaho and Kiowa men. Pratt sold their drawings and crafts. The artists included Making Medicine, Howling Wolf, Bear's Heart and Tichkematse, who were Cheyenne, and Zotom, Wohaw, Etahdleuh Doanmoe and Koba, who were Kiowa. White Bear was Arapaho. More than a thousand of their drawings survive in collections around the world.

Ten prisoners died at Fort Marion. For many years two graves in the St. Augustine National Cemetery, each holding six men, had stones that read only "Six Unknown Indians." Together the two graves hold twelve men. That is two more than the ten who died at the fort, and the records do not explain why. One of the ten, Grey Beard, is buried somewhere else, in Piney Grove Cemetery in Baldwin, Florida. Amy Larner Giroux, of the University of Central Florida's Center for Humanities and Digital Research, spent five years searching army records. A January 2023 article in UCF News states that she found the names of the ten who died: Grey Beard, Lean Bear, Big Moccasin, Starving Wolf, Spotted Elk and Heap of Birds, who were Cheyenne, Sun, Mah-mante and Ih-pa-yah, who were Kiowa, and Nad-a-with-t, who was Comanche. The National Cemetery Administration is the federal office that runs national cemeteries. In 2023 its officials said they would replace the two markers with headstones that give the prisoners' names.

After their release, Bear's Heart, Koba and Buffalo Meat died of tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs. Making Medicine became a deacon, a church minister, in the Episcopal Church in 1881. In 1879 Pratt founded the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, a boarding school for Native children. Staff there cut the children's hair, took away their clothes, gave them English names and forbade them to speak their own languages.

### Howling Wolf

> **Who:** A Southern Cheyenne man who lived through the Sand Creek Massacre at 15 and became a ledger artist while a prisoner at Fort Marion.
> **When and where:** Born about 1849. The southern Plains, and St. Augustine, Florida, from 1875 to 1878. Died in 1927.

Howling Wolf drew the attack he had lived through as a boy. His Cheyenne name was Ho-na-nist-to. He belonged to the band of the Cheyenne leader Black Kettle.

On November 29, 1864, Colonel John Chivington and the First Regiment of Colorado Volunteers attacked Black Kettle's camp at Sand Creek, in Colorado. Howling Wolf was 15. He and his father, Eagle Head, were in the camp. The counts of Cheyenne and Arapaho people killed there run from about 70 to about 230. Most of the dead were women, children and old people. Among his drawings is a picture of the massacre at Sand Creek.

In 1875 army officers sent Howling Wolf to Fort Marion with the other prisoners. There he served as a sergeant in charge of a guard company made up of prisoners. He drew his people's lives and wars.

He was released in 1878 and went home to the Cheyenne reservation in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. His eyesight began to fail, and he sailed to Boston for an operation on his eyes. He spoke out against the Dawes Act of 1887. Under that law, federal officials broke up land that each nation held together into small plots for single owners and sold what they called the "surplus." Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887 and about 48 million acres by 1934. Howling Wolf died in a car accident in 1927. He was on his way home to Oklahoma after performing in Houston, Texas, in a Wild West show, a traveling show of riding, shooting and scenes of frontier life.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters after the war" -->
Mary Cassatt showed her paintings in Paris with the Impressionists, a group of painters in France. She was born in Allegheny City, now part of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, on May 22, 1844. She studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts from 1861 to 1865 and went to Europe in 1866. In 1874 she settled in Paris. The French painter Edgar Degas admired her drawing. At his request she showed her work with the Impressionists in 1879, and again in 1880, 1881 and 1886. Britannica's writers count her as the only American who showed with them in those years. She died near Paris on June 14, 1926.

Thomas Eakins, born in Philadelphia on July 25, 1844, painted *The Gross Clinic* in 1875. He taught at the Pennsylvania Academy from 1876 and became head of its school in 1882. In 1886 he had to resign, and the record does not name the people who forced him out. During a class that women students attended, he had removed the cloth that covered a male model's private parts. Some of his students then formed their own school, the Art Students' League of Philadelphia, and he taught there. One of his students at the academy was Henry Ossawa Tanner. Eakins died on June 25, 1916.

### Mark Twain

> **Who:** A writer, born Samuel Langhorne Clemens, whose books were sold door to door.
> **When and where:** Born November 30, 1835, in Florida, Missouri. Died in Redding, Connecticut, on April 21, 1910.
> **Movie:** *Mark Twain* (2001), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about his whole life.

Mark Twain sold his books through salesmen who went from door to door. His real name was Samuel Langhorne Clemens. He was born in Florida, Missouri, on November 30, 1835.

His book *The Innocents Abroad* came out on July 20, 1869. Elisha Bliss's American Publishing Company sold it as a subscription book. A salesman came to people's homes and showed them the book. They ordered a copy, and it was delivered later. *The Innocents Abroad* sold more than 70,000 copies in its first year. While Twain lived, it sold better than any of his other books.

*The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* came out in 1876. *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* came out in Britain and Canada on December 10, 1884, and in the United States on February 18, 1885.

In 1885 the members of the library committee in Concord, Massachusetts, decided to keep *Huckleberry Finn* out of the town's public library. According to the *Boston Transcript*, one member of the committee called it "the veriest trash," which meant the worst kind of trash. Twain died in Redding, Connecticut, on April 21, 1910.

### Henry Ossawa Tanner

> **Who:** A painter who studied under Thomas Eakins and made his career in France.
> **When and where:** Born in Pittsburgh on June 21, 1859. Philadelphia. Paris from 1891. Died in Paris on May 25, 1937.

Henry Ossawa Tanner was a Black painter who left the United States to work in France. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, was a bishop, a high leader, of the African Methodist Episcopal Church. In 1909 Tanner wrote about looking for a painting teacher when he was a boy. "The question was not, would the desired teacher have a boy who knew nothing and had little money, but would he have me, or would he keep me after he found out who I was," he wrote.

From 1879 to 1885 he studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, on and off, under Thomas Eakins. Joseph Pennell, a fellow student, later wrote about a night when other students attacked Tanner. "One night his easel was carried out into the middle of Broad Street and, though not painfully crucified, he was firmly tied to it and left there," Pennell wrote. Crucified means fastened to a cross. Pennell did not name the students who did it.

In 1891 Tanner went to Paris to study at a school called the Académie Julian. A bishop from Atlanta, Joseph Crane Hartzell, and his wife paid for the trip by buying every painting in a show of Tanner's work. In Paris other American students mocked him. In 1893 he came home sick with typhoid fever, a disease spread by dirty water and food. In Philadelphia he painted *The Banjo Lesson*. Then he went back to Paris. Apart from short visits home, he lived there for the rest of his life.

His painting *The Resurrection of Lazarus* won a third-place medal at the Paris Salon, a large yearly art show, in 1897. French government officials then bought it. In 1923 he was made a member of the Legion of Honor, an order of people honored by France for their work. He died in Paris on May 25, 1937.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Shaw Memorial" -->
In August 1882 a committee of 21 people in Boston hired the sculptor Augustus Saint-Gaudens to make a memorial to Colonel Robert Gould Shaw and the men of the 54th Massachusetts. The regiment had attacked Fort Wagner, in South Carolina, in July 1863. Joshua Bowen Smith, a Black man in Boston who had worked for the Shaw family, is credited with first proposing it. The writer Lydia Maria Child wrote that "the colored soldiers who fell ought to have a monument also."

Saint-Gaudens worked on it for 14 years, longer than on anything else he made. He did not use photographs of the real soldiers or meet them. Instead he hired Black men he met on the street near his studio and made about 40 studies of their heads. Shaw's sister, Josephine Lowell, asked for the names of all the men of the 54th killed at Fort Wagner and afterward to be carved on the base. Her request failed, and the records do not say who turned it down. When the memorial went up, its base carried only the names of the white officers. The names of the Black soldiers were added in the early 1980s. The National Park Service account of the memorial does not say who added them.

The memorial was unveiled on Boston Common on May 31, 1897. Sixty-five veterans of the 54th led the parade, and Sergeant William Carney carried the flag.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dickinson's poems, and Riis's flash photographs" -->
Emily Dickinson of Amherst, Massachusetts, wrote nearly 1,800 poems. Only 10 were published while she was alive. She died on May 15, 1886. Her sister Lavinia found the poems after her death. Thomas Wentworth Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd chose and prepared the poems for the first book of them, which came out in 1890.

That same year Jacob Riis published *How the Other Half Lives*, about the tenements of New York, the crowded buildings where poor families rented rooms. To photograph the inside of the dark tenements, he used magnesium flash powder, which burns with a sudden bright light.
