## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 22 minutes**

City buyers paid for paintings of the Hudson valley and the Catskills in the same years that federal officials, the officials of the national government, forced Native nations off their land. From about 1825 a group of painters known as the Hudson River School made landscapes, pictures of the land and sky, of the Hudson River valley and the Catskill Mountains. The painter George Catlin travelled west and painted the people of about fifty nations.

Enslaved artists worked too. In South Carolina an enslaved potter named Dave began cutting his own verses into his jars in 1834, the year the state's lawmakers made it a crime for enslaved people to learn to read and write, and later he signed them "Dave." News of photography reached the United States in 1839. By the early 1850s, a small photograph in New York cost as little as 25 cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Black artists in Philadelphia and Baltimore" -->
Moses Williams, the son of the two people a planter gave Charles Willson Peale as payment for portraits, was held by Peale until about 1802. Then he cut silhouettes at Peale's museum in Philadelphia, portraits that show only the outline of a face from the side. Williams used a machine called a physiognotrace, which traced the outline of a sitter's face onto paper through a set of connected rods.

Peale's own children learned painting from their father. Karl Cole's account of Williams's life for Davis Art says Williams was not given the chance to paint. The Philadelphia Museum of Art holds silhouettes he cut of the Peale family between 1803 and 1805.

Joshua Johnson, the Maryland painter whose white father bought him and freed him in 1782, was listed as a portrait painter in the Baltimore city directories, books that listed the town's people and their trades, from 1796 to 1824.

**The Academy.** Peale, the sculptor William Rush and other artists and business leaders founded the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in Philadelphia in 1805. The academy's leaders very seldom let Black students in.

Robert Douglass Jr., a Black painter of Philadelphia who lived from 1809 to 1887, was one of the few. He trained at the academy and studied under the painter Thomas Sully. In 1834 he painted the portraits of William Lloyd Garrison and James Forten, two men who worked to end slavery. An article by the Library Company of Philadelphia describes him as Philadelphia's first African American photographer.

**Women at the Academy.** The academy's leaders let women in one step at a time. In 1844 they gave women artists the room of statues to draw in, on three mornings a week. In 1860 they let women take classes on the body's bones and muscles and draw from copies of ancient statues. They opened life classes, where students draw from a model without clothes, to women in 1868, with women as the models. Men became the models for these classes in 1874.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters on the road" -->
Many portrait painters of these years had taught themselves. They travelled from town to town and charged somewhat less than painters with training. A story told later says these painters painted bodies ahead of time and added the customer's face when they arrived. The unfinished portraits that survive show the opposite. They have the face done and the body not yet painted, so the painters began with the head.

**The "Border Limner."** Ammi Phillips travelled and painted portraits in Connecticut, Massachusetts and New York from the middle of the 1810s until the early 1860s. He was born in Colebrook, Connecticut, on April 24, 1788. For years nobody knew who had painted many of his pictures, and collectors called the unknown painter the "Border Limner," limner being an old word for a painter of portraits. The collectors Barbara and Larry Holdridge matched those paintings to Phillips.

In the mid-1830s, in Dutchess County, New York, Phillips painted *Girl in Red Dress with Cat and Dog*. It was bought for the American Folk Art Museum in 1985 for one million dollars. Phillips died on July 11, 1865.

**The Peaceable Kingdom.** Edward Hicks, born in Pennsylvania on April 4, 1780, became an apprentice, a young worker learning a trade by working for its masters, to coach makers at 13. From 1812 he was a minister of the Quakers, a Christian group.

He painted 62 versions of *The Peaceable Kingdom*, based on a passage in the book of Isaiah in the Bible. Many versions show William Penn meeting Native people, who stand for the Lenape, on whose land Penn founded his colony. In his own lifetime, Hicks was better known as a minister than as a painter. He died on August 23, 1849.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-whiting-stock" name="Joseph Whiting Stock" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Whiting Stock

> **Who:** A travelling portrait painter who could not walk. He kept a journal of the paintings he made and what he was paid for them.
> **When and where:** Born in Springfield, Massachusetts, on January 30, 1815. Painted in Massachusetts, Rhode Island and New York from 1832. Died in 1855, at the age of forty.

**An oxcart fell on him at eleven, and he painted 900 pictures.**

Joseph Whiting Stock painted more than 900 pictures, although he could not walk. When he was eleven, an oxcart turned over and fell on him. He was paralyzed from the waist down, which means he could not move or feel his legs. He could not leave his bed until he was nineteen.

**A doctor's idea.** A doctor suggested that he take up art. He began lessons at seventeen, with a painter named Franklin White, and tried a portrait of his sister Eliza. "I attempted my sister Eliza's portrait and made so good a likeness as to induce my friends to encourage me by their patronage," he wrote in his journal (patronage meant paying him for pictures).

**A wheelchair.** In 1834 a doctor named James Swan hired Stock to make drawings of the human body. Swan also built him a wheelchair. With it, Stock could paint large pictures and be lifted onto trains to travel for work.

His first painting trip was a month in Wilbraham, Massachusetts, in 1836. He later spent two years in Warren, Rhode Island, and New Bedford, Massachusetts, and several years in Orange County, New York. He lived with local families while he worked, and sometimes he paid for his room and meals with paintings.

His journal lists more than 900 paintings and what he was paid for them. About a hundred of those paintings are known today. He died of tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs, in 1855, at forty. The Springfield Museums hold his journal.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-whiting-stock" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hudson River School, and whose land it painted" -->
The Hudson River School was a group of American landscape painters who worked from about 1825 to 1870. They painted American land in careful, true-to-life detail, often with quiet farms next to the last of the wild forest. Many of them believed that God could be seen in the American landscape. Thomas Cole, Thomas Doughty and Asher B. Durand were the first leaders, and many of the group painted the Catskill Mountains, west of the Hudson River.

The Hudson valley was the homeland of the Mohican people. Cole made his home in the village of Catskill, New York, in 1826. That land is the homeland of the Muh-he-conneok, the People of the Waters That Are Never Still, who today are called the Stockbridge-Munsee Band of Mohican Indians. A marker at Cole's house states that they were forced from there.

**Stockbridge.** Through the 1600s and 1700s, before Cole was born, settlers pushed the Mohican out of the Hudson valley. Most moved to Stockbridge, Massachusetts, a mission town, which means a town built around a church where ministers taught Christianity. From 1764 white settlers poured into Stockbridge and took thousands of acres of Mohican land. They squatted on it, which means they settled on it without any right, and they bought it in ways the law did not allow. They also took land to pay off Mohican debts.

The Mohican had lost all their land in the town by 1783. In 1785 a group of them founded New Stockbridge in upstate New York, on land of the Oneida nation.

**To Wisconsin.** The Mohican were made to move again in the same years Cole began to paint the Catskills. Agents from New York and commissioners from the War Department arranged treaties, written agreements between governments. Under them, the Stockbridge, as the Mohican were by then called, and the Munsee had to leave New York for Wisconsin.

The moves began after an 1822 treaty, three years before Cole put his landscapes in a New York shop window. By 1831, five years after Cole settled in Catskill, 225 Stockbridge people and 100 Munsee people had gone. From 1832 to 1834, federal officials made them move once more, to the east shore of Lake Winnebago. Their reservation, land set aside for them by treaty, is in northeastern Wisconsin today.

**Removal.** While Cole was selling paintings of the Catskills, federal officials were moving Native nations west. President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could make treaties in which Native nations gave up their lands east of the Mississippi River for lands in the west.

Federal officials and soldiers forced more than 16,000 Cherokee people from their homeland to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma, in 1838 and 1839. At least 4,000 of them died of cold, hunger and disease along the way, by the usual count, and other counts are higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-cole" name="Thomas Cole" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Cole

> **Who:** A painter of landscapes and one of the first leaders of the Hudson River School.
> **When and where:** Born February 1, 1801, in Bolton-le-Moors, Lancashire, England. Came to America in 1818. Lived in Catskill, New York, from 1826. Died there on February 11, 1848.

**The cleared strips on the far hill seem to spell "Noah" in Hebrew.**

Thomas Cole was one of the first leaders of the Hudson River School. He came to America from England with his family in 1818, and they lived first in Philadelphia and then in Steubenville, Ohio. A travelling portrait painter named Stein gave him lessons. Then he studied for two years at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts.

**A shop window.** Cole put some landscapes in the window of a shop in New York City in 1825. The painters John Trumbull and Asher B. Durand saw them and bought them. The next year Cole moved to Catskill. He made pencil sketches outdoors, and in winter he painted in his studio. He also spent years painting in Europe, mostly in Italy, from 1829 to 1832 and again from 1841 to 1842.

**The Oxbow.** In 1836 he painted *The Oxbow*, a view from Mount Holyoke, near Northampton, Massachusetts. It shows the valley of the Connecticut River, not the Hudson, and Cole joined two views from the mountain into one picture. On the far hill, the bare strips where trees were cut seem to spell the Hebrew letters for "Noah." The art historian Matthew Baigell was the first to notice them.

Cole made the painting for a show in New York in April 1836. His patron, a person who pays an artist to make work, was Luman Reed, and Reed had suggested it. In March 1836 Cole wrote to Reed, "Fancy pictures seldom sell & they generally take more time than views so I have determined to paint one of the latter." By "fancy pictures" he meant pictures from his imagination, and by "views" he meant real places.

That same year he finished *The Course of Empire*, a set of five paintings. The Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York has owned *The Oxbow* since 1908.

Cole died in Catskill on February 11, 1848. Durand painted a picture called *Kindred Spirits* in his memory. Cole's house and studio in Catskill are now the Thomas Cole National Historic Site.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thomas-cole" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Writers" -->
Some writers of these years turned to the American land and to nature, as the painters did. Washington Irving set his 1819 story "Rip Van Winkle" in the Catskill Mountains of New York, the same mountains the Hudson River School painted a few years later. Ralph Waldo Emerson's long essay *Nature* appeared in 1836.

James Fenimore Cooper's novel *The Last of the Mohicans* came out in Philadelphia on February 4, 1826. The Mohican people named in its title were living in New York and Wisconsin that year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="George Catlin and the people he painted" -->
**By 1840:** Painters were selling views of the Catskills in New York City, and federal officials and soldiers had forced the Mohican, the Munsee and the Cherokee out of their homelands.

George Catlin was a lawyer who became a painter. He lived from 1796 to 1872. In 1830 he went to St. Louis to meet William Clark, the federal official in charge of Indian affairs there. From St. Louis, Catlin made five trips west between 1830 and 1836, and in 1832 he travelled more than 2,000 miles up the Missouri River.

He wrote, "If my life be spared, nothing shall stop me from visiting every nation of Indians on the Continent of North America." By one count he visited 48 nations, and by another, fifty.

**Four Bears.** At a Mandan village in what is now North Dakota, Catlin painted Four Bears, a Mandan leader, in 1832. His Mandan name was Máh-to-tóh-pa. Catlin called him "the first and most popular man in the nation." Four Bears was a painter too, and he painted his own battles on buffalo robes. Catlin got one of those robes in 1832, and it is now at the Smithsonian.

A German prince, Maximilian of Wied, travelled up the Missouri with the painter Karl Bodmer in 1833 and 1834. He took another Four Bears robe to Germany, where it is in the Linden Museum in Stuttgart.

Catlin sometimes got objects from the people he painted, as proof that his pictures were accurate. One was a Sioux cradle. In 1836 he went to the place in southwestern Minnesota where people dug red stone for pipes. Sioux elders protested strongly, and he went anyway.

**Smallpox.** Five years after Catlin painted the Mandan, smallpox killed almost all of them, Four Bears among them. Smallpox was a disease caused by a virus. It spread when people were close together, face to face. It caused a fever and a rash that turned into scabs, and about 3 of every 10 people who caught it died.

President Jackson signed the Indian Vaccination Act on May 5, 1832, a law to give Native people vaccinations. To vaccinate a person, a doctor scratched fluid from a sore of cowpox, a mild disease of cows, into the skin, and it protected the person against smallpox. Secretary of War Lewis Cass ran the program through federal Indian agents. He left the Mandan, the Hidatsa and the Arikara of the upper Missouri out of it.

The American Fur Company's steamboat *St. Peters* reached Fort Clark, next to the Mandan villages, on June 18, 1837. The fort's trader, Francis Chardon, learned from the captain, Bernard Pratte Jr., that people on board had smallpox. The crew unloaded goods and loaded furs in less than a day, and the boat went on up the river. It had brought smallpox to the Mandan villages. About 2,000 Mandan lived there that spring. By October, 138 were alive.

Four Bears caught smallpox and died on July 30, 1837. Chardon wrote down his last speech: "I have loved the Whites ... and how have they repaid it! With ingratitude!" (ingratitude means not being thankful).

**Osceola.** The Seminole leader Osceola sat for Catlin in January 1838 as a dying prisoner. General Thomas Jesup had ordered soldiers to seize Osceola near St. Augustine, Florida, on October 21, 1837, while he was under a white flag of truce, a sign that the two sides have stopped fighting to talk. Osceola was held at Fort Marion in St. Augustine and then at Fort Moultrie in South Carolina.

There he was very sick with malaria, a disease spread by mosquitoes that brings fever and chills, and with other illnesses. Three painters, Catlin, W. M. Laning and Robert John Curtis, talked him into letting them paint him anyway. Osceola died a prisoner on January 30, 1838. The army doctor, Frederick Weedon, cut off his head and then buried the body. Weedon kept the head and showed it in his drugstore.

**The Indian Gallery.** Catlin opened his paintings to the public at Clinton Hall in New York on September 23, 1837, at 50 cents a visit. He called the show his Indian Gallery. He tried to sell it to the United States government, and members of Congress did not buy it. In November 1839 he sailed for England with the gallery, a tepee of buffalo hide and two live bears. He showed the gallery in London, Brussels and Paris.

Groups of Native people who were touring Europe with promoters joined him there, 21 Ojibwe and 14 Iowa. Catlin talked them into acting out hunts, dances and even scalpings for the crowds. Scalping means cutting the skin and hair from the top of an enemy's head. In Paris in 1845, the Ojibwe group caught smallpox, and two of them died. The account that records their deaths does not name them.

**In debt.** Catlin ran out of money in 1852 and was put in a London prison for debt. Members of Congress still would not buy the gallery, even after he lowered his price from $65,000 to $25,000. Joseph Harrison, a Pennsylvania railroad owner, paid Catlin's debts and got the gallery for $20,000. He stored it in his boiler factory in Philadelphia. In 1879 Harrison's widow gave the paintings to the Smithsonian.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Audubon's birds, and the people he sold" -->
John James Audubon's *The Birds of America* was printed in England from 1827 to 1838. It has 435 pictures of birds, each bird shown at its real size.

**The people the Audubons sold.** The historian Gregory Nobles records that the Audubons held nine enslaved people in their household in Henderson, Kentucky, in the 1810s. By the end of that decade, short of money, they sold them. In early 1819 Audubon took two enslaved men down the Mississippi River to New Orleans on a small boat, and there he offered the boat and the two men for sale.

The family bought more enslaved people in the 1820s and sold them in 1830, when Audubon moved to England to oversee the printing of the birds.

Audubon wrote to his wife in 1834 about the freeing of the enslaved people in Britain's colonies in the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean. He wrote that the British government had "acted imprudently and too precipitously," which means without enough care and too fast. The surviving record counts the people Audubon held and sold. It does not give their names.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="dave-drake" name="Dave (David Drake)" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Dave (David Drake)

> **Who:** An enslaved potter and poet who cut his name and his own verses into large jars of hard-fired clay.
> **When and where:** Born about 1800 in South Carolina. Worked in the potteries of the Edgefield District from about 1818 to 1864. Listed in the 1870 census as "David Drake, Turner." Believed to have died in the 1870s.

**He cut verses into clay when learning to read was a crime.**

Dave was an enslaved potter who wrote his own verses on the jars he made. He is the only Black potter in Edgefield before the Civil War known to have signed his own work. He was born into slavery around 1800. In the Edgefield District of South Carolina, white factory owners made their potteries grow by using the unpaid work of enslaved men, women and children. At least 76 enslaved people worked at the district's 12 potteries.

**Mortgaged.** The first record of Dave is dated June 13, 1818. It describes "a boy about 17 years old country born," which meant born in America and not brought from Africa. His enslaver, Harvey Drake, mortgaged him to a man named Eldrid Simkins. That meant Drake used Dave to guarantee a loan: if Drake did not pay the money back, Simkins could take Dave.

**Passed from man to man.** At least five men held Dave in slavery during his life. Harvey Drake was the first, until he died in 1832. His brother Reuben Drake held Dave next, until 1835, and then the Reverend John Landrum. When Landrum died in 1846, all 18 of the people he enslaved were put up for sale. The records do not name who sold them. Franklin Landrum held Dave for a time. In 1849 Lewis Miles, who owned the Stony Bluff pottery, bought him.

**Words on a jar.** South Carolina's lawmakers made it a crime for enslaved people to learn to read and write in 1834. That same year, Dave cut words into a jar: "put every bit all between / surely this jar will hold 14." On the jar's other side he pressed fourteen dots. Arthur F. Goldberg and James Witkowski, two scholars who studied his pots, think such dots showed how many gallons a jar held, so the 14 means 14 gallons.

He wrote on another jar on July 31, 1840: "Dave belongs to Mr. Miles / wher the oven bakes and the pot biles." So by his own words, Lewis Miles held him in 1840, nine years before the 1849 sale. Accounts of his life do not agree on when Miles first held him or how.

**Not known:** how he learned to read, though he may have learned while he worked at a newspaper, the *Edgefield Hive*, printed by Harvey Drake's business partner Abner Landrum.

Dave shaped the bottom of each jar on a potter's wheel. Then he built up the top by hand with thick coils of clay. Two of his jars at the Charleston Museum each hold more than 40 gallons.

**What was taken from him.** His family was sold away from him. The records do not say who sold them or where they were taken. One of his legs was amputated, which means it was cut off.

**Not known:** how he lost the leg. One account says an enslaver beat him for writing, and another says he was hurt in a train accident. Neither has been proved.

He wrote three verses on his pots in 1857, eight in 1858 and seven in 1859. On August 16, 1857, he wrote: "I wonder where is all my relation / Friendship to all, and every nation."

His last known poem, from 1862, during the Civil War, reads: "I made this Jar all of cross, If you dont repent, you will be, lost." To repent means to be sorry for doing wrong and to change. Goldberg and Witkowski write that the poem carries political and religious meaning, because he wrote it in a time of slavery and civil war.

**Free, and counted.** In a survey of 2006, Goldberg and Witkowski counted 169 pots made by Dave or credited to him. More than a hundred, dated from 1849 to 1864, are signed "Dave." His verses and marks appear on 50 of them. Sometimes he added the names of other enslaved potters beside his own: Mark, Abram and Baddler. His last dated pot is from 1864.

Dave was free by the end of the Civil War in 1865. The Smithsonian American Art Museum credits his freedom to the Emancipation Proclamation, President Abraham Lincoln's order freeing enslaved people in the states at war with the United States. He took the last name Drake, probably from Harvey Drake, the first man who had held him.

The census of 1870, the government's count of everyone in the country, lists him as "David Drake," and his work as "turner," a person who shapes pots on a wheel. His name is not in the census of 1880. He is believed to have died in the 1870s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="dave-drake" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photography arrives, 1839" -->
The French artist Louis Daguerre worked out a way of making pictures with light, and news of his process reached the United States in late August and September 1839. In October or November 1839, Robert Cornelius of Philadelphia took a picture of himself. It is the oldest American portrait photograph that survives.

These first photographs, called daguerreotypes, were each made directly on a copper plate coated with silver. Each was a single picture, and it could not be copied.

**Studios on Broadway.** A history of New York's photographers, published by the Bard Graduate Center, says the first American studio that sold portrait photographs opened in New York in 1840. By 1850 New York had 71 daguerreotype studios, and by 1853 it had more of them than all of England. Mathew Brady opened his first studio at 205 Broadway in 1844.

Cheaper studios on Broadway and on the Bowery made portraits for working people. In the early 1850s, a very small daguerreotype, about 1 5/8 inches by 1 3/8 inches, cost 25 cents. A cheap portrait from a "picture factory," a shop that turned out many portraits fast, cost 50 cents. A medium-sized portrait at Brady's studio cost $2.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-douglass-art" name="Frederick Douglass" movie="Becoming Frederick Douglass (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A writer and speaker who escaped slavery. He wrote the story of his own life and sat for 160 known photographs.
> **When and where:** Born in Talbot County, Maryland, in February 1818. Escaped slavery on September 3, 1838. Lived in Massachusetts, then Rochester, New York, then Washington. Died in 1895.
> **Movie:** *Becoming Frederick Douglass* (2022), a documentary by Stanley Nelson, shown on PBS. It is about his life.

**He sat for 160 photographs, more than Lincoln's 126.**

By the count of the historians John Stauffer, Zoe Trodd and Celeste-Marie Bernier, Frederick Douglass sat for more photographs than any other American of the 1800s: 160 of them, against 126 of Abraham Lincoln. He was born into slavery in Maryland. He escaped on September 3, 1838, by train through Baltimore, dressed in a sailor's clothes. Anna Murray, a free Black woman, helped him, and he married her.

**The book.** His book came out from the Anti-Slavery Office in Boston on May 1, 1845. It was called *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave. Written by Himself*. By one count it sold 4,500 copies in four months, and by another 5,000. By 1860 almost 30,000 copies had sold.

**Pictures by white artists.** In 1849 Douglass wrote about pictures of Black people drawn by white artists. He wrote that "negroes can never have impartial portraits at the hands of white artists" (negroes was a word of that time for Black people, and impartial means fair). "It seems to us next to impossible for white men to take likenesses of black men, without most grossly exaggerating their distinctive features," he wrote.

Douglass sat for photographs again and again. He said that "the great cheapness and universality of pictures must exert a powerful, though silent, influence upon the ideas and sentiment of present and future generations." By universality he meant that pictures were everywhere.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 23 minutes**

Cameras and cheap printing put pictures and books in front of millions of Americans after 1850. Many Black and Native artists of the same years did their work far from home or as prisoners. Photographers showed Americans the bodies of soldiers killed in the Civil War, and the valleys and mountains of the far West. The first ten-cent novel, in 1860, sold more than 65,000 copies in a few months, and *Uncle Tom's Cabin* sold 300,000 in the United States in its first year.

The sculptor Edmonia Lewis said she was "practically driven to Rome" by prejudice against her color, and the painter Henry Ossawa Tanner made his career in Paris. At an army fort in Florida, Plains men held as prisoners without trial drew their lives in account books. In New York, the Metropolitan Museum of Art opened in 1872.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Writers and cheap books" -->
Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, a novel against slavery, sold more copies than any other book of the 1800s except the Bible. It first ran in parts in a newspaper, *The National Era*, from 1851, and came out as a book in 1852. In the United States it sold 10,000 copies in its first week and 300,000 in its first year.

Herman Melville's *Moby-Dick* sold far fewer. The novel, about a whaling captain who hunts the giant white whale that bit off his leg, came out in 1851, and its first printing in New York was 2,915 copies. It was out of print when Melville died in 1891. Readers began to call it a great American novel in the 1900s.

Walt Whitman paid to print his own book of poems, *Leaves of Grass*, on July 4, 1855. The first edition held twelve poems. Whitman kept adding to it and changing it until he died in 1892.

**Dime novels.** The brothers Erastus and Irwin Beadle began selling novels of about 100 pages for ten cents each in 1860. People called them dime novels, because a dime is ten cents. The first, Ann S. Stephens's *Malaeska, the Indian Wife of the White Hunter*, sold more than 65,000 copies in its first few months. The Beadles printed 321 of these books in the series.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Robert S. Duncanson in Cincinnati" -->
Robert S. Duncanson was a free Black painter who had taught himself to paint. He was born in about 1821 in Fayette, New York, the son of a free Black tradesman. In 1840 he moved to Cincinnati, Ohio, where about 3,000 Black people lived.

**Belmont.** Nicholas Longworth hired Duncanson in about 1850 or 1851 to paint eight landscapes on the walls of the entrance hall of his house, Belmont. Each is about nine feet high. The house is now the Taft Museum of Art. In 1853 Duncanson painted *Uncle Tom and Little Eva*, from Stowe's novel, which is at the Detroit Institute of Arts.

During the Civil War he left the United States. He showed his work in Montreal, Canada, in 1864 and in Dublin, Ireland, in 1865. He died in Detroit on December 21, 1872.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Photographs of the Civil War dead" -->
Alexander Gardner, born in Paisley, Scotland, in 1821, worked for the New York photographer Mathew Brady from 1856, and in 1858 Brady put him in charge of his studio in Washington. In September 1862 Gardner and his team photographed the dead soldiers on the battlefield at Antietam, in Maryland. The American Battlefield Trust estimates that 22,717 soldiers were killed, wounded or missing in that battle.

**"The Dead of Antietam."** Brady showed the photographs at his gallery in New York in October 1862, under a sign that read "The Dead of Antietam." A writer for *The New York Times* reported on October 20 that crowds kept climbing the stairs to see them. "If he has not brought bodies and laid them in our door-yards and along the streets, he has done something very like it," the writer said (door-yards are the yards in front of houses). The article gave Brady the credit.

**Whose name went on the picture.** Brady put the words "Photographed by Brady" on pictures his employees took. In about November 1862 Gardner stopped working for Brady, probably in part because of this. In May 1863 Gardner and his brother James opened their own studio in Washington and hired many of Brady's former workers.

Gardner published a book of 100 of the war's photographs in two volumes in 1866. Under each picture he printed the name of the man who took it, among them Timothy H. O'Sullivan and James F. Gibson. The book did not sell well.

**A body moved for the camera.** After the Battle of Gettysburg, on July 6, 1863, Gardner's team photographed a dead soldier among the rocks at a place called Devil's Den. Gardner later called him a Confederate, a soldier of the Southern states that fought the United States to keep slavery. He titled the picture *Home of a Rebel Sharpshooter*, a sharpshooter being a soldier who shoots at a distance with a rifle.

The historian William A. Frassanito showed in 1975 that the same body appears in six photographs. The photographers had carried the body from the south edge of Devil's Den to a stone wall, and the rifle beside it was probably put there for the picture. In 1998 the artist James Groves argued that the body was carried the other way, out of the wall and to the first spot.

**Known:** the photographers moved the body, which both men agree on.
**Not known:** the soldier's name.

### Winslow Homer

> **Who:** A painter who drew the Civil War for a magazine and later painted the sea.
> **When and where:** Born in Boston on February 24, 1836. Drew the Civil War for *Harper's Weekly*. Lived in England from 1881 and in Prouts Neck, Maine, from 1883. Died there on September 29, 1910.

**He sketched the Civil War at the front for *Harper's Weekly*.**

Winslow Homer drew the Civil War at the front for *Harper's Weekly*, a magazine, and he drew everyday life in the soldiers' camps. He began as an apprentice at a lithography company, which printed pictures from drawings made on stone. Then he drew pictures for magazines, among them *Ballou's Pictorial* and *Harper's Weekly*.

**The sea.** He began painting in watercolor in 1873. In 1881 he went to live in Cullercoats, a town in England. In 1883 he moved to Prouts Neck, on the coast of Maine, and the sea became his main subject. He painted people at sea, in pictures such as *The Life Line* and *Fog Warning*. He died at Prouts Neck on September 29, 1910.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edmonia-lewis" name="Edmonia Lewis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Edmonia Lewis

> **Who:** A sculptor of Mississauga Ojibwe and African American descent, who carved in marble in Rome after she was beaten and falsely charged at college in Ohio.
> **When and where:** Born about July 4, 1844, in Greenbush, New York. Oberlin, Ohio, from 1859. Boston. Rome from 1865 or 1866. Died in London on September 17, 1907.

**She sold 100 plaster copies of a bust to pay her way to Rome.**

Edmonia Lewis carved statues in marble in Rome. Before that, at college in Ohio, a group of white men beat her, officials charged her with poisoning two classmates, and college officials kept her from graduating.

She was born in Greenbush, New York, in about 1844. On official papers she gave her birth year as 1842, 1844 and 1854 at different times. Her mother, Catherine Mike Lewis, was of Mississauga Ojibwe and African American descent. Edmonia's name at birth was Wildfire. By the age of nine she had lost both parents. As a girl she and her aunts sold Ojibwe baskets, moccasins and embroidered blouses to tourists at Niagara Falls. Her brother Samuel paid for her schooling with money he made in the gold rush.

**Oberlin.** From 1859 she studied at Oberlin, in Ohio. In the winter of 1862, she gave spiced wine to two white students she lived with, Maria Miles and Christina Ennes, before a sleigh ride. They fell sick. Doctors said they had been poisoned with cantharides, a poison made from blister beetles. Both young women got well.

**Beaten.** One night soon after, Lewis was walking home alone. A group of white men dragged her into an open field, beat her badly and left her for dead. No one ever identified the men who did it. A few days later, officials arrested her for poisoning the two students. She was hurt so badly she could not walk, so her trial was put off.

John Mercer Langston, the first Black lawyer in Ohio, defended her. The court dropped the charges, because no one had tested what was in the young women's stomachs, so there was no proof of poison.

About a year later she was accused of stealing art supplies. The record does not name who accused her. That case was dropped too. But Oberlin officials did not let her sign up for her last term, so she could not graduate. In 2022, more than a hundred years after her death, the leaders of Oberlin College gave her a degree.

**The bust.** She went to Boston and made a bust, a sculpture of a person's head and shoulders, of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw. Shaw had led the 54th Massachusetts, a regiment of Black soldiers, in the Civil War. Lewis sold 100 plaster copies of the bust for $15 each, and the money paid her way to Rome.

Her passport of 1865 says she was "sent by subscription to Italy having displayed great talents as a sculptor" (sent by subscription means that a group of people paid for the trip). In Rome she carved her work in marble.

She told *The New York Times* in 1878: "I was practically driven to Rome in order to obtain the opportunities for art culture, and to find a social atmosphere where I was not constantly reminded of my color. The land of liberty had no room for a colored sculptor." (Colored was a word of that time for Black people.)

**Cleopatra.** Her marble statue *The Death of Cleopatra* was shown at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. In 1878 the critic William J. Clark called the way it showed death "ghastly" and "absolutely repellant," which means disgusting. Later a gambler named "Blind John" Condon bought it from a saloon in Chicago. He used it to mark the grave of a racehorse named Cleopatra.

The Smithsonian American Art Museum's record of the statue states that it was thought lost for almost a century. It was later moved to a construction yard in Cicero, Illinois. The scholars Marilyn Richardson and Dorothy Porter Wesley told George Gurney, a curator at the Smithsonian, one of the museum workers who choose and care for its art, about it. In 1994 the Historical Society of Forest Park, Illinois, gave it to the Smithsonian American Art Museum.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pictures of the West, and whose land they showed" -->
**By 1866:** Americans had seen photographs of the dead at Antietam and Gettysburg, and Gardner had printed the name of each man who took them under the war's photographs.

Carleton Watkins carried a very large camera into Yosemite Valley, in California, in July 1861. It took pictures on glass plates 18 inches by 22 inches. He came back with 30 of these large plates and 100 smaller negatives. His photographs were shown at a gallery in New York in 1862, and they helped persuade members of Congress to protect the valley. President Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant on June 30, 1864.

**The Ahwahneechee.** Yosemite Valley was the home of the Ahwahneechee people. California's governor, John McDougal, raised a force of about 200 armed volunteers on horseback in 1851, called the Mariposa Battalion. Under Major James D. Savage, they invaded the valley in March 1851. According to the National Park Service, the battalion's men burned villages and food supplies, one after another, and forced men, women and children away from their homes.

They marched Tenaya, the leader of the Ahwahneechee, and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River. The National Park Service counts that by 1910, more than 90 out of every 100 of the Ahwahneechee who first lived there were dead or missing.

**Yellowstone.** The photographer William Henry Jackson and the painter Thomas Moran went to Yellowstone with the Hayden survey, a government expedition, in 1871. Jackson took the first known photographs of the Yellowstone falls. When President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law on March 1, 1872, Yellowstone became a national park. That year members of Congress paid Moran $10,000 for his painting *The Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone* and hung it in the Capitol. It shows four small figures in front, and one of them is a Native man.

The Tukudika, a band of Mountain Shoshone, had lived in the Yellowstone area for thousands of years. According to the National Park Service, they were forced onto reservations. The Park Service account does not say who first forced them. In the fall of 1879, the park's superintendent, Philetus W. Norris, found one of their camps. He asked the government agent at Fort Washakie for help. The agent sent a party of Shoshone men to take the Tukudika to the Wind River Reservation.

Timothy O'Sullivan, who had photographed the war, later photographed for the Wheeler survey, an army survey of the West, from 1872 to 1879. The army's orders for that survey included a duty to "discover the numbers, habits, and disposition of Indians." Disposition here meant whether they were likely to be friendly or to fight.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why was the Met closed on Sundays?" -->
Many of the founders of the Metropolitan Museum of Art were Protestants, Christians outside the Catholic Church, who refused to open the museum on Sundays. Sunday was the one day off that most working New Yorkers had.

New York's lawmakers approved the founding of the museum on April 13, 1870. It opened on February 20, 1872, on Fifth Avenue, and moved to Central Park in 1880. City officials demanded Sunday opening. In 1885 officials of the city's parks department threatened to cut the museum's money if it stayed closed on Sundays. The trustees, the people in charge of the museum, agreed in the end, although they did not want to. The museum first opened on a Sunday on May 31, 1891, after nearly twenty years of argument.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort Marion: drawings by Plains prisoners" -->
Plains artists had long painted their deeds on buffalo hides. About 30 million buffalo lived on the Plains in the early 1800s, and fewer than 1,000 were left by 1900. Hunters killed them for their hides, and army officers encouraged the killing, because the herds fed the Plains nations. Disease and dry years also cut the herds, and so did the railroads built across the Plains.

With the buffalo gone, Plains artists drew on paper instead, often in ledgers, lined account books meant for keeping records of money. This is now called ledger art. Prisoners held at an army fort in Florida made many ledger drawings.

**Held without trial.** US Army soldiers fought Comanche, Kiowa, Cheyenne and other Plains people on the southern Plains in 1874 and 1875, in what is called the Red River War. After it, army officers charged 72 Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche and Caddo people with war crimes, acts in war that break the rules of war, or with being "ringleaders," the leaders of the fighting. General Philip Sheridan ordered them sent "to an eastern fort to be held without trial for an undefined time."

They were 71 men and one woman, named Mochi. A girl of about nine, Black Horse's daughter, went with them. The trip from Fort Sill, in Indian Territory, took 26 days by wagon, train, steamship and oxcart. The poet Sidney Lanier saw them arrive "in leg-irons," chains locked around their ankles.

**Fort Marion.** They were held at Fort Marion, in St. Augustine, Florida, from 1875 to April 1878. Spanish builders had begun the fort in 1672 as the Castillo de San Marcos. Lieutenant Richard Henry Pratt, who was in charge of them, took off their chains. Then he cut the prisoners' hair, put them in army uniforms, made them march in drills, taught them English and sent them out to work for others as laborers.

Army officers kept them in an old fort without proper living quarters. In the Florida summer heat, many prisoners fell sick, and many were deeply sad and homesick. The writer Judith Lee Wilmot says the sickness, the sadness, the heat and the poor quarters together raised the number of prisoners who died.

**"Only give us our women and children."** Mah-mante, a Kiowa leader, asked for that in June 1875. President Grant agreed, and then changed his mind. In 1877 Making Medicine, a Cheyenne man, wrote for the young prisoners: "We have lived in this old place two years. It is old and we are young." He went on: "We want Washington to give us our wives and children, our fathers and mothers. We are willing to learn and want to work."

**The drawings.** Pratt gave the prisoners pencils, ink, crayons, watercolor paint and paper. Twenty-six of them made drawings, all younger Cheyenne, Arapaho and Kiowa men, and Pratt sold their drawings and crafts. The artists included Making Medicine, Howling Wolf, Bear's Heart and Tichkematse, who were Cheyenne, and Zotom, Wohaw, Etahdleuh Doanmoe and Koba, who were Kiowa. White Bear was Arapaho. More than a thousand of their drawings survive in collections around the world.

**The dead.** Ten of the prisoners died in Florida. Amy Larner Giroux, of the University of Central Florida's Center for Humanities and Digital Research, spent five years searching army records for their names. A January 2023 article in UCF News states that she found all ten.

Six of the dead were Cheyenne: Grey Beard, Lean Bear, Big Moccasin, Starving Wolf, Spotted Elk and Heap of Birds. Three were Kiowa: Sun, Mah-mante and Ih-pa-yah. The tenth, Nad-a-with-t, was Comanche.

Grey Beard died on the way to the fort. He jumped out of a window of the train that was taking the prisoners to St. Augustine. The guards had orders to catch him again, but he was shot instead, and the UCF article does not say who fired. He is buried in Piney Grove Cemetery in Baldwin, Florida.

For many years two graves in the St. Augustine National Cemetery, each holding six men, had stones that read only "Six Unknown Indians." That makes twelve men, three more than the nine prisoners who died at the fort. The UCF article does not say who the twelve men are.

Officials of the National Cemetery Administration, the federal office that runs national cemeteries, said in 2023 that they would replace the two markers with headstones that give the prisoners' names.

After their release, Bear's Heart, Koba and Buffalo Meat died of tuberculosis. Making Medicine became a deacon, a church minister, in the Episcopal Church in 1881. Pratt founded the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania, a boarding school for Native children, in 1879. Staff there cut the children's hair, took away their clothes, gave them English names and forbade them to speak their own languages.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="howling-wolf" name="Howling Wolf" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Howling Wolf

> **Who:** A Southern Cheyenne man who lived through the Sand Creek Massacre at 15 and became a ledger artist while a prisoner at Fort Marion.
> **When and where:** Born about 1849. The southern Plains, and St. Augustine, Florida, from 1875 to 1878. Died in 1927.

**He drew the massacre at Sand Creek, which he lived through at 15.**

Howling Wolf drew the attack he had lived through as a boy. His Cheyenne name was Ho-na-nist-to. He belonged to the band of the Cheyenne leader Black Kettle.

**Sand Creek.** Colonel John Chivington and the First Regiment of Colorado Volunteers attacked Black Kettle's camp at Sand Creek, in Colorado, on November 29, 1864. Howling Wolf was 15. He and his father, Eagle Head, were in the camp. The counts of Cheyenne and Arapaho people killed there run from about 70 to about 230. Most of the dead were women, children and old people. Among Howling Wolf's drawings is a picture of the massacre.

Army officers sent Howling Wolf to Fort Marion with the other prisoners in 1875. There he served as a sergeant in charge of a guard company made up of prisoners. He drew his people's lives and wars.

**Home.** He was released in 1878 and went home to the Cheyenne reservation in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. His eyesight began to fail, and he sailed to Boston for an operation on his eyes.

He spoke out against the Dawes Act of 1887. Under that law, federal officials broke up land that each nation held together into small plots for single owners and sold what they called the "surplus." Native nations held about 138 million acres in 1887 and about 48 million acres by 1934, a loss of about 90 million acres.

Howling Wolf died in a car accident in 1927. He was on his way home to Oklahoma after performing in Houston, Texas, in a Wild West show, a traveling show of riding, shooting and scenes of frontier life.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Painters after the war" -->
Mary Cassatt showed her paintings in Paris with the Impressionists, a group of painters in France. She was born in Allegheny City, now part of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, on May 22, 1844. She studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts from 1861 to 1865, went to Europe in 1866 and settled in Paris in 1874.

The French painter Edgar Degas admired her drawing. At his request she showed her work with the Impressionists in 1879, and again in 1880, 1881 and 1886. Britannica's writers count her as the only American who showed with them in those years. She died near Paris on June 14, 1926.

**Forced out.** Thomas Eakins, born in Philadelphia on July 25, 1844, painted *The Gross Clinic* in 1875. He taught at the Pennsylvania Academy from 1876 and became head of its school in 1882.

During a class that women students attended, he removed the cloth that covered a male model's private parts. In 1886 he had to resign, and the record does not name the people who forced him out. Some of his students then formed their own school, the Art Students' League of Philadelphia, and he taught there. One of his students at the academy was Henry Ossawa Tanner. Eakins died on June 25, 1916.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mark-twain-art" name="Mark Twain" movie="Mark Twain (2001)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Mark Twain

> **Who:** A writer, born Samuel Langhorne Clemens, whose books were sold door to door.
> **When and where:** Born November 30, 1835, in Florida, Missouri. Died in Redding, Connecticut, on April 21, 1910.
> **Movie:** *Mark Twain* (2001), a documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about his whole life.

**Salesmen going door to door sold 70,000 copies in one year.**

Mark Twain sold his books through salesmen who went from door to door. His real name was Samuel Langhorne Clemens.

**A subscription book.** His book *The Innocents Abroad* came out on July 20, 1869. Elisha Bliss's American Publishing Company sold it as a subscription book. A salesman came to people's homes and showed them the book. They ordered a copy, and it was delivered later. *The Innocents Abroad* sold more than 70,000 copies in its first year. While Twain lived, it sold better than any of his other books.

*The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* came out in 1876. *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* came out in Britain and Canada on December 10, 1884, and in the United States on February 18, 1885.

**Kept off the shelf.** In 1885 the members of the library committee in Concord, Massachusetts, decided to keep *Huckleberry Finn* out of the town's public library. According to the *Boston Transcript*, one member of the committee called it "the veriest trash," which meant the worst kind of trash.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ossawa-tanner" name="Henry Ossawa Tanner" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ossawa Tanner

> **Who:** A Black painter who studied under Thomas Eakins and made his career in France.
> **When and where:** Born in Pittsburgh on June 21, 1859. Philadelphia. Paris from 1891. Died in Paris on May 25, 1937.

**Students tied him to his easel in the middle of Broad Street.**

Henry Ossawa Tanner was a Black painter who left the United States to work in France. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, was a bishop, a high leader, of the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

Tanner wrote in 1909 about looking for a painting teacher when he was a boy. "The question was not, would the desired teacher have a boy who knew nothing and had little money, but would he have me, or would he keep me after he found out who I was," he wrote. He meant that a teacher might turn him away, or send him away later, because he was Black.

**The academy.** From 1879 to 1885 he studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, on and off, under Thomas Eakins. Joseph Pennell, a fellow student, later wrote about a night when other students attacked Tanner. "One night his easel was carried out into the middle of Broad Street and, though not painfully crucified, he was firmly tied to it and left there," Pennell wrote (crucified means fastened to a cross). Pennell did not name the students who did it.

**Paris.** Tanner went to Paris in 1891 to study at a school called the Académie Julian. A bishop from Atlanta, Joseph Crane Hartzell, and his wife paid for the trip by buying every painting in a show of Tanner's work. In Paris other American students mocked him. In 1893 he came home sick with typhoid fever, a disease spread by dirty water and food. In Philadelphia he painted *The Banjo Lesson*. Then he went back to Paris. Apart from short visits home, he lived there for the rest of his life.

His painting *The Resurrection of Lazarus* won a third-place medal at the Paris Salon, a large yearly art show, in 1897, and French government officials then bought it. In 1923 he was made a member of the Legion of Honor, an order of people honored by France for their work.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ossawa-tanner" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Shaw Memorial" -->
A committee of 21 people in Boston hired the sculptor Augustus Saint-Gaudens in August 1882 to make a memorial to Colonel Robert Gould Shaw and the men of the 54th Massachusetts. The regiment had attacked Fort Wagner, in South Carolina, in July 1863. Joshua Bowen Smith, a Black man in Boston who had worked for the Shaw family, is credited with first proposing it. The writer Lydia Maria Child wrote that "the colored soldiers who fell ought to have a monument also."

**Fourteen years.** Saint-Gaudens worked on it for 14 years, longer than on anything else he made. He did not use photographs of the real soldiers or meet them. Instead he hired Black men he met on the street near his studio and made about 40 studies of their heads.

**Whose names.** Shaw's sister, Josephine Lowell, asked for the names of all the men of the 54th killed at Fort Wagner and afterward to be carved on the base. Her request failed, and the records do not say who turned it down. When the memorial went up, its base carried only the names of the white officers. The names of the Black soldiers were added in the early 1980s. The National Park Service account of the memorial does not say who added them.

The memorial was unveiled on Boston Common on May 31, 1897. Sixty-five veterans of the 54th led the parade, and Sergeant William Carney carried the flag.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dickinson's poems, and Riis's flash photographs" -->
Emily Dickinson of Amherst, Massachusetts, wrote nearly 1,800 poems. Only 10 were published while she was alive. She died on May 15, 1886, and her sister Lavinia found the poems after her death. Thomas Wentworth Higginson and Mabel Loomis Todd chose and prepared the poems for the first book of them, which came out in 1890.

That same year Jacob Riis published *How the Other Half Lives*, about the tenements of New York, the crowded buildings where poor families rented rooms. To photograph the inside of the dark tenements, he used magnesium flash powder, which burns with a sudden bright light.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** By 1900 photographs and cheap books reached millions of Americans, and the Black and Native artists of the time had worked in Rome and Paris or drawn as prisoners in Florida.

**In 1850:** a photograph was a single picture on a silvered copper plate that could not be copied, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art did not yet exist.
**In 1900:** the Met had been open on Sundays since 1891, Gardner's war photographs were in print with each photographer's name, and Jacob Riis had photographed the inside of New York's tenements by flash.

**Next:** In the next fifty years, a show of modern European paintings draws jokes in New York, and federal officials begin paying artists a weekly wage.
