## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
No company existed in this land before 1500. A company is a group of people who put their money together to run a business and share what it earns. The kind of company that outlives its first owners, called a corporation, needs a written grant from a government. No such grant had been written here.

Native nations did trade goods over long distances. Copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf of Mexico and turquoise from the Southwest have been found hundreds of miles from where they came from. People at Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, traded stone hoe blades that have turned up more than 400 miles away. That trade passed between nations, towns and families. None of it was organized as a company with owners and shares.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="before-1500" -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Europeans who reached this coast in the 1500s came on expeditions paid for by kings and queens. No business owned by private investors traded here during that century. The kind of company that later founded colonies here was being worked out in England at the same time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The joint-stock company, an English idea" -->
In the 1500s, merchants in London began to form joint-stock companies. In a joint-stock company, many investors each buy a share. A share is one part of the ownership. Each investor gets part of the profit, in proportion to the shares he owns. The money he paid in is called stock.

In 1555 the navigator Sebastian Cabot and a group of London merchants formed the Muscovy Company. The company held a monopoly on English trade with Russia. A monopoly is the right to be the only seller of something, or the only trader in a place. Earlier investors had taken their money back after each voyage. The Muscovy Company kept its investors' money in the business from one voyage to the next. It was the first English joint-stock company to work that way.

The Levant Company was founded on 11 September 1592, when the Venice Company and the Turkey Company joined together. It traded in the eastern Mediterranean Sea. Neither company traded in North America. The English East India Company received its charter on 31 December 1600. A charter is the written grant from a government that creates a company and lists its rights.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Companies owned by investors founded the first lasting English colonies in this land. Investors bought shares, a king signed a charter, and the shareholders expected the colony to earn them a profit. Under the charters, the companies claimed land where Native nations already lived. Most of these companies lost money. The Virginia Company and the Massachusetts Bay Company both lost their charters.

Other companies made their profit from people. The Dutch West India Company owned the enslaved men who dug the trench for a wall at New Amsterdam. From 1672, ships paid for by the Royal African Company carried more enslaved Africans across the ocean than those of any other single business.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Virginia Company, a colony run as a business" -->
In 1606 King James I of England gave a charter to the Virginia Company of London. It allowed the company to plant a colony in North America. The company's settlers founded Jamestown on 13 May 1607. They built it inside Tsenacomoco, the land of the Powhatan chiefdom. A chiefdom is a group of towns under one leading chief.

A share in the company cost 12 pounds and 10 shillings, and it lasted seven years. The first sale of shares may have raised as much as 10,000 pounds. In 1616 the company paid its shareholders in land, because it had no money to pay them with.

Under a second charter, dated 23 May 1609, the king gave up some of his power over the company. From then on the investors elected a treasurer to run it. Under a third charter, dated 12 March 1612, the company's lands took in the island of Bermuda, and the company could run lotteries. A lottery is a game in which people buy tickets and a few tickets win prizes. By May 1620 lotteries had brought in 7,000 pounds of the company's 9,831 pounds in cash for the year. In February 1621 members of the House of Commons, part of England's Parliament, attacked the lotteries in a debate. King James I banned them soon after.

Thousands of the settlers the company sent to Virginia died there. In 1619 about 700 settlers lived in the colony. Over the next three years the company shipped 3,570 more men, women and children there, for a total of 4,270. On 22 March 1622 an alliance of Virginia Indians led by Opechancanough attacked the English settlements. Opechancanough was the leading chief of the Powhatan. The English had been spreading onto Powhatan land to grow tobacco. The attackers killed as many as 347 colonists.

After the attack, 1,240 settlers were left alive. An investor named Samuel Wrote asked what had happened to the other 2,683 people. Historians have since found that most of them died of disease.

In May 1623 King James I's advisers on his Privy Council, the king's inner council, set up a royal commission to look into the company's business. On 24 May 1624 the king took away the company's charter. From then on Virginia was a royal colony, run by officials the king chose.

In 1619, while the company ran Virginia, a ship called the *Treasurer* brought a stolen cargo of enslaved Africans to the colony. That same year about 20 Africans arrived at Jamestown. They had been bought from Dutch privateers. A privateer was a ship's captain with his government's permission to attack and rob enemy ships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="edwin-sandys" name="Sir Edwin Sandys" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sir Edwin Sandys

> **Who:** The treasurer who ran the Virginia Company in its last five years.
> **When and where:** London, 1619 to 1624.

Sir Edwin Sandys ran the Virginia Company in the years when most of its settlers died. The company's investors elected him treasurer on 28 April 1619.

Under Sandys, the company used a plan called the headright to draw settlers. The company promised 50 acres of land to anyone who paid a person's fare to Virginia. The settler then paid the company a quitrent of one shilling a year. A quitrent is a small yearly rent paid on land. The land handed out this way was Powhatan land.

The company shipped settlers faster than the colony could feed them or house them. Of the 4,270 people counted in the colony from 1619 to 1622, 1,240 were still alive at the end of that time. Historians, among them Edmund S. Morgan in a book published in 1975, have written about these years. In the words of Encyclopedia Virginia, which sums up their work, "any responsible monarch would have been obliged to stop the reckless shipment of his subjects to their deaths." A monarch is a king or a queen. King James I took the company's charter away in 1624.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="edwin-sandys" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dutch West India Company" -->
On 3 June 1621, members of the States General, the lawmaking assembly of the Netherlands, gave a charter to the Dutch West India Company. For 24 years, no one else from the Netherlands could sail to or trade with the coast of Africa south of the Tropic of Cancer or with the Americas.

In 1624 men working for the company built a trading post called Fort Orange, where Albany, New York, stands today. It was the first lasting Dutch settlement in New Netherland, the colony along the Hudson River. The upper Hudson was the country of the Mohican people. The company's settlers then moved onto Manhattan Island, the home of the Lenape people. Company officials said they bought Manhattan in 1626 for trade goods worth 60 guilders, which were Dutch coins. Historians say the Dutch most likely misunderstood what the Lenape meant by that exchange.

The company owned people as well as ships and forts. In 1653 men whom the company enslaved dug the trench for a wall at New Amsterdam, the town on Manhattan. Three of them are known by name: Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo and Anthony Portuguese.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Massachusetts Bay Company" -->
The colony of Massachusetts began as a trading company, and its charter became the colony's government. King Charles I signed the charter of the Massachusetts Bay Company in March 1629. In late August 1629, twelve of its shareholders signed a paper called the Cambridge Agreement. Among them were John Winthrop, Richard Saltonstall and Thomas Dudley. They promised to sail to New England if the company's charter and its government moved there with them.

In 1630 Winthrop and about 1,000 Puritan settlers arrived at the Shawmut Peninsula. The land around it belonged to the Massachusett people, whose leading chief was Chickataubut. According to the West End Museum in Boston, an epidemic from 1616 to 1618 had killed about 75,000 of the roughly 100,000 people in the region. An epidemic is a disease that spreads quickly through many people. Winthrop renamed the peninsula Boston. In 1633 Chickataubut signed a treaty with Winthrop that gave up his rights to the peninsula. He died of smallpox that same year.

On 23 October 1684 an English court cancelled the company's charter. The charges were that the colonists traded with other countries against English trade laws, made their own coins without the king's picture on them, and passed laws against Quakers and members of the Church of England.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hudson's Bay Company" -->
King Charles II gave a charter to the Hudson's Bay Company on 2 May 1670. His cousin Prince Rupert had persuaded him, along with a group of merchants and noblemen, to back it. The charter gave the company the only right to trade in all the lands whose rivers drained into Hudson Bay, in what is now Canada.

The Hudson's Bay Company was British. Its traders reached what is now the United States much later. In 1825 its officers opened Fort Vancouver on the Columbia River, in what is now Washington state. Chinookan people had lived along that river for thousands of years. They called the place where the fort stood Skatcutxat, which means "Mud Turtles."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Royal African Company" -->
The Royal African Company's ships carried more enslaved Africans to the Americas than the ships of any other single business. King Charles II gave it a charter in 1672, and his brother James, Duke of York, led it. It held the only English right to trade with West Africa. That trade included gold, silver and enslaved people. James later became King James II.

Between 1672 and 1731, ships that the company paid for carried 186,748 enslaved people on 652 voyages to English colonies in the Americas. Of those people, 16,077 died on the way. The count comes from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, a record of known slave voyages. From 1680 to 1686, the company's ships carried about 5,000 people a year. Most of them went to English colonies in the Caribbean and to Virginia.

Thousands of the people on the company's ships arrived branded. To brand a person means to press a red-hot metal stamp against the skin. The heat burns the letters into the flesh and leaves a raised scar for life. Many people arrived with the letters DoY, for Duke of York, burned into their chests. Others carried the letters RAC. The records do not name the men who held the irons.

The historian William Pettigrew wrote in his 2013 book *Freedom's Debt* that the company "shipped more enslaved African women, men and children to the Americas than any other single institution during the entire period of the transatlantic slave trade." He also wrote that its investors knew what the company did and meant to make money from it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1700 to 1750 the largest businesses in the colonies were merchant houses, family firms that owned a few ships. Corporations were rare. The economic historian Robert E. Wright counts only 8 business corporations chartered in the colonies before independence. He writes, "Before the Revolution, American business corporations were few, small, and largely inconsequential." Inconsequential means unimportant. Under British rules a charter cost a lot to get and gave its owners little in return, so most colonists in business chose other ways to organize.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Partners, and the trade in people" -->
A colonial business was most often a partnership. Two or three men put their money together and shared the profits. Under the law of the time, each partner was liable for all of the firm's debts. Liable means that a person has to pay. If the firm failed, the people it owed could make any one partner pay all of it from his own property.

In 1698 members of the English Parliament passed a law that ended the Royal African Company's monopoly. Under that law any English merchant could carry enslaved people from Africa, as long as he paid the company a fee of 10 percent on the goods he shipped out of Africa. The Royal African Company itself stopped trading in enslaved people in 1731.

Some of the largest merchants in Boston bought and sold people. Charles Apthorp, a Boston merchant born in Britain, ran a firm that imported enslaved people and sold them along with other goods. In the 1730s and 1740s he placed advertisements in the *Boston Gazette*, a newspaper, for "a parcel of likely negros just imported." A parcel meant a group. Likely meant strong and able to work. Negro was a word used then for a Black person. Apthorp was also a business partner of Thomas Hancock, one of the richest merchants in the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hancock" name="Thomas Hancock" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hancock

> **Who:** A Boston merchant who built one of the largest businesses in the colonies. With his partner Charles Apthorp, he rented out the ships that carried the Acadians into exile in 1755.
> **When and where:** Boston, from about 1724 until his death in 1764.

Thomas Hancock built one of the largest fortunes in New England. Part of his firm's business was carrying people whom British officials had forced from their homes. He was born in Lexington, Massachusetts, on 17 July 1703. At 13 or 14 (the sources differ), he was apprenticed to a bookseller in Boston. An apprentice works for a master for a set number of years to learn a trade. Hancock opened his own bookshop in 1723 or 1724. In 1730 he married Lydia Henchman. She was the daughter of Daniel Henchman, a Boston bookseller who was Hancock's business partner.

Hancock traded in many goods. With different partners he made paper. He shipped codfish, whale oil, logwood and potash to other countries. Logwood is a tree whose wood gives a dye, and potash is a salt made from wood ashes. He sold rum and molasses to the fishing fleet off Newfoundland, and he controlled a group of fishing boats.

Hancock also smuggled. Smuggling means moving goods in or out of a place in secret, against the law. English laws controlled where colonial merchants could buy and sell. Hancock carried tea and paper from Amsterdam to the West Indies. He hid French molasses that his ships brought into Boston.

Hancock's partner Charles Apthorp worked as a paymaster and agent for the British army and navy. He supplied goods and money to British forces in Nova Scotia and in Boston. In the autumn of 1755, British officials forced the Acadians out of Nova Scotia. The Acadians were French-speaking Catholic farm families. The officials who gave the orders were Governor Charles Lawrence of Nova Scotia and Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts. British officials took the Acadians' houses and farms.

Lawrence and Shirley hired about 24 sailing ships from the firm of Apthorp and Hancock to carry the Acadians away. They paid by the month, and they paid a fixed fee for each person carried. The partners charged 40 to 48 pounds a month for each ship. In Boston, they had the ships refitted to hold two people for every ton the ship could carry. Some ships held 300 to 500 people. The historian Mark Peterson writes that the ships were "fitted out in the manner of slave ships." The ships carried the Acadians to British colonies along the Atlantic coast, among them Maryland and South Carolina.

Because the ships were poorly fitted, Acadians on board went without enough food, caught diseases and died. No one knows how many died at sea. No passenger lists survive, so there is no record of the dead.

In 1763 Hancock made his nephew John Hancock his equal partner. Thomas Hancock died of a stroke on 1 August 1764, as he entered the council chamber in Boston. He left an estate worth about 70,000 pounds.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1773 colonists in Boston destroyed the tea of a company that British lawmakers had handed the American market. After independence, Americans began chartering companies of their own. State lawmakers wrote each charter one at a time, for banks, bridges, canals, toll roads and water works. Robert E. Wright counts 319 business corporations chartered in the United States before 1801. Only 8 of them came from the colonial years, and 21 more from 1776 to 1789. The rest were chartered from 1790 to 1800.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tea Act and the East India Company" -->
Members of the British Parliament passed the Tea Act on 10 May 1773. The British East India Company held about 17 million pounds of tea, by weight, that it could not sell. Under the Tea Act the company could ship tea straight to the American colonies and sell it there at a lower price, through its own agents. The Act added no new tax. A tax on tea that members of Parliament had set in 1767 stayed in place. The company's agents could sell tea more cheaply than colonial merchants, so the merchants who sold tea in the colonies lost business.

Ships carrying the company's tea reached Boston in late 1773. Thomas Hutchinson, the royal governor of Massachusetts, refused to let the *Beaver*, the *Dartmouth* and the *Eleanor* leave the harbor until they had unloaded. On the night of 16 December 1773, members of a protest group called the Sons of Liberty boarded the three ships. Some were dressed as Mohawk men. They smashed open 340 chests of tea with axes and dumped them into Boston Harbor. The tea weighed more than 92,000 pounds. Many accounts say 342 chests, but the company's own papers say 340.

In February 1774 the company's directors sent a bill for 9,659 pounds, 6 shillings and 4 pence to the Earl of Dartmouth, the British minister in charge of the American colonies. They asked to be paid back for the tea.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Americans start chartering companies" -->
The Bank of North America was the first commercial bank in the United States. A commercial bank keeps people's money safe and lends money to merchants and businesses. Robert Morris planned the bank, and members of the Continental Congress approved his plan on 26 May 1781. They granted the charter on 31 December 1781. The bank opened on 7 January 1782 on Chestnut Street in Philadelphia.

Morris used a shipment of French silver worth $450,000, which had been sent to Congress, to start the bank. The bank then lent the money back to Congress. Ninety-nine Philadelphians and members of Congress bought shares.

Pennsylvania's lawmakers had given the bank a second charter of their own in 1782. Within three years many of them had turned against it. Its critics said the bank's officers favored their friends and charged too much interest on loans. They pointed to the 14 percent dividends the bank paid its shareholders. A dividend is a share of the profits paid out to the owners. The lawmaker William Findley called the bank "a monopoly." In March 1785 members of the Pennsylvania assembly received petitions asking them to cancel the bank's charter. That September they voted to cancel it. In 1787 they gave the bank a narrower charter. It ran for 14 years, and under it the bank's capital, the money its owners put in, could be no more than $2 million.

Most charters of these years went to transportation and to banks. Only 7 of the 319 corporations that Wright counts before 1801 were for making goods. From 1786 to 1798, lawmakers in the Northeast chartered 59 private companies to build toll bridges. A toll is a fee that people pay to cross. The first was the Charles River Bridge in Boston. In its first six years, its investors earned an average of 10.5 percent a year on their money.

Getting a charter cost money. People who wanted one paid for signatures and for help steering a bill through the legislature. Some paid lobbyists and bribes. A lobbyist is a person paid to persuade lawmakers. A bribe is money paid to someone in power to get them to do something.

In a corporation, each owner risks the money he paid for his shares. Limited liability is the rule that owners cannot also be made to pay the company's debts from their own property. The historians Oscar and Mary Handlin found only 16 early charters that promised limited liability in plain words. Wright argues that owners' risk was limited in practice anyway, because a company could not charge its shareholders more than the price of their shares.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-morris" name="Robert Morris" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robert Morris

> **Who:** The merchant who ran the finances of the Revolution and planned the country's first commercial bank. He bought most of the Seneca Nation's remaining land in New York, and he ended up in a debtors' prison.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, 1781 to 1801. Born 1734, died 1806.

Robert Morris ran the finances of the United States near the end of the Revolution, and he ended up in prison for his debts. As Superintendent of Finance for the Continental Congress, he wrote the charter of the Bank of North America. He used the bank to keep the value of the country's money steady, at a time when Congress had no power to tax.

After the war, Morris bought land with borrowed money. Starting in 1793, he and his partners John Nicholson and James Greenleaf bought thousands of building lots in the new District of Columbia. They also bought millions of acres in Pennsylvania, New York, Kentucky, Virginia, Georgia and the Carolinas.

Morris's land in western New York was Seneca land. From 20 August to 16 September 1797, his son Thomas Morris and his agent Charles Williamson met Seneca leaders at Geneseo, New York. Nearly 3,000 Seneca and other Haudenosaunee people came. The Seneca leaders there included Cornplanter, Red Jacket and Handsome Lake, along with the clan mothers. At this meeting, the Treaty of Big Tree, the Seneca gave up almost all of their remaining land in New York, nearly 3.5 million acres. They kept 12 small pieces of land.

Morris paid $100,000. The money was put into shares of the Bank of the United States, which paid the Seneca up to $6,000 a year. Morris asked that the $100,000 go back to his own heirs if the Seneca nation should "become extinct." The federal secretaries of the Treasury and of State refused. Most of the land went on to a group of Dutch investors called the Holland Land Company.

By 1797 Morris and Nicholson owed their creditors about $12 million. A creditor is a person owed money. In February 1798 officers arrested Morris at his home. They took him to the Prune Street debtors' apartment, next to the Walnut Street Prison in Philadelphia. In those years a person who could not pay his debts could be locked up until he did. Morris stayed there about three and a half years.

In 1800 members of Congress passed a bankruptcy law. Bankruptcy is a legal process for a person who cannot pay his debts. A court divides what he owns among the people he owes, and he is freed from the rest of the debt. Some historians argue that Morris's time in prison helped get that law passed. He was released under it in August or October 1801 (accounts differ). At his release, the bankruptcy commissioners found that he owed $2,948,711.11.
