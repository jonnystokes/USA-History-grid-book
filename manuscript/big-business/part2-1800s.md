## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans formed corporations by the thousands between 1800 and 1850. At the start of the century each charter needed its own act of a state legislature. Under a Connecticut law of 1837, people could start a corporation for any lawful business by following set steps. Merchants in Boston used corporations to build the country's largest cotton mills. In 1819 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that a state could not change a charter it had already granted. In the 1830s President Andrew Jackson fought the Second Bank of the United States, one of the largest companies in the country, and its federal charter ended in 1836.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From special charter to general law" -->
Before 1811, a group that wanted a corporation had to ask lawmakers to pass a special law just for them. On 22 March 1811, New York's lawmakers passed the first general incorporation law in the country. A general incorporation law lets any group form a corporation by following set steps, without a special vote.

The New York law covered only makers of goods. It named cloth of wool, cotton or linen, and goods of iron, steel, copper and lead. Any five or more people could form such a company. The charter lasted 20 years, and the company could raise no more than $100,000. A 1972 study in the *Business History Review* calls the law "essentially an emergency measure." Under two federal laws, the Embargo of 1807 and the Non-Intercourse Act of 1809, cloth from Europe had stopped coming in. New York's lawmakers wanted thread made at home so that families could weave their own cloth.

In 1837 Connecticut's lawmakers passed a law that let people incorporate for "any other lawful business." It was the first such law that did not list which trades were allowed or set a time limit on the corporation's life. Banks still needed a special act.

In some Southern states, lawmakers shut particular groups out of their general incorporation laws, most often people who were not white. The economic historian Eric Hilt describes these rules. In those states a Black American could not form a corporation under the general law.

The number of corporations grew fast. The economic historians Richard Sylla and Robert E. Wright count 22,419 business corporations chartered by special acts of state legislatures from 1790 to 1860. Together their charters allowed their owners to raise at least $4.5 billion. Several thousand more corporations formed under general laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 1819" -->
In 1819 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that a charter is a contract, and that a state cannot change it at will. The case was about a college. The same rule covered every business charter.

King George III had granted Dartmouth College its charter in 1769. In 1815 the college's trustees removed its president, John Wheelock. Wheelock then persuaded the governor of New Hampshire to change the college's charter. In 1816 New Hampshire's lawmakers rewrote it. Under the new charter the governor chose trustees, a state board could overrule them, and the college was renamed Dartmouth University. The old trustees sued William Woodward, who served as secretary under the new plan.

Daniel Webster argued the trustees' case before the Supreme Court. He said, "It is, sir, as I have said, a small college, and yet there are those who love it!" Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the Court's opinion. He held that the 1769 charter was a contract. The Constitution states that no state may pass a law "impairing the obligation of contracts." Impairing means weakening. New Hampshire's lawmakers had broken that rule.

After the ruling, a charter granted in one year could not be taken back the next. Justice Joseph Story, in his own opinion in the case, pointed to a way around this. A state could write into a charter, at the start, a power "to alter, modify, or repeal" it. State lawmakers began writing such clauses into new charters. Lawyers and judges still cite the Dartmouth case as a basis for the Constitution's protection of corporations. It has been cited in recent Supreme Court cases, among them *Citizens United v. FEC* and *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston Associates and the mills" -->
A group of Boston merchants, later called the Boston Associates, built the largest cotton mills in the country. In 1813 Francis Cabot Lowell, Patrick Tracy Jackson and Nathan Appleton organized the Boston Manufacturing Company. They raised $400,000 by selling shares. That was more than ten times the money behind the average cotton mill in Rhode Island. By 1820 they had raised $200,000 more.

In 1814 they built a mill at Waltham, on the Charles River. It was the first mill in the world to spin cotton into thread and weave it into cloth under one roof. Raw cotton went in one end, and finished cloth came out the other. Within six years the mill ran 5,376 spindles and 175 power looms. A spindle is the rod that twists cotton fibers into thread, and a power loom is a weaving machine driven by water power. The mill used 450,000 pounds of cotton a year. A typical Rhode Island mill ran fewer than 1,000 spindles. By 1820 the Waltham mill used all the power the Charles River could give.

The same investors then built mills at Pawtucket Falls, on the Merrimack River. The falls were a fishing place of the Pennacook people. In the 1600s the Native towns of Pawtucket and Wamesit stood there, under the leaders Passaconaway and his son Wannalancet. On 1 March 1826 the mill village was made a town and named Lowell. It had 2,500 people in 1826 and 6,477 in 1830.

The cotton the mills spun was grown by enslaved people in the South. The mills also made a cheap cloth that planters bought to clothe the people they enslaved. People at the time called it "negro cloth." Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts called the tie between the mill owners and the slaveholders an "unholy union" between the lords of the loom and the lords of the lash. The lash is a whip.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Nicholas Biddle

> **Who:** The president of the Second Bank of the United States, one of the largest companies in the country, during President Andrew Jackson's fight to end it.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1822 or 1823 to 1839.

Nicholas Biddle ran the Second Bank of the United States during the fight that ended its federal charter. President James Madison signed the bank's charter into law on 10 April 1816. The charter ran for 20 years. The bank had $35 million in capital. That was three and a half times the $10 million of the first national bank. The federal government owned a fifth of the bank's stock, and the president chose 5 of its 25 directors. The bank opened in Philadelphia in January 1817. The Federal Reserve's history of the bank calls it "one of the largest U.S. corporations."

Biddle became the bank's president in 1822 or 1823. The sources differ. By the end of the 1820s the bank had 29 branches, by one count, and did about $70 million of business a year. Another count gives 25 branches. The Federal Reserve's history says that Biddle "was not above allowing the Bank to make loans to his friends while denying loans to those less friendly."

Andrew Jackson became president in 1829. That year he called the bank unconstitutional, which means against the Constitution, and he said it was corrupt. In January 1832 a request went to Congress to recharter the bank, which means to renew its charter, four years before it ran out. Majorities in both houses of Congress voted yes. Jackson vetoed the bill, which means he refused to sign it. The bank's supporters could not get the two-thirds vote needed to pass it anyway.

Jackson won a second term in 1832. He then ordered the government's money moved out of the bank and into banks chartered by the states. By late 1833 most of it was gone. Biddle answered by cutting the bank's lending and by calling in loans, which means he told borrowers to pay back what they owed. He believed that the shortage of credit might turn people against Jackson. In April 1834 a majority of the House of Representatives voted against renewing the charter.

The federal charter ran out in 1836. Pennsylvania's lawmakers chartered the bank again as a state bank. In 1839 the bank suspended payment, which means it stopped handing out coins to people who brought in its paper notes. Biddle resigned that year. He was later sued for nearly $25 million. He was charged with criminal conspiracy and found not guilty. An investigation found "massive fraud" in the bank's business, and the bank closed for good on 4 April 1841.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
A few companies came to control whole industries between 1850 and 1900. In 1880 the capital of one railroad, the Pennsylvania, was larger than everything the federal government took in that year. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of the country's oil by 1880. By 1895 one company controlled about 98 percent of the country's sugar refining.

Members of Congress wrote the first federal rules for business in 1887 and 1890. In the 1890s the justices of the Supreme Court let the sugar trust stand under those rules, and federal judges used them against a railroad strike. When workers struck, owners and officials answered with armed guards, court orders and soldiers. Henry Clay Frick hired the Pinkerton guards who fought steelworkers at Homestead in 1892. Governor Robert Pattison sent the Pennsylvania National Guard there. In 1894 President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops against the Pullman strike.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The railroads" -->
In 1880 the largest corporation in the country was a railroad. That year the Pennsylvania Railroad had 30,000 workers and $400 million in capital. It carried 10 percent of all the freight in the United States and 20 percent of the passengers. The federal government collected about $334 million in all in 1880, less than the railroad's capital. By 1882 the Pennsylvania was the largest corporation in the world.

Federal and state officials also gave the railroads land. A land grant is a gift of land from a government. From 1850 to 1871, federal officials granted about 129 to 131 million acres to railroad companies. The states granted about 49 to 51 million acres more. Counts differ, depending on which grants are counted and which ones lapsed.

The first federal grant went to the Illinois Central in 1851, under a law passed the year before. Of the 3.75 million acres in that law, nearly 2.6 million went to the Illinois Central.

The granted land came from land that federal officials had taken, or were taking, from Native nations. The Pacific Railroad Act of 1 July 1862 states: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." Indian titles meant the Native nations' rights to their own land. To extinguish them meant to end them. In that law, members of Congress committed federal officials to ending the Native nations' rights to the land along the railroad as fast as they could.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Men Who Built America (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller

> **Who:** The Cleveland businessman who built Standard Oil and set up the first large trust.
> **When and where:** Cleveland and New York, 1870 to 1911.
> **Movie:** *The Men Who Built America* (2012), a History Channel series about Rockefeller, Carnegie, J. P. Morgan and others. It mixes historians' comments with scenes played by actors, and the acted scenes are drama, not evidence.

John D. Rockefeller used secret deals with the railroads to take over nearly all of the country's oil refining. Refining is the work of cleaning crude oil from the ground and separating it into products that people can use.

On 10 January 1870 Rockefeller formed the Standard Oil Company in Ohio with his brother William Rockefeller and his partners Samuel Andrews, Henry M. Flagler and Stephen V. Harkness. The company had $1,000,000 in capital. Its refineries in Cleveland could handle about 1,500 barrels of crude oil a day. All the refineries in Cleveland together handled about 10,000 to 12,000 barrels a day.

In 1872 Rockefeller joined the South Improvement Company. Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad had set it up late in 1871. It was a secret deal between a few refiners and the railroads. Under the deal, the railroads' price for carrying crude oil to New York was set at $2.56 a barrel. South Improvement members got back $1.06 on every barrel they shipped. That payment was called a rebate. The members also got $1.06 in cash on every barrel that their rivals shipped. That payment was called a drawback. A rival refiner paid the full price, and part of what he paid went to his own competitors.

Oil producers in western Pennsylvania learned of the plan and fought it. They organized, they raided the shipments of the scheme's members, and they appealed to the Pennsylvania legislature and to Congress. On 2 April 1872 the South Improvement Company's charter was suspended. The company never shipped any oil.

While the scheme stood, in six weeks to three months of early 1872, Rockefeller bought out 21 or 22 of the 26 refiners in Cleveland. The sources give both numbers. One refiner later testified that "our establishment was worth $150,000, and we sold it for about $65,000." That was about forty or forty-five cents for each dollar his business was worth. Rockefeller told the sellers, "Take Standard Oil Stock, and your family will never know want." In three months Standard Oil went from refining about 1,500 barrels a day to 10,000. According to Ida Tarbell, it then held more than a fifth of the country's refining capacity.

Rockefeller kept buying out rivals and making deals with railroads for lower rates. By 1880 Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of all the oil produced in the United States.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a trust actually was" -->
A trust was a way to run many companies as one business, with one small group in charge. The Standard Oil Trust was the first large one. On 2 January 1882, Standard Oil's shareholders signed the Standard Oil Trust Agreement. Sources differ on how many signed: 41 in one count, and about 50 in Ida Tarbell's. They handed over their shares in about 40 companies to nine trustees. A trustee is a person who holds and manages property for others. Rockefeller was one of the nine, and the nine trustees themselves owned most of the stock. The trust was valued at about $70 million. In time the trustees ran some 40 corporations, 14 of them fully owned.

Owners in other industries copied the plan. The word "trust" came to mean any very large combination of companies. Laws against them are still called antitrust laws.

In 1892 the justices of the Ohio Supreme Court ruled that the Standard Oil Trust was a monopoly that broke Ohio law. Rockefeller moved the property to companies in other states. Ida Tarbell later wrote of the trust, "You could argue its existence from its effects, but you could not prove it."

On 31 January 1890 the tobacco firm of James B. Duke, W. Duke, Sons and Company of Durham, North Carolina, joined four other firms to form the American Tobacco Company. Duke had gone into the cigarette business in 1881. He leased new machines that rolled cigarettes, advertised heavily and bought up smaller rivals. The new company was the largest tobacco business in the world. In sugar, the American Sugar Refining Company controlled about 98 percent of the country's sugar refining by 1895.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Franklin Tarbell

> **Who:** An independent oil producer in western Pennsylvania who fought Standard Oil for twenty years. He was the father of Ida Tarbell, the reporter who later investigated Standard Oil.
> **When and where:** Oil Creek and Titusville, Pennsylvania, from 1859 until his death in 1905.

Franklin Tarbell fought Standard Oil for twenty years, and in 1893 he had to mortgage his family's home to pay a ruined partner's debts. Much of what is known about him comes from the memoir of his daughter, Ida Tarbell, published in 1939.

He was born in Oxford, New York, on 21 October 1829. In 1859 he was on his way home to Iowa when he heard about the first oil well at Titusville, Pennsylvania. He guessed that oil men would need tanks to hold their oil. He believed he could build a wooden tank that held 500 barrels or more. A buyer told him, "Show me a model that won't leak, and I'll give you an order." He made the model and got the order. He built the first large round wooden tank on Oil Creek, and he built tanks for ten years.

In 1867 he began to produce oil himself, on farms near Rouseville. In 1870 he moved his family into a new house he had built in Titusville. His daughter Ida was thirteen.

In 1872 the oil producers learned of the South Improvement scheme. Ida remembered her father coming home one night with a grim look on his face. He told the family that he and scores of other producers had signed a pledge. They would not sell oil to Standard Oil, the one company that had gained from the scheme. Ida was fifteen. She later wrote that in 1872 "there was born in me a hatred of privilege, privilege of any sort."

The fight between the independent producers and Standard Oil went on for twenty years. The producers waited years for decisions from the Interstate Commerce Commission, and some businesses were ruined while they waited. Between 1891 and 1893, while Ida was living in Paris, one of her father's business partners was ruined and shot himself. Ida's memoir does not give the man's name. The partner left Franklin Tarbell owing money on notes, which are written promises to repay loans. To pay them, during the money panic of 1893, he mortgaged the family's home. A mortgage is a loan that a person gets by pledging a house. If the loan is not repaid, the lender can take the house.

Ida Tarbell spent most of five years investigating Standard Oil. According to a 2012 account in *Smithsonian* magazine, her father warned her that Rockefeller and Standard Oil could crush her, as they had crushed her home town of Titusville. Franklin Tarbell died in Titusville on 1 March 1905.

### Andrew Carnegie

> **Who:** The Pittsburgh steelmaker whose company owned each step of making steel, from the iron ore to the finished steel. His general manager broke the steelworkers' union at Homestead in 1892.
> **When and where:** Pittsburgh, from the 1870s to 1901.
> **Movie:** *Andrew Carnegie: The Richest Man in the World*, an American Experience documentary shown on PBS on 20 January 1997.

Andrew Carnegie made steel more cheaply than his rivals by owning every step of the work. This way of running a business is called vertical integration. It means that one company owns each stage of making a product, from the raw material to the finished goods.

In the 1890s Carnegie Steel took a controlling share of Henry Clay Frick's coke business. Coke is a fuel made by baking coal, and steel makers burn it to melt iron ore. Carnegie leased newly found iron ore deposits in the Mesabi Range of Minnesota. The name Mesabi comes from the Ojibwe name for the range, Misaabe Wajiw, which means Big Man Mountain. It was Ojibwe country. In treaties of 1854 and 1855, Ojibwe bands gave up northeastern Minnesota to the United States. In 1854 federal officials wanted a vein of copper on the north shore of Lake Superior. In 1855 they wanted land for logging and mining.

Carnegie also bought ships on the Great Lakes to carry his ore. He bought a railroad and extended it to link his steel works to Lake Erie. Since he did not have to buy coke, ore or shipping from other companies, he could sell steel at prices his competitors could not match.

By the end of the 1890s Carnegie Steel was the largest steel maker in the world. It made a quarter of all the steel produced in the United States. In 1900 it earned $40 million in profits.

Carnegie was in Scotland in the summer of 1892. In the words of the encyclopedia Britannica, he gave his general manager, Henry Clay Frick, "carte blanche to break the union" at the Homestead works. Carte blanche means full freedom to act.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892, from the company's side" -->
In 1892 Henry Clay Frick used a fence, a lockout and hired gunmen to break the steelworkers' union at the Homestead works near Pittsburgh. Frick was the general manager of Carnegie Steel and its second-largest owner. The workers' union was the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, and its contract ran out on 1 July 1892.

Frick cut wages. He had a fence built around the plant, topped with barbed wire. The workers called it "Fort Frick." In late June he locked the workers out, which means he closed the plant to them. On 2 July he fired all 3,800 workers.

Frick signed a contract with William and Robert Pinkerton, who ran the Pinkerton National Detective Agency. They agreed to send 300 armed men, at five dollars a day for each man. The agency gathered 316 men in New York and Chicago. Some were Pinkerton regulars. Others had no jobs, and a few were college students earning money for the summer. Their crates held 250 Winchester rifles and 300 pistols. Before dawn on 6 July 1892, two barges carrying the men were towed up the Monongahela River to the plant. A gun battle followed.

Sources give different death counts. The encyclopedia Britannica counts at least 7 workers and 3 Pinkerton men killed in the battle and the days after it. The historian Leon Wolff counts about 9 strikers and 7 Pinkerton men dead from bullets, beatings, drowning and suicide. He writes that about 40 strikers and 20 Pinkerton men were shot. By the time the strike ended in November, he counts 35 men dead from the battle and the violence that followed.

Wolff names some of the dead. A Pinkerton guard named Klein died on the gangplank, shot through the head. Joseph Sotak went to help a wounded worker, Martin Murray, and was shot in the mouth and killed. A worker named Streigle was shot through the throat and died at once. No record names the men who fired the shots that killed them.

After the battle, Frick asked Governor Robert Pattison of Pennsylvania for help. Pattison sent about 8,500 soldiers of the state National Guard. Another count gives 8,000. The soldiers took over the plant on 12 July. By 15 July the plant was running again, with replacement workers. Many of the replacement workers were Black men brought from the South, because the union did not let Black workers join. In November 1892 about 2,000 white workers attacked Black workers and their families, and several people were badly wounded by gunfire. The union gave up on 21 November.

Officials charged scores of union leaders and workers with crimes. Almost all of them were acquitted, which means found not guilty. Carnegie Steel's managers cut wages in all of Carnegie's mills by about half. A heater's helper was paid by the ton of steel. He earned 4.85 cents a ton in February 1892 and 2.22 cents a ton in February 1894. The managers enforced the twelve-hour workday. The members of the union's advisory committee were blacklisted for life. A blacklist is a list of workers that employers agree never to hire. Wolff does not say which employers kept the list. Members of Congress investigated the use of private armed guards at Homestead, and Frick testified before them.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-w-holway" name="John W. Holway" movie="The River Ran Red (1993)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John W. Holway

> **Who:** A 23-year-old medical student hired as a Pinkerton guard for the Homestead job.
> **When and where:** Chicago to Homestead, Pennsylvania, July 1892.
> **Movie:** *The River Ran Red* (1993), a documentary about the Homestead lockout, directed by Steffi Domike and Nicole Fauteux. It tells the story from the workers' side, and Holway is not its subject.

John W. Holway was hired to guard the Homestead works, and a crowd there kicked him and beat him with stones. He was 23 years old and studying to be a doctor. He was one of the college students who signed up with the Pinkerton agency for summer work at five dollars a day.

The agency's Chicago superintendent would not tell the men where they were going. He told them, "The element of danger which is usually found in such expeditions will be here entirely lacking." After dark, Holway and the other Chicago men were put on a train at the Lake Shore depot. Pinkerton detectives stood guard to stop anyone from getting off, even at the stops in Toledo and Cleveland. The thought struck Holway, Wolff writes, that he was "a sort of prisoner." He also had a feeling that there would be shooting.

On 6 July he was on the barges at Homestead. According to Wolff, he does not seem to have fired a shot all day. After the Pinkerton men surrendered, the strikers and townspeople marched them through the town. Three strikers knocked Holway down. One of them said, "You have killed two men this morning. I saw you!" As they pushed him up the hill, a stone hit him in the head.

Holway described what happened next under oath to the congressional committee. "I ran about half a mile, I suppose, but was rather weak and had had nothing to eat or drink, and my legs gave out, could not run any further, and some man got hold of me by the back of my coat, and about 20 or 30 men came up and kicked me and pounded me with stones." He said, "I thought I was about going and commenced to scream." About going meant about to die. Two or three strikers with rifles then ran up and kept the crowd off him.

Henry Clay Frick had signed the contract with William and Robert Pinkerton for the guards. Their Chicago office hired Holway without telling him where he was going.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-w-holway" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal rules, and how they were used" -->
Members of Congress passed the first federal laws to control big business in 1887 and 1890. In the 1890s federal judges let the sugar trust stand, and federal officials used the second law to break a railroad strike.

The Interstate Commerce Act became law on 4 February 1887. It set up the Interstate Commerce Commission, a five-member board, to watch over the railroads. Interstate commerce means trade between states. The railroads became the first industry under federal regulation, which means rules made and enforced by the national government. The commission was the first federal regulatory board.

The Sherman Antitrust Act became law on 2 July 1890. It was named for Senator John Sherman of Ohio. It made illegal every contract or combination "in restraint of trade," which means any deal that blocks free buying and selling between the states. It also outlawed attempts to monopolize. A person found guilty could be fined $5,000 and sent to jail for a year. People harmed could sue for three times their losses. The law did not define the words "trust," "combination," "conspiracy" or "monopoly."

In 1895, in *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled on the sugar trust. The American Sugar Refining Company controlled about 98 percent of all sugar refining in the United States. The justices ruled that the company had not broken the law. They reasoned that making sugar was manufacturing, and that manufacturing was not trade.

In 1894, federal officials used the Sherman Act against workers. In 1893, during a depression, managers of the Pullman Palace Car Company laid off more than 2,000 workers and cut wages by 25 percent. A depression is a long period when many businesses fail and many people lose their jobs. The company owned the town where its workers lived, and its managers did not lower the rents. Company president George M. Pullman refused to meet the workers' representatives. Between 125,000 and 250,000 railroad workers in 27 states joined a boycott of Pullman cars, refusing to handle trains that carried them.

Edwin Walker was a special lawyer working for Attorney General Richard Olney. Walker claimed that the strike broke the Sherman Act. On 2 July 1894 federal judges William Woods and Peter S. Grosscup granted him an injunction. An injunction is a court order that tells people to stop doing something. Anyone who breaks it can be jailed. President Grover Cleveland used the injunction as his reason to send federal troops.
