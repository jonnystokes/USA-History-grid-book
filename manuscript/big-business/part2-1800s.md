## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 9 minutes**

State lawmakers chartered thousands of corporations, companies the law treats as one body, in the first half of the 1800s, and the fights over them reached the Supreme Court and the president. At the start of the century the lawmakers had to pass a special law for each charter. The justices of the Supreme Court ruled in 1819 that a state's lawmakers could not change a charter they had already granted. By 1837 a Connecticut law let people start a corporation for any lawful business by following set steps.

**The mills and the bank.** Merchants in Boston used corporations to build cotton mills, and by 1850 their group controlled a fifth of the country's cotton cloth making. In the 1830s President Andrew Jackson fought the Second Bank of the United States, one of the largest companies in the country, and its federal charter ran out in 1836.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From special charter to general law" -->
A group that wanted a corporation before 1811 had to ask lawmakers to pass a special law just for them. New York's lawmakers changed that on March 22, 1811, when they passed the country's first general incorporation law, a law that let any group form a corporation by following set steps, without a special vote.

Under the New York law, only makers of goods could form such a company: makers of goods of wool, cotton or linen, and of iron, steel, copper, lead and similar metals. Any five or more people could form one. Its charter lasted 20 years, and it could raise no more than $100,000.

**An emergency law.** The historian Seavoy called the law "essentially an emergency measure" in a 1972 article in the *Business History Review*. Under two federal laws that banned trade, the Embargo of 1807 and the Non-Intercourse Act of 1809, cloth from Europe had stopped coming in. New York's lawmakers wanted thread made at home so that families could weave their own cloth.

Connecticut's lawmakers went further in 1837. Their new law let people incorporate, or form a corporation, for "any other lawful business." It was the first such law that did not list which trades were allowed or set a time limit on the corporation's life. Banks still needed a special act.

**Who was shut out.** Lawmakers in some Southern states shut particular groups out of their general incorporation laws, most often people who were not white. In those states, the economic historian Eric Hilt found, a Black American could not form a corporation under the general law.

**How many.** State lawmakers created 22,419 business corporations by special laws, one charter at a time, from 1790 to 1860, by the count of the economic historians Richard Sylla and Robert E. Wright. That is about 70 times the 319 corporations chartered before 1801. Together their charters allowed their owners to raise at least $4.5 billion. Several thousand more corporations formed under general laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Could lawmakers take back a charter? The Dartmouth case, 1819" -->
The justices of the Supreme Court ruled in 1819 that a charter is a contract, and that a state's lawmakers cannot change it at will. The case was about a college's charter, but business corporations could rely on the same rule to protect their own.

**The case.** King George III had granted Dartmouth College its charter in 1769. The college's trustees, the board that ran it, removed its president, John Wheelock, in 1815. Wheelock then persuaded the governor of New Hampshire to change the charter, and in 1816 the state's lawmakers rewrote it. Under the new charter the governor chose trustees and a state board could overrule them. The lawmakers also renamed the college Dartmouth University.

The old trustees sued William Woodward, who served as secretary under the new plan. Daniel Webster argued the trustees' case before the Supreme Court. "It is, sir, as I have said, a small college, and yet there are those who love it!" he said. Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the Court's decision and ruled that the 1769 charter was a contract. The Constitution states that no state may pass a law "impairing," or weakening, "the obligation of contracts," and New Hampshire's lawmakers had broken that rule.

**The way around it.** After the ruling, state lawmakers could not take back a charter that earlier lawmakers had granted. Justice Joseph Story, in his own written opinion in the case, pointed to a way around this. Lawmakers could write into a charter, at the start, a power "to alter, modify, or repeal" it, which means to change it or cancel it. State lawmakers began writing such lines into new charters.

Dartmouth College's own history of the case describes it as a basis for the Constitution's protection of corporations. Supreme Court justices still cite it, as they did in *Citizens United v. FEC*, a 2010 case about money spent on elections, and in *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Boston Associates and the mills" -->
A group of Boston merchants, known today as the Boston Associates, built cotton mills with far more money behind them than their rivals had. By 1850, the National Park Service counts, they controlled a fifth of the country's cotton cloth making.

Francis Cabot Lowell, Patrick Tracy Jackson and Nathan Appleton organized the Boston Manufacturing Company in 1813. It started with $400,000 in capital, the money its owners put in, more than ten times the money behind the average cotton mill in Rhode Island. By 1820 the owners had put in $200,000 more.

**Waltham.** They built a mill at Waltham, on the Charles River, in 1814. It was the first mill in the world to spin cotton into thread and weave it into cloth under one roof, so every step from raw cotton to finished cloth happened in one building.

Within six years the mill ran 5,376 spindles, the rods that twist cotton fibers into thread, and 175 power looms, weaving machines driven by water power. A typical Rhode Island mill ran fewer than 1,000 spindles. The Waltham mill used 450,000 pounds of cotton a year, and by 1820 it used all the power the Charles River could give.

**Lowell.** The same investors then built mills at Pawtucket Falls, on the Merrimack River, a fishing place of the Pennacook people. In the 1600s the Native towns of Pawtucket and Wamesit stood there, under the leaders Passaconaway and his son Wannalancet. The mill village became a town named Lowell on March 1, 1826. It had 2,500 people that year and 6,477 in 1830.

**Whose cotton.** Enslaved people in the South grew the cotton the mills spun. The mills also made a cheap cloth, which people at the time called "negro cloth," that planters bought to clothe the people they enslaved. Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts called the tie between the mill owners and the slaveholders an "unholy union" between the lords of the loom and the lords of the lash, or whip.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nicholas-biddle" name="Nicholas Biddle" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Nicholas Biddle

> **Who:** The head of the Second Bank of the United States, one of the largest companies in the country, who cut off its loans in his fight with President Andrew Jackson.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, from 1822 or 1823 to 1839.

**He cut off loans, hoping people would blame Jackson.**

Nicholas Biddle became president of the Second Bank of the United States in 1822 or 1823. The Federal Reserve's own history of the bank describes it as "one of the largest U.S. corporations." Its charter, which President James Madison signed into law on April 10, 1816, ran for 20 years, and the bank opened in Philadelphia in January 1817.

The bank had $35 million in capital, three and a half times the $10 million of the first national bank. The federal government owned a fifth of its stock, and the president chose 5 of its 25 directors, the board that ran it. By the end of the 1820s the bank had 25 or 29 branches, depending on the count, and did about $70 million of business a year.

The Federal Reserve's history states that Biddle "was not above allowing the Bank to make loans to his friends while denying loans to those less friendly."

**The fight with Jackson.** Andrew Jackson, president from 1829, called the bank unconstitutional, or against the Constitution, and corrupt. Both houses of Congress voted to renew its charter early, in 1832, 28 to 20 in the Senate and 107 to 85 in the House, and Jackson vetoed the bill, refusing to sign it. Chapter 17 tells that fight in full.

After Jackson won a second term, he ordered the government's money moved out of the bank and into banks chartered by the states, and by late 1833 most of it was gone.

**Biddle's answer.** Biddle cut the bank's lending and called in loans, telling borrowers to pay back what they owed. He believed that a shortage of credit, money to borrow, might turn people against Jackson. In April 1834 a majority of the House of Representatives voted against renewing the charter.

The federal charter ran out in 1836, and Pennsylvania's lawmakers chartered the bank again as a state bank. In 1839 the bank's officers suspended payment: they stopped handing out coins to people who brought in its paper notes. Biddle resigned that year.

**What the stockholders found.** The bank's stockholders, the people who owned its shares, chose a committee in 1841 to look into its books. The committee members reported that Biddle had used the bank's money to buy cotton, tobacco and other crops and ship them to Europe. The bank closed for good on April 4, 1841.

**Charged with a crime.** A grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there is enough proof for a trial, charged Biddle and two other bank officers with a crime on December 10, 1841. The charge stated that the three men had cheated the stockholders out of $400,000 in 1836 and had tried to cover it up with false records in 1841. Biddle was arrested. Judges later dropped the charges.

Biddle paid the bank's creditors, the people it owed money, with what was left of his own fortune. He faced lawsuits until he died in 1844. In one of them he was sued for nearly $25 million. The histories of the bank do not name who brought it.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 18 minutes**

A few companies came to control whole industries between 1850 and 1900. One railroad, the Pennsylvania, had more capital in 1880 than the federal government took in that whole year. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of the country's oil by 1880, and by 1895 one company controlled about 98 percent of the country's sugar refining.

**Rules and soldiers.** Members of Congress wrote the first federal rules for business in 1887 and 1890. In 1895 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the sugar company had not broken those rules, and federal lawyers used the second rule to get a court order against a railroad strike.

When workers struck, owners and officials answered with armed guards, court orders and soldiers. Henry Clay Frick hired the armed Pinkerton guards who fought steelworkers at Homestead in 1892, and Governor Robert Pattison sent the Pennsylvania National Guard there. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops against the Pullman strike in 1894.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big was the biggest railroad?" -->
The largest corporation in the country in 1880 was a railroad, the Pennsylvania. That year it had 30,000 workers and $400 million in capital, and it carried 10 percent of all the freight in the United States and 20 percent of the passengers. Federal officials collected about $334 million in all in the year that ended on June 30, 1880, less than the railroad's capital. By 1882 the Pennsylvania was the largest corporation in the world.

**Free land.** Federal and state officials also gave the railroads land grants, gifts of land from a government. Federal officials granted about 129 to 131 million acres to railroad companies from 1850 to 1871, and state lawmakers granted about 51 million acres more. Counts of the federal grants differ by a few million acres. The Illinois Central got the first federal grant in 1851, under a law passed the year before: nearly 2.6 million of the law's 3.75 million acres.

**Whose land.** The granted land came from land that federal officials had taken, or were taking, from Native nations. The Pacific Railroad Act of July 1, 1862, states: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." In that law, members of Congress told federal officials to end the Native nations' rights to the land along the railroad as fast as they could.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Men Who Built America (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller

> **Who:** The Cleveland businessman who built Standard Oil and set up the first large trust.
> **When and where:** Cleveland and New York, 1870 to 1911.
> **Movie:** *The Men Who Built America* (2012), a History Channel series about Rockefeller, Carnegie, J. P. Morgan and others. It is a docudrama: actors play the real people in scripted scenes.

**"Take Standard Oil Stock, and your family will never know want."**

John D. Rockefeller used secret deals with the railroads to take over nearly all of the country's oil refining, the work of cleaning crude oil from the ground and separating it into products that people can use.

Rockefeller formed the Standard Oil Company in Ohio on January 10, 1870, with his brother William Rockefeller, Henry M. Flagler and two other partners. The company had $1,000,000 in capital. Its refineries in Cleveland could handle about 1,500 barrels of crude oil a day, out of about 10,000 to 12,000 barrels a day for all the refineries in Cleveland together.

**The secret deal.** Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad set up the South Improvement Company late in 1871, a secret deal between a few refiners and the railroads. Rockefeller and his partners controlled 900 of its 2,000 shares. Under the deal, the railroads would charge $2.56 a barrel to carry crude oil to New York, and members would get back $1.06 on every barrel they shipped. That payment was called a rebate.

Members would also get $1.06 in cash on every barrel that their rivals shipped, a payment called a drawback. A rival refiner would pay the full price, and part of what he paid would go to his own competitors.

Oil producers in western Pennsylvania learned of the plan and fought it. They organized, raided the shipments of the scheme's members and asked Pennsylvania's lawmakers and Congress for help. Pennsylvania officials suspended the South Improvement Company's charter on April 2, 1872, and the company never shipped any oil.

**The Cleveland buyout.** While the scheme stood, in early 1872, Rockefeller bought out 21 or 22 of the 26 refiners in Cleveland. One account puts the buying between mid-February and mid-March, and Ida Tarbell says it took three months. One refiner later testified that "our establishment was worth $150,000, and we sold it for about $65,000," about forty or forty-five cents for each dollar his business was worth. Rockefeller told the sellers, "Take Standard Oil Stock, and your family will never know want," meaning they would never be poor.

In three months Standard Oil went from refining about 1,500 barrels a day to 10,000, and according to Ida Tarbell it then held more than a fifth of the country's refining capacity. Rockefeller kept buying out rivals and making deals with railroads for lower rates. By 1880 Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of all the oil produced in the United States.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a trust was" -->
A trust was a way to run many companies as one business, with one small group in charge, and the Standard Oil Trust was the first large one. The people who owned shares in Standard Oil signed the Standard Oil Trust Agreement on January 2, 1882. Accounts differ on how many signed: 41 in one count, and about 50 in Ida Tarbell's.

They handed over their shares in about 40 companies to nine trustees, people who hold and manage property for others. Rockefeller was one of the nine, and the nine trustees themselves owned most of the stock. The trust was valued at about $70 million. In time the trustees ran some 40 corporations, 14 of them fully owned.

Owners in other industries copied the plan, and laws against such combinations of companies are still called antitrust laws.

**The Ohio ruling.** The justices of the Ohio Supreme Court ruled in 1892 that the Standard Oil Trust was a monopoly, a business with no real rivals, and that it broke Ohio law. Rockefeller moved the property to companies in other states. Ida Tarbell later wrote of the trust, "You could argue its existence from its effects, but you could not prove it."

**Tobacco and sugar.** James B. Duke had gone into the cigarette business in 1881. He leased new machines that rolled cigarettes, advertised heavily and bought up smaller rivals. On January 31, 1890, Duke and the owners of four other firms joined their businesses into one, the American Tobacco Company, the largest tobacco business in the world. Duke's own firm was W. Duke, Sons and Company of Durham, North Carolina. In sugar, the American Sugar Refining Company controlled about 98 percent of the country's sugar refining by 1895.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Franklin Tarbell

> **Who:** An oil producer who fought Standard Oil from 1872 into the 1890s, and the father of the reporter Ida Tarbell.
> **When and where:** Oil Creek and Titusville, Pennsylvania, from 1859 until his death in 1905.

**He and scores of other producers pledged not to sell to Standard Oil.**

Franklin Tarbell fought Standard Oil for twenty years, and in 1893 he had to mortgage his family's home to pay a ruined partner's debts. His daughter, the reporter Ida Tarbell, told his story in her memoir, published in 1939.

**Tanks.** Tarbell was on his way home to Iowa in 1859 when he heard about the oil well at Titusville, Pennsylvania. He guessed that oil men would need tanks to hold their oil, and he believed he could build a wooden one that held 500 barrels or more. He was told, "Show me a model that won't leak, and I'll give you an order." He made the model, got the order and built the first large round wooden tank on Oil Creek. For ten years he built tanks.

He began to produce oil himself in 1867, on farms near Rouseville, and in 1870 he moved his family into a new house he had built in Titusville. Ida was thirteen.

**The pledge.** The oil producers learned of the South Improvement scheme in 1872. Ida remembered her father coming home one night with a grim look on his face. He told the family that he and scores of other producers had signed a pledge not to sell oil to Standard Oil, the one company that had gained from the scheme. Ida, fifteen that year, later wrote that in 1872 "there was born in me a hatred of privilege, privilege of any sort."

**Twenty years.** The fight between the independent producers and Standard Oil went on for twenty years. The producers waited for slow decisions from the Interstate Commerce Commission, the federal board set up in 1887 to watch over the railroads, and some of their businesses failed while they waited.

One of Franklin Tarbell's business partners lost everything in this fight and shot himself between 1891 and 1893, while Ida was living in Paris. Ida's memoir does not state the man's name. The partner left Franklin Tarbell owing money on notes, written promises to repay loans. To pay them, during the money panic of 1893, he mortgaged the family's home: he borrowed money and pledged the house, which the lender could take if the loan was not repaid.

**His warning.** Ida Tarbell later spent most of five years investigating Standard Oil. Her father warned her that Rockefeller and Standard Oil could crush her, just as, in his view, they had crushed her home town of Titusville, a 2012 account in *Smithsonian* magazine says. Franklin Tarbell died in Titusville on March 1, 1905.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" name="Andrew Carnegie" movie="The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Carnegie

> **Who:** The Pittsburgh steelmaker whose company owned every step of making steel, and whose manager broke the union at Homestead.
> **When and where:** Pittsburgh, until he sold his company in 1901.
> **Movie:** *Andrew Carnegie: The Richest Man in the World*, an American Experience documentary shown on PBS on January 20, 1997.

**One company owned the coke, the ore, the ships and the railroad.**

Andrew Carnegie cut his costs and raised his profits by owning every step of making steel. That way of running a business is called vertical integration: one company owns each stage of making a product, from the raw material to the finished goods.

**Coke and ore.** Carnegie already owned 73.5 percent of Henry Clay Frick's coke company by 1888. The company made coke, a fuel baked from coal that steel makers burn to melt iron ore. Carnegie leased newly found iron ore deposits in the Mesabi Range of Minnesota in the 1890s. The name Mesabi comes from the Ojibwe name for the range, Misaabe Wajiw, which means Big Man Mountain.

**Ojibwe land.** The Mesabi Range was Ojibwe country. Ojibwe bands gave up northeastern Minnesota to the United States in a treaty of 1854. Federal officials wanted a vein of copper there, on the north shore of Lake Superior. In a treaty of 1855 Ojibwe bands gave up most of north-central Minnesota, where federal officials wanted land for logging and mining.

**Ships and a railroad.** Carnegie bought ships on the Great Lakes to carry his ore, and he bought a railroad and extended it to link his steel works to Lake Erie. He no longer had to buy coke, ore or shipping from other companies, and that cut his costs. Carnegie Steel was the largest steel maker in the world by the end of the 1890s, and it made a quarter of all the steel produced in the United States. In 1900 it earned $40 million in profits.

**Homestead.** Carnegie was in Scotland in the summer of 1892. In the words of the encyclopedia Britannica, he gave his general manager, Henry Clay Frick, "carte blanche," or full freedom, "to break the union" at the Homestead works.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homestead, 1892" -->
**By 1892:** Standard Oil had refined 90 to 95 percent of the country's oil as early as 1880. The American Tobacco Company was the largest tobacco business in the world, and Andrew Carnegie owned most of Henry Clay Frick's coke company.

Henry Clay Frick broke the steelworkers' union at the Homestead works near Pittsburgh in 1892 with a fence, a lockout and about 300 armed Pinkerton men. Frick was the general manager of Carnegie Steel. That year he became its chairman, and he owned 11 percent of it. The union was the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, and its contract ran out on July 1, 1892.

**The lockout.** Frick cut wages and had a fence built around the plant, topped with barbed wire, which the workers called "Fort Frick." In late June he locked the workers out, closing the plant to them. On July 2 he fired all 3,800 workers.

**The Pinkertons.** Frick signed a contract with William and Robert Pinkerton, who ran the Pinkerton National Detective Agency, for 300 armed men at five dollars a day for each man. Agency officials gathered 316 men in New York and Chicago. Some were Pinkerton regulars. Others had no jobs, some were criminals on the run, and a few were college students earning money for the summer. Their crates held 250 Winchester rifles and 300 pistols.

Early on July 6, 1892, tugboats towed two barges carrying the men up the Monongahela River to the plant. A gun battle followed.

**The dead.** Counts of the dead differ. The encyclopedia Britannica lists at least 7 workers and 3 Pinkerton men killed in the battle and the days after it. The historian Leon Wolff counts about 9 strikers and 7 Pinkerton men dead from bullets, beatings, drowning and suicide.

Wolff writes that about 40 strikers and 20 Pinkerton men were shot, and he names some of the dead. A Pinkerton guard named Klein died on the gangplank, shot through the head. Joseph Sotak went to help a wounded worker, Martin Murray, and was shot in the mouth and killed. A worker named Streigle was shot through the throat and died at once. Neither Wolff nor Britannica names the men who fired the shots that killed them.

**The soldiers.** After the battle, Frick asked Governor Robert Pattison of Pennsylvania for help. Pattison sent about 8,500 soldiers of the state National Guard (another count gives 8,000), and they took over the plant on July 12. By July 15 Carnegie Steel's managers had the plant running again with replacement workers.

Many of the replacement workers were Black men from the South, and the union did not let Black workers join it. In November 1892 about 2,000 white workers attacked Black workers and their families, and gunfire badly wounded several people. The accounts do not say who fired. The union gave up on November 21. By then, Wolff counts, 35 men had died from the battle and the violence that followed.

**After the strike.** Officials charged scores of union leaders and workers with crimes, and almost all of them were found not guilty. Carnegie Steel's managers cut wages in all of Carnegie's mills by about half. A heater's helper, one of the workers who heated the steel, was paid by the ton of steel: he earned 4.85 cents a ton in February 1892 and 2.22 cents a ton in February 1894. The managers enforced the twelve-hour workday.

The members of the union's advisory committee were blacklisted for life, put on a list of workers that employers agree never to hire. Leon Wolff does not name the employers who kept the list. Members of Congress investigated the use of private armed guards at Homestead, and Frick testified before them.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-w-holway" name="John W. Holway" movie="The River Ran Red (1993)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John W. Holway

> **Who:** A 23-year-old medical student hired as a Pinkerton guard for the Homestead job.
> **When and where:** Chicago to Homestead, Pennsylvania, July 1892.
> **Movie:** *The River Ran Red* (1993), a documentary about the Homestead lockout, directed by Steffi Domike and Nicole Fauteux. It tells the story from the workers' side.

**The agency promised "the element of danger" would be "entirely lacking."**

John W. Holway was hired to guard the Homestead works, and a crowd there kicked him and beat him with stones. He was studying to be a doctor and was one of the college students who signed up with the Pinkerton agency for summer work at five dollars a day.

**The train.** The man in charge of the agency's Chicago office would not tell the men where they were going. "The element of danger which is usually found in such expeditions will be here entirely lacking," he told them.

After dark, agency men put Holway and the other Chicago men on a train at the Lake Shore depot. Pinkerton detectives stood guard to stop anyone from getting off, even at the stops in Toledo and Cleveland. The thought struck Holway, Wolff writes, that he was "a sort of prisoner," and he had a feeling that there would be shooting.

**The barges.** Holway was on the barges at Homestead on July 6. According to Wolff, he does not seem to have fired a shot all day. After the Pinkerton men surrendered, they walked to the town through a crowd that beat them. Three strikers knocked Holway down, and one of them said, "You have killed two men this morning. I saw you!" As they pushed him up the hill, a stone hit him in the head.

Holway described what happened next under oath to the congressional committee. "I ran about half a mile, I suppose, but was rather weak and had had nothing to eat or drink, and my legs gave out, could not run any further, and some man got hold of me by the back of my coat, and about 20 or 30 men came up and kicked me and pounded me with stones."

"I thought I was about going," he said, meaning about to die, "and commenced to scream." Two or three strikers with rifles then ran up and kept the crowd off him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-w-holway" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first federal rules, and how they were used" -->
Members of Congress passed the first federal laws to control big business in 1887 and 1890. In the 1890s the justices of the Supreme Court let the sugar trust stand, and federal officials used the second law to break a railroad strike.

**The railroads.** The Interstate Commerce Act, which set rules for interstate commerce, or trade between the states, became law on February 4, 1887. Under the Act, the five members of a new board, the Interstate Commerce Commission, watched over the railroads. The railroads became the first industry under federal regulation, rules made and enforced by the national government, and the commission was the first federal board of its kind.

**The Sherman Act.** The Sherman Antitrust Act, named for Senator John Sherman of Ohio, became law on July 2, 1890. Under it, every contract or combination "in restraint of trade" became illegal, meaning any deal that blocks free buying and selling between the states. Trying to monopolize, or to become the only seller of something, also became a crime.

A person found guilty could be fined $5,000 and sent to jail for a year, and people harmed could sue for three times their losses. The law did not define the words "trust," "combination," "conspiracy" or "monopoly."

**The sugar case.** The justices of the Supreme Court ruled on the sugar trust in 1895, in *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.* The American Sugar Refining Company controlled about 98 percent of all sugar refining in the United States, and the justices ruled that it had not broken the law. They reasoned that making sugar was manufacturing, and that manufacturing was not trade.

**Pullman.** Managers of the Pullman Palace Car Company laid off more than 2,000 workers and cut wages by 25 percent in 1893, during a depression, a long time when many businesses fail and many people lose their jobs. The company owned the town where its workers lived, and its managers did not lower the rents. Company president George M. Pullman refused to meet the workers' representatives.

Railroad workers in 27 states, between 125,000 and 250,000 of them, joined a boycott of Pullman cars in 1894, refusing to handle trains that carried them. That year federal officials used the Sherman Act against the workers. Edwin Walker, a special lawyer working for Attorney General Richard Olney, claimed that the strike broke the Sherman Act.

**The court order.** On July 2, 1894, federal judges William Woods and Peter S. Grosscup granted Walker an injunction, a court order that tells people to stop doing something and lets judges jail anyone who breaks it. President Grover Cleveland used the injunction as his reason to send federal troops. The soldiers arrested boycotters and rioters and sometimes shot them, according to the Federal Judicial Center's history of the case.

About 30 people were killed across the country during the strike, and the histories of it do not say who killed each of them.
