## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1895 and 1904, owners and bankers joined more than 1,800 manufacturing firms into 157 large corporations, and federal lawyers and judges then began to break some of the biggest apart. The largest, U.S. Steel, issued more than a billion dollars in stock in 1901. Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft had their lawyers sue the biggest combinations. In 1911 the justices of the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil and the American Tobacco Company split apart.

Members of Congress passed two more antitrust laws in 1914. After the stock market crash of 1929, they passed a law in 1934 that made companies selling stock report their finances to the public. During the Second World War, federal officials gave about two-thirds of the value of their main war contracts to 100 companies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great merger movement and U.S. Steel" -->
In a merger, two or more companies join into one. Most of the 157 large combinations formed in a few years, and more than 60 of them formed in 1899 alone. The historian Naomi Lamoreaux names the causes. Factories cost a great deal to build, industries grew fast after 1887, and the depression of 1893 set off price wars. The owners' old agreements to hold prices up could not stop those wars.

Lamoreaux writes that "there was nothing natural or inevitable" about these combinations. Most of them made goods less cheaply than the new rivals that soon appeared, and they "quickly lost their positions of market dominance." The combinations that lasted longer were the ones that blocked rivals, such as U.S. Steel through its control of iron ore.

U.S. Steel was incorporated in New Jersey in February 1901. The banker J. P. Morgan put it together. Its largest piece was Carnegie Steel, which Morgan bought for Andrew Carnegie's asking price of $480 million. Eight other steel companies went in with it, among them Federal Steel, National Steel, National Tube and American Bridge. So did the Lake Superior Consolidated Iron Mines, whose ore came from Ojibwe land in Minnesota. Charles M. Schwab was the first president, and Elbert H. Gary was the chairman.

The new company issued stock with a stated value of about $1.4 billion. Sources give figures from $1.403 billion to $1.466 billion. It was the first company in the world with more than a billion dollars in stock. Government investigators at the federal Bureau of Corporations later put the real value of what the company owned at about $682 million, about half the stock issued. Stock issued for more than the property behind it is called watered stock. According to the financial data firm Finaeon, when the shares first traded in 1901, buyers valued them at about $765 million in all. The market value of the stock did not pass $1 billion until 1916.

U.S. Steel made about two-thirds of all the steel in the United States at the start. The historian Kenneth Warren counts 65.7 percent in 1901. Another count gives 67 percent in 1902. That year the company employed 168,000 people. By 1911 its share had fallen to between 40 and 50 percent, depending on the source.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan

> **Who:** The New York banker who put companies together, among them U.S. Steel in 1901 and the Northern Securities Company.
> **When and where:** New York, 1901 to 1904.

J. P. Morgan was a banker who joined rival companies into single giant ones. He raised the money, bought the companies and set the terms.

In December 1900 Charles M. Schwab, then president of Carnegie Steel, gave a speech at the University Club. The speech led Morgan to the steel deal. Carnegie named a price of $480 million for his company, and Morgan accepted it at once. Morgan then formed U.S. Steel in 1901 and sat on its finance committee.

In 1901 Morgan and the railroad owner James J. Hill also formed the Northern Securities Company. It was a holding company, which is a company that owns controlling shares in other companies and does little else. Northern Securities controlled three railroads: the Great Northern, the Northern Pacific, and the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy. In 1902 President Theodore Roosevelt's Justice Department lawyers sued to break it up.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" name="Ida Tarbell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ida Tarbell

> **Who:** The reporter whose history of Standard Oil, built from court records and sworn testimony, showed how John D. Rockefeller took over the oil business.
> **When and where:** New York and the Pennsylvania oil region, 1900 to 1904.

Ida Tarbell spent about four years tracing, from public records, how John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil. She grew up in Titusville, where her father, Franklin Tarbell, had fought Standard Oil since 1872. She began the work in 1900.

Tarbell read hundreds of thousands of documents from across the country. She used court records and depositions. A deposition is sworn testimony that is taken outside a courtroom and written down. She also interviewed employees and lawyers. The writer Mark Twain arranged for her to meet Henry H. Rogers, one of Standard Oil's top officers, and she interviewed him too.

Her *History of the Standard Oil Company* came out in 19 parts in *McClure's* magazine, from November 1902 to October 1904. In 1904 it was published as a book in two volumes.

One part told the story of a Cleveland widow, Mrs. Backus, who sold her late husband's plant for making oil for machines to Rockefeller in 1878. Tarbell wrote that he paid her next to nothing. Rockefeller's biographer Ron Chernow gives a different account. He says the plant was out of date, that the widow asked for as much as $200,000, and that Rockefeller paid $79,000, which included $10,000 added on top. Historians still disagree about that part. Chernow calls Rockefeller's buyouts of his Cleveland rivals in 1872 "one of the most shameful episodes in his career."

Rockefeller called Tarbell "That poisonous woman." He told his advisers, "Not a word about that misguided woman."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt

> **Who:** The president called the trust-buster. His government won the Northern Securities case, and it brought 44 antitrust suits in seven and a half years.
> **When and where:** Washington, 1901 to 1909.
> **Movie:** *TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt*, an American Experience documentary first shown on PBS in October 1996.

Theodore Roosevelt was called the trust-buster, but the president after him brought more antitrust suits. In 1902 Roosevelt's Justice Department lawyers sued the Northern Securities Company, J. P. Morgan and James J. Hill's railroad holding company, under the Sherman Act.

On 14 March 1904 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 for the government, and the company was broken up. Justice John M. Harlan wrote for the majority. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes disagreed. He wrote, "Great cases like hard cases make bad law."

Lawyers in Roosevelt's government brought 44 antitrust suits in seven and a half years. Lawyers under William Howard Taft, the next president, brought more in four years. One count gives 75 and another gives 99. Roosevelt divided trusts into good ones and bad ones. In 1911 Taft's lawyers sued U.S. Steel. Roosevelt then accused Taft of being unable to tell the good trusts from the bad ones.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standard Oil and American Tobacco are broken up, 1911" -->
In May 1911 the justices of the Supreme Court ordered the two largest trusts in the country split apart. On 15 May they ruled that Standard Oil of New Jersey had restrained trade and monopolized the oil business. Chief Justice Edward D. White wrote the opinion. He wrote that the Sherman Act banned only "unreasonable" limits on trade. This test is called the rule of reason. The justices gave the company six months to break itself up. Standard Oil was split into 34 separate companies.

Rockefeller was worth about $300 million in May 1911 and more than $900 million by the end of 1913. According to Chernow, Roosevelt said that Wall Street's prayer was now, "Oh Merciful Providence, give us another dissolution." In 1916 newspaper headlines called Rockefeller the world's first billionaire. Companies that grew out of Standard Oil later became Exxon, Mobil and Chevron.

On 29 May 1911 the justices ruled against the American Tobacco Company. They found that James B. Duke, Benjamin N. Duke, George W. Watts, R. J. Reynolds and others had broken the Sherman Act "by seeking to monopolize practically all sectors of the tobacco industry." In November 1911 a federal court in New York divided the company's property among a new American Tobacco Company, Liggett and Myers, P. Lorillard, R. J. Reynolds and others. James B. Duke's tie to American Tobacco ended that year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two new laws, 1914" -->
In 1914 members of Congress passed two laws that named business practices the Sherman Act had left vague. President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Trade Commission Act on 26 September 1914. It set up the Federal Trade Commission to stop unfair ways of competing. The commission opened on 16 March 1915.

Wilson signed the Clayton Antitrust Act on 15 October 1914. It banned several practices by name. One was price discrimination, which means charging different buyers different prices for the same goods. Others were deals that tied a buyer to one seller, mergers that cut down competition, and the same people sitting on the boards of competing companies. The Clayton Act also stated "that the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce." A commodity is a thing that is bought and sold. Under that section, unions could no longer be treated as illegal combinations the way the Pullman strikers had been in 1894.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ludlow, 1914, from the owners' side" -->
In 1914 at least 19 people died at Ludlow, Colorado, when fighting broke out between the Colorado National Guard and striking coal miners. The mine owners paid the Guard's costs. Most of the strikers worked for the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, whose main stockholder was John D. Rockefeller Jr.

The miners struck in September 1913. In October a company armored car, which the miners called the "Death Special," fired on their tent camp at Forbes and killed one person. Governor Elias M. Ammons sent the Colorado National Guard, and the mine owners paid for it.

On 20 April 1914 fighting broke out at the tent camp at Ludlow. Nobody knows who fired the first shot. Guardsmen set the tents on fire. Thirteen women and children had hidden in a pit dug under a tent, and they suffocated there. Suffocate means to die from lack of air. One guardsman and five miners also died. Guardsmen shot the strike leader Louis Tikas three times in the back. President Wilson sent the U.S. Army on 28 April. The strike ended in December 1914, and the union won none of its goals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaded gasoline, 1924 to 1926" -->
In the 1920s, three of the largest companies in the country kept selling a lead fuel additive after workers who made it died. Tetraethyl lead is a liquid made with lead. Researchers at General Motors added it to gasoline to stop engines from knocking. In August 1924 General Motors and Standard Oil of New Jersey formed the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation to sell it. DuPont made it.

Workers died at plants and a laboratory of all three companies. In October 1924, at Standard Oil's Bayway refinery in Elizabeth, New Jersey, seven men died and 33 went to the hospital, according to the historian Bill Kovarik. Other accounts count five dead. Kovarik counts ten more dead at a DuPont plant and at least two dead at General Motors' laboratory in Dayton, Ohio. Poisoned workers at Bayway were "black and blue from uncontrolled muscle spasms." Some had to be tied into straitjackets, which are coats that hold a person's arms tight against the body.

On 20 May 1925 the Surgeon General, the head of the federal public health service, called a meeting about the additive. Frank Howard of Standard Oil said the industry could not give up "what has come to the industry like a gift from heaven." The companies kept selling it. Robert Kehoe, a scientist paid by the Ethyl company, then set out a theory of a "safe level" of lead that the industry used for decades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who controlled the corporations, 1932" -->
In 1932 two writers showed that a few hundred corporations held about half of the country's corporate wealth, and that hired managers, not owners, ran them. In their book *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*, Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means counted the 200 largest corporations. By 1930 those 200 held 49.2 percent of all corporate wealth. The scholar Gerald F. Davis later worked out that if that trend had continued, they would have held all of it by 1959.

Berle and Means also found that ownership of these companies was split among many scattered shareholders. The people who actually made the decisions were the managers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Securities and Exchange Commission, 1934" -->
After the stock market crash of 1929, members of Congress passed a law that made companies selling stock report their finances to the public. President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Securities Exchange Act on 6 June 1934. It set up the Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC, to watch over the stock markets. The yearly and quarterly reports that such companies still file with the SEC come from this system.

Roosevelt made Joseph P. Kennedy the first chairman of the SEC in the summer of 1934. Sources give June or 1 July. Kennedy had made money in the stock market using the same practices the new law banned. He had given, lent and raised money for Roosevelt's 1932 campaign. Newspaper writers said the choice was "like putting the fox in the hen house." In private, Roosevelt answered, "set a thief to catch a thief." Accounts give his words in different forms. Kennedy resigned in September 1935.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spies, guns and company police, 1936 to 1940" -->
From 1936 to 1940, a Senate committee led by Senator Robert M. La Follette Jr. of Wisconsin held hearings on how employers fought unions. Witnesses told the committee that companies had planted spies inside unions. The companies had stored up weapons, among them submachine guns, rifles and tear gas. They had also hired their own police forces.

On Memorial Day in 1937, during a steel strike, Chicago police killed 10 people and shot 40 at the Republic Steel plant in Chicago. There were 264 officers at the plant. The La Follette committee found that the police had used "excessive force." Republic Steel's chairman was Tom M. Girdler.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii's Big Five" -->
In Hawaii, five companies known as the Big Five controlled about 94 percent of the islands' sugar crop by 1920. They were C. Brewer and Company, Theo H. Davies and Company, H. Hackfeld and Company, Castle and Cooke, and Alexander and Baldwin. The historian Carol MacLennan gives the 94 percent figure. The same men sat on the boards of several of the firms, and together they controlled shipping, banking and stores in the islands. Four of the firms together owned nearly three-quarters of the Matson shipping line, which carried 80 percent of the cargo between the mainland and Hawaii.

In 1893 members of a group called the Committee of Safety organized the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom. They pressed for the United States to annex the islands, which means to take them over. Some sons of Big Five partners were members of that committee. The Big Five's plantations stood on the land of Native Hawaiians, the Kanaka Maoli. From 1893 into the 1950s, the owners of the Big Five dominated Hawaii's economy and government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War contracts, 1940 to 1944" -->
During the Second World War, a small number of large companies received most of the government's war business. The economist Robert Higgs gives the figures for prime contracts from 1940 to 1944. A prime contract is a contract made directly between the government and a company. The top 100 contractors received about two-thirds of the value of those contracts. The top 10 received about 30 percent. General Motors alone received nearly 8 percent.

Federal officials also paid for new factories and leased them to private companies. As of 30 June 1944, just 26 companies had the use of half the value of all those government-paid factories.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1950 the largest American companies employed hundreds of thousands of people each. General Motors had 618,365 workers in the United States at its peak in 1979. The Bell telephone system had about a million in the early 1980s. From the 1970s, business leaders organized to win influence in Washington. The number of firms with registered lobbyists there grew from 175 in 1971 to 2,445 in 1982.

Some companies kept selling products that studies linked to disease and death. Tobacco companies paid for advertisements in 1954 that disputed studies linking smoking to lung cancer. In 1994 heads of tobacco companies told a House hearing that they did not believe nicotine is addictive. Under an agreement with federal lawyers, AT&T's Bell System was split up in 1984. Federal lawyers won a monopoly ruling against Microsoft in 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="General Motors" -->
In the 1950s General Motors was the country's largest car maker, and in 1953 it was the largest defense contractor. In 1954 it sold 54 percent of new cars, by the Associated Press's count. The encyclopedia Britannica gives GM 40 to 45 percent of all car sales through the 1950s and 1960s. In 1955 GM became the first company to earn more than a billion dollars in profit in one year. In the first three quarters of 1955 alone it made $912,887,537 in profit. *TIME* magazine wrote, "Never before in history has one corporation made so much in so short a time." GM's workforce in the United States peaked at 618,365 in 1979. That made it the largest private employer in the country.

In 1953 GM's president, Charles E. Wilson, was named Secretary of Defense. He gave up a salary of $600,000 a year for one of $22,000. Wilson owned 40,000 shares of GM stock, and at his hearing senators pressed him on that conflict. Senator Robert C. Hendrickson asked whether Wilson could make a decision that went strongly against GM's interest. Wilson answered, "Yes, sir, I could. I cannot conceive of one because, for years, I thought what was good for our country was good for General Motors, and vice versa." Vice versa means the other way around. People soon repeated his words as "What is good for General Motors is good for the country."

In 1965 Ralph Nader wrote a book called *Unsafe at Any Speed*. It criticized the safety of GM's Corvair car. GM managers hired private detectives to look into Nader's private life. In 1966 GM's president, James M. Roche, apologized to a Senate committee. That same year members of Congress passed the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act, the first federal rules for how cars must be built. Nader sued GM for invading his privacy and settled for $425,000. He used the money to start groups that work for consumers. GM stopped making the Corvair in 1969.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eisenhower's warning, 1961" -->
On 17 January 1961, in his farewell speech, President Dwight D. Eisenhower warned the country about the power of the arms makers and the armed forces together. He said, "in the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." Unwarranted means without good reason. By the military-industrial complex he meant the ties between the armed forces and the companies that made their weapons. Those were the same kinds of large companies that had received most of the war contracts of 1940 to 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The conglomerate" -->
A conglomerate is one company that owns many businesses that have nothing to do with each other. Harold Geneen ran International Telephone and Telegraph, or ITT, from 1959 to 1977. In those years ITT bought between 250 and 350 companies, depending on the count, in more than 80 countries. They included the Sheraton hotel chain, Avis car rental, the Hartford insurance company and Continental Baking, the maker of Wonder Bread. ITT's yearly sales grew from about $700 million to $17 billion, and it became the eleventh-largest corporation in the country. Geneen believed that good managers could make a success of any business.

In 1966 ITT's leaders tried to buy the ABC television network for $700 million, and federal antitrust officials stopped the deal. In Chile, President Salvador Allende's government had taken over ITT's telephone company. The company later admitted that it had given the CIA, the American spy agency, $350,000 for "political" purposes in Chile.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Business organizes in Washington" -->
In the 1970s, heads of large companies built up their own political power in Washington. On 23 August 1971 the lawyer Lewis F. Powell Jr. sent a private memo to Eugene B. Sydnor Jr. of the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, a national group of businesses. Its title was "Attack On American Free Enterprise System." It urged business leaders to organize and defend themselves in politics, in the courts, in the press and in the universities. On 21 October 1971, less than two months later, President Richard Nixon nominated Powell to the Supreme Court. The newspaper columnist Jack Anderson made the memo public in September 1972.

On 13 October 1972 three groups of company heads joined to form the Business Roundtable. Its founders included John D. Harper of Alcoa and Roger Blough, the retired head of U.S. Steel. They set out to fight the growing power of unions, especially construction unions.

Companies opened offices in Washington and hired lobbyists. The number of corporations with public affairs offices in Washington grew from 100 in 1968 to more than 500 in 1978. The number of firms with registered lobbyists grew from 175 in 1971 to 2,445 in 1982. Companies also set up political action committees. A political action committee, or PAC, collects money and gives it to candidates. The Federal Election Commission counted 89 company PACs at the end of 1974, 294 in May 1976 and 1,206 at the end of 1980.

Members of Congress then voted to remove federal rules on several industries, and President Jimmy Carter signed each law: for airlines on 24 October 1978, for banks on 31 March 1980 and for trucking on 1 July 1980.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Products that hurt people, and what the companies said" -->
From 1954 to 1994, heads of tobacco companies publicly disputed the evidence that smoking causes disease and that nicotine is addictive. On 4 January 1954 the major American tobacco companies ran an advertisement called "A Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers" in more than 400 newspapers. It reached about 43 million people. It disputed new scientific studies that linked smoking cigarettes to lung cancer. The public relations firm Hill and Knowlton designed it.

On 17 July 1963 Addison Yeaman, the chief lawyer of the Brown and Williamson tobacco company, wrote a memo. In it he said that to protect its earnings, the industry must "either a) disprove the theory of causal relationship or b) discover the carcinogen or carcinogens...and demonstrate our ability to remove or neutralize them." A causal relationship here meant that smoking causes cancer. A carcinogen is a substance that causes cancer.

On 14 April 1994, at a hearing in the House of Representatives, Representative Ron Wyden asked seven tobacco company heads whether nicotine is addictive. William Campbell of Philip Morris answered, "I believe nicotine is not addictive, yes." James Johnston of R. J. Reynolds said cigarettes "clearly do not meet the classic definition of addiction." Joseph Taddeo of U.S. Tobacco said, "I don't believe that nicotine or our products are addictive."

On 23 November 1998 the four largest cigarette makers signed an agreement with the attorneys general of 46 states. An attorney general is a state's chief lawyer. The states had sued to get back what they spent treating sick smokers. The companies agreed to pay at least $206 billion over the first 25 years and to limit their advertising.

Asbestos is a mineral that separates into thin, strong fibers. Builders used it to fireproof and insulate buildings. When people breathe asbestos fibers in, the fibers can stay in the lungs for years. They scar the lungs and can cause asbestosis, a lung disease that brings shortness of breath, coughing and lasting lung damage. They can also cause mesothelioma, a cancer of the thin linings of the chest and belly. The illness can take 10 to 40 years or more to show. Johns-Manville was the largest maker of asbestos in the country. In August 1982, facing thousands of lawsuits from people who said asbestos had made them sick, the company filed for bankruptcy. Filing stopped all the injury lawsuits against it. By the end of 1988, a trust set up in the bankruptcy had paid more than 12,600 claims, for almost $500 million in all.

On 3 December 1984 more than 40 tons of a poison gas called methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India. Union Carbide India Limited ran the plant. It was part of the American company Union Carbide. A broken valve let about a ton of water into a tank of the gas. A machine meant to clean escaping gas had been turned off three weeks before, and the plant's cooling unit had been drained. The gas damaged people's eyes, causing redness and sores, and it filled their lungs with fluid. The British government's safety agency counts about 2,000 people dead in the first hours. A 2005 medical review counts about 3,800 dead at once, and 15,000 to 20,000 early deaths reported over the next twenty years. At first Union Carbide's leaders tried to separate the company from legal responsibility. Later the company accepted moral responsibility and paid $470 million to the government of India.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bell System is split, 1984" -->
In the early 1980s AT&T's Bell System held about $150 billion in property and employed about a million people. Justice Department lawyers sued AT&T in 1974 for breaking antitrust law. In January 1982 the company and the department agreed that AT&T would give up its local telephone companies. Judge Harold H. Greene approved the deal with changes, and the case was closed on 24 August 1982.

On 1 January 1984 the local companies became seven regional companies, called the Baby Bells. AT&T kept its long-distance service, its research center, Bell Labs, and its equipment maker, Western Electric. It kept $34 billion of the $149.5 billion in property it had held the day before. It also shed 636,000 employees.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bill-gates" name="Bill Gates" movie="Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bill Gates

> **Who:** The head of Microsoft when federal and state lawyers sued it for keeping an illegal monopoly on the software that runs personal computers.
> **When and where:** Redmond, Washington, and Washington, D.C., 1998 to 2001.
> **Movie:** *Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates* (2019), a three-part Netflix series directed by Davis Guggenheim. It is mostly about his later charity work, and the antitrust case is not its subject.

Bill Gates ran Microsoft when a federal judge found that it held an illegal monopoly. Microsoft made Windows, the operating system that ran most personal computers. An operating system is the main program that runs a computer and the other programs on it.

On 18 May 1998 the Justice Department, the attorneys general of twenty states and the District of Columbia sued Microsoft. Their lawyers argued that the company had used its control of Windows to shut out rival web browsers. A web browser is the program people use to look at websites.

On 5 November 1999 Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found that Microsoft held a monopoly. On 7 June 2000 he ordered the company split into two, one for Windows and one for its other software. On 28 June 2001 seven judges of the federal appeals court upheld the finding that Microsoft had illegally kept its monopoly. They threw out the breakup order, partly because Jackson had talked about the case to reporters while it was still going on.

On 6 September 2001, Attorney General John Ashcroft's Justice Department said it would no longer seek a breakup. On 1 November 2001 the government settled with Microsoft. Microsoft agreed to share some of its technical information with other companies. It also accepted a panel of three investigators with full access to its systems. Microsoft lost on the law and stayed in one piece.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bill-gates" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="steve-young-vlasic" name="Steve Young" movie="Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Steve Young

> **Who:** A Vlasic pickle executive who described what it was like to sell to the largest store company in the world.
> **When and where:** The late 1990s to January 2001. His account was published in December 2003.
> **Movie:** *Is Wal-Mart Good for America?* (FRONTLINE, PBS, 2004), an investigation into Walmart's power over the companies that supply it. Young is not in it, and it covers the same subject.

Steve Young watched Walmart's buyers push his company's pickles to a price that left almost no profit. He was vice president of grocery marketing for pickles at Vlasic. The reporter Charles Fishman told his story in *Fast Company* magazine in December 2003.

In the late 1990s a Walmart buyer saw Vlasic's gallon jar of pickles. The buyer began talking about it with Pat Hunn, the head of Vlasic's Walmart sales team. The jar went into every Walmart, about 3,000 stores, at $2.97. At that price Vlasic and Walmart each made a penny or two a jar, if that. Young said each store sold about 80 jars a week, which came to 240,000 gallons of pickles a week.

Walmart was 30 percent of Vlasic's business. Shoppers who used to buy Vlasic's pickle spears and chips at supermarkets bought the cheap gallon jar instead. Young says Vlasic's profits from pickles fell by 25 percent or more.

Young says Vlasic asked to raise the price. He remembers the Walmart buyers' answer: "If you do that, all the other products of yours we buy, we'll stop buying." He called it "a clear threat." Hunn remembers the buyers saying, "We want the $2.97 gallon of pickles. If you don't do it, we'll see if someone else might." Later, Walmart's buyers let Vlasic shrink the jar to just over half a gallon, at $2.79. Vlasic filed for bankruptcy in January 2001. Everyone involved agrees the gallon jar was not the main cause.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In September 2026 three American companies, Nvidia, Apple and Alphabet, were each worth more than $4 trillion, according to the investment website The Motley Fool. In the same years federal lawyers took four of the largest technology companies to court over monopoly power. Walmart employed about 1.6 million people in the United States in 2026, by its own count. That is about two and a half times General Motors' American workforce at its peak in 1979.

A federal jury convicted Enron's former chief executives of fraud in 2006. Lehman Brothers failed in 2008, and members of Congress set aside $700 billion that year to rescue banks and other companies. The four technology companies taken to court were Google, Meta, Amazon and Apple. By September 2026 judges had ruled twice that Google broke the law and once for Meta, and the Amazon and Apple cases had not come to trial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big, in numbers" -->
A company's market value is the price of one share times the number of shares. It changes every day the stock market is open. On 29 October 2025 Nvidia became the first company worth $5 trillion, according to the business news network CNBC. On 7 September 2026, The Motley Fool listed the three most valuable companies in the world. Nvidia was worth about $5.6 trillion, Apple about $4.7 trillion and Alphabet about $4.1 trillion.

Walmart reported $713.2 billion in revenue, the money it took in, for the year that ended on 31 January 2026. Its 2026 annual report counts about 2.1 million workers around the world, about 1.6 million of them in the United States. About 92 percent of its American workers were paid by the hour. At that time it had more than 10,900 stores in 19 countries. Amazon reported in its annual filing for 2025 that it employed about 1,576,000 people at the end of that year. It also used independent contractors and temporary workers, who are not counted in that number.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fraud and harm at the top" -->
Courts found, and companies admitted in guilty pleas, that several large companies of these years had deceived the public. Enron was an energy company in Houston. On 2 December 2001 it filed for bankruptcy. Enron's workers had much of their retirement savings in Enron stock. At the end of 2000, 62 percent of the money in the company's retirement plan was in its own shares, according to the Congressional Research Service, which does research for Congress. The shares fell from more than $80 each in January 2001 to less than 70 cents in January 2002. A lawsuit charged that the company had kept its workers from moving their retirement money out of Enron stock while the price fell. On 25 May 2006 a federal jury in Houston convicted Enron's former chief executives, Kenneth L. Lay and Jeffrey K. Skilling, of conspiracy and fraud, according to the Justice Department.

On 17 August 2006 federal Judge Gladys Kessler ruled that the major tobacco companies had broken the federal racketeering law. Racketeering here means running a business through a pattern of fraud. She wrote that the companies had "marketed and sold their lethal products with zeal, with deception, with a single-minded focus on their financial success, and without regard for the human tragedy or social costs that success exacted." She ordered them to publish statements about the harm of smoking and about how addictive nicotine is.

Purdue Pharma made OxyContin, a pain pill. OxyContin is an opioid, a drug from the same family of chemicals as heroin. In May 2007 the company pleaded guilty to misleading the public about the risk of becoming addicted to OxyContin, and it agreed to pay $600 million. Its president, Michael Friedman, its top lawyer, Howard R. Udell, and its former chief medical officer, Paul D. Goldenheim, also pleaded guilty. They admitted to misbranding, which means selling a drug with false or misleading claims about it, and together they paid $34.5 million. On 24 November 2020 the company pleaded guilty to three federal felonies, which are serious crimes. No member of the Sackler family, which owned Purdue, was charged with a crime.

On 16 June 2025, 55 state and territorial attorneys general joined a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sacklers, according to the New York attorney general's office. On 28 April 2026 federal Judge Madeline Cox Arleo sentenced Purdue to pay $5.5 billion, as CNBC and Reuters reported. She said she could not send Purdue's executives or owners to prison, because the Justice Department had charged only the company. To the victims in her courtroom she said, "Your government failed you." On 1 May 2026 New York's attorney general announced that Purdue had shut down. A new company, Knoa Pharma, took over, with no role for the Sacklers.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 2008 rescue" -->
In 2008 the investment bank Lehman Brothers failed, and members of Congress voted public money to rescue banks and other companies. On 15 September 2008 Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in the country's history. It had about $639 billion in property and $619 billion in debts, according to the reference publisher EBSCO. Its chief executive was Richard S. Fuld Jr. EBSCO reports that Fuld walked away with $480 million.

In March 2010 Anton R. Valukas, an examiner named by the bankruptcy court, reported on deals that Lehman called Repo 105. Lehman's managers had used them to make the firm's books look stronger at the end of each quarter. In 2011 the Securities and Exchange Commission reported that it would be hard to prove that Lehman had broken the law. No Lehman executive was charged with a crime.

On 3 October 2008 members of Congress passed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, which allowed up to $700 billion to rescue banks and other companies. Federal officials paid out $443.5 billion in all, and most of it was paid back. In December 2023 the Government Accountability Office, which checks federal spending for Congress, put the final cost at $31.1 billion. The programs for banks made a profit of $16.3 billion. The rescue of the insurance company AIG cost $15.2 billion, the loans to car makers cost $12.1 billion and the housing programs cost $31.4 billion. The car loans began with $13.4 billion that President George W. Bush announced in December 2008.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Antitrust returns" -->
In the 2020s, government lawyers brought monopoly cases against four of the largest technology companies. By September 2026 they had won two cases against Google and lost one against Meta, and two more were waiting for trial.

On 5 August 2024 federal Judge Amit P. Mehta ruled that Google had illegally kept monopolies in general internet search and in search advertising. On 2 September 2025 he refused to make Google sell its Chrome browser or its Android phone software. On 5 December 2025 he ordered Google to share its search index and some user data with rival companies. A search index is the huge list of web pages that a search engine keeps. A committee of five technical experts was to oversee it. Google appealed. In a brief filed in late July 2026, Justice Department lawyers asked the appeals court to keep the ruling and to add a ban on Google's large payments to companies such as Apple for making Google the search engine their products use unless the user changes it, according to the news service Courthouse News.

In April 2025 federal Judge Leonie Brinkema ruled that Google had illegally monopolized the tools that websites use to sell advertising space. On 16 September 2026 her opinion on what Google must do was made public. She refused to make Google sell its advertising exchange. She ordered new rules for how the tools work. The National Law Review reported the opinion in an article by a writer for Chamber of Progress, a group funded by the technology industry.

The Federal Trade Commission's case against Meta was about its purchases of Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. The trial began on 14 April 2025. On 18 November 2025 Judge James Boasberg ruled for Meta. He found that Meta did not hold a monopoly, because TikTok and YouTube competed with it. The FTC appealed on 20 January 2026, and in August 2026 the appeals court had not yet set a date to hear the case.

On 26 September 2023 the FTC and 17 state attorneys general sued Amazon for keeping an illegal monopoly. Judge John H. Chun set the trial for 29 March 2027. He will decide the case himself, without a jury. On 21 March 2024 the Justice Department and 16 state attorneys general sued Apple for monopolizing the market for smartphones. On 30 June 2025 Judge Julien Neals refused Apple's request to throw the case out.

### Lina Khan

> **Who:** A law student whose 2017 article argued that antitrust law missed Amazon's kind of power, and who later led the Federal Trade Commission.
> **When and where:** New Haven, Connecticut, and Washington, D.C., 2017 to 2025.

Lina Khan argued that antitrust law had stopped looking at the kind of power Amazon held, and she later ran the agency that sued Amazon. In 2017, while she was a law student, she published an article called "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" in the *Yale Law Journal*. A paradox is something that seems to contradict itself. She argued that antitrust law had come to judge competition mainly by whether prices went up. That test missed the power Amazon built by accepting losses while it spread into selling, delivery, cloud computing and publishing. Amazon could use its place in one business to gain in another, even while its prices stayed low.

On 15 June 2021 members of the Senate confirmed her to the Federal Trade Commission by a vote of 69 to 28. President Joe Biden chose her as the commission's chair, and she was sworn in the same day. At 32 she was the youngest chair in the agency's history, according to CNBC. Under her, FTC lawyers sued Amazon in 2023 and carried on the case against Meta.

In 2023 the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, a research group funded partly by technology companies, published a report arguing that her 2017 analysis misread the economics of online markets. Khan served as chair until 20 January 2025, and she left the commission on 31 January 2025. The FTC lost the Meta case at trial in November 2025.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corporate money in politics" -->
Since 2010, corporations and unions have been able to spend without limit on elections, as long as they do not work directly with a candidate. On 21 January 2010, in *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the government may not limit such independent political spending. Committees called super PACs now raise and spend unlimited sums on that condition. The Brennan Center for Justice, a law and policy institute, counts about $6.4 billion that super PACs spent on federal elections from 2010 to 2022. In the 2024 election they spent at least $2.7 billion. That year, spending by groups that do not have to name their donors passed $1 billion. Such spending is called dark money.

Companies and other groups also pay lobbyists. In 2025 organizations spent $5.08 billion on lobbying the federal government, according to OpenSecrets, a group that tracks money in politics. It was the first year above $5 billion. That year 15,768 organizations reported lobbying. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce spent the most, $72.1 million.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Working for a company without being employed by it" -->
Some companies now pay people for work without hiring them as employees. Ride and delivery companies call their drivers independent contractors. An independent contractor is a worker who is paid for each job and is not counted as an employee. The federal law on the minimum wage and overtime pay, the Fair Labor Standards Act, covers employees. It does not cover independent contractors, according to the Department of Labor.

How many Americans do this kind of work depends on the question asked. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency that counts jobs, found 1.6 million people in May 2017 who got short jobs through an app or a website. That was 1.0 percent of all workers. The Pew Research Center, a research group, found in 2021 that 16 percent of American adults had ever earned money through an online job app, and 9 percent had done so in the past year.
