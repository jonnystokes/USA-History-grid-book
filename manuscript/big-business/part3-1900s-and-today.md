## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 15 minutes**

The biggest companies the country had yet seen took shape in these years, and presidents and judges began to break some of them apart. Between 1895 and 1904 owners and bankers joined more than 1,800 manufacturing firms into 157 large corporations. The largest, US Steel, issued more than a billion dollars in stock, shares sold to investors, in 1901.

**Breakups and new rules.** Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft had their lawyers sue the biggest combinations. In 1911 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that Standard Oil and the American Tobacco Company had broken the law, and under court orders both were split apart. Members of Congress passed two more antitrust laws in 1914. In 1934, after the stock market crash of 1929, they passed a law that required companies selling stock to report their finances to the public.

Soldiers of the Colorado National Guard, whose costs the mine owners paid, burned a strikers' tent camp at Ludlow in 1914, and Chicago police killed 10 people during a steel strike in 1937. During the Second World War, federal officials gave about two-thirds of the value of their main war contracts to 100 companies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The great merger movement and US Steel" -->
The 157 large combinations of 1895 to 1904 were mergers, in which two or more companies join into one, and more than 60 of them formed in 1899 alone.

Naomi Lamoreaux names the causes in her book *The Great Merger Movement in American Business*. Factories cost a great deal to build, and industries grew fast after 1887. After the depression of 1893 began, rival owners cut their prices against each other in price wars. The owners had made agreements, called pools, to hold prices up, but the price wars went on anyway.

Lamoreaux writes that "there was nothing natural or inevitable," nothing certain to happen, about these combinations. Most of them used more money and work to make the same goods than the new rivals that soon appeared, and in her words they "quickly lost their positions of market dominance," their lead in their markets. The firms that built barriers against rivals, such as US Steel through its control of iron ore, kept their lead longer.

**US Steel.** The banker J. P. Morgan put US Steel together as a corporation in New Jersey in February 1901. He bought Carnegie Steel for Andrew Carnegie's asking price of $480 million. Other companies went in with it, among them makers of steel, pipe and bridges and the Lake Superior Consolidated Iron Mines, whose ore came from Ojibwe land in Minnesota. Charles M. Schwab was president from 1901 to 1903, and Elbert H. Gary was chairman from 1901 to 1927.

**A billion dollars in stock.** US Steel was the first company in the world with more than a billion dollars in stock. It issued stock with a stated value of about $1.4 billion.

Government investigators at the federal Bureau of Corporations put the real value of what the company owned at about $682 million, about half the stock issued. So about half of the stock was watered stock, stock issued for more than the property behind it.

**Two-thirds of the steel.** US Steel made about two-thirds of all the steel in the United States at the start. In 1902 the company employed 168,000 people. By 1911 its share had fallen to less than half.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jp-morgan" name="J.P. Morgan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### J.P. Morgan

> **Who:** The Wall Street banker who joined rival companies into giant ones, among them US Steel in 1901.
> **When and where:** New York, 1901 to 1904.

**Carnegie named $480 million. Morgan said yes at once.**

J. P. Morgan, a banker on Wall Street in New York City, led a wave of mergers around 1900, joining rival companies into single giant ones.

**The steel deal.** Charles M. Schwab, then president of Carnegie Steel, gave a speech at the University Club in December 1900, and the speech led Morgan to the steel deal. Carnegie named a price of $480 million for his company, and Morgan accepted it at once. Morgan then formed US Steel in 1901 and sat on its finance committee.

**Northern Securities.** Morgan and the railroad owner James J. Hill also formed the Northern Securities Company in 1901. It was a holding company, a company that owns controlling shares in other companies and does little else. Through it they controlled three railroads: the Great Northern, the Northern Pacific, and the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy. President Theodore Roosevelt's Justice Department lawyers sued to break it up in 1902.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jp-morgan" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" name="Ida Tarbell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ida Tarbell

> **Who:** The reporter whose history of Standard Oil, pieced together from court records and sworn testimony, showed how Rockefeller took over the oil business.
> **When and where:** New York and the Pennsylvania oil region, 1900 to 1904.

**Rockefeller called her "That poisonous woman."**

Ida Tarbell spent most of five years, by her own count, tracing from public records how John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil. She began in 1900. Her father was Franklin Tarbell, one of the oil producers who had signed the 1872 pledge not to sell oil to Standard Oil.

**The records.** Tarbell read hundreds of thousands of documents from across the country, among them court records and depositions, sworn testimony taken outside a courtroom and written down. She interviewed employees and lawyers. The writer Mark Twain arranged for her to meet Henry H. Rogers, an officer of Standard Oil, and she interviewed him too.

Her *History of the Standard Oil Company* came out in 19 parts in *McClure's* magazine from November 1902 to October 1904, and as a book in two volumes in 1904.

**The widow's plant.** In one part, Tarbell told the story of a Cleveland widow, Mrs. Backus, who sold her late husband's plant for making oil for machines to Rockefeller in 1878. Tarbell wrote that he paid her next to nothing. Rockefeller's biographer Ron Chernow tells it differently: the plant was out of date, the widow asked for as much as $200,000, and Rockefeller paid $79,000, including $10,000 added on top. The two accounts of that sale do not agree.

Chernow does call Rockefeller's buyouts of his Cleveland rivals in 1872 "one of the most shameful episodes in his career." Rockefeller called Tarbell "That poisonous woman." He told his advisers, "Not a word about that misguided woman."

**What came of it.** Her series made many Americans furious with Standard Oil. Smithsonian magazine writes that this public fury is credited with the company's breakup. The breakup came after the justices of the Supreme Court ruled against Standard Oil in 1911.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-tarbell-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" name="Theodore Roosevelt" movie="TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt (American Experience, 1996)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Theodore Roosevelt

> **Who:** The president known as the trust-buster, whose lawyers won the Northern Securities case.
> **When and where:** Washington, 1901 to 1909.
> **Movie:** *TR: The Story of Theodore Roosevelt*, an American Experience documentary first shown on PBS in October 1996.

**His lawyers brought 44 antitrust suits. Taft's brought more.**

Theodore Roosevelt is known as the trust-buster, but the president after him had his lawyers bring more antitrust suits. Roosevelt's Justice Department lawyers sued the Northern Securities Company, J. P. Morgan and James J. Hill's railroad holding company, in 1902 under the Sherman Act, the federal law of 1890 against deals that block trade.

**The ruling.** The justices of the Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 for the government on March 14, 1904, and upheld the order to break the company up. Justice John M. Harlan wrote for the majority. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes disagreed, writing, "Great cases like hard cases make bad law." He meant that a case the whole country is excited about stirs up feelings that can bend the judges' thinking.

**Who brought more.** Roosevelt's lawyers brought 44 antitrust suits in seven and a half years. William Howard Taft, the next president, had his lawyers bring more in four years, at least 75. Taft's lawyers sued US Steel in 1911, and Roosevelt then accused Taft of being unable to tell the good trusts from the bad ones.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="theodore-roosevelt-big-business" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standard Oil and American Tobacco are broken up, 1911" -->
The justices of the Supreme Court ruled against Standard Oil and the American Tobacco Company in May 1911, and both companies were split apart. On May 15 they ruled that Standard Oil of New Jersey had broken the Sherman Act. Chief Justice Edward D. White wrote the court's decision. He wrote that the Sherman Act banned only "unreasonable" limits on trade, a test called the rule of reason. The justices gave the company's owners six months to break it up, and Standard Oil was split into 34 separate companies.

**Richer after the breakup.** Rockefeller was worth about $300 million in May 1911 and more than $900 million by the end of 1913, three times as much. According to Chernow, Roosevelt said that Wall Street's prayer was now "Oh Merciful Providence, give us another dissolution," asking God for another breakup. In 1916 newspaper headlines called Rockefeller the world's first billionaire. Companies that grew out of Standard Oil later became Exxon, Mobil and Chevron.

**Tobacco.** The justices ruled against the American Tobacco Company on May 29, 1911. They found that James B. Duke, Benjamin N. Duke, George W. Watts, R. J. Reynolds and others had broken the Sherman Act "by seeking to monopolize practically all sectors of the tobacco industry." Federal judges in New York divided the company's property in November 1911 among a new American Tobacco Company, Liggett and Myers, P. Lorillard, R. J. Reynolds and others. James B. Duke's tie to American Tobacco ended that year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two new laws, 1914" -->
Members of Congress passed two laws in 1914 that added to the Sherman Act. President Woodrow Wilson signed the first, the Federal Trade Commission Act, on September 26, 1914. Under it, "unfair methods of competition" became illegal, and the members of a new Federal Trade Commission had the power to make companies obey that rule. The commission opened on March 16, 1915.

**The Clayton Act.** The second law named business practices the Sherman Act had left vague. Wilson signed it, the Clayton Antitrust Act, on October 15, 1914. It banned price discrimination, charging different buyers different prices for the same goods. It also banned deals that tied a buyer to one seller, mergers that cut down competition, and the same people sitting on the boards of competing companies.

The Clayton Act also stated "that the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce," not a thing to be bought and sold. Under that section, federal lawyers and judges could no longer treat unions as illegal combinations, as they had used the Sherman Act against the railroad workers of the Pullman boycott in 1894.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ludlow, 1914" -->
Soldiers of the Colorado National Guard, the state's part-time army, burned the tent camp of striking coal miners at Ludlow, Colorado, on April 20, 1914. The mine owners were paying the Guard's costs. At least 19 people were killed, 13 of them women and children. Most of the strikers worked for the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, whose main stockholder, the person who owned the most shares, was John D. Rockefeller Jr.

**The strike.** The miners struck in September 1913. In October men in a company armored car known as the "Death Special" fired on their tent camp at Forbes and killed one person. Governor Elias M. Ammons sent the Colorado National Guard.

**The fire.** Fighting broke out at the tent camp at Ludlow on April 20, 1914. Nobody knows who fired the first shot. Guardsmen set the tents on fire. Thirteen women and children had hidden in a pit dug under a tent, and they suffocated there, dying for lack of air. One guardsman and five miners were also killed in the fighting. Guardsmen shot the strike leader Louis Tikas three times in the back.

President Wilson sent the US Army on April 28. The strike ended in December 1914, and the union won none of its goals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaded gasoline, 1924 to 1926" -->
The leaders of three of the largest companies in the country kept selling a lead fuel additive in the 1920s after workers who made it died. The additive was tetraethyl lead, a liquid made with lead. Researchers at General Motors added it to gasoline to stop engine knock, a pinging sound in the engine. General Motors and Standard Oil of New Jersey formed the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation in August 1924 to sell it, and DuPont made it. Chapter 13 tells the whole story of the additive.

**The dead.** Workers died at plants and a laboratory of all three companies. Seven men died and 33 went to the hospital after working at Standard Oil's Bayway refinery in Elizabeth, New Jersey, in October 1924, by the count of Bill Kovarik, who wrote a history of leaded gasoline in 2005. Other accounts count five dead. Kovarik counts ten more dead at a DuPont plant and at least two dead at General Motors' laboratory in Dayton, Ohio.

Poisoned workers at Bayway were "black and blue from uncontrolled muscle spasms." Some had to be tied into straitjackets, coats that hold a person's arms tight against the body.

**"A gift from heaven."** The Surgeon General, the head of the federal public health service, called a meeting about the additive on May 20, 1925. Frank Howard of Standard Oil said there that the industry could not give up "what has come to the industry like a gift from heaven." The companies kept selling it. Robert Kehoe, a scientist paid by the Ethyl company, then set out a theory of a "safe level" of lead that the companies' leaders relied on for decades.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who ran the corporations? 1932" -->
Hired managers, not owners, ran the 200 largest corporations outside banking, two writers showed in 1932, and those 200 held about half of the country's corporate wealth. Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means counted them in their book *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*. By 1930 the 200 held 49.2 percent of the country's corporate wealth. The scholar Gerald F. Davis later worked out that at the same pace they would have held all of it by 1959.

Berle and Means also found that the ownership of these companies was split among many scattered shareholders, so the managers made the decisions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Securities and Exchange Commission, 1934" -->
After the stock market crash of 1929, members of Congress passed a law that required companies selling stock to report their finances to the public. President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it, the Securities Exchange Act, on June 6, 1934. Under it, the members of a new Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC, watched over the stock markets. The yearly and quarterly reports that such companies still file with the SEC began with this law.

**The fox and the hen house.** Roosevelt made Joseph P. Kennedy the first chairman of the SEC in the summer of 1934. Kennedy had made money in the stock market using the same practices the new law banned, and he had given, lent and raised money for Roosevelt's 1932 campaign.

Newspaper writers said the choice was "like putting the fox in the hen house." In private, Roosevelt answered, "set a thief to catch a thief." Both sayings meant the same thing: Roosevelt had put a man who had used those practices in charge of stopping them. Kennedy resigned in September 1935.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spies, guns and company police, 1936 to 1940" -->
Senators on a committee led by Senator Robert M. La Follette Jr. of Wisconsin held hearings from 1936 to 1940 on how employers fought unions. Witnesses told the senators that company managers had planted spies inside unions and hired their own police forces. The managers had also stored up weapons, among them submachine guns, rifles and tear gas.

**Memorial Day, 1937.** Several hundred men, women and children marched toward the Republic Steel plant in Chicago on Memorial Day, May 30, 1937, to support striking steelworkers. Republic Steel's chairman was Tom M. Girdler.

At the plant, 264 Chicago police threw tear gas at the marchers, fired their guns and beat them with clubs. The police killed 10 people and shot 40, and more than 90 people were hurt in all. The senators on the La Follette committee found that the police had used "excessive force," far more force than was needed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hawaii's Big Five" -->
Five companies in Hawaii, known as the Big Five, controlled about 94 percent of the islands' sugar crop by 1920, by the count of the historian Carol MacLennan. Two of them were Castle and Cooke and Alexander and Baldwin.

The same people sat on the boards of several of the firms, and together they controlled shipping, banking and stores in the islands. Four of the firms together owned nearly three-quarters of the Matson shipping line, which carried 80 percent of the cargo between the mainland and Hawaii.

**The overthrow.** Hawaii was a kingdom with its own rulers until 1893. On January 17 of that year, a group of mostly American businessmen calling themselves the Committee of Safety moved against Queen Liliʻuokalani. John L. Stevens, the United States minister to Hawaii, was the American government's representative there. He had 162 US sailors and Marines brought ashore from a warship.

The queen's soldiers and police did not fight. She gave up her power under protest, to the United States rather than to the committee. Chapter 23, America and the World, tells the overthrow in full.

Some children of Big Five partners were members of the Committee of Safety, which then pressed for the United States to annex the islands, or take them over. The Big Five's plantations stood on the land of Native Hawaiians, the Kanaka Maoli. From 1893 into the 1950s, the owners of the Big Five dominated Hawaii's economy and government.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War contracts, 1940 to 1944" -->
A small number of large companies received most of the government's war business during the Second World War. Robert Higgs, writing for the Independent Institute in 1995, gives the figures for prime contracts, contracts made directly between the government and a company, from 1940 to 1944. The 100 companies with the most contract money received about two-thirds of the value of those contracts, the top 10 received about 30 percent, and General Motors alone received nearly 8 percent.

Federal officials also paid for new factories and rented them to private companies. Just 26 companies had the use of half the value of all those government-paid factories as of June 30, 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1950" -->
**If you remember one thing:** Judges split up Standard Oil and American Tobacco in 1911, but the biggest corporations kept growing through the next forty years.
**One number:** 49.2 percent. By 1930 the 200 largest corporations outside banking held that share of the country's corporate wealth, and at the same pace, Gerald F. Davis worked out, they would have held all of it by 1959.
**Next:** After 1950 the largest companies had hundreds of thousands of workers each, and their leaders organized to win influence in Washington.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 15 minutes**

Companies grew so large in these years that General Motors had 618,365 workers in the United States in 1979, and the Bell telephone system had more than a million that year. From the 1970s, business leaders organized to win influence in Washington. By 1982 more than 2,400 firms paid lobbyists registered there to persuade lawmakers.

**Products and disease.** The leaders of some companies kept selling products that studies linked to disease and death. Tobacco companies paid for advertisements in 1954 in which their leaders disputed studies linking smoking to lung cancer, and in 1994 heads of tobacco companies told a House hearing that they did not believe nicotine is addictive.

**Breakups.** AT&T's leaders agreed with federal lawyers to split up the Bell System, and the split took effect in 1984. Federal lawyers won a ruling in 1999 that Microsoft held a monopoly, and in 2001 an appeals court agreed that Microsoft had broken the law to keep it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="General Motors" -->
General Motors was the country's largest car maker in the 1950s, and in 1953 it sold more to the armed forces than any other company. In 1954 it sold about half of all new cars in the country, 54 percent by the Associated Press's count.

**A billion dollars.** In 1955 GM became the first company to earn more than a billion dollars in profit in one year. In the first three quarters of that year alone it made $912,887,537 in profit. *TIME* magazine wrote, "Never before in history has one corporation made so much in so short a time."

**"Good for General Motors."** President Dwight D. Eisenhower chose GM's president, Charles E. Wilson, as Secretary of Defense in January 1953. Wilson gave up a salary of $600,000 a year for one of $22,000. He owned 40,000 shares of GM stock, and GM sold more to the armed forces than any other company. As Secretary of Defense he could make decisions that helped or hurt GM, so at his hearing senators pressed him on that conflict.

Senator Robert C. Hendrickson asked whether Wilson could make a decision that went strongly against GM's interest. Wilson answered, "Yes, sir, I could." But he said he could not imagine one, "because, for years, I thought what was good for our country was good for General Motors, and vice versa," meaning the other way around. People soon repeated his words as "What is good for General Motors is good for the country."

**Ralph Nader.** Ralph Nader criticized the safety of GM's Corvair car in a 1965 book called *Unsafe at Any Speed*. GM managers hired private detectives to look into Nader's private life, and in 1966 GM's president, James M. Roche, apologized to a Senate committee. That same year members of Congress passed the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act, the first federal rules for how cars must be built.

Nader sued GM for invading his privacy and settled for $425,000, which he used to start groups that work for consumers. GM stopped making the Corvair in 1969.

**Workers in 1979.** GM's workforce in the United States peaked at 618,365 in 1979. AT&T's Bell telephone system had more workers than GM that year. By AT&T's own count, it employed 1,029,905 people at the end of 1979.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Eisenhower's warning, 1961" -->
President Dwight D. Eisenhower warned the country about the power of the arms makers and the armed forces together in his farewell speech, on January 17, 1961. "In the councils of government," he said, "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex."

By the military-industrial complex he meant the ties between the armed forces and the companies that made their weapons. He meant that the people making the government's decisions had to keep the arms makers and the armed forces from gaining power they had no good reason to have, whether they went looking for it or not. The weapons makers were the same kinds of large companies that had received most of the war contracts of 1940 to 1944.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What was a conglomerate?" -->
A conglomerate was one company that owned many businesses that had nothing to do with each other. Harold Geneen ran the company ITT from 1959 to 1977, and in those years ITT bought more than 250 companies in more than 80 countries. They included the Sheraton hotel chain, Avis car rental, the Hartford insurance company and Continental Baking, the maker of Wonder Bread.

ITT's yearly sales grew from about $700 million to $17 billion, and it became the eleventh-largest corporation in the country. Geneen believed that good managers could make a success of any business.

**ABC and Chile.** ITT's leaders tried to buy the ABC television network for $700 million in 1966, and federal antitrust officials stopped the deal. In Chile, officials under President Salvador Allende took over ITT's company there. ITT's leaders later admitted that the company had given the CIA, the American spy agency, $350,000 for "political" purposes in Chile.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Business organizes in Washington" -->
Heads of large companies built up their own political power in Washington in the 1970s.

**The Powell memo.** The lawyer Lewis F. Powell Jr. sent a private memo to Eugene B. Sydnor Jr. of the US Chamber of Commerce, a national group of businesses, on August 23, 1971. Its title was "Attack On American Free Enterprise System." Powell wrote that critics of business now came from "the college campus, the pulpit (preachers in churches), the media" and from politicians. He urged business leaders to keep "constant surveillance," or a close watch, on textbooks and television programs.

President Richard Nixon nominated Powell to the Supreme Court on October 21, 1971, less than two months later. The newspaper columnist Jack Anderson made the memo public in September 1972. That year the Chamber's leaders set up a 40-person working group on the memo, and in late 1973 they approved plans for business to act in education, in communications, in politics and in the courts.

**The Business Roundtable.** Three groups of company heads joined to form the Business Roundtable on October 13, 1972. Its founders included John D. Harper of Alcoa and Roger Blough, the retired head of US Steel. They set out to fight the growing power of unions, especially construction unions.

**Offices and lobbyists.** Company heads opened offices in Washington, and by 1978 more than 500 corporations had one. They also hired lobbyists to persuade lawmakers. Firms with registered lobbyists went from 175 in 1971 to 2,445 in 1982, about 14 times as many.

**Money for candidates.** Companies also set up political action committees, or PACs, groups that collect money and give it to candidates. Officials of the Federal Election Commission counted 89 company PACs at the end of 1974 and 1,206 at the end of 1980, more than 13 times as many.

**Rules removed.** In the same years, members of Congress voted to take federal rules off three industries, and President Jimmy Carter signed each law. The airline law of 1978 began ending federal officials' control over which routes airlines flew and what they charged, and the federal board that had set them closed in 1984.

The banking law of 1980 ended, over six years, the limits on the interest banks could pay savers. The trucking law of 1980 let trucking companies set many of their own prices and made it easier for new companies to start. Each law took power over prices from federal officials and gave it to the companies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What did the companies say when their products hurt people?" -->
Heads of tobacco companies publicly disputed the evidence that smoking causes disease and that nicotine is addictive for forty years, from 1954 to 1994. The major American tobacco companies ran an advertisement called "A Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers" in more than 400 newspapers on January 4, 1954, and it reached about 43 million people.

In it, the companies' leaders disputed new scientific studies that linked smoking cigarettes to lung cancer. The public relations firm Hill and Knowlton, a company paid to shape what the public thinks of its clients, designed it.

**The 1963 memo.** Addison Yeaman, the chief lawyer of the Brown and Williamson tobacco company, wrote a memo on July 17, 1963. To protect its earnings, he wrote, the industry must "either a) disprove the theory of causal relationship or b) discover the carcinogen or carcinogens...and demonstrate our ability to remove or neutralize them." He meant that the companies had to either show that smoking does not cause cancer, or find the substances in smoke that do cause it and show they could take them out or make them harmless.

**The 1994 hearing.** Representative Ron Wyden asked seven tobacco company heads whether nicotine is addictive at a hearing in the House of Representatives on April 14, 1994. William Campbell of Philip Morris answered, "I believe nicotine is not addictive, yes." James Johnston of R. J. Reynolds said cigarettes "clearly do not meet the classic definition of addiction." Joseph Taddeo of US Tobacco said, "I don't believe that nicotine or our products are addictive."

**The settlement.** Four cigarette makers, Philip Morris, R. J. Reynolds, Brown and Williamson and Lorillard, signed an agreement on November 23, 1998, with the attorneys general, the chief lawyers, of 46 states. The attorneys general had sued to get back what their states spent treating sick smokers. The companies' leaders agreed to pay at least $206 billion over the first 25 years and to limit their advertising.

**Asbestos.** Asbestos is a mineral that separates into thin, strong fibers, and builders used it to fireproof and insulate buildings. When people breathe asbestos fibers in, the fibers can stay in the lungs for years. They scar the lungs and can cause asbestosis, a lung disease that brings shortness of breath, coughing and lasting lung damage. They can also cause mesothelioma, a cancer of the thin linings of the chest and belly. The illness can take 10 to 40 years or more to show.

Johns-Manville was once the largest maker of asbestos products in the country. Andrew Hansen began working at a shipyard in 1940, tearing old insulation out of ships and putting new insulation in. Most of it was made by Johns-Manville and contained asbestos. In 1978, at 65, he was found to have asbestosis and mesothelioma, and he died 13 months later. His widow sued the company.

**What the company knew.** Dr. Kenneth Smith, Johns-Manville's medical director until 1966, gave sworn testimony in the case. He said that in the 1940s he had told the company's leaders about the danger asbestos posed to insulation workers and had urged warnings on its insulation products, and that they did not act on his advice.

Johns-Manville's lawyers answered that the company began putting warnings on its products in 1964, as soon as science proved a link between its products and asbestosis. The jury found for Hansen's widow, and in 1984 a federal appeals court upheld that finding against the company.

By 1981, while her case was still in the courts, Johns-Manville faced 9,300 lawsuits brought by 12,800 people who sued over illness from asbestos. Its leaders filed for bankruptcy, a court process for a company that cannot pay its debts, in August 1982. Under the filing, all the injury lawsuits against the company were put on hold. By the end of 1988, more than 12,600 claims, almost $500 million in all, had been paid from a fund set up in the bankruptcy to pay for injuries.

**Bhopal.** More than 40 tons of a poison gas called methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India, a factory that made poisons to kill insects, on December 3, 1984. Union Carbide India Limited, part of the American company Union Carbide, ran the plant.

**How it happened.** Union Carbide's leaders had planned to close the plant, which was losing money. Instead they cut many jobs to run it for less, among them maintenance engineers. In June 1984 the unit that kept the gas cold was shut down, to cut the plant's use of electricity by 18 percent. Weeks before the leak, the machine meant to clean escaping gas was turned off too: on October 23 by the date in a report by France's environment ministry, or three weeks before by a 2005 medical review.

On December 2, 1984, the plant's senior managers ordered some pipes cleaned with water. A broken valve let about a ton of that water into a tank holding 40 tons of the gas, and on December 3 the gas escaped into the air. Neither report names the people who turned off the gas-cleaning machine or shut down the cooling unit.

**What the gas did.** The gas damaged people's eyes, causing redness and sores, and it filled their lungs with fluid. It killed about 2,000 people in the first hours, according to Britain's Health and Safety Executive, a government safety agency. The 2005 medical review, by Edward Broughton, gives about 3,800 dead at once, and 15,000 to 20,000 early deaths reported over the next twenty years.

At first Union Carbide's leaders tried to keep the company free of legal blame. Later they said the company accepted "moral responsibility," and the company paid $470 million to the government of India.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bell System is split, 1984" -->
AT&T's Bell System held about $150 billion in property and employed about a million people in the early 1980s. Justice Department lawyers had sued AT&T in 1974. In January 1982 AT&T's leaders and the Justice Department's lawyers agreed that AT&T would give up its local telephone companies.

In return, a 1956 court order that had kept AT&T out of the computer business was lifted, and most newspapers reported the deal as a win for AT&T. Judge Harold H. Greene approved the deal with changes, and the case was closed on August 24, 1982.

**The Baby Bells.** The local companies became seven regional companies, called the Baby Bells, on January 1, 1984. AT&T kept its long-distance service, its research center, Bell Labs, and its equipment maker, Western Electric. It kept $34 billion of the $149.5 billion in property it had held the day before, less than a quarter, and it had 636,000 fewer workers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bill-gates" name="Bill Gates" movie="Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates (2019)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Bill Gates

> **Who:** The head of Microsoft when federal and state lawyers sued it for keeping an illegal monopoly on the software that runs personal computers.
> **When and where:** Redmond, Washington, and Washington, D.C., 1998 to 2001.
> **Movie:** *Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates* (2019), a Netflix series directed by Davis Guggenheim.

**A judge ordered his company split in two.**

Bill Gates founded Microsoft and ran it as its chief executive until January 2000, so he was in charge when a federal judge found that it held an illegal monopoly. Microsoft made Windows, the operating system, or main program, that ran most personal computers and the other programs on them.

**The case.** Lawyers for the Justice Department, the attorneys general of twenty states and the District of Columbia sued Microsoft on May 18, 1998. The main question was whether Microsoft could bundle its web browser, Internet Explorer, the program people use to look at websites, with Windows. To bundle it meant to build it into Windows, which made it harder for rival browsers such as Netscape Navigator to win users.

**On tape.** Gates answered the government lawyers' questions in a deposition that was filmed on videotape. A person who was there called him "evasive and nonresponsive," dodging the questions. According to *Businessweek* magazine, the government lawyers used snippets of emails to show that many of his denials were false.

**The rulings.** Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found on November 5, 1999, that Microsoft held a monopoly. On June 7, 2000, he ordered the company split into two, one for Windows and one for its other software. On June 28, 2001, seven judges of the federal appeals court agreed that Microsoft had illegally kept its monopoly, but they threw out the breakup order, partly because Jackson had talked about the case to reporters while it was still going on.

**No breakup.** Attorney General John Ashcroft announced on September 6, 2001, that Justice Department lawyers would no longer seek a breakup. The government's lawyers settled with Microsoft on November 1, 2001. Microsoft's leaders agreed to share some of the company's technical information with other companies and accepted a panel of three investigators with full access to its systems.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bill-gates" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="steve-young-vlasic" name="Steve Young" movie="Is Wal-Mart Good for America? (FRONTLINE, 2004)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Steve Young

> **Who:** A Vlasic pickle executive who described what it was like to sell to Walmart.
> **When and where:** The late 1990s to January 2001. His account was published in December 2003.
> **Movie:** *Is Wal-Mart Good for America?* (FRONTLINE, PBS, 2004), a documentary about Walmart.

**Walmart sold a gallon of Vlasic pickles for $2.97.**

Steve Young watched Walmart's buyers push his company's pickles to a price that left almost no profit. He was vice president of grocery marketing for pickles at Vlasic. The reporter Charles Fishman told his story in *Fast Company* magazine in December 2003.

**The jar.** A Walmart buyer saw Vlasic's gallon jar of pickles in the late 1990s and began talking about it with Pat Hunn, the head of Vlasic's Walmart sales team. The jar went into every Walmart, about 3,000 stores, at $2.97. At that price Vlasic and Walmart each made a penny or two a jar, if that. Young said each store sold about 80 jars a week, which came to 240,000 gallons of pickles a week.

Walmart was 30 percent of Vlasic's business. Young says shoppers who used to buy Vlasic's pickle spears and chips at supermarkets bought the cheap gallon jar instead, and Vlasic's profits from pickles fell by 25 percent or more.

**The threat.** Young says Vlasic's managers asked to raise the price. He remembers the Walmart buyers' answer: "If you do that, all the other products of yours we buy, we'll stop buying." He called it "a clear threat." Hunn remembers the buyers saying, "We want the $2.97 gallon of pickles. If you don't do it, we'll see if someone else might."

Later, Walmart's buyers let Vlasic shrink the jar to just over half a gallon, at $2.79. Vlasic filed for bankruptcy in January 2001, though everyone involved agrees that the gallon jar was not a main cause.

**Why this story is here:** A buyer as large as Walmart could name its price, even a price that left the seller almost nothing.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 12 minutes**

Three American companies, Nvidia, Apple and Alphabet, the company that owns Google, were each worth more than $4 trillion in September 2026. In the same years government lawyers took four of the largest technology companies to court over monopoly power. Walmart employed about 1.6 million people in the United States in 2026, by its own count, about 2.6 times General Motors' American workforce at its peak in 1979.

**Fraud and a rescue.** A federal jury convicted Enron's former chief executives of fraud in 2006. Purdue Pharma, the maker of the pain pill OxyContin, pleaded guilty to federal crimes in 2007 and again in 2020. Lehman Brothers failed in 2008, and members of Congress set aside $700 billion that year to rescue banks and other companies.

**The four cases.** The four technology companies taken to court were Google, Meta, Amazon and Apple. By September 2026 judges had ruled twice that Google broke the law and once for Meta, and the Amazon and Apple cases had not come to trial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How big are the biggest companies?" -->
Nvidia became the first company worth $5 trillion on October 29, 2025, according to the business news network CNBC. A company's worth, its market value, is the price of one share times the number of shares, and it changes every day the stock market is open. The investment website The Motley Fool listed the three most valuable companies in the world on September 7, 2026: Nvidia at about $5.6 trillion, Apple at about $4.7 trillion and Alphabet at about $4.1 trillion.

**Workers.** Walmart reported $713.2 billion in revenue, the money it took in, for the year that ended on January 31, 2026. Its 2026 annual report records about 2.1 million workers around the world, about 1.6 million of them in the United States, and about 92 percent of its American workers were paid by the hour. At that time it had more than 10,900 stores in 19 countries.

Amazon's yearly report to the government for 2025 states that it employed about 1,576,000 people at the end of that year. It also used independent contractors, workers paid for each job who are not employees, and temporary workers, and neither group is counted in that number.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fraud and harm at the top" -->
Judges and juries found that leaders of several large companies of these years had deceived the public, and Purdue Pharma's leaders admitted it in guilty pleas.

**Enron.** Enron, an energy company in Houston, Texas, filed for bankruptcy on December 2, 2001. Its workers had much of their retirement savings in Enron stock. At the end of 2000, 62 percent of the money in the company's retirement plan was in its own shares, according to the Congressional Research Service, which does research for Congress.

The shares fell from more than $80 each in January 2001 to less than 70 cents in January 2002, less than a hundredth of their old price. A lawsuit claimed that Enron had blocked its workers from moving their retirement money out of Enron stock while the price fell.

A federal jury in Houston convicted Enron's former chief executives, Kenneth L. Lay and Jeffrey K. Skilling, on May 25, 2006. The jury found them guilty of conspiracy, planning a crime together with others, and of fraud, lying or cheating to get money. According to the Justice Department, the two men had lied to investors and others to make Enron look healthy, as if it were growing steadily and had no large debts or losses. Their lies pushed up the price of Enron's stock.

**Tobacco in court.** Federal Judge Gladys Kessler ruled on August 17, 2006, that the major tobacco companies had broken the federal racketeering law, the law against running a business through a pattern of fraud. She found that the companies had "defrauded the American people by lying for decades about the health risks of smoking and their marketing to children."

She wrote that they had "marketed and sold their lethal products with zeal, with deception," meaning with great eagerness and with lies, and that they had cared only about their profits, "without regard for the human tragedy or social costs that success exacted," the price other people paid for it. She ordered them to publish statements about the harm of smoking and about how addictive nicotine is.

**Purdue Pharma.** Purdue Pharma made OxyContin, a pain pill and an opioid, a drug from the same family of chemicals as heroin. The first wave of opioid overdose deaths, from 1999, came from prescription opioids, after doctors wrote more opioid prescriptions in the 1990s, according to the federal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. CDC researchers count about 806,000 Americans who died of opioid overdoses from 1999 to 2023, from all kinds of opioids.

**Guilty pleas.** Purdue pleaded guilty in May 2007 to misleading the public about the risk of becoming addicted to OxyContin, and it agreed to pay $600 million. Its president, Michael Friedman, its top lawyer, Howard R. Udell, and its former chief medical officer, Paul D. Goldenheim, also pleaded guilty. They admitted to misbranding, selling a drug with false or misleading claims about it, and together they paid $34.5 million.

Purdue pleaded guilty again on November 24, 2020, to three federal felonies, which are serious crimes. No member of the Sackler family, which owned Purdue, was charged with a crime.

Attorneys general of 55 states and territories joined a $7.4 billion settlement with Purdue and the Sacklers on June 16, 2025, according to the New York attorney general's office. Federal Judge Madeline Cox Arleo sentenced Purdue to pay $5.5 billion on April 28, 2026, as CNBC and Reuters reported. She said she could not send Purdue's executives or owners to prison, because Justice Department lawyers had charged only the company.

More than 40 people spoke at the sentencing. Judge Arleo said, "Your government failed you." New York's attorney general announced on May 1, 2026, that Purdue had shut down. A new company, Knoa Pharma, took over, with no role for the Sacklers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 2008 rescue" -->
The investment bank Lehman Brothers failed in 2008, and members of Congress voted public money to rescue banks and other companies.

**Lehman Brothers.** Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in the country's history on September 15, 2008. It had about $639 billion in property and $619 billion in debts, according to the reference publisher EBSCO. Its chief executive was Richard S. Fuld Jr.

Investigators later looked into whether Lehman's executives, Fuld among them, had moved money and raised their own pay just before the bankruptcy. EBSCO's entry states that Fuld "walked away with $480 million" but does not say over what years he received it.

**Repo 105.** Anton R. Valukas, an investigator named by the bankruptcy court, reported in March 2010 on deals that Lehman's managers called Repo 105. They had used the deals to make the firm's books look stronger at the end of each quarter, the three-month periods for which companies report their results. Officials of the Securities and Exchange Commission reported in 2011 that it would be hard to prove that Lehman had broken the law. No Lehman executive was charged with a crime.

**The rescue.** Members of Congress passed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act on October 3, 2008. It allowed up to $700 billion to rescue banks and other companies. Federal officials paid out $443.5 billion in all, and most of it was paid back. Auditors at the Government Accountability Office, which checks federal spending for Congress, put the final cost at $31.1 billion in December 2023.

Some parts of the rescue made money, and others lost it. The money put into banks under the Capital Purchase Program earned a profit of $16.3 billion. The rescue of the insurance company AIG, the loans to car makers and the housing programs all cost money, and the housing programs cost the most. The car loans began with $13.4 billion that President George W. Bush announced in December 2008.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Antitrust returns" -->
Government lawyers brought monopoly cases against four of the largest technology companies in the 2020s. By September 2026 they had won two cases against Google and lost one against Meta, and two more were waiting for trial. Neither judge in the Google cases ordered Google to sell off part of its business.

**Google search.** Federal Judge Amit P. Mehta ruled on August 5, 2024, that Google had illegally kept monopolies in general internet search and in search advertising. On September 2, 2025, he refused to make Google sell its Chrome browser or its Android phone software. Then, on December 5, 2025, he ordered Google to share its search index, the huge list of web pages a search engine keeps, and some user data with rival companies. A committee of five technical experts was to oversee it.

Google's lawyers appealed. In late July 2026 Justice Department lawyers filed a brief, a written argument, with the appeals court, according to the news service Courthouse News. They asked the court to keep the ruling and to ban Google's large payments to companies such as Apple. Under those deals, Google is the search engine that the companies' products use unless the user changes it.

**Google advertising.** In a second case, federal Judge Leonie Brinkema ruled in April 2025 that Google had illegally monopolized the tools that websites use to sell advertising space. Her opinion on what Google must do was made public on September 16, 2026. She refused to make Google sell its advertising exchange, the online market where websites sell ad space to advertisers, and ordered new rules for how the tools work.

The National Law Review reported the opinion in an article by a writer for Chamber of Progress, a group funded by the technology industry.

**Meta.** The Federal Trade Commission's case against Meta was about its purchases of Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. The trial began on April 14, 2025, and Judge James Boasberg ruled for Meta on November 18, 2025. He found that Meta did not hold a monopoly, because TikTok and YouTube competed with it. FTC lawyers appealed on January 20, 2026, and in August 2026 the appeals judges had not yet set a date to hear the case.

**Amazon and Apple.** FTC lawyers and 17 state attorneys general sued Amazon on September 26, 2023, for keeping an illegal monopoly. Judge John H. Chun set the trial for March 29, 2027, and he will decide the case himself, without a jury. Justice Department lawyers and 16 state attorneys general sued Apple on March 21, 2024, for monopolizing the market for smartphones. On June 30, 2025, Judge Julien Neals refused Apple's request to throw the case out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lina-khan" name="Lina Khan" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lina Khan

> **Who:** A law student whose 2017 article argued that the people who enforce antitrust law were missing Amazon's kind of power, and who later led the Federal Trade Commission.
> **When and where:** New Haven, Connecticut, and Washington, D.C., 2017 to 2025.

**At 32 she became the youngest head of the FTC.**

Lina Khan argued that the judges and officials who enforce antitrust law had stopped looking at the kind of power Amazon held, and she later ran the agency that sued Amazon. She published her argument in the *Yale Law Journal* in 2017, while she was a student at Yale Law School, under the title "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox," a paradox being something that seems to contradict itself.

**Her argument.** She wrote that those judges and officials had come to judge competition mainly by whether prices went up. In her view, that test missed the power Amazon's leaders built by accepting losses while the company spread into selling, delivery, publishing and cloud computing, renting out computer storage and computing power over the internet. They could use the company's place in one business to gain in another.

**The diapers.** Her article gave an example. In 2009 Amazon's leaders wanted to buy Quidsi, the company that ran the website Diapers.com, and Quidsi's owners said no. Amazon then cut its prices for diapers and other baby goods by up to 30 percent. Quidsi's executives worked out that Amazon was on track to lose $100 million in three months on diapers alone. Amazon bought Quidsi the next year, and by February 2012 it had cut the discounts it gave parents from 30 percent to 20 percent, she wrote.

**At the FTC.** Members of the Senate confirmed her to the Federal Trade Commission by a vote of 69 to 28 on June 15, 2021. President Joe Biden chose her as the commission's chair, and she was sworn in the same day. At 32 she was the youngest chair in the agency's history, according to CNBC. Under her, FTC lawyers sued Amazon in 2023 and carried on the case against Meta.

**Her critics.** Robert D. Atkinson and Michael R. Ward of the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, a research group funded partly by technology companies, argued in a 2023 report that her 2017 article misread how online markets work. Khan served as chair until January 20, 2025.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lina-khan" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Corporate money in politics" -->
Corporations and unions have been able to spend without limit on elections since 2010, as long as they do not work directly with a candidate. The justices of the Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 on January 21, 2010, in *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, that "the Government may not suppress political speech based on the speaker's corporate identity." That meant the government could not silence speech about politics just because a corporation was doing the speaking.

**The case.** The case began with a film that a non-profit corporation had made to attack Hillary Clinton. Federal law had barred the group from advertising it close to the election.

**Super PACs.** After the ruling, committees called super PACs could raise and spend unlimited sums. Researchers at the Brennan Center for Justice, a law and policy institute, count about $6.4 billion that super PACs spent on federal elections from 2010 to 2022. In the 2024 election they spent at least $2.7 billion. That year, spending by groups that do not have to name their donors passed $1 billion, spending people call dark money.

**Lobbying.** Companies and other groups also pay lobbyists. Organizations spent $5.08 billion on lobbying the federal government in 2025, the first year above $5 billion, according to OpenSecrets, a group that tracks money in politics. That year 15,768 organizations reported lobbying, and the US Chamber of Commerce spent the most, $72.1 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Working for a company without being employed by it" -->
Some companies now pay people for work without hiring them as employees. Ride and delivery companies call their drivers independent contractors. The federal law on the minimum wage and overtime pay is the Fair Labor Standards Act, and its rules apply to employees but not to independent contractors, according to the Department of Labor.

**How many.** The count of Americans who do this kind of work depends on the question asked. Researchers at the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency that counts jobs, found 1.6 million people in May 2017 who found work through an app or a website, 1.0 percent of all workers. Researchers at the Pew Research Center found in 2021 that 16 percent of American adults had ever earned money through an online job app, and 9 percent had done so in the past year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- How did a few companies come to control whole industries? Their owners made secret deals with railroads, bought out rivals and joined companies into trusts and mergers. By 1880 Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of the country's oil.
- What did owners do when their workers went on strike? They locked workers out and hired armed guards, and officials sent soldiers and court orders. At Homestead in 1892 Frick's Pinkerton men fought the steelworkers, and at Ludlow in 1914 soldiers paid by the mine owners burned the strikers' tent camp.
- Did breaking up a giant company ever work? Not clearly. Judges split Standard Oil into 34 companies in 1911, yet Rockefeller was three times as rich by 1913. AT&T gave up its local telephone companies in 1984 in a deal most newspapers called a win for AT&T, the order to split Microsoft was thrown out in 2001, and judges in 2025 and 2026 would not make Google sell off parts of its business.

**What changed:** In 1500 no company existed in this land, and by 2026 three American companies were each worth more than $4 trillion.

**Five dates to keep**
- May 13, 1607: The Virginia Company's settlers found Jamestown.
- December 16, 1773: Colonists dump the East India Company's tea into Boston Harbor.
- July 2, 1890: The Sherman Antitrust Act becomes law.
- May 15, 1911: The justices of the Supreme Court rule against Standard Oil.
- January 21, 2010: The justices decide *Citizens United*, on corporate spending in elections.

**Not known:** No record shows which Acadians died at sea on the ships Thomas Hancock's firm rented out in 1755, or how many.

**Where to go:** Lowell National Historical Park stands in Lowell, Massachusetts, the mill town the Boston Associates built.
