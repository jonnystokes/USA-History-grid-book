## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Cotton grown by enslaved people was the country's biggest export in these years. By 1860, enslavers valued the people they held at about 3 billion dollars. In 1800 most American families grew and made most of what they used. By 1850 far more of them grew crops and made goods to sell, and bought what they needed. Historians call this change the market revolution. In the panics of 1819 and 1837, tens of thousands of workers lost their jobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From household to market" -->
The market revolution depended on cheaper ways to move goods. Before the Erie Canal opened in 1825, it cost about 100 dollars to haul a ton of freight by road from Buffalo to New York City. By canal boat the same ton cost about 10 dollars. The trip took about ten days instead of twenty.

The owners of the new cotton mills in New England hired young women from farm families and paid them wages in cash. These women were among the first large groups of Americans who worked for cash wages.

In 1843 a rush for copper began on the Keweenaw Peninsula of Michigan, after the state geologist Douglass Houghton reported on the copper there. By 1849 miners there dug 96 of every 100 pounds of copper mined in the United States.

Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California early in 1848. About 80,000 people went to dig for it in 1849, and about 300,000 people came to California between 1848 and 1855. The merchants who sold them food and tools made steadier money than most of the miners did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="King Cotton" -->
Cotton was the most valuable American export, and enslaved people did the work of growing it. They planted it, picked it and got it ready for sale. In 1840 cotton made up 51.6 percent of the value of everything the United States sold to other countries. It stayed above half almost every year until the Civil War. About three of every four pounds grown here were sent abroad. From 1820 to 1860, about 80 of every 100 pounds of cotton used in the world came from the United States. The crop was known as King Cotton.

Cotton output nearly doubled in six years. Planters grew about 300 million pounds in 1831 and more than 600 million pounds in 1837. To grow it, enslavers forced people out of the older slave states and into the new cotton states. They moved about 155,000 enslaved people this way in the 1820s and at least 250,000 in the 1830s. Between about 1790 and 1860, enslavers moved more than 1 million people from the Upper South to the Deep South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="People counted as property worth billions" -->
Enslavers counted the people they held as capital. Capital is money or property that an owner uses to make more money. In 1860 the census counted 3,953,760 enslaved people in the United States. Enslavers valued them at 3 billion dollars or more. That is the price enslavers put on human beings as property. It was more than all the money invested in American railroads and factories put together.

Enslavers used enslaved people as capital in two ways. The people grew the cotton and were paid nothing for it. Enslavers could also borrow money by pledging the people themselves, which meant that a lender could take and sell them if the loan was not repaid.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banks and bond sellers" -->
In the 1830s, planters in Louisiana and Mississippi set up their own banks. The banks lent planters money on mortgages. A mortgage is a loan in which the borrower promises property to the lender. These mortgages covered land and enslaved people. The banks then sold bonds, which are paper promises to repay a loan with interest, to investors in Europe and in the Northern states. The mortgages on land and people backed those bonds.

The historian Edward E. Baptist of Cornell University names the banks and the firms that sold their bonds. Baring Brothers of London sold 2.5 million dollars in bonds for the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana. Baring Brothers and its New York partners, Prime, Ward and King, sold 7 million dollars in bonds for the Union Bank of Louisiana in 1832. Hope and Company of Amsterdam sold 12 million dollars in bonds for the Citizens' Bank of Louisiana in 1833. The Mississippi Union Bank sold 15.5 million dollars in state bonds. By 1836 the banks of New Orleans held 64 million dollars in capital, more than the banks of any other American city.

In January 2005 the bank JPMorgan Chase reported on two of the older banks it grew out of. The report states that the Citizens Bank and the Canal Bank, both in Louisiana, had accepted about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral on loans to planters. Collateral is property a borrower promises to hand over if the loan is not repaid. When planters failed to pay, the two banks came to own about 1,250 of those people. JPMorgan Chase's leaders apologized and set up a 5-million-dollar scholarship fund for students in Louisiana. They made the report because a 2003 law in Chicago required companies that did business with the city to search their records for ties to slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Insurers, cotton traders and Northern mills" -->
Northern insurance companies sold enslavers policies on the lives of enslaved people. When an enslaved person died, the company paid the enslaver. From 1846 to 1848, New York Life, then called Nautilus Insurance, insured the lives of 520 people who appear to have been enslaved. It paid 15 claims. In March 2000, leaders of the insurer Aetna apologized for policies the company sold on enslaved people's lives in the 1850s. They said Aetna would pay no reparations, which are payments made to make up for a wrong.

The brothers Henry, Emanuel and Mayer Lehman sold goods in Montgomery, Alabama. They took raw cotton grown on slave-labor plantations as payment for goods, and then they began trading in cotton. The 1860 census lists Mayer Lehman as the owner of seven enslaved people. Their firm, Lehman Brothers, lasted until it failed in 2008.

Northern mill owners also made money from slave-grown cotton. Francis Cabot Lowell, Nathan Appleton, Patrick Tracy Jackson and the other Boston merchants who built the Lowell mills were called the Boston Associates. In 1813 they founded the Boston Manufacturing Company. Their mills spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South. The mills also made a cheap cloth known then as "negro cloth." The mill owners sold it to plantations, where planters used it to clothe the people they enslaved. By one account, the Boston Associates controlled almost one-fifth of all the cotton spun in America by 1850. Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts called the tie between planters and mill owners an "unholy union ... between the lords of the lash and the lords of the loom."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1819" -->
The Panic of 1819 was the first major depression that the American public lived through. A panic happens when many people try to get their money out, or to sell what they own, at the same time. A depression is a long stretch of hard times, when businesses close and many people lose their work. This one lasted from about 1819 to 1822.

Several things caused it. The prices that buyers paid for cotton fell sharply. Officers of the Second Bank of the United States called in loans, which means they asked borrowers to pay back money right away. Under an 1817 rule, buyers of federal land had to pay in gold or silver coin. American factories closed because buyers chose cheaper goods from Britain.

Working people and debtors paid for the panic. In New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore, about 50,000 people had no work or only odd jobs. One observer from abroad estimated that half a million people across the country had no work. Nobody made a real count. In Philadelphia, 1,808 people were jailed because they could not pay their debts. In Boston, 3,500 people were jailed for debt. Farmland in Pennsylvania fell from 150 dollars an acre in 1815 to 35 dollars in 1819. Property in Richmond, Virginia, lost half its value. In New York City, the number of paupers, people too poor to feed themselves without help, rose from 8,000 in 1819 to 13,000 in 1820. The city had about 120,000 people. Mathew Carey, a publisher and economist in Philadelphia, estimated that 3 million people, about one-third of the nation, were harmed.

Small cotton planters went bankrupt in 1819, and again in 1837. Going bankrupt means being unable to pay one's debts, so that the lenders can take what the borrower owns. Larger planters bought up the small planters' land and the enslaved people those planters had held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837" -->
The Panic of 1837 began a depression that lasted until about 1843. In the years before it, buyers speculated in western land and in cotton. To speculate is to buy something in the hope of selling it later at a higher price. Then British lenders made loans harder to get, and the price of cotton fell. In New Orleans cotton prices fell by half. On May 10, 1837, the banks of New York City stopped paying out gold and silver.

The sources disagree about how many banks failed. One count says that of about 850 banks in the country, 343 closed and 62 more partly failed. That is about 4 of every 10 banks. A college textbook, *U.S. History I*, says that 200 banks closed. It adds that seven states and one territory failed to pay back their loans.

Nobody counted how many workers lost their jobs in 1837. All the figures are later estimates. One widely repeated estimate says that a third of all workers had no jobs by the fall of 1837. The same estimate says that people who kept their jobs had their pay cut by 30 to 50 percent within two years. Unemployment is the share of people who want work and cannot find it. Other estimates put New York's unemployment at about 25 or 30 percent in 1837 and 1838.

Food had already grown costly before the banks stopped paying. Between 1836 and 1837, a barrel of flour in New York rose from 7 dollars to 12. On February 13, 1837, about 5,000 New Yorkers met in City Hall Park to protest the price of food and fuel. Part of the crowd broke into the store of the flour merchants Eli Hart and Company. They took or destroyed 500 to 600 barrels of flour and 1,000 bushels of wheat.

Mississippi planters owed 33 million dollars to the banks of New Orleans. Their 1837 cotton crop sold for only 10 million dollars. Members of the legislatures of Mississippi and Florida later repudiated the bonds their states had backed. To repudiate a debt is to refuse to pay it. The planters kept their land and the people they enslaved. The investors in Europe and the North who had bought the bonds lost their money.

When debtors could not pay, their creditors seized the people the debtors enslaved and sold them. A creditor is a person who is owed money. Judges ordered the property of debtors taken. Many debtors fled to Texas at night and took the people they enslaved with them. When a debtor failed to pay, a single creditor's claim could force the sale of a whole enslaved community, with its people sold to different buyers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Philip Hone

> **Who:** A New York merchant whose diary recorded the Panic of 1837 as it happened.
> **When and where:** New York City. He kept the diary from 1828 to 1851.

Philip Hone wrote down what the Panic of 1837 did to business in New York City. He lived from 1780 to 1851. He sold goods at auction, and he served as the city's mayor in 1826 and 1827.

Hone kept a diary from 1828 until 1851. During the panic, he wrote: "No goods are selling, no business stirring, no boxes encumber the sidewalks of Pearl Street." Encumber means to crowd or block. Hone lost part of his own fortune in the crisis years. His diary was printed in 1889, after his death, as *The Diary of Philip Hone*.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900 the United States became an industrial country. In an industrial country, much of the work is done in mines, mills and factories. Railroad track grew from about 9,000 miles in 1850 to 193,346 miles by 1900. In 1900 American mills made 11.4 million tons of steel, more than Britain and Germany together. After slavery ended, Southern landowners farmed with sharecroppers who stayed in debt to them. Officials in Southern states leased prisoners, most of them Black, to private companies. In the depressions after the panics of 1873 and 1893, estimates put the share of workers without jobs as high as one in four. The era is called the Gilded Age.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gilded Age" -->
The name Gilded Age comes from a novel of 1873 by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner, *The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today*. The book makes fun of its own time. Gilded means covered with a thin layer of gold. The name means a thin shine of gold over serious problems underneath.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steel, rails, cattle and metal" -->
Steel got cheap in these years. According to histories of the steel industry, a ton of steel rails cost about 140 dollars in 1872, about 35 dollars in 1882 and about 20 dollars by 1900. American steel output passed Britain's in about 1889 or 1890.

Cattle ranchers in Texas drove herds north to the railroads in Kansas. A Texas longhorn worth up to about 5 dollars at home could sell for up to about 40 dollars at a Kansas railroad town. From 1866 to 1884, cowboys drove more than 5 million cattle north. The open-range cattle business collapsed after barbed wire fences, more railroads, a quarantine on cattle in 1885 and 1886, and the winter of 1886 to 1887. A quarantine keeps animals apart from others to stop the spread of a disease.

In 1862 members of Congress passed the Homestead Act. Under it, settlers filed about 4 million claims for land. About 270 million acres, about a tenth of the country's land, became private farms.

The Comstock Lode, found near Virginia City, Nevada, in 1859, was the richest body of silver in North America. Copper from Michigan, and later from Butte, Montana, became the wire for electric lights and telephones. Hall's new process for making aluminum, from 1886, started a new industry, and the Pittsburgh Reduction Company began making the metal in 1888. A pound of aluminum cost about 12 dollars in the early 1880s and less than 1 dollar by the middle of the 1890s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The panics of 1857 and 1873" -->
On August 24, 1857, the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company failed. Its manager in New York had stolen from it, and its officers had made bad loans. News of the failure went out by telegraph. Within days, people in many places rushed to take their money out of banks. This rush is called a bank run. A short, sharp depression followed.

The Panic of 1873 began on September 18, when the investment bank Jay Cooke and Company failed. The bank had put too much money into bonds of the Northern Pacific Railroad. The New York Stock Exchange, where people buy and sell shares of companies, closed for ten days. It had never closed like this before. About 18,000 businesses failed. By the count of the National Bureau of Economic Research, a group of economists that dates downturns, the depression lasted 65 months, until March 1879. It was the longest downturn on record until the 1930s.

By the winter of 1873 to 1874, about one of every four workers in New York City had lost their jobs, according to estimates drawn from newspapers of the time. According to one later account, more than 90,000 homeless workers, almost half of them women, slept in New York police stations.

On January 13, 1874, about 7,000 people with no work met in Tompkins Square Park in New York City. They wanted Mayor William F. Havemeyer to create public jobs. New York police, some of them on horseback, drove the crowd out of the square and arrested 46 people. Mayor Havemeyer and Police Commissioner Abram Duryée were the officials in charge on the police side.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farmers and the railroads" -->
Farmers were angry about what railroads and grain elevators charged them. A grain elevator is a tall building where grain is stored before it is shipped. In 1867 Oliver Hudson Kelley founded the Patrons of Husbandry, known as the Grange. By the middle of the 1870s it had about 800,000 members.

Grange members pushed state lawmakers to pass the Granger laws, which set the highest rates that railroads and grain elevators could charge. In *Munn v. Illinois* in 1877, the justices of the Supreme Court upheld those laws.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1893 and Coxey's Army" -->
In the Panic of 1893, about 500 banks and 15,000 businesses failed. More than 150 railroad companies later went bankrupt. Nobody counted the people out of work at the time. Later estimates for the worst of it run from about 17 to 25 percent of workers. The economic historian Stanley Lebergott estimated that unemployment rose from about 3 percent in 1893 to 18.4 percent in 1894.

In 1894 a businessman named Jacob Coxey led a march of men without work from Massillon, Ohio, to Washington, D.C. About 100 men left on March 25. About 500 arrived on May 1, and a crowd of about 30,000 watched them. The marchers, called Coxey's Army, asked the national government for a 500-million-dollar program of public works. Under it, the government would have hired people without jobs to build public projects. Coxey was arrested for walking on the grass of the Capitol and served 20 days in jail. His was the first large protest march on Washington.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sharecropping and debt" -->
After the Civil War, most freedpeople in the South had no land. Freedpeople were the people freed from slavery. Many families went to work as sharecroppers. Sharecropping was a way of farming someone else's land in return for a share of the crop.

A landowner gave a family a plot of land, usually about twenty acres. The landowner also "furnished" the family with food, seed, tools and a cabin on credit, which means the family would pay later. This was called the furnish. The landowner charged interest on that credit at usurious rates, which means far above a fair rate. At harvest the landowner sold the crop. The landowner took out the cost of the furnish and the interest. Then the landowner gave the family its share, usually half of what was left or less.

Landlords held a lien on the crop. A lien is a legal claim on property until a debt is paid. Landlords also kept the account books. So most sharecroppers were still in debt when the yearly accounts were settled, year after year. A landlord could put a sharecropping family off the land whenever he chose.

Sharecropping lasted into the 1930s. In 1930 the census counted 1,831,470 tenant farmers in the South. Tenant farmers farmed land they did not own, and they were about half of all farmers in the South. About 700,000 of them were share tenants, who owned mules or tools and kept two-thirds to three-quarters of the crop. By 1935, 77 of every 100 Black farmers in the country owned no land. Nearly half of white farmers owned none. In 1937 the President's Committee on Farm Tenancy counted about 775,000 sharecroppers in the South, almost equal numbers of white and Black.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Debt peonage and Alonzo Bailey" -->
Peonage means forcing a person to work to pay off a debt. Members of Congress banned it in the Peonage Abolition Act of 1867. Lawmakers in Southern states then wrote laws that had the same effect. Peonage was most common in the turpentine camps of southern Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and the Florida panhandle, and in the cotton belt, the part of the South where cotton was grown.

Alonzo Bailey was a Black farm laborer in Montgomery County, Alabama. On December 26, 1907, he signed a work contract with H. C. Borden, the manager of the Riverside Company, a farm owned by a corporation. Bailey received 15 dollars in advance, to be paid back out of wages of 12 dollars a month. He quit after about a month. Under Alabama law, a worker who quit without paying back an advance was a criminal. Bailey was fined 30 dollars and sentenced to 136 days of hard labor, heavy work done as a punishment.

A Montgomery lawyer, Edward S. Watts, and a city judge, William H. Thomas, helped Bailey take his case to the United States Supreme Court. Booker T. Washington helped in secret. On January 3, 1911, in *Bailey v. Alabama*, the justices ruled that the Alabama law broke the Thirteenth Amendment, the part of the Constitution that ended slavery. Other states' peonage laws stayed in force until the justices struck down Georgia's and Florida's in the 1940s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convict leasing in Alabama" -->
The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery "except as a punishment for crime." Under that exception, officials in Southern states leased prisoners to private companies. In convict leasing, state and county officials rented out prisoners to private companies. The companies paid the government a fee for each prisoner. The companies' bosses made the prisoners work in mines, on farms and in lumberyards.

Alabama leased prisoners from 1846 to July 1, 1928. After a money crisis in the state government in 1875, the warden John G. Bass leased state prisoners to coal mines, farms and lumberyards for monthly fees. A warden is the official in charge of a prison. From 1883, under the warden John Hollis Bankhead, most state prisoners went to three coal companies. These were the Pratt Coal and Iron Company, the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company, called TCI, and the Sloss Iron and Steel Company. In 1888 TCI signed a ten-year contract for every state prisoner able to work. It paid the state 9 to 18.50 dollars a month for each prisoner.

More than 95 of every 100 county prisoners in Alabama were Black, and so were 90 of every 100 state prisoners. The contractors whipped prisoners to make them work. Coal company officials wrote false reports of "bad conduct" so that the prisoners would have to serve longer sentences.

Alabama's government made money from the lease. Fees for leased prisoners were about 10 of every 100 dollars the state took in during 1883. In 1898 they were nearly 73 of every 100 dollars.

Leased prisoners died in the mines. In 1896, 1,710 state prisoners worked in the mines, and 233 state prisoners had died in the two years before. That year 745 county prisoners worked in the mines, and 181 had died in the two years before. In 1895 Dr. Thomas D. Parke, the health officer of Jefferson County, reported that 90 of every 1,000 prisoners at Sloss's Coalburg mine died each year. According to Digital History, a history website of the University of Houston, 25 of every 100 Black leased prisoners in Alabama died in 1873. The same source says that death rates for leased prisoners were about ten times the rates for prisoners in states that did not lease them.

Sloss kept county prisoners working in its mines until 1928. In 1924 a prisoner died after being lowered into a vat of boiling water. In 1928 Alabama became the last state to end convict leasing, about five years after Florida.

One account of Tennessee's prison system states that TCI's officials admitted their main reason for using prisoners. It was to break strikes and keep unions out of the mines. Tennessee leased prisoners from 1871 to 1896.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Andrew Carnegie

> **Who:** A Scottish immigrant who started work as a mill boy, built a steel company, sold it for 480 million dollars and gave most of the money away.
> **When and where:** Scotland and Pennsylvania, 1835 to 1919. He built steel mills from the 1870s and sold his company in 1901.
> **Movie:** The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997), a documentary about him shown on PBS.

Andrew Carnegie went from a job paying 1.20 dollars a week to selling his steel company for 480 million dollars. He was born in Dunfermline, Scotland, in 1835. In 1848 his family moved to Allegheny, Pennsylvania. At about 13 he went to work in a cotton mill as a bobbin boy, changing the spools that held the thread.

Carnegie worked as a telegraph messenger and then as a manager for the Pennsylvania Railroad. From the 1870s he built steel mills, starting with the Edgar Thomson works. In 1889 he wrote an essay called "The Gospel of Wealth." In 1901 he sold Carnegie Steel to a group led by J. P. Morgan for 480 million dollars. He gave away more than 350 million dollars, including money for about 2,500 public libraries. Counts of the libraries vary, and some go above 2,800 around the world.

In 1892 the men running Carnegie's company broke the steelworkers' union at Homestead. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employer. Ten men died in the battle at Homestead on July 6, 1892.

### John D. Rockefeller

> **Who:** The founder of Standard Oil, which by 1880 controlled about 90 percent of oil refining in the United States.
> **When and where:** Cleveland, Ohio, from 1870. He lived from 1839 to 1937.
> **Movie:** The Rockefellers (American Experience, 2000), a documentary shown on PBS about four generations of his family, not about him alone.

John D. Rockefeller built the company that controlled most of America's oil refining. Refining is the work of turning crude oil from the ground into fuels people can use. He founded the Standard Oil Company in Cleveland in 1870. By 1880 his company controlled about 90 percent of the oil refining in the United States.

Kerosene was the fuel that ordinary families burned in their lamps. In 1870 a gallon of kerosene cost about 26 cents. It cost about 9 cents by 1880 and about 7 cents by 1890. Lamp light became cheap for ordinary homes.

Rockefeller became the richest man in the country. Later he gave large sums of money to universities and to medical research.
