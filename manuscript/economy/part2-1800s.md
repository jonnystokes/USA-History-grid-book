## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Cotton grown by enslaved people was the country's biggest export in these years. By 1860, enslavers valued the people they held at about 3 billion dollars. In 1800 most American families grew and made most of what they used. By 1850 far more of them grew crops and made goods to sell, and bought what they needed. Historians call this change the market revolution. In the panics of 1819 and 1837, tens of thousands of workers lost their jobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From household to market" -->
The market revolution depended on cheaper ways to move goods. Before the Erie Canal opened in 1825, it cost about 100 dollars to haul a ton of freight by road from Buffalo to New York City. By canal boat the same ton cost about 10 dollars. The trip took about ten days instead of twenty.

The owners of the new cotton mills in New England hired young women from farm families and paid them wages in cash. These women were among the first large groups of Americans who worked for cash wages.

In 1843 a rush for copper began on the Keweenaw Peninsula of Michigan, after the state geologist Douglass Houghton reported on the copper there. By 1849 mines there dug 96 of every 100 pounds of copper mined in the United States.

Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California early in 1848. About 80,000 people went to dig for it in 1849, and about 300,000 people came to California between 1848 and 1855. The merchants who sold them food and tools made steadier money than most of the miners did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="King Cotton" -->
Cotton earned more money than any other American export, and enslaved people did the work of growing it. They planted it, picked it and got it ready for sale. In 1840 cotton made up 51.6 percent of the value of everything the United States sold to other countries. It stayed above half almost every year until the Civil War. About three of every four pounds grown here were sent abroad. From 1820 to 1860, about 80 of every 100 pounds of cotton used in the world came from the United States. The crop was known as King Cotton.

Cotton output nearly doubled in six years. Planters grew about 300 million pounds in 1831 and more than 600 million pounds in 1837. To grow it, enslavers forced people out of the older slave states and into the new cotton states. They moved about 155,000 enslaved people this way in the 1820s and at least 250,000 in the 1830s. Between about 1790 and 1860, enslavers moved more than 1 million people from the Upper South to the Deep South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="People counted as property worth billions" -->
Enslavers counted the people they held as capital. Capital is money or property that an owner uses to make more money. In 1860 the census counted 3,953,760 enslaved people in the United States. Enslavers valued them at 3 billion dollars or more. That is the price enslavers put on human beings as property. It was more than all the money invested in American railroads and factories put together.

Enslavers used enslaved people as capital in two ways. The people grew the cotton and were paid nothing for it. Enslavers could also borrow money by pledging the people themselves, which meant that a lender could take and sell them if the loan was not repaid.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banks and bond sellers" -->
In the 1830s, planters in Louisiana and Mississippi set up their own banks. The banks lent planters money on mortgages. A mortgage is a loan in which the borrower promises property to the lender. These mortgages covered land and enslaved people. The banks then sold bonds, which are paper promises to repay a loan with interest, to investors in Europe and in the Northern states. The mortgages on land and people backed those bonds.

The historian Edward E. Baptist of Cornell University names the banks and the firms that sold their bonds. Baring Brothers of London sold 2.5 million dollars in bonds for the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana. Baring Brothers and its New York partners, Prime, Ward and King, sold 7 million dollars in bonds for the Union Bank of Louisiana in 1832. Hope and Company of Amsterdam sold 12 million dollars in bonds for the Citizens' Bank of Louisiana in 1833. The Mississippi Union Bank sold 15.5 million dollars in state bonds. By 1836 the banks of New Orleans held 64 million dollars in capital, more than the banks of any other American city.

In January 2005 the bank JPMorgan Chase reported on two of the older banks it grew out of. The report states that the Citizens Bank and the Canal Bank, both in Louisiana, had accepted about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral on loans to planters. Collateral is property a borrower promises to hand over if the loan is not repaid. When planters failed to pay, the two banks came to own about 1,250 of those people. JPMorgan Chase's leaders apologized and set up a 5-million-dollar scholarship fund for students in Louisiana. They made the report because a 2003 law in Chicago required companies that did business with the city to search their records for ties to slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Insurers, cotton traders and Northern mills" -->
Northern insurance companies sold enslavers policies on the lives of enslaved people. When an enslaved person died, the company paid the enslaver. From 1846 to 1848, New York Life, then called Nautilus Insurance, insured the lives of 520 people who appear to have been enslaved. It paid 15 claims. In March 2000, leaders of the insurer Aetna apologized for policies the company sold on enslaved people's lives in the 1850s. They said Aetna would pay no reparations, which are payments made to make up for a wrong.

The brothers Henry, Emanuel and Mayer Lehman sold goods in Montgomery, Alabama. They took raw cotton grown on slave-labor plantations as payment for goods, and then they began trading in cotton. The 1860 census lists Mayer Lehman as the owner of seven enslaved people. Their firm, Lehman Brothers, lasted until it failed in 2008.

Northern mill owners also made money from slave-grown cotton. Francis Cabot Lowell, Nathan Appleton, Patrick Tracy Jackson and the other Boston merchants who built the Lowell mills were called the Boston Associates. In 1813 they founded the Boston Manufacturing Company. Their mills spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South. The mills also made a cheap cloth known then as "negro cloth." The mill owners sold it to plantations, where planters used it to clothe the people they enslaved. By one account, the Boston Associates controlled almost one-fifth of all the cotton spun in America by 1850. Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts called the tie between planters and mill owners an "unholy union ... between the lords of the lash and the lords of the loom."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1819" -->
The Panic of 1819 was the first great depression that the American public lived through. A panic happens when many people try to get their money out, or to sell what they own, at the same time. A depression is a long stretch of hard times, when businesses close and many people lose their work. This one lasted from about 1819 to 1822.

Several things caused it. The prices that buyers paid for cotton fell sharply. Officers of the Second Bank of the United States called in loans, which means they asked borrowers to pay back money right away. Under an 1817 rule, buyers of federal land had to pay in gold or silver coin. American factories closed because buyers chose cheaper goods from Britain.

Working people and debtors paid for the panic. In New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore, about 50,000 people had no work or only odd jobs. One observer from abroad estimated that half a million people across the country had no work. Nobody made a real count. In Philadelphia, 1,808 people were jailed because they could not pay their debts. In Boston, 3,500 people were jailed for debt. Farmland in Pennsylvania fell from 150 dollars an acre in 1815 to 35 dollars in 1819. Property in Richmond, Virginia, lost half its value. In New York City, the number of paupers, people too poor to feed themselves without help, rose from 8,000 in 1819 to 13,000 in 1820. The city had about 120,000 people. Mathew Carey, a publisher and economist in Philadelphia, estimated that 3 million people, about one-third of the nation, were harmed.

Small cotton planters went bankrupt in 1819, and again in 1837. Going bankrupt means being unable to pay one's debts, so that the lenders can take what the borrower owns. Larger planters bought up the small planters' land and the enslaved people those planters had held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837" -->
The Panic of 1837 began a depression that lasted until about 1843. In the years before it, buyers speculated in western land and in cotton. To speculate is to buy something in the hope of selling it later at a higher price. Then British lenders made loans harder to get, and the price of cotton fell. In New Orleans cotton prices fell by half. On May 10, 1837, the banks of New York City stopped paying out gold and silver.

The sources disagree about how many banks failed. One count says that of about 850 banks in the country, 343 closed and 62 more partly failed. That is about 4 of every 10 banks. A college textbook, *U.S. History I*, says that 200 banks closed. It adds that seven states and one territory failed to pay back their loans.

Nobody counted how many workers lost their jobs in 1837. All the figures are later estimates. One widely repeated estimate says that a third of all workers had no jobs by the fall of 1837. The same estimate says that people who kept their jobs had their pay cut by 30 to 50 percent within two years. Unemployment is the share of people who want work and cannot find it. Other estimates put New York's unemployment at about 25 or 30 percent in 1837 and 1838.

Food had already grown costly before the banks stopped paying. Between 1836 and 1837, a barrel of flour in New York rose from 7 dollars to 12. On February 13, 1837, about 5,000 New Yorkers met in City Hall Park to protest the price of food and fuel. Part of the crowd broke into the store of the flour merchants Eli Hart and Company. They took or destroyed 500 to 600 barrels of flour and 1,000 bushels of wheat.

Mississippi planters owed 33 million dollars to the banks of New Orleans. Their 1837 cotton crop sold for only 10 million dollars. Members of the legislatures of Mississippi and Florida later repudiated the bonds their states had backed. To repudiate a debt is to refuse to pay it. The planters kept their land and the people they enslaved. The investors in Europe and the North who had bought the bonds lost their money.

A creditor is a person who is owed money. When debtors could not pay, creditors seized the people they enslaved and sold them. Judges ordered the property of debtors taken. Many debtors fled to Texas at night and took the people they enslaved with them. When a debtor went under, a single creditor's claim could force the sale of a whole enslaved community, with its people sold to different buyers.

### Philip Hone

> **Who:** A New York merchant whose diary recorded the Panic of 1837 as it happened.
> **When and where:** New York City. He kept the diary from 1828 to 1851.

Philip Hone wrote down what the Panic of 1837 did to business in New York City. He lived from 1780 to 1851. He sold goods at auction, and he served as the city's mayor in 1826 and 1827.

Hone kept a diary from 1828 until 1851. During the panic, he wrote: "No goods are selling, no business stirring, no boxes encumber the sidewalks of Pearl Street." Encumber means to crowd or block. Hone lost part of his own fortune in the crisis years. His diary was printed in 1889, after his death, as *The Diary of Philip Hone*.
