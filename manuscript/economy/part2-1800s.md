## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 11 minutes**

In 1800 most American families grew and made most of what they used. By 1850 far more of them grew crops and made goods to sell, and bought what they needed, a change called the market revolution. The biggest thing Americans sold to other countries was cotton grown by enslaved people, and by 1860 enslavers valued the people they held at 3 billion dollars or more.

**Two panics.** In the panics of 1819 and 1837, many people rushed to get their money out at once, and businesses closed. About 50,000 people in three big cities had no steady work in 1819, and by one estimate a third of all workers had no jobs by the fall of 1837.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From household to market" -->
Farm families and workshops could sell goods far from home once moving them got cheaper. Before the Erie Canal opened in 1825, it cost about 100 dollars to haul a ton of goods by road from Buffalo to New York City. By canal boat the same ton cost about 10 dollars, and the trip took about ten days instead of twenty.

The owners of the new cotton mills in New England hired young women from farm families and paid them wages in cash. These women were among the first large groups of Americans who worked for cash wages.

**Copper and gold.** A rush for copper began on the Keweenaw Peninsula of Michigan, a point of land with water on three sides, in 1843. It began after Douglass Houghton, a scientist who studied rocks for the state as its geologist, reported on the copper there. By 1849 miners there dug 96 of every 100 pounds of copper mined in the United States.

Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California early in 1848. About 80,000 people went to dig for it in 1849, and about 300,000 people came to California between 1848 and 1855. The merchants who sold the miners their supplies made steadier money than most of the miners did.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="King Cotton" -->
Cotton was the most valuable American export, and enslaved people did the work of growing it. They planted it, picked it and got it ready for sale. The crop was known as King Cotton.

**More than half.** Cotton made up 51.6 percent of the value of everything the United States sold to other countries in 1840, and it stayed above half almost every year until the Civil War. About three of every four pounds grown here were sent abroad. From 1820 to 1860, about 80 of every 100 pounds of cotton used in the world came from the United States.

The cotton crop more than doubled in six years. Enslaved people grew about 300 million pounds of cotton in 1831 and more than 600 million pounds in 1837.

**Forced south.** To grow it, enslavers forced people out of the older slave states and into the new cotton states. They moved about 155,000 enslaved people this way in the 1820s and at least 250,000 in the 1830s. Between about 1790 and 1860, enslavers moved more than 1 million people from the Upper South, the older slave states farther north such as Virginia, to the Deep South, the cotton states farther south.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="People counted as property worth billions" -->
Enslavers counted the people they held as capital, money or property that an owner uses to make more money. Census takers, the government workers who count every person in the country, counted 3,953,760 enslaved people in the United States in 1860. Enslavers valued them at 3 billion dollars or more. That was the price enslavers put on human beings as property, and it was more than all the money invested in American railroads and factories put together.

Enslavers used enslaved people as capital in two ways. The people grew the cotton and were paid nothing for it. Enslavers could also borrow money by promising the people themselves to a lender, and if the loan was not repaid, the lender could take the people and sell them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Banks and bond sellers" -->
Planters in Louisiana and Mississippi set up their own banks in the 1830s. The banks lent planters money on mortgages, loans in which the borrower promises property to the lender, and these mortgages covered land and enslaved people. The banks then sold bonds, paper promises to repay a loan with interest, to investors in Europe and in the Northern states. The mortgages on land and people backed those bonds.

**Far from any plantation.** Investors in Europe and the North who bought these bonds made money from loans backed by enslaved people. Bankers in London, Amsterdam and New York sold the bonds to them. Baring Brothers of London and its partners in New York sold 7 million dollars in bonds for the Union Bank of Louisiana in 1832. Hope and Company of Amsterdam sold 12 million dollars in bonds for the Citizens' Bank of Louisiana in 1833.

**A bank's own report.** Two Louisiana banks, the Citizens Bank and the Canal Bank, lent planters money and took about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral, property a borrower promises to hand over if the loan is not repaid. When planters failed to pay, the two banks came to own about 1,250 of those people.

JPMorgan Chase, a bank that grew in part out of those two, made these figures public in a report in January 2005. JPMorgan Chase's leaders apologized and set up a 5-million-dollar scholarship fund for students in Louisiana. They made the report because, under a 2003 Chicago law, companies that did business with the city had to search their records for ties to slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Insurers, cotton traders and Northern mills" -->
Northern insurance companies sold enslavers policies, written deals, on the lives of enslaved people. The enslaver paid the company a fee, and when an enslaved person died, the company paid the enslaver.

**Two insurers.** New York Life, then called Nautilus Insurance, insured the lives of 520 people from 1846 to 1848. Those people appear to have been enslaved, and the company paid out money for 15 of their deaths. In March 2000, leaders of the insurer Aetna apologized for policies the company sold on enslaved people's lives in the 1850s. They said Aetna would pay no reparations, payments made to make up for a wrong.

**The Lehmans.** The Lehman brothers of Montgomery, Alabama, whose story opens this chapter, made their start as merchants who took slave-grown cotton as payment and then traded it.

**The Lowell mills.** Northern mill owners made money from slave-grown cotton too. Francis Cabot Lowell, Nathan Appleton, Patrick Tracy Jackson and the other Boston merchants who built the Lowell mills were called the Boston Associates. They founded the Boston Manufacturing Company in 1813, and their mills spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South.

The mills also made a cheap, rough cloth known then as "negro cloth," from the word white Americans of that time used for Black people. The mill owners sold the cloth to planters, who used it to clothe the people they enslaved. By 1845, 31 cloth companies tied to the Boston Associates, in Massachusetts, New Hampshire and southern Maine, made one-fifth of all the cotton and wool cloth in the United States.

Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts named the two sides by their tools: the lash, a whip, and the loom, a machine that weaves thread into cloth. He called the tie between planters and mill owners an "unholy union ... between the lords of the lash and the lords of the loom."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1819" -->
The Panic of 1819 began the first major depression that the American public lived through, a long stretch of hard times, from about 1819 to 1822, when businesses closed and many people lost their work.

**What caused it.** The prices that buyers paid for cotton fell sharply. Officers of the Second Bank of the United States called in loans, asking borrowers to pay back money right away. Under an 1817 order from Congress, buyers of land from the national government had to pay in gold or silver coin. Owners closed American factories because buyers chose cheaper goods from Britain.

**No work.** During the panic, employers let workers go, and judges jailed people who could not pay their debts. In New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore, about 50,000 people had no work or only odd jobs. One observer from abroad estimated that half a million people across the country had no work. Nobody made a real count.

**Debtors' prison.** In those years, a person who was owed money could sue, and a judge could order the borrower arrested and locked in a debtors' prison, with no set date for release. In Philadelphia, judges sent 1,808 people to debtors' prison this way. In Boston, judges sent 3,500.

Land lost its value. Farmland in Pennsylvania fell from 150 dollars an acre in 1815 to 35 dollars in 1819, and property in Richmond, Virginia, lost half its value.

**The poor.** In New York City, a city of about 120,000 people, the number of paupers, people too poor to feed themselves without help, rose from 8,000 in 1819 to 13,000 in 1820. Mathew Carey, a publisher and economist in Philadelphia, estimated that 3 million people, about one-third of the nation, were hurt by the panic.

Small cotton planters went bankrupt in 1819, and again in 1837: they could not pay their debts, so their lenders could take what they owned. Larger planters bought up the small planters' land and the enslaved people those planters had held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1837" -->
A depression began with the Panic of 1837 and lasted until about 1843. In the years before it, buyers speculated in western land and in cotton, buying in the hope of selling later at a higher price. Then British lenders made loans harder to get, and the price of cotton fell, by half in New Orleans.

**The banks stop paying.** The bankers of New York City stopped paying out gold and silver on May 10, 1837. People who brought in the banks' paper money could no longer trade it for coins.

Of the country's 850 banks, 343 closed, about 4 of every 10, and 62 more partly failed, by the count in a history collection edited by Hubert Howe Bancroft in 1902. A college textbook gives a smaller count, 200 banks closed. The governments of seven states and one territory did not pay back their loans.

**How many lost work.** By one widely repeated estimate, a third of all workers had no jobs by the fall of 1837, and within two years employers cut the pay of people who kept their jobs by 30 to 50 percent. Other estimates put New York's unemployment, the share of people who want work and cannot find it, at about 25 or 30 percent in 1837 and 1838.

Nobody counted the jobless at the time, so these are later estimates, and the teaching guides that repeat them do not say who made them.

**The flour riot.** Food had already grown costly before the banks stopped paying. A barrel of flour in New York rose from 7 dollars to 12 between 1836 and 1837. About 5,000 New Yorkers met in City Hall Park on February 13, 1837, to protest the price of food and fuel.

Part of the crowd broke into the store of the flour merchants Eli Hart and Company. They took or destroyed 500 to 600 barrels of flour and 1,000 bushels of wheat, about 60 pounds of wheat to each bushel.

**Who paid.** Mississippi planters owed 33 million dollars to the banks of New Orleans, and their 1837 cotton crop sold for only 10 million dollars. What happened next depended on whom a debtor owed.

Some planters had borrowed from banks in Mississippi and Florida whose bonds those states had backed. A bond is a paper promise to repay borrowed money with interest, and a state that backed a bank's bonds promised to pay the buyers itself if the bank could not. Lawmakers in both states later refused to pay those bonds. So the investors in Europe and the North who had bought them lost their money, and the planters who owed those banks kept their land and the people they enslaved.

**Families sold apart.** Debtors who owed other creditors, the people they owed money to, could lose the people they enslaved. When such a debtor could not pay, a judge could order his property taken, and the creditors seized the people he enslaved and sold them. Two Louisiana banks came to own about 1,250 enslaved people when planters failed to repay their loans. Many debtors fled to Texas at night and took the people they enslaved with them.

A single creditor's claim against a debtor who failed to pay could force the sale of a whole enslaved community. The creditors sold its people to different buyers and scattered them, and sales like these split families. By the count of the Equal Justice Initiative, a group that studies the history of slavery, enslavers and traders separated nearly half of all the people they moved inside the country from a husband, a wife or a parent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-hone-economy" name="Philip Hone" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Hone

> **Who:** A New York merchant whose diary recorded the Panic of 1837 as it happened.
> **When and where:** New York City. He kept the diary from 1828 to 1851.

**Hone wrote down the crash of 1837 and lost money in it.**

Philip Hone sold goods at auction in New York City, and he had served as the city's mayor in 1826 and 1827. During the Panic of 1837 he wrote in his diary how business in the city stopped: "No goods are selling, no business stirring, no boxes encumber (crowd or block) the sidewalks of Pearl Street."

Hone lost part of his own fortune in the crisis years. His diary was printed in 1889, after his death, as *The Diary of Philip Hone*.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 14 minutes**

American mills made 11.4 million tons of steel in 1900, more than Britain and Germany together. By then the United States was an industrial country, one where much of the work is done in mines, mills and factories. Railroad track grew from about 9,000 miles in 1850 to 193,346 miles by 1900. The era is called the Gilded Age.

**In the South.** After slavery ended, Southern landowners farmed with sharecroppers, families who worked a landowner's land for a share of the crop and stayed in debt to him. Officials in Southern states leased prisoners, renting them out to private companies, and in Alabama 90 or more of every 100 prisoners were Black.

**Hard times.** About one in four workers in New York City lost their jobs after the Panic of 1873, by estimates of the time. For the Panic of 1893, estimates for the whole country run from about 17 to 25 percent of workers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the name Gilded Age comes from" -->
The name Gilded Age comes from an 1873 novel by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner, *The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today*, in which the two writers made fun of their own time. Something gilded is covered with a thin layer of gold, so the name means a thin shine of gold over serious problems underneath.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Steel, rails, cattle and metal" -->
Steel rails got cheap in these years. According to histories of the steel industry, a ton of steel rails cost about 140 dollars in 1872, about 35 dollars in 1882 and about 20 dollars by 1900. American mills made more steel than Britain's by about 1889 or 1890.

**Cattle.** Cattle ranchers in Texas drove herds north to the railroads in Kansas, where a Texas longhorn worth up to about 5 dollars at home could sell for up to about 40 dollars. From 1866 to 1884, cowboys drove more than 5 million cattle north.

Ranchers also kept cattle on the open range, land with no fences, where the animals grazed wherever they wandered. Four things ended the open-range cattle business. Barbed wire fences went up across the range, and more railroads were built. Officials put a quarantine on cattle in 1885 and 1886, keeping animals apart from others to stop the spread of a disease. The last was the winter of 1886 to 1887.

**Homesteads.** Members of Congress passed the Homestead Act in 1862. Under it, a settler could claim up to 160 acres of land and own it after living on it and farming it for five years. Settlers filed about 4 million claims, and about 270 million acres, about a tenth of the country's land, became private farms. US officials had taken that land from Native nations.

**Silver, copper and aluminum.** The Comstock Lode, a long vein of silver running through rock near Virginia City, Nevada, was the richest body of silver in North America. It was found in 1859. Copper from Michigan, and later from Butte, Montana, became the wire for electric lights and telephones.

Charles Martin Hall, a 22-year-old college graduate, made aluminum by a new method in his family's woodshed in Oberlin, Ohio, on February 23, 1886, with help from his sister Julia. The Pittsburgh Reduction Company began making the metal by his method in 1888, and a new industry began. A pound of aluminum cost about 12 dollars in the early 1880s and less than 1 dollar by the middle of the 1890s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The panics of 1857 and 1873" -->
The Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company failed on August 24, 1857. Its manager in New York had stolen from it, and its officers had made bad loans. News of the failure went out by telegraph, and people in many places rushed to take their money out of banks, a rush called a bank run. A short but deep depression followed.

The Panic of 1873 began on September 18, when Jay Cooke and Company failed. It was an investment bank, a firm that sold bonds and shares to raise money for businesses such as railroads, and Jay Cooke and his partners had put too much money into bonds of the Northern Pacific Railroad.

**The exchange closes.** The New York Stock Exchange, where people buy and sell shares of companies, closed for ten days. It had never closed like this before. About 18,000 businesses failed. Economists at the National Bureau of Economic Research set the dates when hard times begin and end, and by their count this depression lasted 65 months, until March 1879. It was the longest stretch of hard times on their record until the 1930s.

**New York's jobless.** About one of every four workers in New York City had lost their jobs by the winter of 1873 to 1874, according to estimates drawn from newspapers of the time. The historian Howard Zinn wrote that more than 90,000 homeless workers, almost half of them women, slept in New York police stations.

**Tompkins Square.** About 7,000 people with no work met in Tompkins Square Park in New York City on January 13, 1874. They wanted Mayor William F. Havemeyer to create public jobs. Soon after 10 in the morning, New York police, some of them on horseback, charged into the crowd. The police commissioner, the official in charge of the city's police, was Abram Duryée.

The police beat people with clubs, and men, women and children ran. The accounts give no count of the people who were hurt. Men from a German workers' club fought back, and someone hit one policeman on the head with a hammer. The police drove the crowd out of the square and arrested 46 people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why farmers joined the Grange" -->
Farmers were angry about what railroads charged to ship their grain and what grain elevators, the tall buildings where grain is stored before it is shipped, charged to hold it. In 1867 Oliver Hudson Kelley founded the Patrons of Husbandry, known as the Grange (husbandry is an old word for farming). By the middle of the 1870s it had about 800,000 members.

Grange members pushed state lawmakers to pass the Granger laws, which set the highest rates that railroads and grain elevators could charge. In *Munn v. Illinois* in 1877, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that those laws were allowed under the Constitution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Panic of 1893 and Coxey's Army" -->
About 500 banks and 15,000 businesses failed in the Panic of 1893, and more than 150 railroad companies later went bankrupt. Nobody counted the people out of work at the time. Later estimates for the worst of it run from about 17 to 25 percent of workers. The economic historian Stanley Lebergott estimated that unemployment rose from about 3 percent in 1893 to 18.4 percent in 1894.

**The march.** A businessman named Jacob Coxey led a march of men without work from Massillon, Ohio, to Washington, D.C., in 1894. About 100 men left on March 25, and about 500 arrived on May 1, while a crowd of about 30,000 watched them. It was the first large protest march on Washington.

The marchers, called Coxey's Army, asked the national government for a 500-million-dollar program of public works. Under it, federal officials would have hired people without jobs to build public projects. When Coxey tried to speak at the Capitol, police arrested him for walking on its grass, and he served 20 days in jail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sharecropping and debt" -->
Most freedpeople in the South, the people freed from slavery, had no land after the Civil War. Many families went to work as sharecroppers.

**The furnish.** A landowner gave a family a plot of land, usually about twenty acres. He also "furnished" the family with food, seed, tools and a cabin on credit, to be paid for later, and this was called the furnish. On the furnish, landowners charged interest, the fee a borrower pays for borrowing, far above a fair rate.

At harvest the landowner sold the crop and took out the cost of the furnish and the interest. Then he gave the family its share, usually half of what was left or less.

Landlords held a lien on the crop, a legal claim on it until the debt was paid, and they kept the account books. So most sharecroppers were still in debt when the landlord settled the yearly accounts, year after year. A landlord could put a sharecropping family off the land whenever he chose.

Sharecropping lasted into the 1930s. Census takers counted 1,831,470 tenant farmers in the South in 1930, farmers who worked land they did not own, about half of all farmers there. About 700,000 of them were share tenants, who owned mules or tools and kept two-thirds to three-quarters of the crop.

Most Black farmers in the country owned no land by 1935: 77 of every 100. Nearly half of white farmers owned none. In 1937 members of the President's Committee on Farm Tenancy counted about 775,000 sharecroppers in the South, almost equal numbers of white and Black.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Debt peonage and Alonzo Bailey" -->
Members of Congress banned peonage, forcing a person to work to pay off a debt, in the Peonage Abolition Act of 1867. Lawmakers in Southern states then wrote laws that had the same effect. Peonage was most common in the cotton belt, the part of the South where cotton was grown, and in the turpentine camps of southern Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and the Florida panhandle, where workers collected sap from pine trees to make turpentine, a liquid used in paint.

Alonzo Bailey, a Black farm laborer in Montgomery County, Alabama, signed a work contract on December 26, 1907, with the manager of the Riverside Company, a company that ran a farm. Bailey received 15 dollars before he started work, to be paid back out of wages of 12 dollars a month. He quit after about a month.

**The charge.** Under Alabama law, a worker who quit without paying back money he got this way was a criminal. An Alabama court found Bailey guilty, fined him 30 dollars and sentenced him to 136 days of hard labor, heavy work done as a punishment.

**The appeal.** A Montgomery lawyer, Edward S. Watts, and a city judge, William H. Thomas, helped Bailey take his case to the United States Supreme Court, and Booker T. Washington helped in secret. On January 3, 1911, in *Bailey v. Alabama*, the justices ruled that the Alabama law broke the Thirteenth Amendment, the part of the Constitution that bans slavery. Other states' peonage laws stayed in force until the justices struck down Georgia's and Florida's in the 1940s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convict leasing in Alabama" -->
The Thirteenth Amendment bans slavery "except as a punishment for crime." Under that exception, state and county officials in the South rented out prisoners to private companies, which was called convict leasing. The companies paid the government a fee for each prisoner, and the companies' bosses made the prisoners work in mines, on farms and in lumberyards.

Officials in Alabama leased prisoners from 1846 to July 1, 1928. After a money crisis in the state government in 1875, the warden John G. Bass, the official in charge of the state's prisoners, leased them to coal mines, farms and lumberyards for monthly fees.

**Three coal companies.** From 1883, under the warden John Hollis Bankhead, officials leased most state prisoners to three coal companies: the Pratt Coal and Iron Company, the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company, called TCI, and the Sloss Iron and Steel Company. In 1888 TCI signed a ten-year contract for every state prisoner able to work, paying the state 9 to 18.50 dollars a month for each prisoner.

More than 95 of every 100 county prisoners in Alabama were Black, and so were 90 of every 100 state prisoners. The companies' bosses whipped prisoners to make them work. Coal company officials wrote false reports of "bad conduct" so that the prisoners would have to serve longer sentences.

**What the state made.** Alabama's state officials collected money from leasing prisoners. Fees for leased prisoners were about 10 of every 100 dollars the state took in during 1883, and in 1898 they were nearly 73 of every 100 dollars.

**Deaths.** Leased prisoners died at about ten times the rate of prisoners in states that did not lease them, according to Digital History, a history website of the University of Houston. By the same source, 25 of every 100 Black leased prisoners in Alabama died in 1873. At Sloss's Coalburg mine, 9 of every 100 prisoners died each year, Dr. Thomas D. Parke, the health officer of Jefferson County, reported in 1895.

Alabama's mines held 1,710 state prisoners and 745 county prisoners in 1896. In the two years before, 233 state prisoners and 181 county prisoners had died.

**James Knox.** A prisoner named James Knox, from Mobile, was killed at Sloss's Flat Top mine in 1924. Men there whipped him with a steel wire and lowered him into a vat of boiling water. The published accounts of his death do not name the men who did it.

His death was first reported as a suicide by poison, and it led to an investigation. An autopsy, an examination of a dead body by doctors, found that his heart had stopped and that the poison had been put into his body after he died. Investigators found that the whipping and the boiling water caused the injuries that killed him.

Sloss's managers kept county prisoners working in the company's mines until 1928. That year Alabama's officials ended convict leasing. Alabama was the last state to end it, about five years after Florida.

**Breaking strikes.** TCI's managers also used leased prisoners in Tennessee, where officials leased prisoners from 1871 to 1896. A TCI official told the *New York Times* that "one of the chief reasons" the company took up convict leasing "was the chance it offered for overcoming strikes," the times when workers stop working until their employer agrees to better pay or conditions.

The official said free miners would not want to strike once they saw the company had plenty of prisoners to put to work. The account of the interview does not give his name. TCI's managers used prisoners to keep unions, groups of workers who join together to bargain with their employer, out of the mines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-carnegie-economy" name="Andrew Carnegie" movie="The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Carnegie

> **Who:** A Scottish immigrant who started work as a mill boy, built a steel company, sold it for 480 million dollars and gave away more than 350 million dollars.
> **When and where:** Scotland and Pennsylvania, 1835 to 1919. He built steel mills from the 1870s and sold his company in 1901.
> **Movie:** The Richest Man in the World: Andrew Carnegie (American Experience, 1997), a documentary about him shown on PBS.

**His first job, at about 13, paid 1.20 dollars a week.**

Andrew Carnegie's family moved from Dunfermline, Scotland, to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, in 1848. At about 13 he went to work in a cotton mill as a bobbin boy, changing the spools that held the thread. He worked as a telegraph messenger and then as a manager for the Pennsylvania Railroad. From the 1870s he built steel mills, among them the Edgar Thomson works.

**Selling and giving.** In 1889 Carnegie wrote an essay called "The Gospel of Wealth." In 1901 he sold Carnegie Steel to a group led by J. P. Morgan for 480 million dollars. He gave away more than 350 million dollars, including money for about 2,500 public libraries. Counts of the libraries vary, and some go above 2,800 around the world.

**Homestead.** Workers at Carnegie Steel's works in Homestead, Pennsylvania, went on strike in 1892. Henry Clay Frick, one of the men running Carnegie's company, hired 300 armed agents of the Pinkerton detective agency, a private company that hired out guards and detectives, to take back the plant. On July 6 the Pinkertons came up the river on barges, and they and the strikers fought a gun battle.

Seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed, and some accounts count more. No record names the men who fired the shots that killed them. Governor Robert E. Pattison of Pennsylvania sent about 8,000 state soldiers. By November the strike had failed, and Frick and the company's other managers had broken the steelworkers' union at Homestead.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-carnegie-economy" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-d-rockefeller-economy" name="John D. Rockefeller" movie="The Rockefellers (American Experience, 2000)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John D. Rockefeller

> **Who:** The founder of Standard Oil, which by 1880 controlled about 90 percent of oil refining in the United States.
> **When and where:** Cleveland, Ohio, from 1870. He lived from 1839 to 1937.
> **Movie:** The Rockefellers (American Experience, 2000), a documentary shown on PBS about four generations of his family, not about him alone.

**Lamp oil dropped from about 26 cents a gallon to 7 cents.**

John D. Rockefeller built the company that controlled most of America's oil refining, the work of turning crude oil from the ground into fuels people can use. He founded the Standard Oil Company in Cleveland in 1870, and by 1880 his company controlled about 90 percent of the oil refining in the United States.

**Cheap light.** Kerosene was the fuel that ordinary families burned in their lamps. A gallon of it cost about 26 cents in 1870, about 9 cents by 1880 and about 7 cents by 1890, so lamp light became cheap for ordinary homes.

Rockefeller became the richest man in the country. Later he gave large sums of money to universities and to medical research.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-d-rockefeller-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="By 1900" -->
**If you remember one thing:** After slavery ended, Southern landowners and state officials used debt and prison leasing to keep many Black workers laboring for little or nothing.
**One number:** 73. In 1898 nearly 73 of every 100 dollars that Alabama's state government took in came from leasing prisoners, up from about 10 in 1883.
**Next:** After 1900 Henry Ford roughly doubled his workers' pay, and after the crash of 1929 hungry families stood in breadlines.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1850-1900" -->
