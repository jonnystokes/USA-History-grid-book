## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 16 of 37 · Economy · 10 eras · about 80 minutes**

**Much of this country's wealth came from work that people were forced to do for little or no pay, and in crash after crash, the poorest families lost the most.**

The census of 1860 lists Mayer Lehman, a merchant in Montgomery, Alabama, as the owner of seven enslaved people. He and his brothers, Henry and Emanuel, took cotton grown by enslaved people as payment for the goods they sold, and then they began trading cotton. Their company was called Lehman Brothers. On September 15, 2008, the same company failed, owing about 619 billion dollars. It was the largest bankruptcy, the court case of a company that cannot pay its debts, in the country's history.

This chapter follows how people here made a living, from Native trade before 1500 to the jobs of 2026. They made it first mostly by farming, then more and more in mines, mills and factories, and by 2026 mostly in services: stores, offices, hospitals and schools. Two things come back in era after era. One is who did the work and who kept the money: servants, enslaved people, convicts and sharecroppers did much of it for little or nothing. The other is the crash: the panics of 1819, 1837, 1857, 1873 and 1893, the Great Depression of the 1930s and the crash of 2008. In each one, watch who lost a job, a farm or a home.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did slavery make money for people far from any plantation?
- What happened to ordinary families when a boom turned into a crash?
- When did the national government start helping people who lost their jobs?

**Five minutes?** Read "The Panic of 1837" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Shell, copper and obsidian", "Parliament's molasses and iron laws", "Where the name Gilded Age comes from", "Why farmers joined the Grange". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 2 minutes**

Native people here traded without money. They had economies of their own, ways of making, sharing and trading the things they needed. Nobody had coins or wages, and nothing had a set market price. They traded by barter, swapping one good for another, and by giving gifts between people and between nations. Stone tools, shells and copper traveled hundreds of miles this way. No record names any one person who took part in this trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How far did Cahokia's hoe blades travel?" -->
The people of Cahokia controlled the making and trading of stone blades for hoes, farm tools for breaking up soil. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past from the things people left behind, have found these blades more than 400 miles away, from Louisiana to Minnesota and in the Arkansas River valley.

**The city.** Cahokia stood near what is now St. Louis. It was the largest city north of Mexico, and it was at its biggest from about 1050 to 1150. Its farmers grew more corn than they ate, and that surplus, the extra corn, fed specialists: people who spent most of their time on one kind of work, such as making tools, instead of growing food.

**The stone.** Workers chipped the blades from Mill Creek chert, a tough stone found only in southern Illinois. They dug it out of quarries, open pits for stone, and some of the pits were 10 to 20 feet deep.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell, copper and obsidian" -->
People carried the shells of whelks, large sea snails, north from the coast of the Gulf of Mexico. Traders moved copper from the land around Lake Superior. At Cahokia, workers made shell beads in large numbers, and families on small farms made them too.

In the West, people traded obsidian, a natural glass that forms in volcanoes, across their own trade networks. Archaeologists have traced pieces found in western Canada back to Obsidian Cliff, in what is now Wyoming, and Bear Gulch, in what is now Idaho. Some pieces had traveled as far as 750 miles from where the stone formed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 2 minutes**

Explorers found no gold or silver in the land that became the United States, though the kings and queens of Spain, France and England paid for their voyages in the 1500s to find it. The Spanish silver came from mines in Mexico and South America. Fishing was the first steady European business in these waters. Some ship crews traded with Native people on the coast, and some of that trade was for furs. Almost no Europeans settled here yet, so there was no colonial economy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fish, furs and a rope from a cliff" -->
European fishing crews worked the cod waters off the northeast coast all through the 1500s and dried their catch on shore.

**The rope.** Abenaki people on the coast of what is now Maine traded with the crew of Giovanni da Verrazzano's ship in 1524 from the top of a cliff. They would not let the crew come ashore, so they lowered baskets of goods to the ship on a rope.

Crews of earlier European ships had already taken Native people from the Maine coast to sell as slaves. That is why the Abenaki kept this crew off the land. Verrazzano called the place "the land of bad people." The records do not say whether the baskets held furs.

**Furs on sticks.** Mi'kmaq traders held up beaver furs on sticks to the crew of Jacques Cartier at Chaleur Bay, north of Maine in what is now Canada, in July 1534. They traded the furs for knives and other iron goods. Cartier wrote the trade down in his own account of the voyage, one of the earliest records of furs traded between Europeans and Native people on the Atlantic coast of North America. The fur trade reached the land that became the United States in the next century.

**St. Augustine.** The Spanish town of St. Augustine in Florida began in 1565 as a military post, and the Spanish king's government paid its costs. Unlike the English colonies that came later, it was not set up to make a profit.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 7 minutes**

Tobacco became Virginia's main business in the 1600s. The planters who owned the fields changed who did the work. At first the field workers were indentured servants from England, people who had signed contracts to work for a master for a set number of years. Near 1700 the planters switched to buying enslaved Africans, whom they could hold for life. Farther north, Dutch traders shipped beaver skins to Europe, and lawmakers in London wrote rules that required colonists to ship their main crops only to England or its colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco in the Chesapeake" -->
The first colonists at Jamestown hoped to find gold. John Smith wrote that there was "no talk, no hope, no work but dig gold, wash gold, refine gold, load gold." They found none. Colony officials tried glassmaking and other small businesses, and none of them made money.

**Tobacco.** Then Virginia's planters began to grow tobacco as a cash crop, a crop grown to sell rather than to eat. They shipped about 2,300 pounds of tobacco in 1615 and 1616, about 20,000 pounds by 1617, and about 1.5 million pounds by 1629 and 1630.

The trade kept growing. Planters in Virginia and Maryland, the two colonies around Chesapeake Bay called the Chesapeake colonies, together sent about 22 million pounds of tobacco a year to England by 1700. They also used tobacco as money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-rolfe-economy" name="John Rolfe" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Rolfe

> **Who:** A Jamestown colonist whose tobacco shipments to London began Virginia's trade in its main crop.
> **When and where:** Jamestown, Virginia, 1612 to 1614.

**Rolfe grew tobacco from West Indies seed and shipped it to London.**

John Rolfe began Virginia's export trade in tobacco, the business of sending it away to be sold in another place. He lived at Jamestown, the English colony in Virginia. By July 1612 he was growing tobacco from seed brought in from the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean Sea. He sent his first shipments to London in 1614.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-rolfe-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who worked the tobacco fields?" -->
Indentured servants did the tobacco work for most of the 1600s. Each servant's contract was called an indenture. In return for the servant's years of work, the master paid for the trip across the ocean and gave food, clothing and a place to sleep.

**The terms.** Adults usually served four to seven years, and children sometimes served much longer. At the end of the term, the master owed the servant "freedom dues." Depending on the contract and the colony, these could be land, tools, clothes or farm animals.

**How many.** Servants were about three out of every four people who came to the Chesapeake colonies between 1630 and 1680, about 50,000 people in all. Of about 120,000 English people who moved to Virginia and Maryland in the 1600s, about 90,000 came as servants. These counts are estimates made from ship records and court records.

For all the British colonies, the historian Abbot Emerson Smith estimated that one-half to two-thirds of the white people who moved there from the 1630s to the Revolution came as indentured servants.

**The headright.** Planters gained from each servant in two ways. Under Virginia's headright rule, whoever paid for a person's passage, the cost of the ocean trip, received 50 acres of land for that person. So a planter who paid a servant's passage got the servant's years of work and 50 acres as well. The large tobacco planters built their estates, their large holdings of land, this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What servants went through" -->
Officers of the Virginia Company could hang, burn to death or shoot runaway servants they caught, under the company's first form of contract, used from 1609 to 1619. The Virginia Company of London, a group of investors in England, started Jamestown in 1607 and ran the colony until 1624. That year the king of England took the colony away from the company and put it under his own control.

**Whippings and added years.** Masters could whip their servants. When a servant ran away, judges in the colony's courts added years to the servant's term. They also added years when a woman servant had a child outside marriage. Under a Virginia law of March 1662, she served two more years or paid her master 2,000 pounds of tobacco.

**When the master was the father.** Some of the fathers were the masters themselves. Virginia's lawmakers wrote a second law in December 1662 about a woman servant "gott with child by her master," that is, made pregnant by him. When her years ran out, officers of the local church, called churchwardens, sold her to work two more years "for the good of the parish," the church district where she lived. Under this law the churchwardens sold the woman, not the master who was the father.

Some masters also forced women servants into sex. Julie Richter, a historian of colonial Virginia, writes that some masters "physically and sexually abused their servants." She gives no count of how many.

**The rules for masters.** A Virginia law required masters to give servants "Competent Dyet, Clothing & Lodging," which meant enough food, clothes and shelter. The same law required masters not to "exceed the Bounds of moderation in correcting them," which meant not to go too far in punishing them.

Many servants died of disease before their contracts ended. Most of those who lived to the end of their terms stayed poor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From servants to enslaved Africans" -->
About 300 Africans lived in Virginia by 1650, next to about 4,000 white servants. In the 1660s, members of Virginia's assembly, the colony's lawmakers, passed the colony's first laws that treated Africans as slaves for life.

**The first law.** Under a law of March 1661, an English servant who ran away together with Africans had to serve extra time for the Africans' missing time as well as his own. Judges punished runaway servants by adding years to their terms. But the law's words said the Africans were "incapable of makeing satisfaction by addition of time." Judges could add no years to their time, because they were already held for life.

Servants and enslaved Africans fought on the same side in Bacon's Rebellion of 1676 and 1677, an armed uprising in Virginia. After it, the planters who ruled Virginia passed new laws. Historians at the College of Charleston describe the purpose of those laws as "to harden Virginia's racial caste system," a system that sorts people at birth into ranks they can never leave.

**The slave code.** Members of Virginia's assembly gathered the colony's slave laws into one slave code in 1705. Under it, enslaved people were property, and being baptized, going through the church ceremony that makes a person a Christian, did not free anyone. A master who killed an enslaved person while "correcting" them faced no punishment.

**The planters' choice.** Planters chose to buy enslaved Africans instead of servants near 1700. Fewer English servants were coming by then, because life in England had improved. In the same years the Atlantic slave trade grew, as ship crews carried captive Africans across the ocean to be sold.

The economist David Galenson, a scholar who studies how people make and spend money, explains the choice in money terms. A servant worked for a master for about five years. A planter who bought an enslaved person held that person, and that person's children, for life. So the planter got a whole lifetime of unpaid work, and the work of the children too, instead of five years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beaver skins, and rules from London" -->
One ship of the Dutch West India Company, the *Arms of Amsterdam*, carried 7,246 beaver skins to Amsterdam in a single cargo in 1626, along with the skins of otters, minks, lynxes and muskrats. A letter known as the Schaghen letter lists the cargo. People in Europe bought hats made of felt from beaver fur, and those buyers were the reason traders wanted the skins.

English leaders followed an idea called mercantilism, the belief that colonies existed to make the home country richer. Colonists were to send raw goods, such as tobacco, to England and buy back finished goods, things already made in England.

**The Navigation Acts.** Members of Parliament, England's lawmakers, wrote this idea into laws called the Navigation Acts. Under the act of 1651, colonists had to ship their trade goods only in English or colonial ships. The act of 1660 listed "enumerated goods," a phrase that means listed goods, and colonists could ship these only to England or to other English colonies. The list included tobacco, sugar, cotton, ginger and indigo, a plant used to make blue dye.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 6 minutes**

Much of the colonies' wealth, most of all in the South, came from the work of enslaved people, who were paid nothing. By 1774 free colonists earned more on average than the people of England. The economic historians Peter Lindert and Jeffrey Williamson published that finding in 2016. They also found that income among free colonists was spread more evenly than in any other place they could measure at that time. From 1718, British judges also sent about 50,000 convicts across the ocean to be sold as laborers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the colonies sold" -->
Tobacco came from the Chesapeake colonies, wheat and flour from the middle colonies, such as Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey, and fish, rum and ships from New England. In the Lowcountry of South Carolina, the low land along the coast, enslaved workers grew rice and indigo and prepared them for sale.

Timber cost little in the colonies, so colonial shipbuilders could build ships for less than builders in England. By the Revolution, about one-third of the British merchant fleet, all the ships that carried Britain's goods for trade, had been built in America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rice, and the people who knew how to grow it" -->
Enslaved Africans built and worked the rice fields of the South Carolina Lowcountry. Rice was the Lowcountry's main cash crop from the early 1700s. Enslaved Africans were a majority of the people in South Carolina from about 1708, and by 1740 they were about two out of every three.

Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library writes that enslaved people "moved earth on a massive scale" to build the rice fields. By the 1720s, Carolina planters were asking slave traders for Africans from the region called the Rice Coast, because people from there already knew how to grow rice. The wealth from rice, Butler writes, "remained concentrated in the hands of a small minority of white planters."

**The children.** Historians at the College of Charleston record that enslaved children in the Lowcountry died at especially high rates. Their account gives no count of those children's deaths in the early 1700s. The number of enslaved people there did not grow through births until the 1760s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney

> **Who:** A young woman who ran her father's three South Carolina plantations from the age of 16 and directed the colony's first successful indigo dye crop.
> **When and where:** South Carolina, 1744 to the 1750s.

**Her first crop, in 1744, made 17 pounds of blue dye.**

Eliza Lucas directed the first successful crop of indigo dye in South Carolina in 1744 and shared her seed with neighboring planters. From the age of 16 she had run her father's three plantations. She later married and became Eliza Lucas Pinckney.

**What followed.** South Carolina's indigo exports grew from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 and 1746 to about 130,000 pounds by 1748. By the middle of the 1750s, planters there shipped more than a million pounds a year. Indigo became the colony's second biggest export after rice, and by the Revolution it made up more than a third of the value of the colony's exports.

**Whose work.** Enslaved workers on her father's plantations did the growing. Her father sent skilled dye-makers from the Caribbean, and enslaved workers did the processing, the work of turning the plant into dye. She relied on their knowledge and their labor.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts sold at the dock, 1718 to 1775" -->
British judges sent about 50,000 people convicted of crimes to the North American colonies between 1718 and 1775, and merchants sold them there as laborers. The convicts were about one in four of all the people who moved from Britain to colonial America in those years.

**The law.** Members of Parliament passed the Transportation Act in 1718. Transportation here meant sending convicts away across the ocean as a punishment. Under the act, a judge could send a person convicted of a felony, a serious crime, to the colonies instead of hanging them: for seven years for one of the less serious felonies, and for fourteen years for worse crimes.

**Sold at the dock.** Most convicts went to Maryland and Virginia. There, merchants sold them at the dock as bound laborers, people who had to work for whoever bought them until their term ended. In the records of four Maryland counties, about 82 of every 100 convicts were men and 18 were women. On two ships of 1721 and 1724, most convicts were between 20 and 29 years old, and about half were laborers with no trade.

**The money.** British government officials paid a London merchant named Jonathan Forward £3, three British pounds, for each convict he carried, and later £5. A Maryland firm, Stevenson, Randolph and Cheston, sold convicts in Maryland from 1767 to 1775. The firm's own sales books record that most men sold for £11 to £14 and most women for £9 to £10. The planters who bought them got years of work without paying wages.

Convicts died on the crossing. On 38 voyages from London between 1718 and 1736, the ships carried 3,599 convicts, and 385 of them died, about 11 of every 100. The historian A. Roger Ekirch points out that the records of some of these voyages list no deaths at all. If those blanks are gaps in the clerks' records, the true count was about 15 of every 100. On 12 voyages from Bristol in the 1770s, 23 of 990 convicts died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rhode Island's slave ships" -->
Merchants in Rhode Island sent out at least 1,000 slaving voyages in the century before 1807. That year members of Congress made a law that banned bringing captive Africans into the United States, starting on January 1, 1808. The Rhode Island ships carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery.

About 60 of every 100 slaving voyages that left North America sailed from Rhode Island, and in some years more than 90 of every 100 did. These numbers come from *Slavery and Justice*, a 2006 report from Brown University.

**The loop.** The merchants who sent out these ships made their money by buying and selling human beings. Their crews sailed a loop around the Atlantic Ocean. Colonists made rum from Caribbean molasses, a thick, dark syrup left over when sugar is made.

Captains carried the rum and other goods to West Africa and traded them for captive people. Crews then carried the captives across the ocean, on the crossing called the Middle Passage, to be sold in the Caribbean and on the mainland. The ships brought sugar and molasses back north.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Parliament's molasses and iron laws" -->
Members of Parliament passed the Molasses Act in 1733. Under it, colonists had to pay a duty, a tax on goods brought in, on molasses from colonies that did not belong to Britain. The duty was sixpence, six British pennies, for each gallon. Many colonists dodged it by smuggling, bringing the molasses in secretly.

Under the Iron Act of 1750, raw iron from the colonies could enter Britain with no duty. The act forbade colonists to build new steel furnaces, rolling and slitting mills or plating forges, the works that turned raw iron into finished goods. Colonists built them anyway.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 6 minutes**

Americans paid for the Revolution with paper money and debt. In 1790 the new national government and the states still owed about 79 million dollars for the war, and Alexander Hamilton wrote the plan to pay those debts in full. Members of Congress taxed whiskey to help pay them, and in 1794 farmers in western Pennsylvania rose up against that tax. Both before and after the war, Rhode Island merchants sent ships to Africa to buy captive people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Browns and the slave ship Sally" -->
Four merchant brothers of Providence, Rhode Island, sent their ship *Sally* to West Africa to buy people in 1764. They were Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown. The ship carried 17,274 gallons of rum, along with chains, guns and shackles, metal rings locked around a person's wrists or ankles. By that year Rhode Island had about 30 rum distilleries, places where rum was made, and 22 of them were in Newport. The distillers made the rum from molasses shipped from the Caribbean.

**The voyage.** The captain, Esek Hopkins, bought 196 Africans, and at least 109 of them died. Hopkins kept the ship on the African coast for more than nine months while he bought them. The captives died from disease and starvation, by killing themselves, and in an uprising.

Nineteen died before the ship left Africa. They included several children and a woman who hanged herself below deck. On the day the ship sailed, another woman was "left for dead," in the words of Brown University's report, and the report does not say who left her or what became of her. Four more, a woman and three children, died in the first week at sea.

Sixty-eight people died during the whole crossing, and 20 more died soon after the ship reached the West Indies. One more captive died on the way to the Brown brothers in Providence. The report's stage counts of 19, 68 and 20 add up to 107, but it gives the total at that point as 108 and does not explain the difference. With the death on the way to Providence, the historians who wrote the report count at least 109 deaths among the 196.

**The uprising.** When the captives rose up, Hopkins's crew fired on them. Hopkins wrote in his account book: "Slaves Rose on us was obliged fire on them and Destroyed 8 and Several more wounded badly." By "Destroyed 8" he meant that his crew killed eight people. The survivors were so sick that buyers paid only £5 for some of them.

**Brown's next ship.** John Brown kept trading in people. In 1795 the crew of his ship *Hope* took 229 Africans from the Gold Coast of Africa, and 198 of them lived to be sold in Cuba. Under a law of 1794, Americans could not own, equip, put money into or work on ships that carried captive people to other countries.

**The trial.** Members of the Providence Abolition Society, a group working to end slavery, took Brown to a United States court for breaking that law. On August 5, 1797, he became the first American tried under it. The jury found him not guilty and ordered the society's members who had charged him to pay his court costs. He did not get the *Hope* back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Paying for the war with paper money" -->
Members of the Continental Congress had almost no power to tax. From 1775 they paid for the war by printing paper money called Continental dollars, and the paper dollars quickly lost their value. By the end of 1777 it took about 4 paper dollars to buy what 1 silver dollar bought, and by 1781 it took about 100. The paper money was close to worthless. The saying "not worth a Continental," meaning worth nothing at all, comes from this money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** The author of the plan to pay the Revolution's debts in full.
> **When and where:** The new United States government, 1790 and 1791.
> **Movie:** Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007), a two-hour documentary shown on PBS, with Brían F. O'Byrne as Hamilton.

**After a dinner deal, members of Congress voted his debt plan through.**

Alexander Hamilton planned how the new United States would pay the debts of the Revolution. He set out the plan in his First Report on Public Credit in January 1790. He counted the national debt at about 54 million dollars, and the states owed about 25 million dollars more for the war.

**Paying in full.** Hamilton proposed that the national government pay every debt in full and take over the states' war debts, a step called assumption. The people who were owed money would then look to the new government to be paid, and they would want it to last so that they got their money. Paying in full would also build the government's credit, the trust lenders have that a borrower will pay back what it borrows.

**The dinner.** Assumption did not have enough votes to pass in the House of Representatives in June 1790. Thomas Jefferson invited Hamilton and James Madison to dinner, and the three men made a deal. After it, members of Congress voted assumption through, and they placed the country's permanent capital on the Potomac River.

**Factories.** Hamilton's Report on Manufactures is dated December 5, 1791. In it he laid out a plan to grow American manufacturing, the making of goods in workshops and mills, with tariffs (taxes on goods brought in from other countries) and subsidies (money paid to help a business grow). Members of Congress never brought the plan to a vote.

Hamilton also helped found the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures. Through it, he and the other founders started a factory town at the falls of the Passaic River, which is Paterson, New Jersey.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why farmers fought the whiskey tax" -->
Farmers in western Pennsylvania sent their corn to market as whiskey, so a tax on whiskey fell on the crop they sold. Members of Congress passed that tax in 1791 to help pay the war debt. It was an excise, a tax on goods made and sold inside the country, of 7 cents a gallon on distilled spirits such as whiskey.

**Tar and feathers.** A gang in disguise caught a tax collector named Robert Johnson in September 1791 and tarred and feathered him: they coated him with hot tar and stuck feathers to it.

**Bower Hill.** About 600 armed men attacked the house of John Neville, the tax inspector, at a place called Bower Hill in July 1794. When their leader, James McFarlane, stepped into the open, someone inside the house shot and killed him. The accounts do not say who fired. The attackers then set the house on fire. This uprising is called the Whiskey Rebellion. President George Washington called up about 13,000 militia, soldiers drawn from the states, and the rebels broke up without a battle.

**The Dreadful Night.** Troops under General Henry Lee broke into houses on the night of November 13, 1794, pulled people from their beds and arrested about 150 of them. They held the prisoners in animal pens and basements. For years afterward, people in the area called it "the Dreadful Night."

The troops took 20 of the prisoners to Philadelphia for trial. Two men, Philip Wigle and John Mitchell, were found guilty of treason, the crime of making war against their own country. Washington pardoned them both, setting aside their punishment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1750-1800" -->
