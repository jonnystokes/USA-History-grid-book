## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500, Native nations here ran economies of their own. An economy is the way people make, share and trade the things they need. People here had no coins and no wages, and nothing had a set market price. They traded by barter, which means swapping one good for another, and by giving gifts between people and between nations. Goods such as stone tools, shells and copper traveled hundreds of miles. No record names any one person who took part in this trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia and its hoe blades" -->
Cahokia stood near what is now St. Louis. It was the largest city north of Mexico, and it was at its biggest from about 1050 to 1150. Its farmers grew more corn than they ate. That extra corn, called a surplus, fed specialists. A specialist is a person who spends most of their time on one kind of work, such as making tools, instead of growing food.

The people of Cahokia controlled the making and trading of hoe blades. A hoe is a farm tool for breaking up soil. The blades were chipped from Mill Creek chert, a hard stone that workers dug out of quarries, open pits for stone, in southern Illinois. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past from the things people left behind, have found these blades more than 400 miles away, in places from Louisiana to Minnesota and in the Arkansas River valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell, copper and obsidian" -->
People carried whelk shells north from the coast of the Gulf of Mexico. A whelk is a large sea snail. Traders also moved copper from the land around Lake Superior. At Cahokia, workers made shell beads in large numbers, and families on small farms made them as well.

In the West, people traded obsidian across their own networks of trails. Obsidian is a natural glass that forms in volcanoes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The kings and queens of Spain, France and England paid for voyages to this coast in the 1500s to find gold and silver. In the land that became the United States, their explorers found none. The Spanish silver came from mines in Mexico and South America. The European businesses that lasted here were fishing and trading with Native people for furs. Almost no Europeans settled here yet, so there was no colonial economy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fish, furs and a rope from a cliff" -->
European fishing crews worked the cod waters off the northeast coast all through the 1500s. They dried their catch on shore. The one Spanish town of the century, St. Augustine in Florida, began in 1565 as a military post. Money from the Spanish king's treasury paid for it. It was not set up to make a profit.

In 1524 the crew of Giovanni da Verrazzano's ship met Abenaki people on the coast of what is now Maine. The Abenaki would not let the crew come ashore. Instead they traded from the top of a cliff, lowering baskets of goods to the ship on a rope. Crews of earlier European ships had already taken Native people from the Maine coast to sell as slaves. That is why the Abenaki kept this crew off the land. Verrazzano called the place "the land of bad people." The records do not say whether the baskets held furs.

In July 1534, at Chaleur Bay, north of Maine in what is now Canada, Mi'kmaq traders held up beaver furs on sticks to the crew of Jacques Cartier. They traded the furs for knives and other iron goods. Cartier wrote the trade down in his own account of the voyage. It is one of the earliest recorded trades of furs between Europeans and Native people on this coast. The fur trade reached the land that became the United States in the next century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Tobacco became Virginia's main business in the 1600s. Indentured servants from England did the early work in the tobacco fields. Near 1700, the planters who owned the fields switched to buying enslaved Africans, whom they could hold for life. Dutch traders shipped beaver skins to Europe. Lawmakers in London wrote rules that required colonists to ship their main crops only to England or its colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tobacco in the Chesapeake" -->
The colonists at Jamestown found no gold, so Virginia's planters grew tobacco as a cash crop. A cash crop is a crop grown to sell rather than to eat. Planters in Virginia shipped about 2,300 pounds of tobacco in 1615 and 1616. By 1617 they shipped about 20,000 pounds, and by 1629 and 1630 about 1.5 million pounds.

By 1700, planters in Virginia and Maryland together sent about 22 million pounds of tobacco a year to England. These two colonies, around Chesapeake Bay, are called the Chesapeake colonies. Planters there also used tobacco as money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-rolfe-economy" name="John Rolfe" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Rolfe

> **Who:** A Jamestown colonist whose tobacco shipments to London began Virginia's trade in its main crop.
> **When and where:** Jamestown, Virginia, 1612 to 1614.

John Rolfe began Virginia's export trade in tobacco. An export is a good sent away to be sold in another place.

Rolfe lived at Jamestown, the English colony in Virginia. By July 1612 he was growing tobacco from seed brought in from the West Indies, the islands of the Caribbean Sea. He sent his first shipments of tobacco to London in 1614.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-rolfe-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indentured servants" -->
For most of the 1600s, the tobacco work was done by indentured servants. An indentured servant signed a contract, called an indenture, to work for a master for a set number of years. In return, the master paid for the servant's trip across the ocean and gave food, clothing and a place to sleep. Adults usually served four to seven years. Children sometimes served much longer. At the end of the term, the master owed the servant "freedom dues." Depending on the contract and the colony, these could be land, tools, clothes or farm animals.

Historians have estimated the numbers from ship records and court records. About 50,000 servants came to the Chesapeake colonies between 1630 and 1680. They were about three out of every four people who arrived there. Of about 120,000 English people who moved to Virginia and Maryland in the 1600s, about 90,000 came as servants. The historian Abbot Emerson Smith estimated that, across all the British colonies, one-half to two-thirds of white immigrants from the 1630s to the Revolution came under indenture.

Planters gained from each servant in two ways. Under Virginia's headright rule, whoever paid for a person's passage, the cost of the ocean trip, received 50 acres of land for that person. A planter who paid a servant's passage got the servant's years of work and 50 acres as well. The large tobacco planters built their estates, their large holdings of land, this way.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What servants went through" -->
Masters could whip their servants. When a servant ran away, judges in the colony's courts added years to the servant's term. They also added years when a woman servant became pregnant. A Virginia law told masters to give servants "Competent Dyet, Clothing & Lodging," which meant enough food, clothes and shelter. The same law told masters not to "exceed the Bounds of moderation in correcting them." Correcting meant punishing. Under that law, masters could whip servants as long as they stayed within that limit.

From 1609 to 1619, the colony belonged to the Virginia Company, a company in England. Under the company's first form of contract, its officers in Virginia could hang, burn to death or shoot runaway servants they caught.

Many servants died of disease before their contracts ended. Most of those who lived to the end of their terms stayed poor.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From servants to enslaved Africans" -->
By 1650, about 300 Africans lived in Virginia, next to about 4,000 white servants. In the 1660s, members of Virginia's assembly, the colony's lawmakers, passed the colony's first laws that treated Africans as slaves for life. One source dates the first of these laws to 1661.

In Bacon's Rebellion of 1676 and 1677, an armed uprising in Virginia, servants and enslaved Africans fought on the same side. After it, the planters who ruled Virginia passed new laws. Historians at the College of Charleston describe the purpose of those laws as "to harden Virginia's racial caste system." A caste system sorts people into ranks at birth, and they cannot leave their rank.

Near 1700, the planters chose to buy enslaved Africans instead of servants. Fewer English servants were coming by then, because life in England had improved. In the same years, the Atlantic slave trade grew. Ship crews in that trade carried captive Africans across the ocean to be sold. The economist David Galenson gives the planters' reason in money terms. A servant worked for a master for about five years. A planter who bought an enslaved person held that person, and that person's children, for life.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Beaver skins, and rules from London" -->
In 1626, one ship of the Dutch West India Company, the *Arms of Amsterdam*, carried 7,246 beaver skins to Amsterdam in a single cargo. It also carried the skins of otters, minks, lynxes and muskrats. The cargo is listed in a letter known as the Schaghen letter. People in Europe bought hats made of felt from beaver fur, and those buyers were the reason traders wanted the skins.

English leaders followed an idea called mercantilism. They held that colonies existed to make the home country richer. Colonists were to send raw goods, such as tobacco, to England and buy English finished goods back. Members of England's Parliament wrote this idea into laws called the Navigation Acts. The act of 1651 required colonial trade to travel in English or colonial ships. The act of 1660 listed "enumerated goods," a phrase that means listed goods. Colonists could ship these only to England or to other English colonies. The list included tobacco, sugar, cotton, indigo and ginger.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By 1774, free colonists earned more on average than the people of England. The economic historians Peter Lindert and Jeffrey Williamson published that finding in 2016. They also found that income among free colonists was spread more evenly than in any other place they could measure at that time. Much of the colonies' wealth, most of all in the South, came from the work of enslaved people who were paid nothing. From 1718, British judges also sent about 50,000 convicts across the ocean to be sold as laborers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What the colonies sold" -->
Tobacco came from the Chesapeake colonies, wheat and flour from the middle colonies, and fish, rum and ships from New England. In the Lowcountry of South Carolina, the low land along the coast, enslaved workers grew rice and indigo and prepared them for sale.

Timber cost little in the colonies, so colonial shipbuilders could build ships for less than builders in England. By the Revolution, about one-third of the British merchant fleet had been built in America. A merchant fleet is all the ships that carry a country's goods for trade.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rice, and the people who knew how to grow it" -->
The rice fields of the South Carolina Lowcountry were built and worked by enslaved Africans. Rice was the Lowcountry's main cash crop from the early 1700s. From about 1708, enslaved Africans were a majority of the people in South Carolina. By 1740 they were about two out of every three.

Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library writes that enslaved people "moved earth on a massive scale" to build the rice fields. By the 1720s, Carolina planters were asking slave traders for Africans from the region called the Rice Coast. The planters wanted them because people from there already knew how to grow rice.

The wealth from rice, Butler writes, "remained concentrated in the hands of a small minority of white planters." Historians at the College of Charleston record that enslaved children in the Lowcountry died at especially high rates. The number of enslaved people there did not grow through births until the 1760s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" name="Eliza Lucas Pinckney" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Eliza Lucas Pinckney

> **Who:** A young woman who ran her father's three South Carolina plantations from the age of 16 and grew the colony's first successful indigo dye crop.
> **When and where:** South Carolina, 1744 to the 1750s.

Eliza Lucas grew the first successful crop of indigo dye in South Carolina, in 1744. Indigo is a plant used to make blue dye. She later married and became Eliza Lucas Pinckney.

From the age of 16, she ran her father's three plantations. Her 1744 crop made 17 pounds of dye, and she shared the seed with neighboring planters. South Carolina's indigo exports grew from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 and 1746 to about 130,000 pounds by 1748. By the middle of the 1750s, planters there shipped more than a million pounds a year. Indigo became the colony's second biggest export after rice. By the Revolution, it made up more than a third of the value of the colony's exports.

Other people did the work of making the dye. Her father sent skilled dye-makers from the Caribbean, and enslaved workers did the processing, the work of turning the plant into dye. Their knowledge and their labor were part of her success.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="eliza-lucas-pinckney-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Convicts sold at the dock, 1718 to 1775" -->
From 1718 to 1775, about 50,000 people convicted of crimes in Britain were shipped to the North American colonies and sold there as laborers. In 1718 members of Parliament passed the Transportation Act. Under it, British judges could send a person convicted of a lesser felony, a serious crime, to the colonies for seven years instead of hanging them. For worse crimes, judges could send a person for fourteen years. The convicts were about one in four of all the people who moved from Britain to colonial America in those years.

Most convicts went to Maryland and Virginia. There, merchants sold them at the dock as bound laborers, people who had to work for whoever bought them until their term ended. In the records of four Maryland counties, about 82 of every 100 convicts were men and 18 were women. On two ships of 1721 and 1724, most convicts were between 20 and 29 years old, and about half were laborers with no trade.

British government officials paid a London merchant named Jonathan Forward £3 for each convict he carried, and later £5. The pound, written £, was British money. A Maryland firm, Stevenson, Randolph and Cheston, sold convicts in Maryland from 1767 to 1775. The firm's own sales books record that most men sold for £11 to £14 and most women for £9 to £10. The planters who bought them got years of work without paying wages.

Convicts died on the crossing. On 38 voyages from London between 1718 and 1736, the ships carried 3,599 convicts, and 385 of them died. That is about 11 of every 100. The historian A. Roger Ekirch points out that some of these voyages list no deaths at all. If those blanks are gaps in the clerks' records, the true count was about 15 of every 100. On 12 voyages from Bristol in the 1770s, 23 of 990 convicts died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Rhode Island's slave ships" -->
Merchants in Rhode Island sent out at least 1,000 slaving voyages in the century before 1807, the year the slave trade became illegal under United States law. Their ships carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery. About 60 of every 100 slaving voyages that left North America sailed from Rhode Island, and in some years more than 90 of every 100 did. These numbers come from a 2006 report by Brown University called *Slavery and Justice*.

The ships ran a loop around the Atlantic Ocean. Colonists turned molasses from the Caribbean into rum. Captains carried the rum and other goods to West Africa and traded them for captive people. Crews then carried the captives across the ocean, on the crossing called the Middle Passage, to be sold in the Caribbean and on the mainland. The ships brought sugar and molasses back north. The merchants who sent out these ships made their money by buying and selling human beings.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="London's rules tighten" -->
In 1733 members of Parliament passed the Molasses Act. Under it, colonists had to pay a duty, a tax on goods brought in, of sixpence a gallon on molasses from islands that did not belong to Britain. Many colonists dodged the duty by smuggling, which means bringing goods in secretly.

Under the Iron Act of 1750, raw iron from the colonies could enter Britain with no duty. The act forbade colonists to build new steel furnaces, rolling and slitting mills or plating forges. Those were the works that turned raw iron into finished goods. Colonists built them anyway.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans paid for the Revolution with paper money and debt. When the war was over, the new national government and the states owed about 79 million dollars. Alexander Hamilton wrote the plan to pay those debts in full, and farmers in western Pennsylvania rose up against the whiskey tax that helped pay them. All through these years, Rhode Island merchants kept sending ships to Africa to buy captive people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Browns and the slave ship Sally" -->
In 1764 four merchant brothers of Providence, Rhode Island, sent their ship *Sally* to West Africa to buy people. They were Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown. By that year the colony had about 30 rum distilleries, places where rum is made, and 22 of them were in Newport. The distillers made the rum from molasses shipped from the Caribbean. The *Sally* carried 17,274 gallons of rum, along with chains, shackles and guns. Shackles are metal rings locked around a person's wrists or ankles.

The captain, Esek Hopkins, bought 196 Africans. At least 109 of them died. Nineteen died before the ship left Africa, 68 died during the crossing, and 20 died soon after the ship reached the West Indies. When the captives rose up, Hopkins's crew fired on them. Hopkins wrote in his account book: "Slaves Rose on us was obliged fire on them and Destroyed 8." By "Destroyed 8" he meant that his crew killed eight people. The survivors were so sick that some were sold for £5.

John Brown kept trading in people. In 1795 his ship *Hope* took 229 Africans from the Gold Coast of Africa, and 198 of them lived to be sold in Cuba. Members of the Providence Abolition Society, a group working to end slavery, took him to federal court for illegal slave trading. It was the first American prosecution for that crime, which means the first court case in which someone was charged with it. A jury found him not guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A war fought on paper" -->
Members of the Continental Congress had almost no power to tax. From 1775 they paid for the war by printing paper money called Continental dollars. The paper dollars quickly lost their value. By the end of 1777, it took about 4 paper dollars to buy what 1 silver dollar bought. By 1781 it took about 100, and the paper money was close to worthless. The saying "not worth a Continental," meaning worth nothing at all, comes from this money.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" name="Alexander Hamilton" movie="Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Alexander Hamilton

> **Who:** The author of the plan under which the new national government paid the Revolution's debts in full.
> **When and where:** The new United States government, 1790 and 1791.
> **Movie:** Alexander Hamilton (American Experience, 2007), a two-hour documentary shown on PBS, with Brían F. O'Byrne as Hamilton.

Alexander Hamilton planned how the new United States would pay the debts of the Revolution. In January 1790 he set out the plan in his First Report on Public Credit. He counted the national debt at about 54 million dollars. The states owed about 25 million dollars more for the war.

Hamilton proposed that the national government pay every debt in full. He also proposed that it take over the states' war debts, a step called assumption. The people who were owed money would then look to the new government to be paid, and that would tie them to it. Paying in full would also establish the government's credit. Credit means that lenders trust a borrower to pay back what it borrows.

In June 1790, assumption was losing in the House of Representatives. Jefferson invited Hamilton and Madison to dinner, and the three men made a deal. Assumption passed, and the country's permanent capital was placed on the Potomac River.

Hamilton's Report on Manufactures is dated December 5, 1791. In it he laid out a plan to grow American manufacturing, the making of goods in workshops and mills, with tariffs and subsidies. A tariff is a tax on goods brought in from other countries. A subsidy is money paid to help a business grow. Members of Congress never brought the plan to a vote. Hamilton also helped found the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures. Through it, he and the other founders started an industrial town at the falls of the Passaic River, which is Paterson, New Jersey.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="alexander-hamilton-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The whiskey tax" -->
In 1791 members of Congress passed a federal excise on distilled spirits, such as whiskey, to help pay the war debt. An excise is a tax on goods made and sold inside the country. The tax was 7 cents a gallon. Farmers in western Pennsylvania sent their corn to market as whiskey, so the tax fell on the crop they sold.

The farmers resisted the tax, and in 1794 their resistance became the Whiskey Rebellion. Washington called up about 13,000 militia, soldiers drawn from the states. The rebellion broke up without a battle. About 150 people were arrested, and 20 of the leaders were taken away as prisoners.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
