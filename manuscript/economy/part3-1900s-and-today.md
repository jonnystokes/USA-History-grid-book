## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these fifty years the country went from a boom to the Great Depression, and then back to full work during the Second World War. After stock prices crashed in October 1929, about one of every four workers had no job by 1933. In Chicago and Pittsburgh, more than half of Black workers had no job in 1932, by later estimates. From 1935 the national government paid old-age pensions and unemployment insurance for the first time. Farmworkers and domestic workers were shut out of those payments, and most Black workers did those two jobs. Two accounts disagree about who made that choice. In 1944, with factories making goods for the war, unemployment fell to 1.2 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-economy" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford

> **Who:** The carmaker who roughly doubled his workers' pay in 1914 and cut the price of his Model T car from 825 dollars to 260 dollars.
> **When and where:** Ford's Highland Park car factory. He announced the five-dollar day on January 5, 1914.
> **Movie:** Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013), a documentary about him shown on PBS.

Henry Ford roughly doubled his workers' pay in 1914, and workers who earned more could buy the cars they built. He did it because workers kept quitting. An assembly line moves each car past a row of workers, and each worker does one step over and over. After Ford's managers put in the assembly line, the work never let up and was dull. In 1913 turnover at the Highland Park factory reached 370 percent. Turnover counts the workers who quit and had to be replaced. At 370 percent, managers had to hire almost four new workers in a year for every job.

On January 5, 1914, Ford announced five dollars for an eight-hour day. Before, a Ford worker earned about 2.34 dollars for a nine-hour day. The new rate began on January 12.

The raise came with conditions. Ford paid the extra money as profit-sharing, a share of the company's profits, and a worker qualified only after six months. Staff of the company's Sociological Department inspected workers' homes before granting the full rate. They checked for thrift, which means careful saving, and for sobriety, which means not drinking. They also checked for what they called "proper" living. Men under 22 were left out, and at first most women were too.

After the raise, fewer workers quit. The price of the Model T kept falling, from 825 dollars in 1908 to 260 dollars by 1925.

Ford fought unions for decades. His newspaper, the *Dearborn Independent*, printed antisemitic articles through the 1920s. Antisemitic means hateful toward Jewish people. Ford later apologized in public for the articles.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The crash and the Great Depression" -->
Stock prices fell by 89 percent between 1929 and 1932. A stock is a share in the ownership of a company. The Dow Jones average is a number built from the stock prices of a group of large companies. It rose from 63 in August 1921 to 381 on September 3, 1929. Prices fell hard on Black Thursday, October 24, 1929, and again on Black Tuesday, October 29. By July 8, 1932, the Dow stood at 41.

The hard times of the 1930s are called the Great Depression. By 1933 about 25 percent of workers, one in four, had no job. Government officials and scholars made that estimate later, because nobody counted the jobless every month at the time.

GDP is the total value of all the goods and services made in the country in a year. It fell from about 104 billion dollars in 1929 to about 56 billion dollars in 1933. Counted as the amount of goods and services, output fell by about 30 percent. About 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks failed by 1933.

Hungry people stood in breadlines, which are lines for free food. Some people lived in shantytowns, groups of shacks, which people called Hoovervilles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bonus Army" -->
In the summer of 1932, veterans of World War I camped in Washington, D.C., to ask for a bonus they were owed for their war service. The bonus was not due until 1945, and the veterans asked to be paid early in cash. Estimates of their number run from about 10,000 to more than 20,000. Many had their families with them. On June 17, members of the Senate voted the payment down.

On July 28, police tried to clear veterans out of some buildings in the city. Two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson, were shot dead.

President Hoover then ordered the Army to clear the camps. General Douglas MacArthur sent in infantry, who are soldiers on foot, and cavalry, who are soldiers on horseback, with tanks and tear gas. Tear gas stings the eyes, nose and throat, so people cough and their eyes stream with tears. MacArthur's soldiers burned the main camp.

Members of Congress later voted to pay the bonus over the veto of President Franklin D. Roosevelt. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a bill. The payments began in June 1936.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-roth-economy" name="Benjamin Roth" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Roth

> **Who:** A lawyer in Youngstown, Ohio, who kept a diary of the Great Depression for more than ten years.
> **When and where:** Youngstown, Ohio. He began the diary in June 1931.

Benjamin Roth, a lawyer in Youngstown, Ohio, kept a record of the Depression from June 1931 for more than ten years. He lived from 1894 to 1978.

In his diary Roth listed the banks that closed and the stocks that became worthless. He wrote down prices, and what lawyers and workers earned. In 2009 his son, Daniel B. Roth, and James Ledbetter published the diary as a book, *The Great Depression: A Diary*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-roth-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Last hired, first fired" -->
Black workers lost their jobs first and in the largest numbers. The saying of the time was "last hired, first fired." By 1932 nearly half of all Black Americans had no work, according to the National Museum of African American History and Culture.

The website History.com, drawing on the Library of Congress, gives more detail. In 1932 more than half of Black workers in Chicago and Pittsburgh had no job. About a quarter of white workers in Northern cities had none. In Atlanta in 1934, nearly 70 percent of Black workers had no job. These numbers are all later estimates, because nobody kept an official count by race.

From 1929, government officials and private employers forced people whose families came from Mexico to leave for Mexico, and California's lawmakers found in 2005 that about 400,000 American citizens and legal residents were sent from California alone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New Deal" -->
Before 1933, the federal government paid no unemployment insurance and no old-age pensions. After 1935 it paid both. Unemployment insurance pays money to workers who have lost their jobs. A pension is a regular payment to a person who has stopped working because of age. The programs of these years are called the New Deal.

The CCC was a federal jobs program for young men that began in March 1933. About 3 million young men worked in it over the years it ran. They earned 30 dollars a month and sent about 25 dollars of it home.

The Works Progress Administration, or WPA, ran from 1935 to 1943. About 8.5 million people worked for it. WPA workers built about 650,000 miles of roads, 125,000 public buildings and 75,000 bridges.

On August 14, 1935, President Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act. Under it, the national government paid old-age pensions and unemployment insurance.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frances-perkins-economy" name="Frances Perkins" movie="Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frances Perkins

> **Who:** The Secretary of Labor who chaired the committee that planned Social Security, and the first woman in a United States Cabinet.
> **When and where:** Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945. She lived from 1880 to 1965.
> **Movie:** Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020), a documentary about her.

Frances Perkins chaired the committee that planned Social Security. She was Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945. She was the first woman in a United States Cabinet, the group of the president's top officials.

The Committee on Economic Security was set up on June 29, 1934, with Perkins as its chair. Its members planned old-age pensions and unemployment insurance. They sent their report to President Roosevelt on January 15, 1935. He signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935.

Perkins wanted farmworkers and domestic workers covered by the new law, and so did Harry Hopkins, the official in charge of aid to the jobless. Those workers were not in the law as it passed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frances-perkins-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who Social Security left out" -->
Under the Social Security Act of 1935, farmworkers and domestic workers could not get old-age pensions or unemployment insurance. Domestic workers cleaned, cooked and cared for children in other people's homes. In 1930, 65 of every 100 Black workers did one of these two kinds of work. So about two-thirds of Black workers could get neither payment, and in the South the share was higher. Derrick Johnson, president of the NAACP, a civil rights group, gave these figures in 2020.

At the hearings on the bill in 1935, Charles Hamilton Houston spoke for the NAACP. He said the bill would leave out "Negro sharecroppers and Negro cash tenants, who are just about at the bottom of the economic scale." He called the law "a sieve with the holes just big enough for the majority of Negroes to fall through." A sieve is a strainer full of holes.

Two accounts disagree about who made the choice to leave these workers out. Jon Dubin, in a 2024 article in a Stanford law journal, points to Southern members of Congress. Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia opposed a rule that payments be high enough for "decency and health." He said that rule "might serve as an entering wedge for federal interference with the handling of the Negro question." An entering wedge is a first step that opens the way to more. Byrd did not want national officials to have a say in how the South treated Black people.

Edwin Witte, executive director of the Committee on Economic Security, reported that Byrd's position was "supported by nearly all of the southern members of both committees" in Congress. Representative Howard Smith of Virginia argued for keeping Black sharecroppers and domestic workers dependent on their employers.

Larry DeWitt, a historian at the Social Security Administration, gave a different account in 2010. He found that the exclusion came from changes to the bill asked for by Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau, for reasons of tax collection. DeWitt wrote that "the allegations of racial bias in the founding of the Social Security program, based on the coverage exclusions, do not hold up under detailed scrutiny." Racial bias means unfair treatment because of race. Scrutiny means close study.

Members of Congress changed the law in 1950 to cover farm and domestic workers who worked regularly for one employer. In 1954 they covered the rest. Representative Smith opposed these changes too. Of eight House members who voted against them, six were Southern Democrats. Senators John Stennis of Mississippi and Harry Byrd also opposed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farm payments and evicted sharecroppers" -->
Federal farm payments in the 1930s went to landowners, and many sharecroppers lost their places on the land. The AAA was a federal farm program that began in May 1933. Its officials paid farmers to grow less. That year farmers plowed under about 10 million acres of growing cotton, and about 6 million pigs were bought and killed.

Under the AAA's cotton contracts of 1934 and 1935, officials paid landowners to take land out of cotton. Agriculture Secretary Henry Wallace "rented" about three-eighths of the country's cotton land in this way. Sharecroppers got only about 11 percent of the payments. A landlord could keep the whole check by evicting the sharecroppers and hiring day laborers, who were paid by the day. Evicting means putting people off the land or out of their homes. When planters cut their cotton land by 40 or 50 percent, they cut their tenants by the same share.

More than two-thirds of Black sharecroppers made no profit or went into debt, according to the National Museum of African American History and Culture.

In the summer of 1934, Black and white sharecroppers in eastern Arkansas founded the Southern Tenant Farmers' Union to protest the evictions. Planters put down the union with violence.

A group of AAA lawyers, led by Jerome Frank, ruled that landlords had to keep the same tenants. In February 1935, AAA administrator Chester Davis had Wallace fire those lawyers. This firing is called the AAA purge.

In 1936 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the AAA broke the Constitution. Members of Congress wrote a new version in 1938. Paying farmers became a lasting part of federal policy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War work ends mass unemployment" -->
During the Second World War, factory owners hired workers to make war goods, and unemployment fell from 14.6 percent in 1939 and 1940 to 1.2 percent in 1944. The 1.2 percent is the lowest rate in the official records, which begin in 1944.

Workers in American factories built 299,230 military aircraft from January 1940 to August 1945. Real GDP counts output with the changes in prices taken out. It rose about 72 percent from 1940 to 1945. By 1945 the United States made about half of all the manufactured goods in the world.
