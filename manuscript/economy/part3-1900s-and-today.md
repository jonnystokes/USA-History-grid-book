## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In these fifty years Americans lived through a stock boom, the Great Depression and the Second World War. A stock is a share in the ownership of a company. After stock prices crashed in October 1929, about one of every four workers had no job by 1933. In Chicago and Pittsburgh, more than half of Black workers had no job in 1932, by later estimates. In 1935 members of Congress and President Franklin D. Roosevelt set up old-age pensions and unemployment insurance in the Social Security Act. A pension is a regular payment to a person too old to work, and unemployment insurance pays workers who have lost their jobs. The law left out farmworkers and domestic workers, people who cleaned, cooked and cared for children in other people's homes. Most Black workers did those two jobs. Two accounts disagree about who made that choice. In 1944, with factories making goods for the war, unemployment fell to 1.2 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="henry-ford-economy" name="Henry Ford" movie="Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Henry Ford

> **Who:** The carmaker who roughly doubled his workers' pay in 1914 and cut the price of his Model T car from 825 dollars to 260 dollars.
> **When and where:** Ford's Highland Park car factory. He announced the five-dollar day on January 5, 1914.
> **Movie:** Henry Ford (American Experience, 2013), a documentary about him shown on PBS.

Henry Ford roughly doubled his workers' pay in 1914. He did it to stop workers from quitting, and workers who earned more could also buy the cars they built. An assembly line moves each car past a row of workers, and each worker does one step over and over. After Ford's managers put in the assembly line, the work never let up and was dull. In 1913 turnover at the Highland Park factory reached 370 percent. Turnover counts the workers who quit and had to be replaced. At 370 percent, managers had to hire almost four new workers in a year for every job.

On January 5, 1914, Ford announced five dollars for an eight-hour day. Before, a Ford worker earned about 2.34 dollars for a nine-hour day. The new rate began on January 12.

The raise came with conditions. Ford paid the extra money as profit-sharing, a share of the company's profits, and a worker qualified only after six months. Staff of the company's Sociological Department inspected workers' homes before granting the full rate. They checked whether workers saved their money and whether they drank alcohol. They also checked for what they called "proper" living. Ford left out men under 22, and at first most women.

After the raise, fewer workers quit. The price of the Model T kept falling, from 825 dollars in 1908 to 260 dollars by 1925.

Ford fought unions for decades. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employer. Ford owned a newspaper, the *Dearborn Independent*, and he published antisemitic articles in it through the 1920s. Antisemitic means hateful toward Jewish people. Ford later apologized in public for the articles.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="henry-ford-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The crash and the Great Depression" -->
The Dow Jones average fell by 89 percent between September 1929 and July 1932. The Dow is a number built from the stock prices of a group of large companies. It rose from 63 in August 1921 to 381 on September 3, 1929. Prices fell hard on Black Thursday, October 24, 1929, and again on Black Tuesday, October 29. By July 8, 1932, the Dow stood at 41.

The hard times of the 1930s are called the Great Depression. By 1933 about 25 percent of workers, one in four, had no job. Government officials and scholars made that estimate later, because nobody counted the jobless every month at the time.

GDP is the total value of all the goods and services made in the country in a year. It fell from about 104 billion dollars in 1929 to about 56 billion dollars in 1933. Prices fell too. From October 1929 to April 1933, the prices of the things people bought every day dropped by about 27 percent. Counted as the amount of goods and services, with the fall in prices taken out, output fell by about 30 percent. About 9,000 of the country's 25,000 banks failed and closed by 1933.

Hungry people stood in breadlines, which are lines for free food. Some people lived in shantytowns, groups of shacks, which people called Hoovervilles.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bonus Army" -->
In the summer of 1932, veterans of the First World War camped in Washington, D.C., to ask for a bonus they were owed for their war service. The bonus was not due until 1945, and the veterans asked to be paid early in cash. Estimates of their number run from about 10,000 to more than 20,000. Many had their families with them. On June 17, members of the Senate voted the payment down.

On July 28, police tried to clear veterans out of some buildings in the city. Police shot and killed two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson. Hushka had come from Lithuania. He sold his butcher shop to join the army in the First World War and became a citizen while he served. He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery less than a week after he was shot.

President Hoover then ordered the Army to clear the camps. General Douglas MacArthur sent in infantry, who are soldiers on foot, and cavalry, who are soldiers on horseback, with tanks and tear gas. Tear gas stings the eyes, nose and throat, so people cough and their eyes stream with tears. MacArthur's soldiers burned the main camp.

Members of Congress later voted to pay the bonus over the veto of President Franklin D. Roosevelt. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a bill. The payments began in June 1936.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-roth-economy" name="Benjamin Roth" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Roth

> **Who:** A lawyer in Youngstown, Ohio, who kept a diary of the Great Depression for more than ten years.
> **When and where:** Youngstown, Ohio. He began the diary in June 1931.

Benjamin Roth, a lawyer in Youngstown, Ohio, kept a record of the Depression from June 1931 for more than ten years. He lived from 1894 to 1978.

In his diary Roth wrote about the banks that closed and the stocks that became worthless. He wrote down prices, and what lawyers and workers earned. In 2009 his son, Daniel B. Roth, and James Ledbetter edited the diary, and the publisher PublicAffairs printed it as a book, *The Great Depression: A Diary*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-roth-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Last hired, first fired" -->
Black workers lost their jobs first and at higher rates than white workers. Employers hired Black workers last and fired them first, a pattern called "last hired, first fired." By 1932 nearly half of all Black Americans had no work, according to the National Museum of African American History and Culture.

History.com, drawing on the Library of Congress, reports that in 1932 more than half of Black workers in Chicago and Pittsburgh had no job. About a quarter of white workers in Northern cities had none. In Atlanta in 1934, nearly 70 percent of Black workers had no job. These numbers are all later estimates, because nobody kept an official count by race.

From 1929, government officials and private employers forced people whose families came from Mexico to leave for Mexico. In 2005 California's lawmakers found that about 400,000 American citizens and legal residents had been forced to go from California alone. They estimated that about two million people were forced out across the country, and that about 1.2 million of them had been born in the United States. Local officials sold some families' property to pay for sending them away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The New Deal" -->
Before 1933, the federal government, the national government in Washington, paid no unemployment insurance and no old-age pensions. Members of Congress set up both in the Social Security Act of 1935. President Roosevelt and his officials started many programs from 1933 on, including the CCC, the WPA and Social Security. Together they are called the New Deal.

President Roosevelt set up the Civilian Conservation Corps, or CCC, on March 31, 1933. It was a federal jobs program for young men. About 3 million young men worked in it over the years it ran. They earned 30 dollars a month and sent about 25 dollars of it home.

The Works Progress Administration, or WPA, ran from 1935 to 1943. About 8.5 million people worked for it. WPA workers built about 650,000 miles of roads, 125,000 public buildings and 75,000 bridges.

On August 14, 1935, President Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act. The first monthly old-age check went to Ida May Fuller of Ludlow, Vermont, on January 31, 1940. It was for 22.54 dollars. She had paid a total of 24.75 dollars in Social Security taxes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frances-perkins-economy" name="Frances Perkins" movie="Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frances Perkins

> **Who:** The Secretary of Labor who chaired the committee that planned Social Security, and the first woman in a United States Cabinet.
> **When and where:** Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945. She lived from 1880 to 1965.
> **Movie:** Summoned: Frances Perkins and the General Welfare (2020), a documentary about her.

As chair of the Committee on Economic Security, Frances Perkins led the planning of Social Security. She was Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945. She was the first woman in a United States Cabinet, the group of the president's top officials.

President Roosevelt set up the committee on June 29, 1934. Its members planned old-age pensions and unemployment insurance. They sent their report to President Roosevelt on January 15, 1935. He signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935.

According to the historian Larry DeWitt, Perkins wanted farmworkers and domestic workers covered by the new law, and so did Harry Hopkins, the official in charge of aid to the jobless. Those workers were not in the law as it passed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frances-perkins-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who Social Security left out" -->
Under the Social Security Act of 1935, farmworkers and domestic workers could not get old-age pensions or unemployment insurance. Domestic workers cleaned, cooked and cared for children in other people's homes. In 1930, 65 of every 100 Black workers did one of these two kinds of work. So about two-thirds of Black workers could get neither payment, and in the South the share was higher. Derrick Johnson, president of the NAACP, a civil rights group, gave these figures in 2020.

At the hearings on the bill in 1935, Charles Hamilton Houston spoke for the NAACP. He said the bill would leave out "Negro sharecroppers and Negro cash tenants, who are just about at the bottom of the economic scale." Negro was a common word for Black people at that time. A sharecropper farmed a landowner's land and paid with part of the crop. A cash tenant rented land and paid in money. Houston called the law "a sieve with the holes just big enough for the majority of Negroes to fall through." A sieve is a strainer full of holes.

Two accounts disagree about who made the choice to leave these workers out. Jon Dubin, in a 2024 article in a Stanford law journal, points to Southern members of Congress. Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia opposed a rule that payments be high enough for "decency and health." He said that rule "might serve as an entering wedge for federal interference with the handling of the Negro question." An entering wedge is a first step that opens the way to more. Byrd did not want federal officials to have a say in how matters touching Black people were handled.

Edwin Witte, executive director of the Committee on Economic Security, reported that Byrd's position was "supported by nearly all of the southern members of both committees," the two committees in Congress that worked on the bill. Representative Howard Smith of Virginia argued for keeping Black sharecroppers and domestic workers dependent on their employers.

Larry DeWitt, a historian at the Social Security Administration, gave a different account in 2010. He found that Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau asked for the changes to the bill that left these workers out. DeWitt said Morgenthau's reasons had to do with collecting the tax. DeWitt wrote that the charges of "racial bias in the founding of the Social Security program" did "not hold up under detailed scrutiny." Racial bias means unfair treatment because of race. Scrutiny means close study.

Members of Congress changed the law in 1950 to cover farm and domestic workers who worked regularly for one employer. In 1954 they covered the rest. Representative Smith opposed these changes too. Of eight House members who voted against them, six were Southern Democrats. Senators John Stennis of Mississippi and Harry Byrd also opposed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Farm payments and evicted sharecroppers" -->
Federal farm payments in the 1930s went to landowners, and many sharecroppers lost their places on the land. The landowners are also called landlords, or planters. In May 1933 members of Congress passed the Agricultural Adjustment Act. Under it, officials of the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, or AAA, ran a farm program under Agriculture Secretary Henry Wallace. They paid farmers to grow less. That year farmers plowed under about 10 million acres of growing cotton, which means they turned the plants back into the soil. AAA officials bought about 6 million pigs and had them killed.

Under the AAA's cotton contracts of 1934 and 1935, officials paid landowners to take land out of cotton. Agriculture Secretary Henry Wallace "rented" about three-eighths of the country's cotton land in this way. Sharecroppers got only about 11 percent of the payments. A landlord could keep the whole check by evicting the sharecroppers and hiring day laborers, who were paid by the day. Evicting means putting people off the land or out of their homes. When planters cut their cotton land by 40 or 50 percent, they cut the number of their tenants, the families who farmed their land, by the same share.

More than two-thirds of Black sharecroppers made no profit or went into debt, according to the National Museum of African American History and Culture.

In July 1934, seven Black men and eleven white men met at the Sunnyside School in Poinsett County, Arkansas, and founded the Southern Tenant Farmers' Union to protest the evictions. Planters and the men who worked for them attacked the union. In January 1935 lawmen at Marked Tree arrested and jailed a white organizer, Ward Rodgers. At a union rally at Gilmore, four armed white men broke in. They ordered the Black members to leave and told them they would be lynched, killed by a mob, if they were caught at another union meeting. Many union members were beaten and threatened with guns. The accounts do not name the men who beat them. On March 15, 1935, at Birdsong, about forty armed planters, their riding bosses and local lawmen broke up a union crowd. Riding bosses were men on horseback who watched over the field workers for the planters. Later that month a mob attacked the home of the union's lawyer, Cornelius Tyree Carpenter.

A group of AAA lawyers, led by Jerome Frank, ruled that landlords had to keep the same tenants. In February 1935, AAA administrator Chester Davis had Wallace fire those lawyers. This firing is called the AAA purge.

In 1936 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled the AAA unconstitutional, which means against the Constitution. Members of Congress wrote a new version in 1938. Federal officials have paid farmers ever since.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="War work ends mass unemployment" -->
During the Second World War, factory workers made war goods. Unemployment fell from 14.6 percent in 1939 and 1940 to 1.2 percent in 1944. The figure for 1939 and 1940 was estimated later. The official yearly records begin in 1944, and 1.2 percent is the lowest rate in them.

Workers in American factories built 299,230 military aircraft from January 1940 to August 1945. Real GDP counts output with the changes in prices taken out. It rose about 72 percent from 1940 to 1945. By 1945 the United States made about half of all the manufactured goods in the world.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The income of the typical American family roughly doubled between 1947 and 1973, even after counting the rise in prices. Families who were poor, in the middle and rich all gained. In the 1970s prices and unemployment rose at the same time. Factory jobs peaked at 19.6 million in June 1979. By the 2000s, more than 80 of every 100 jobs outside farming were in services, in places such as stores, offices, hospitals and schools. In the Mahoning Valley around Youngstown, Ohio, about 50,000 people lost jobs in steel and related work within about five years of 1977. In the 1980s about 300,000 farms could not repay their loans. From March 1991 to March 2001, the country went 120 months in a row without a downturn.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The postwar boom" -->
For about twenty-five years after the Second World War, pay rose for families at every level of income. Between 1947 and 1973, the median family income roughly doubled, counted with the rise in prices taken out. The median is the middle one. Half of all families earned more than the median family, and half earned less. A general rise in prices is called inflation. Inflation means each dollar buys less than before.

The economists Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo studied the gap between high wages and low wages. They found that the gap narrowed sharply in the 1940s and stayed narrow into the 1970s. They named this the Great Compression. To compress means to squeeze together.

Under the G.I. Bill, veterans of the Second World War could use federal money for school or job training, and federal officials backed their home loans. By July 1956, when the first version ended, 7.8 million of the 16 million veterans had used its money for education or job training. From 1944 to 1952, officials of the VA, the federal agency for veterans, backed about 2.4 million home loans. Backing a loan means promising to repay the lender if the borrower cannot.

In the Employment Act of 1946, members of Congress made "maximum employment, production, and purchasing power" a stated duty of the federal government. Purchasing power means how much people can buy with their money. Under the same act they created the Council of Economic Advisers, a group of economists who advise the president. The ideas behind the act came from the British economist John Maynard Keynes. Keynes argued that government spending could fight slumps, the times when business falls off. Members of Congress took a promise of full employment out of the bill before they passed it.

On April 26, 1956, Malcom McLean's ship *Ideal X* carried 58 large shipping boxes, called containers, from Port Newark to Houston. By the economist Marc Levinson's calculation, loading loose cargo onto a ship by hand cost about 5.86 dollars a ton in 1956. Loading it in containers cost about 16 cents a ton.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stagflation in the 1970s" -->
In the 1970s prices and unemployment rose together. Most economists had thought this could not happen. The mix of inflation and a stagnant economy, one that is not growing, was called stagflation.

In October 1973, leaders of Arab oil-selling countries stopped the sale of their oil to the United States. Such a ban on trade is called an embargo. The price of oil roughly quadrupled in six months. Consumer prices, the prices of the things people buy for daily life, rose about 11 percent in 1974. Unemployment reached 9.0 percent in May 1975.

Inflation passed 13 percent in 1979 and 1980. In 1980 prices rose 13.5 percent. In November and December 1982, unemployment reached 10.8 percent. That was the highest rate since the Great Depression.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Factory jobs fall" -->
After 1950, a shrinking share of American workers had jobs in manufacturing, the making of goods in factories. In 1955, 30.6 of every 100 jobs outside farming were in manufacturing. By 2005 about 11 of every 100 were, and by the 2020s about 8. The number of factory jobs peaked at 19.6 million in June 1979. By the 2000s, more than 80 of every 100 jobs outside farming were in service industries.

On September 19, 1977, the managers of Youngstown Sheet and Tube announced that they would close the company's Campbell Works steel mill in Youngstown, Ohio. About 5,000 workers lost their jobs at once. The day became known as Black Monday. Within about five years, about 50,000 people in the Mahoning Valley around Youngstown lost jobs in steel and related work. These numbers come from later news reports.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gerald-dickey-economy" name="Gerald Dickey" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Gerald Dickey

> **Who:** A Youngstown steelworker who proposed that the workers and the town buy their closed mill and reopen it.
> **When and where:** Youngstown, Ohio, 1977 to 1979.

After the Campbell Works closed, Gerald Dickey proposed that the steelworkers and the people of Youngstown buy the mill and reopen it. From 1976 to 1979 he was the recording secretary of Local 1462 of the United Steelworkers union. A union is a group of workers who join together to bargain with their employer. A local is a union's branch in one place. A recording secretary keeps the written record of its meetings.

Church leaders led the Ecumenical Coalition of the Mahoning Valley, and its members took up Dickey's plan. Ecumenical means bringing together people of different churches.

The plan needed about 100 million dollars in federal loan guarantees. A loan guarantee is a promise that the government will repay a loan if the borrowers cannot. Officials in President Carter's government first signaled that they supported the guarantees. In 1979 officials of the Economic Development Administration, a federal agency, turned the guarantees down, and the mill stayed closed. Accounts differ a little on when officials first signaled support. All of them agree on the refusal and the closing.

Dickey and the coalition spread the idea of workers owning their own workplaces through the labor movement.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gerald-dickey-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The farm crisis of the 1980s" -->
In the 1980s about 300,000 farms defaulted, which means their owners could not repay their loans. About 300 farm banks failed.

In the 1970s farmers sold more crops abroad, planted more of their land and borrowed at low rates of interest. Interest is the extra money a borrower pays a lender for a loan. Then officials of the Federal Reserve raised interest rates under their chairman, Paul Volcker. The Federal Reserve is the country's central bank, the government's bank that sets interest rates for other banks. On January 4, 1980, President Jimmy Carter stopped many sales of American farm goods to the Soviet Union after Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan. The Soviet Union was a large country, ruled from Moscow in Russia, and the main rival of the United States. Carter's order canceled contracts for about 17 million metric tons of wheat, corn and soybeans. A metric ton is about 2,205 pounds. Under this grain embargo, farmers lost some sales abroad, and exports fell. Farmers had borrowed against the value of their land. In parts of the Midwest, farmland lost up to 60 percent of its value from 1981 to 1986. Farm debt reached about 215 billion dollars by 1984, double the debt of 1978.

On September 22, 1985, Willie Nelson, John Mellencamp and Neil Young played the first Farm Aid concert, at Memorial Stadium in Champaign, Illinois. About 80,000 people came. The concert raised more than 7 million dollars for farm families.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1980s and 1990s" -->
Income tax rates on the highest incomes fell in the 1980s. Under the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, President Reagan's tax cut, the top income-tax rate fell from 70 percent to 50 percent. Under the Tax Reform Act of 1986, it fell to 28 percent. The top rate is the tax on each dollar of income above a high level.

Inflation fell from 13.5 percent in 1980 to between 3 and 4 percent by the middle of the 1980s. The national debt roughly tripled during the 1980s. The national debt is the total that the federal government owes on money it has borrowed.

From March 1991 to March 2001, the country went 120 months in a row without a downturn. A stretch of growth like this is called an expansion. It was the longest expansion on record until then, according to the National Bureau of Economic Research. That group of economists dates the country's booms and downturns. Unemployment fell to 3.9 percent in the fall of 2000. In the government's budget years of 1998 through 2001, federal tax money coming in was more than federal spending. A year like that is called a budget surplus.

The NASDAQ is an index, a single number built from the stock prices of many companies, including many technology companies. It rose about 600 percent from 1995 to March 2000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="economy" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000 the country has gone through three recessions. A recession is a stretch of months when output falls and people lose their jobs. The longest of the three ran from December 2007 to June 2009, after home prices crashed. In the crash, lenders took homes from Black and Latino families at higher rates than from white families. This is called foreclosure: a lender takes a home because the borrower cannot keep up the loan payments. In August 2026 the unemployment rate was 4.1 percent, and about 86 of every 100 jobs outside farming were in services. From April to June 2026, the richest tenth of households held 68.9 percent of all household wealth, and the poorer half held 2.3 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The dot-com crash" -->
Technology stock prices crashed from 2000 to 2002. The NASDAQ index peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000. By October 2002 it had fallen about 78 percent. The boom and the crash are called the dot-com bubble, after companies that did business on the internet. A bubble is a time when prices climb far above what things are worth and then fall.

A recession followed, from March to November 2001. It lasted eight months.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The crash of 2008" -->
The Great Recession, from December 2007 to June 2009, began with a crash in home prices. Home prices rose for years before the crash. Lenders made home loans, called mortgages, that many borrowers could not repay. Bankers bundled many of these mortgages together into investments and sold the investments as safe. When home prices fell, the investments failed, and some of the biggest financial firms began to fail too.

The financial firm Lehman Brothers failed on September 15, 2008. It owed about 619 billion dollars. It was the largest bankruptcy in the country's history. Bankruptcy is a court process for a person or a company that cannot pay its debts.

In October 2008 members of Congress passed a rescue plan for financial firms, the Troubled Asset Relief Program, or TARP, worth up to 700 billion dollars. Treasury officials paid out about 443 billion dollars under it, and the firms that got the money paid most of it back.

Employers cut about 8.7 million jobs in the Great Recession. Unemployment peaked at 10.0 percent in October 2009. Lenders foreclosed on about 3.8 million homes from 2007 to 2010. Estimates for the whole crisis run from 7.7 million to 10 million homes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who lost homes and wealth" -->
Black and Latino homeowners lost their homes at higher rates than white homeowners, and they lost more of their wealth. Latino and Hispanic both name Americans whose families came from Spanish-speaking countries. African American is another name for Black Americans.

The Center for Responsible Lending, a research group that campaigns on lending, studied people who took out home loans from 2005 to 2008. By 2010, lenders had foreclosed on about 8 percent of the African American borrowers and 8 percent of the Latino borrowers. For non-Hispanic white borrowers the figure was 4.5 percent. Counting all homeowners, 17 percent of Latino homeowners and 11 percent of African American homeowners had lost a home or were close to losing one. For white homeowners the figure was 7 percent. White families still made up about 56 percent of all completed foreclosures, because there were more white borrowers.

Lawyers at the Justice Department said that from 2004 to 2008, Countrywide Financial, a mortgage lender, charged at least 200,000 qualified Black and Latino borrowers higher fees and rates or steered them into subprime loans. A subprime loan has a higher interest rate and is meant for borrowers that lenders think are more likely to fall behind. Bank of America had bought Countrywide. In December 2011 its officers agreed to pay 335 million dollars to settle the case. It was the largest settlement up to that time in a case about lenders treating borrowers unfairly because of their race. A settlement is an agreement to pay money to end a legal case.

On July 12, 2012, officers of the bank Wells Fargo agreed to pay at least 175 million dollars. Of that, 125 million dollars went to borrowers, and 50 million dollars went to help with down payments. A down payment is the part of a home's price that the buyer pays at the start. The Justice Department's lawyers said Wells Fargo charged Black and Latino borrowers higher rates and fees, and steered some into subprime loans when they qualified for regular ones. The lawyers reviewed 2.7 million loans made from 2004 to 2009. They said at least 34,000 borrowers in 36 states and Washington, D.C., were treated this way.

Assistant Attorney General Tom Perez said that a Black borrower in the Chicago area with one kind of Wells Fargo loan paid on average 2,937 dollars more than a similar white borrower. Wells Fargo's officers denied that the bank discriminated, which means treating people unfairly because of their race. They said they settled to avoid a fight in court.

Wealth, or net worth, is what a household owns minus what it owes. The Pew Research Center, a research group, reported in 2011 on wealth from 2005 to 2009, using surveys by the Census Bureau. With inflation taken out, the median wealth of Hispanic households fell 66 percent. For Black households it fell 53 percent, and for white households 16 percent.

In 2009 the typical Black household had 5,677 dollars in wealth, and the typical Hispanic household had 6,325 dollars. The typical white household had 113,149 dollars. The typical white household's wealth was 20 times that of the typical Black household and 18 times that of the typical Hispanic household. These were the largest gaps since the government began collecting the numbers in 1984. About 35 percent of Black households and 31 percent of Hispanic households owned nothing or owed more than they owned. For white households the figure was 15 percent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ed-neufeldt-economy" name="Ed Neufeldt" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Ed Neufeldt

> **Who:** A worker who built cabinets for recreational vehicles in Elkhart, Indiana, lost his job in 2008 and introduced President Obama in 2009.
> **When and where:** Elkhart, Indiana, from 2008 into the middle of the 2010s.

In the fall of 2008, after 32 years of building cabinets at Monaco Coach, an RV plant in Elkhart, Indiana, Ed Neufeldt lost his job. An RV, or recreational vehicle, is a home on wheels. Elkhart was known as the "RV capital of the world." As the RV plants shut, unemployment in Elkhart's county went from about 5 percent to nearly 20 percent in about a year.

On February 9, 2009, Neufeldt introduced President Obama at a town hall meeting in Elkhart. Obama was on his first trip to promote the stimulus bill, a plan to spend federal money to bring back jobs.

Reporters from ProPublica, NPR and NBC News followed Neufeldt for years. He worked three low-wage jobs. Later he got a factory job again. NPR reporters visited him again in 2015 and 2016.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ed-neufeldt-economy" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The long recovery and the shift to services" -->
The recovery after 2009 was slow at first, with years of high unemployment. It then became the longest expansion in the country's records, which go back to 1854. It lasted 128 months, from June 2009 to February 2020. The National Bureau of Economic Research announced these dates on June 8, 2020.

By August 2021, 16 percent of American adults had earned money at some time through an online gig platform, according to the Pew Research Center. A gig platform is an app or a website that connects workers with short, one-time jobs.

In August 2026 there were 159.1 million jobs outside farming. About 12.6 million of them, about 8 percent, were in manufacturing. About 137.5 million, about 86 percent, were in services, counting government jobs. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, the government office that counts jobs and prices, gave these numbers. They are early figures that the bureau can still change.

The shortest recession on record lasted two months, from February to April 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic began. A pandemic is a disease that spreads around the world. Unemployment reached 14.8 percent in April 2020.

On March 27, 2020, members of Congress passed the CARES Act, a relief law of about 2 trillion dollars. It was the largest relief law up to that time. From April to June 2020, GDP fell at a yearly rate of 31.4 percent. A yearly rate shows how far output would fall in a full year at that speed. From July to September, output rose at the fastest rate on record.

Consumer prices rose 9.1 percent in the 12 months to June 2022, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That was the largest rise since November 1981. Economists disagree about the causes. Some blame breakdowns during the pandemic in supply chains, the chains of factories, ships and trucks that bring goods to stores. Others point to federal relief spending, or to higher energy prices after Russian forces invaded Ukraine. By the middle of 2023, prices were rising near 3 percent a year.

In August 2026 the unemployment rate was 4.1 percent, the same as in July. That was about 7.0 million people. Unemployment was 3.7 percent for white workers, 6.0 percent for Black workers, 4.8 percent for Hispanic workers and 3.2 percent for Asian workers. Employers added 162,000 jobs that month. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released these figures on September 4, 2026.

Consumer prices rose 3.4 percent in the 12 months to August 2026. Leaving out food and energy, they rose 2.4 percent. Energy prices rose 16.3 percent, and food prices rose 2.7 percent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released these figures on September 11, 2026.

Real GDP grew 2.1 percent in 2025, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the government office that measures the country's output. From April to June 2026, real GDP grew at a yearly rate of 1.5 percent, by the bureau's estimate of August 26, 2026. As of September 2026, the National Bureau of Economic Research had announced no recession since the two months in 2020.

Economists at the Federal Reserve, the country's central bank, publish figures on who holds the country's wealth. From April to June 2026, American households held about 185.7 trillion dollars in net worth. The richest 10 percent of households held 68.9 percent of it. Within that group, the richest one household in every thousand held 15.0 percent. The next 40 percent of households held 28.8 percent. The poorer half of all households together held 2.3 percent. Households that Federal Reserve economists count as white held 82.4 percent of household wealth. Black households held 3.2 percent, and Hispanic households held 2.4 percent.
