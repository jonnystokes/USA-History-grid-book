## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 9 of 37 · Science · 10 eras · about 95 minutes**

**Science in America grew from a few people watching the sky into labs with thousands of workers, and along the way many scientists used people who never agreed to be studied.**

A dead moose reached General John Sullivan in New Hampshire on April 3, 1787, badly rotted. Thomas Jefferson had asked for its skin, skeleton and horns. He wanted to show a French scientist named Buffon that American animals were not small and weak. The antlers could not be saved, so Sullivan sent another animal's antlers and wrote that they "may be fixed on at pleasure." In the book where he argued with Buffon, Jefferson also called Black people inferior to white people. He called that claim "a suspicion only."

This chapter follows American science from the sunrise posts at Cahokia to the James Webb Space Telescope. Most of it is people measuring things: a comet, the speed of light, the carbon dioxide in the air. Watch also for the people who were measured. From the 1830s to the 1970s, scientists and doctors dug skulls out of graves, had enslaved people photographed as "evidence" about race, and injected hospital patients with plutonium. The last eras show what families and Native nations did about it. Watch, too, who gets the credit: more than once it went to someone other than the person who did the work.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did American scientists learn how far away the stars are?
- Why did scientists collect human skulls and brains, and where are those remains now?
- Who did the work but was left out of the credit?

**Five minutes?** Read "How did Henrietta Leavitt measure space?" in 1900 to 1950, then "The shape of DNA, and who got the credit" in 1950 to 2000.
**Can skip:** "Charting the winds at sea", "The physics inside the transistor, 1947", "Heavy elements, made by many countries", "Computers that predict the shapes of proteins". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 3 minutes**

The people of Native nations here watched the sky for many generations and learned where the sun rose at each time of year. They studied plants, fire, the weather and the tides as well. Each generation taught what it knew to the next.

They marked what they knew about the sky with posts and with carvings in rock. At Cahokia, people set up circles of wooden posts that marked where the sun rose. At Chaco Canyon, sunlight falls on two carved spirals in set places at certain times of year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Woodhenge at Cahokia" -->
The people of Cahokia, in what is now Illinois, tracked the sunrise with circles of upright wooden posts between about the years 900 and 1100. Chapter 8 tells how they built the circles. The place where the sun comes up moves along the horizon over a year, and the posts marked that movement. The circles are known today as Woodhenge. Nations such as the Chickasaw and the Osage descend from the people who lived at Cahokia.

The posts marked the sunrise on the solstices, the longest and the shortest days of the year. They also marked it on the equinoxes, the two days each year when day and night are about the same length.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How does the Sun Dagger at Chaco Canyon work?" -->
Sunlight slips past three slabs of sandstone and lands on two spirals carved into the rock. The slabs lean against a cliff near the top of Fajada Butte, a steep hill with a flat top in Chaco Canyon, in what is now New Mexico. The canyon was a center for the ancestral Pueblo people from 850 to 1250. Their descendants today include the Hopi, the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico and the Navajo Nation.

**The daggers.** The light falls on the spirals as thin blades, called daggers, and they land in set places at the solstices and the equinoxes. Researchers in a group called the Solstice Project argue that the spirals also mark a cycle of the moon. Over 18.6 years, the points where the moon rises and sets swing out to their widest and back again. Other researchers who study ancient astronomy still debate that claim.

**Worn away.** Anna Sofaer, a volunteer making a record of rock art, recorded the daggers at the summer solstice of 1977. They no longer form the way she saw them. By the summer solstice of 1989, two of the three slabs had shifted. Park officials had limited visits to the butte, but visitors walking there had made the ground wear away faster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Knowledge of living things" -->
Native nations built up knowledge of ecology, the ways living things depend on each other and on the land, by watching closely over many generations. Scientists today call it Traditional Ecological Knowledge, or TEK for short. Part of it is knowledge of how fire changes the land and the living things on it.

The Ecological Society of America, a group of scientists who study ecology, has a section for TEK. A botanist, or plant scientist, named Robin Wall Kimmerer helped to found it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robin-wall-kimmerer" name="Robin Wall Kimmerer" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Robin Wall Kimmerer

> **Who:** A botanist of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation who uses Native knowledge and laboratory science together in her work.
> **When and where:** The present day. The State University of New York's College of Environmental Science and Forestry.

**Her 2013 book has been a New York Times bestseller since 2020.**

The book is *Braiding Sweetgrass*, and it has been on the list since February 2020. Kimmerer is a member of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation. In her work she studies plants in two ways together. One is the knowledge that Native nations have built up and taught. The other is the science of the laboratory.

**Sweetgrass at a Mohawk farm.** In a study published in 2005, Kimmerer and a fellow researcher worked to bring back sweetgrass, a plant used to make baskets, at Kanatsiohareke, a Mohawk farm. Members of the Mohawk community chose the spot, because sweetgrass had grown there in the past. The researchers then tested four ways of planting it. The sweetgrass grew best beside a crop called hairy vetch.

She earned a PhD in botany, the highest degree a university gives, at the University of Wisconsin. She teaches at the College of Environmental Science and Forestry, part of the State University of New York, where she founded the Center for Native Peoples and the Environment. In 2022 the MacArthur Foundation named her a MacArthur Fellow.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 1 minute**

Europeans who crossed the ocean in the 1500s studied this land only on short visits. They collected what they found, described it in writing and published their descriptions in Europe.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Thomas Hariot's report" -->
Thomas Hariot, an English mathematician, spent a year on Roanoke Island and wrote down the plants, the animals and the foods he found. The island lies among the shallow waters of the Outer Banks, in what is now North Carolina. Hariot went there in 1585 and 1586 with the expedition that Walter Raleigh sent.

He also wrote about the Native nations who lived there, such as the Secotan. They spoke Algonquian languages, a group of languages related to each other.

**The book.** Hariot published *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* in 1588. It was the first book in English about North America written by someone who had been there. A publisher named Theodor de Bry printed it again in 1590, with pictures made from drawings by John White.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 3 minutes**

A few colonists kept careful records of the sky and the weather in the 1600s, and some of them still read the sky as a message from God. English colonists in New England founded Harvard, the first college in their colonies, in 1636, and printed their first almanac three years later. One of them, Increase Mather, measured comets but still wrote that they were warnings from God.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A college, an almanac and a telescope" -->
Members of the Massachusetts Bay General Court, the colony's lawmakers, voted in 1636 to found a college. It was the first college in the English colonies. John Harvard died in 1638 and left the college his books and half of everything else he owned. On March 13, 1639, the same lawmakers voted to name the college Harvard, after him.

**The almanac.** That same year a sailor named William Pierce worked out an almanac, a yearly booklet with a calendar and tables of the sun and moon. Its title was *An Almanack for New England for the Year 1639*. Stephen Daye printed it at Cambridge, in Massachusetts, on the first printing press in the colonies. It was one of the first two things printed there.

For the next two hundred years, ordinary families learned about astronomy, the study of the sun, the moon, the planets and the stars, from almanacs.

**The telescope.** John Winthrop Jr., a governor of Connecticut, visited London from 1661 to 1663. While he was there, members of the Royal Society, a group of scientists in London, elected him a fellow, or full member. He was one of the first colonists to join. In about 1672 he gave Harvard his telescope, which was 3.5 feet long. It was the college's first scientific instrument.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The comet of 1680" -->
Thomas Brattle followed the Great Comet of 1680 with a telescope from Boston. He had finished his studies at Harvard in 1676. The comet showed in the sky in November and again in December. Brattle worked out that the two sightings were one comet, which had swung around the sun in between.

**A warning and a measurement.** Increase Mather wrote a book about comets in 1683, called *Kometographia*. In it he listed comets as warnings sent by God. Yet he measured comets with parallax, which means looking at an object from two different places. A near object seems to shift against the stars far more than a distant one. His measurements showed that comets were distant bodies, not vapors, a kind of gas.

That same year Mather founded the Boston Philosophical Society, modeled on the Royal Society in London. People then called the study of nature natural philosophy, so a philosophical society was a group for studying nature. Mather's group met for about three years.

The English scientist Isaac Newton published his book the *Principia* in 1687. In it Newton set out his laws of motion and of gravity, and he wrote about Brattle's New England observations of the comet of 1680.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-campanius-holm" name="Rev. John Campanius Holm" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rev. John Campanius Holm

> **Who:** A chaplain in the colony of New Sweden who kept the first known weather record in what is now the United States.
> **When and where:** Near what is now Wilmington, Delaware, 1644 and 1645.

**Twice a day, with no instruments, he wrote down the weather.**

John Campanius Holm kept the first known record of the weather in what is now the United States. He wrote it down twice a day through 1644 and 1645. He followed the same plan day after day, and he had no instruments at all.

Holm was a chaplain, a minister who serves people far from home. He served New Sweden, a Swedish colony on the Delaware River, near what is now Wilmington, Delaware.

Officials of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, the US government's weather and ocean agency, now give the John Campanius Holm Award. It goes to volunteers who take weather readings, up to 25 people each year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-campanius-holm" -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 3 minutes**

Colonists who studied nature began to share their work in these years, with each other and with scientists in London. A farmer named John Bartram and Benjamin Franklin of Philadelphia got them started. Bartram traveled through the colonies from 1730 to collect and study American plants. Franklin organized a society in Philadelphia for this kind of work in 1743. From 1747 he ran experiments with electricity and mailed the results to London. Scientists in Britain read those letters, and Franklin became the first American scientist known outside the colonies.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The American Philosophical Society, 1743" -->
Benjamin Franklin organized the American Philosophical Society in Philadelphia in 1743. It is the oldest learned society in the United States, a group of people who meet to share and discuss what they have found out.

The botanist John Bartram first suggested the idea in 1739. Franklin started the society with a pamphlet, a short printed booklet. It was titled *A Proposal for Promoting Useful Knowledge among the British Plantations in America*, and in that title the word plantations means colonies.

The society grew out of the Junto, a club Franklin had started in 1727 with about twelve friends, most of them tradesmen. Its members met on Friday nights to argue about right and wrong, about government and about nature. Franklin meant the new society to be an American answer to the Royal Society in London.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-science" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="Benjamin Franklin (2022)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Philadelphia man whose experiments with electricity made him the first American scientist known in other countries.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, experiments from 1747 to 1751.
> **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), a two-part documentary by Ken Burns, shown on PBS. It is about Franklin's life.

**Scientists still say "positive" and "negative" because Franklin chose those words.**

Benjamin Franklin ran experiments with electricity in Philadelphia from 1747 to 1751. He reported his results in letters to Peter Collinson, a member of the Royal Society in London.

Franklin thought of electricity as a single fluid. He called an object positive when it held more of this fluid than normal, and negative when it held less. He argued that electric charge is never made or destroyed. It only moves from one object to another, an idea scientists call the conservation of charge. He also proposed that lightning is electricity.

His letters came out in London in April 1751 as a book, *Experiments and Observations on Electricity*. In 1753 members of the Royal Society gave him the Copley Medal, their highest honor. He was the first person from outside Britain to receive it, and the first American to be known in other countries as a scientist.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-science" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-bartram" name="John Bartram" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Bartram

> **Who:** A Quaker farmer who studied plants, built the oldest surviving botanic garden in America, and first suggested the American Philosophical Society.
> **When and where:** His farm at Kingsessing, on the Schuylkill River, from 1728. He lived from 1699 to 1777.

**He and his sons gave science more than 200 new American plants.**

Scientists first learned of more than 200 American plants through John Bartram and his sons. Bartram was a farmer and a Quaker, a member of a Christian group also called the Society of Friends.

He bought a farm of 102 acres at Kingsessing, on the Schuylkill River, in 1728. There he built a botanic garden, where plants are grown so they can be studied. It is the oldest botanic garden in America that survives today.

He traveled from 1730 to 1765 to collect plants, from New York as far south as Florida. The idea that became the American Philosophical Society was his, in 1739. King George III named him the King's Botanist for North America in 1765.

Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish botanist who made the system scientists use to name living things, reportedly called Bartram "the greatest natural botanist in the world."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-bartram" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 7 minutes**

Scientists in Europe began reading and reprinting American work after 1750. Benjamin Franklin designed the 1752 test that showed lightning is electricity. Men in France ran it first, and Franklin wrote that he then ran it with a kite. In 1769 four men at Norriton timed the planet Venus as it crossed the sun, and members of the Royal Society in London reprinted their results.

Thomas Jefferson used evidence in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*, and later a moose, to argue with a scientist in France about the size of American animals. In that same book he wrote that Black people were inferior to white people, meaning less able, in body and mind. He called the claim "a suspicion only." Benjamin Banneker, a free Black farmer who taught himself astronomy, sent Jefferson an almanac he had calculated as proof against it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who ran the lightning test first?" -->
Men in France ran Franklin's lightning test before Franklin did. Franklin had published a plan for a test that would show whether lightning is electricity. Thomas-François Dalibard followed the printed plan at Marly-la-Ville on May 10, 1752, with a tall iron rod. He reported the test to the French Academy, a society of scholars, on May 13. The test showed that lightning is electricity.

**The kite.** Franklin wrote that he ran his own test in Philadelphia with a kite. He first described it in print on October 19, 1752, in the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, a newspaper. Nobody recorded the exact day or the exact spot. The date most often given, June 1752, comes from an account Joseph Priestley wrote in 1767.

Few writers have doubted that the kite flight took place, and no record from the day settles the question either way. One who did doubt it, Tom Tucker, argued in a 2004 book called *Bolt of Fate* that Franklin never flew the kite at all.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Timing Venus, 1769" -->
Four men at Norriton, in Pennsylvania, timed the planet Venus as it passed across the face of the sun on June 3, 1769. An event like this is called a transit of Venus. Astronomers in many parts of the world timed the same transit. They planned to use the times to work out the distance from Earth to the sun.

Members of the American Philosophical Society chose teams to watch from three places. It was the first large, organized science project in the colonies.

**Norriton.** The Norriton team worked at the home of David Rittenhouse, in what is now Montgomery County, Pennsylvania. Rittenhouse built most of the instruments they used. He, William Smith, John Lukens and John Sellers made the timings. Their results filled the first volume of the society's *Transactions*, its printed record of research, which came out in 1771. Members of the Royal Society in London reprinted the results.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="david-rittenhouse" name="David Rittenhouse" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### David Rittenhouse

> **Who:** A Pennsylvania instrument maker and astronomer who taught himself both trades. One of the four men who timed Venus at Norriton in 1769.
> **When and where:** Norriton, Pennsylvania, in 1769, and Philadelphia. He lived from 1732 to 1796.

**He taught himself to build machines that showed the planets moving.**

David Rittenhouse taught himself to make scientific instruments and to study the stars. He built orreries, machines that show the planets moving around the sun. When an orrery's gears turn, small models of the planets move along their paths.

He followed Franklin as president of the American Philosophical Society in 1791. Later he became the first director of the United States Mint, where the country's coins are made.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="david-rittenhouse" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson, Buffon and the moose" -->
A scientist in France named Buffon taught that animals and people grew "degenerate" in the Americas, by which he meant smaller and weaker. Thomas Jefferson argued against him with evidence in his book *Notes on the State of Virginia*. Jefferson wrote the book to answer a list of questions a French official had sent. He called each chapter a Query, an old word for a question.

**A claim with no evidence.** In the chapter called Query XIV, Jefferson wrote that Black people "are inferior to the whites in the endowments both of body and mind." Inferior means lower or less able, and endowments here means natural abilities. He called the claim "a suspicion only." In reason, he wrote, Black people were "much inferior, as I think one could scarcely be found capable of tracing and comprehending the investigations of Euclid," a mathematician of ancient Greece.

Jefferson himself enslaved more than 600 people during his life.

**The moose.** Jefferson's other evidence against Buffon was a moose. He was then the United States minister in Paris, the country's official representative to the French government. On January 7, 1786, he wrote to John Sullivan of New Hampshire, a former general and governor. He asked for "the skin, the skeleton, and the horns of the Moose."

Sullivan's party of men killed a moose in Vermont. Its body reached Sullivan on April 3, 1787, badly rotted. The skull and antlers could not be saved, so Sullivan sent the antlers of another animal. He wrote that they were "not the horns of this moose but may be fixed on at pleasure," meaning Jefferson could attach them however he liked.

The shipment reached France in about late September 1787. Around October 1, Jefferson gave Buffon the moose's bones and skin, along with the other animal's antlers. Jefferson wrote that the moose "convinced Mr. Buffon. He promised in his next volume to set these things right." Buffon died in April 1788 without printing a correction.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-banneker-science" name="Benjamin Banneker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Banneker

> **Who:** A free Black tobacco farmer who taught himself mathematics and astronomy and calculated almanacs for the years 1792 through 1797.
> **When and where:** Baltimore County, Maryland, where he was born on November 9, 1731. He died in 1806.

**He sent Thomas Jefferson a handwritten almanac as proof.**

Benjamin Banneker taught himself mathematics and astronomy from books and instruments that he borrowed. He was a free Black tobacco farmer in Baltimore County, Maryland. For six years of printed almanacs, 1792 through 1797, he worked out where the sun, the moon and the planets would be on each day.

He correctly predicted the solar eclipse of April 14, 1789, when the moon passed between the earth and the sun. Better-known almanac makers had calculated it differently.

**The letter.** On August 19, 1791, Banneker sent Jefferson his almanac in manuscript, written by hand, as proof against Jefferson's claim about Black people. In the letter that went with it, he challenged Jefferson about slavery. "However variable we may be in society or religion," he wrote, "however diversified in situation or colour, we are all of the same family."

Jefferson wrote back on August 30, 1791: "no body wishes more than I do to see such proofs as you exhibit (show), that nature has given to our black brethren (brothers), talents equal to those of the other colours of men." Jefferson sent the almanac on to a man named Condorcet in Paris.

**The almanacs sell.** Banneker's first almanac, for 1792, went on sale in December 1791. Goddard & Angell of Baltimore printed it, and it sold in Baltimore, Philadelphia and Alexandria. His Quaker neighbors, Joseph and George Ellicott, had already interested other Quakers in his work, and the abolition societies of Baltimore and Philadelphia, groups that worked to end slavery.

Members of the abolition societies of Pennsylvania and Maryland promoted the almanacs. Several printers published them, and they sold in the United States and in England. The 1792 almanac opened with support from James McHenry, a Maryland politician, who used the word of the time for a Black person: "I consider this Negro as fresh proof that the powers of the mind are disconnected with the colour of the skin."

**Jefferson, later.** Banneker died in 1806. Three years later, on October 8, 1809, Jefferson wrote to Joel Barlow: "we know he had spherical trigonometry enough to make almanacs, but not without the suspicion of aid from Ellicot, who was his neighbor & friend, & never missed an opportunity of puffing him." Spherical trigonometry is the mathematics of angles and distances on a sphere, such as the sky, and puffing meant praising someone too much. Jefferson was hinting that Ellicott had helped with the mathematics. He himself called it a suspicion.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-banneker-science" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Jefferson's fossil, 1797" -->
Jefferson presented a paper about a fossil animal named Megalonyx, which means "giant claw," to the American Philosophical Society on March 10, 1797. The paper was printed in 1799. It was the first fossil animal from the United States to get a genus name, the name for a group of closely related kinds of animals or plants.

Caspar Wistar wrote a second paper to go with Jefferson's. With it, Wistar began the study of paleontology, ancient life through fossils, in the United States.
