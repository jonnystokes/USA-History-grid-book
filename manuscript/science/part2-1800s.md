## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans founded the country's first lasting science journal and a national institution for science. Benjamin Silliman began teaching chemistry at Yale in 1802 and founded the journal in 1818. The Smithsonian Institution was founded in Washington in 1846, with money left by an Englishman who never visited the country. Its first head, Joseph Henry, set up a network of volunteers who reported the weather.

In Philadelphia in the same years, a doctor named Samuel George Morton collected hundreds of human skulls. Some of the men who supplied him robbed graves to get them. Morton measured the skulls and used the numbers to rank the races of people. The idea that skull size shows the intelligence or worth of a group of people is false.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A professor, a fallen stone and a journal" -->
Benjamin Silliman became Yale's professor of chemistry and natural history in 1802. Chemistry is the study of what things are made of and how substances change. Natural history is the study of plants, animals and rocks. Silliman gave his first lectures in 1804.

On December 14, 1807, a meteorite fell at Weston, Connecticut. A meteorite is a rock from space that falls to the ground. Silliman and James Kingsley wrote a careful record of the fall. They also ran chemical tests to learn what the meteorite was made of. It was the first meteorite fall in the Americas that scientists recorded and studied in this way.

In 1818 Silliman founded the *American Journal of Science*. A scientific journal is a magazine in which scientists publish their research. His was the first one in the country that lasted. For decades people called it "Silliman's Journal."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Joseph Henry, magnets and credit" -->
Joseph Henry discovered self-induction and published it in July 1832, in Silliman's journal. When the electric current in a coil of wire changes, the coil makes its own push of electricity against that change. That effect is self-induction.

Henry was a teacher at the Albany Academy, a school in Albany, New York. His best years of research there ran from 1826 to 1832. He built the strongest electromagnets of his day. An electromagnet is a magnet made by sending electricity through a coil of wire.

Henry also saw a related effect, called electromagnetic induction. It is the making of an electric current with a magnet. Michael Faraday in London published his discovery of it first, in 1831. Henry had found it on his own but published later. Faraday keeps the credit for that discovery, and Henry has the credit for self-induction. The unit for measuring inductance, the strength of this effect in a coil, is called the henry, after him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samuel Morton's skulls" -->
Samuel George Morton was a Philadelphia doctor who collected human skulls and used them to rank the races of people. He lived from 1799 to 1851. He was a member of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, a society of scientists. In 1830 he prepared a lecture on how skulls differ. In the early 1830s he began to collect them. When he died in 1851, he owned 867 human skulls.

He got the skulls through 138 contacts: scientists, merchants, military men and missionaries. Army surgeons at far-off posts were his most useful suppliers. Some of his collectors robbed graves. About a dozen skulls came from a potter's field in Philadelphia, a burial ground for poor people. Poor Black Philadelphians were buried there. More than 50 skulls in his collection were dug out of a burial ground of enslaved Africans in Cuba. Others came from battlefields and from gallows, the frames on which people were hanged. Many of the skulls were those of Native Americans.

Morton filled each skull with peppercorns, and later with lead shot, which is tiny balls of lead. Then he poured them out and measured how much the skull had held. He believed that a bigger skull meant a more intelligent person. He ranked the races by the average size of their skulls, with white Europeans at the top.

His book *Crania Americana*, Latin for "American skulls," came out in 1839. It made him famous in other countries. Defenders of slavery used his rankings to argue for slavery and to claim that some races stood above others. The Penn Museum's own account states that he is sometimes called the "father of scientific racism." Scientific racism means using the look of science to claim that some races are better than others.

Scholars still argue about how carefully Morton measured. In 1978, and again in a 1981 book, Stephen Jay Gould of Harvard wrote that Morton's numbers were biased. That means his mistakes leaned toward the answer he wanted. In 2011 six scholars led by Jason E. Lewis measured 308 of the 670 skulls Morton had published. They found his errors small and random. A 2016 paper disputed parts of their work. The argument is only about whether Morton measured carelessly. His main idea, that skull size ranks the intelligence or worth of groups of people, is false.

The skulls are now in the Penn Museum, at the University of Pennsylvania. In 2021 its director, Christopher Woods, apologized for "the unethical possession of human remains in the Morton Collection." He announced a plan to return them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="james-smithson" name="James Smithson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### James Smithson

> **Who:** An English chemist who left his fortune to the United States to found the Smithsonian Institution. He never set foot in the country.
> **When and where:** Died in Genoa, Italy, on June 27, 1829. The Smithsonian was founded in Washington on August 10, 1846.

James Smithson paid for the Smithsonian Institution, even though he never set foot in the United States. He was an English chemist. He died in Genoa, Italy, on June 27, 1829.

His will left his money to his nephew. If the nephew died without heirs, meaning children to inherit it, the money was to go "to the United States of America, to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment for the increase & diffusion of knowledge." Diffusion means spreading. Smithson never explained why he left his fortune to a country he had never seen, and nobody knows the reason.

The nephew died without heirs in 1835. In September 1838, more than 100,000 gold coins called sovereigns reached the mint in Philadelphia, where the country's coins are made. Workers there turned them into American coins worth more than $500,000. Members of Congress then argued for eight years about what to do with the money. On August 10, 1846, President James K. Polk signed the law that founded the Smithsonian.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-smithson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-henry" name="Joseph Henry" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Henry

> **Who:** A teacher at the Albany Academy who discovered self-induction and became the first Secretary, or head, of the Smithsonian Institution.
> **When and where:** Albany, New York, for his research of 1826 to 1832. Princeton from 1832. Washington from 1846.

Joseph Henry was the first Secretary, or head, of the Smithsonian Institution. He was elected to the job on December 3, 1846.

Before that he had taught at the Albany Academy, where he discovered self-induction. In 1832 he became a professor at Princeton.

At the Smithsonian, from 1849, Henry ran the Smithsonian Meteorological Project. Meteorology is the science of weather. About 150 volunteers wrote down the weather where they lived. They sent their reports by mail, and some by telegraph at no charge. A telegraph sent messages along a wire as bursts of electricity. Henry used the reports to build the first weather maps of the whole country.

A decade later, about 600 observers were sending reports. The project ran from 1849 to 1870. The National Weather Service, the government's weather agency today, began from it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-henry" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charting the winds at sea" -->
Matthew Fontaine Maury made charts of the winds and currents at sea from thousands of old ships' logs. A log is the daily record a ship's crew keeps of its position, the wind and the sea. A current is a stream of moving water inside the ocean. A chart is a map for sailors.

From 1842 Maury ran the Navy's Depot of Charts and Instruments, where old logs were stored. He went through thousands of them for facts about winds and currents. In 1847 he issued the first Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic. He then collected logs from ship captains around the world, all kept in the same format. Captains who used his charts cut their sailing times.

In 1861 Maury resigned from the US Navy and joined the Confederacy. The Confederacy was the group of Southern states that broke away from the United States and fought against it in the Civil War.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maria-mitchell" name="Maria Mitchell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maria Mitchell

> **Who:** A librarian and astronomer on Nantucket who found a comet in 1847. She was the first American and the first woman to win the King of Denmark's gold medal for finding a comet.
> **When and where:** Nantucket, Massachusetts. She found the comet on October 1, 1847.

On October 1, 1847, Maria Mitchell found a comet that no chart of the sky showed. A comet is a frozen body that travels around the sun. She was on the roof of the Pacific National Bank building on Nantucket, an island off Massachusetts. Her father worked at the bank. She was sweeping the sky with a small telescope, moving it slowly across the stars. The comet is known as "Miss Mitchell's Comet."

The King of Denmark offered a gold medal for each new comet found with a telescope. Mitchell won it. She was the first American and the first woman to receive it. An Italian astronomer named de Vico saw the same comet two days after she did.

In 1836 she had become the first librarian of the Nantucket Atheneum, a library on the island. She kept that job for 20 years. She read in the library by day. At night she watched the sky with her father from the roof of the bank, where he was the head officer.

In 1848 Mitchell became the first woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, a society of scholars.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Louis Agassiz, the most famous scientist in America, used his fame to argue that the races of people were created separately. In 1850 he had seven enslaved people in South Carolina stripped and photographed as "evidence" for that claim. From 1868, officials of an Army museum in Washington asked Army doctors to send them the skulls of Native Americans. Scientists there measured the skulls to rank the races. Agassiz also attacked Charles Darwin's book *On the Origin of Species*, and the botanist Asa Gray defended it.

In 1879 Albert Michelson measured the speed of light to within about 0.05 percent of the value used today. Josiah Willard Gibbs of Yale founded the science of energy in chemical changes with a paper published in 1876 and 1878. Williamina Fleming, first hired as a maid, sorted more than 10,000 stars at Harvard's observatory.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The argument over Darwin's book" -->
Charles Darwin's book *On the Origin of Species* came out in London in November 1859. In it, Darwin argued that species were not each created separately. A species is one kind of living thing. The book reached Harvard quickly, and two American scientists took opposite sides.

Asa Gray, a botanist, defended Darwin's theory. Gray had worked out the terms for publishing Darwin's book in America. Louis Agassiz was a zoologist, a scientist who studies animals, and the most famous scientist in America. He attacked the book. He insisted that every species had been created separately.

The two men argued it out at the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in early 1860. Agassiz opened his attack there on January 10, 1860. Gray also defended Darwin in essays in the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine. In 1876 he collected them in a book called *Darwiniana*. In it he argued that a person could accept science and keep a religious faith at the same time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Agassiz and the fifteen photographs" -->
Louis Agassiz used his standing as America's most famous scientist to push a racist idea called polygenism. Polygenism is the false claim that the races of people were created separately. In the winter of 1850, Agassiz hired a photographer named Joseph T. Zealy to photograph enslaved people in South Carolina as "evidence" for it.

On a plantation tour, Agassiz looked for people who had been born in Africa. A plantation is a large farm. The people who did the work on South Carolina's plantations were enslaved. Agassiz thought that people born in Africa were racially "pure."

Zealy made 15 daguerreotypes. A daguerreotype is an early kind of photograph made on a polished metal plate. The pictures show seven people: Renty Taylor, his daughter Delia, Alfred, Drana, Fassena, Jack and Jem. On Agassiz's order, Zealy photographed them stripped. He posed Renty and Delia with no shirts on and photographed them from several angles.

In 1976 the daguerreotypes were found again at Harvard's Peabody Museum. They are the earliest known photographs of enslaved people in America.

Tamara Lanier sued Harvard over the pictures in 2019. She identifies Renty as her great-great-great-grandfather. In 2022 the judges of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts, the state's highest court, let her claim for emotional distress go forward. Emotional distress means harm to a person's mind and feelings. On May 28, 2025, she and Harvard settled the case, and Harvard agreed to give up the images. In December 2025 they went from the Peabody Museum to the International African American Museum in Charleston, South Carolina. They were shown to the public there for the first time on March 11, 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Skulls for the Army Medical Museum" -->
From the 1860s, Army doctors and other collectors sent the skulls of Native Americans to the US Army Medical Museum in Washington. Many were taken from graves and battlefields. Scientists at the museum measured the skulls to rank the races.

In May 1864, a Dakota man was killed near an Army outpost in rural Minnesota. He was about 25 to 35 years old. Nobody recorded his name. Alfred Muller, an Army doctor with the title of acting assistant surgeon, examined the man's remains and sent them to the Army Medical Museum. They ended up at the Smithsonian.

In 1867 officials in the Army's Surgeon General's Office, which ran the Army's doctors, sent out an order called Circular No. 2. It listed skulls among the things Army medical officers should collect. It also listed Native weapons, clothing, tools, food and medicine. From 1868, officials at the Army Medical Museum asked Army doctors in "Indian country" to send them Native American skulls.

The museum's scientists did craniometry, which means measuring skulls. Craniometry was part of a false "racial science." People who did it used the measurements to sort humans into races and to rank the races by how intelligent they believed each one was. George A. Otis, the museum's curator, collected and measured hundreds of the skulls. A curator is the person in charge of a museum's collection.

Collectors dug some of the skulls out of graves, sometimes months after the burial. Others came from battlefields, taken after killings, and some from whole cemeteries that collectors emptied. Some skulls arrived at the museum without anyone asking for them.

By about 1900 the museum's collection held about 3,000 skulls. In 1898 and again in 1904, Army officials transferred the Native remains to the Smithsonian's national museum. The only ones kept back were those of interest for the study of surgery. One record counts 2,000 skulls in the transfer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colleges and farm research in every state" -->
The land-grant colleges began with the Morrill Act, a law of July 2, 1862. They are called land-grant colleges because each state received federal land under the law to pay for them. Through these colleges, students in every state could study science.

Under the Hatch Act of 1887, federal money paid for an agricultural experiment station in every state. An agricultural experiment station is a place where scientists run tests to improve farming. Scientists at the stations did research for the public in every state, including places far from the old college towns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maria Mitchell's eclipse trip, 1878" -->
In 1878 Maria Mitchell led a team of women about 2,000 miles by train to Denver to watch a total solar eclipse. In a total solar eclipse, the moon blocks all of the sun's light for a short time. The eclipse took place on July 29, 1878. Her team was made up of students and graduates of Vassar College, a college for women. The women ran the trip themselves, and reporters for newspapers across the country followed it.

Mitchell had become a professor at Vassar in 1865. She was the first American woman to be a professor of astronomy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Josiah Willard Gibbs

> **Who:** A Yale physicist whose long paper of 1876 and 1878 founded chemical thermodynamics, the science of energy in chemical changes.
> **When and where:** Yale, in New Haven, Connecticut. Professor from 1871.

Josiah Willard Gibbs founded chemical thermodynamics. Thermodynamics is the science of heat and energy. Chemical thermodynamics uses its rules to explain what happens to energy when substances change.

He published this work in two parts, in 1876 and 1878. It was called *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances*. Equilibrium means a steady balance. Heterogeneous substances are mixtures of different materials. The work came out in the *Transactions of the Connecticut Academy*, a journal that few scientists read. It was also very hard to follow.

Scientists in Europe understood the work before scientists in America did. The Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell built a plaster model of a curved surface that Gibbs had described in his mathematics. Maxwell made it with his own hands and mailed it to New Haven.

Gibbs had earned the first American doctorate in engineering, at Yale in 1863. A doctorate is the highest degree a university gives. From 1871 he was Yale's professor of mathematical physics. For his first nine years in that job he received no pay, and he lived on money he had inherited. In 1880 Johns Hopkins University offered him a job, and after that Yale began to pay him.

In 1901 he received the Copley Medal, at that time the top science honor in the world.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-willard-gibbs" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-michelson" name="Albert Michelson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Michelson

> **Who:** An immigrant from Prussia, raised in mining towns in the West, who measured the speed of light to within about 0.05 percent of today's value.
> **When and where:** Annapolis, Maryland, in 1879. Cleveland, Ohio, in 1887.

In 1879, at Annapolis in Maryland, Albert Michelson measured the speed of light. His result came within about 0.05 percent of the value scientists use today. That is an error of about 1 part in 2,000. He measured 299,864 kilometers per second, in air. Some books print his figure as 299,910.

Michelson was born in 1852 in Strelno, Prussia, a town that is now in Poland. He grew up in mining towns in California and Nevada. He graduated from the US Naval Academy at Annapolis.

In July 1887, in Cleveland, Ohio, Michelson and Edward Morley ran the test now called the Michelson-Morley experiment. Scientists then thought that light traveled as waves through an unseen substance they called the ether. Michelson and Morley found no trace of the ether. A result like this, where the test finds nothing, is called a null result. Their null result was an early clue for the theory of relativity, which came later.

In 1907 Michelson became the first American to win a Nobel Prize in science.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-michelson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="williamina-fleming" name="Williamina Fleming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Williamina Fleming

> **Who:** An immigrant from Scotland, hired as a maid in the house of the Harvard College Observatory's director, who went on to classify more than 10,000 stars.
> **When and where:** Harvard College Observatory, from about 1879.

Williamina Fleming sorted more than 10,000 stars by type for the Draper Catalogue, a list of stars published in 1890. She was an immigrant from Scotland. In about 1879 she was hired as a maid in the house of Edward Pickering, the director of the Harvard College Observatory. An observatory is a building for studying the sky. In 1881 she moved onto the observatory's staff.

She was one of the observatory's "computers." At that time a computer was a person whose job was to do calculations and measurements. Fleming studied photographic plates, which are glass sheets that hold photographs of the night sky. On a plate from 1888 she spotted the Horsehead Nebula. A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust in space.

Fleming discovered 59 nebulae, more than 310 variable stars and 10 novae. A variable star is a star whose brightness changes. A nova is a star that suddenly grows much brighter for a time. In 1899 she was named Curator of Astronomical Photographs, the person in charge of the observatory's photographs of the sky.

Harvard's time capsule of 1900, called the Chest of 1900, holds an entry by Fleming. A time capsule is a box of papers sealed to be opened in the future. Her entry records that she earned $1,500 a year, while men in similar jobs earned $2,500. She wrote: "Does he ever think that I have a home to keep and a family to take care of as well as the men?"
