## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 10 minutes**

Americans set up their own places for science between 1800 and 1850. Benjamin Silliman, Yale's professor of chemistry from 1802, founded the country's first lasting science journal, a magazine where scientists publish their research, in 1818. President James K. Polk signed a law in 1846 to set up the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, paid for by an Englishman who never visited the country. Its first head, Joseph Henry, set up a network of volunteers who reported the weather.

In Philadelphia in the same years, a doctor named Samuel George Morton collected hundreds of human skulls. Some of the men who supplied him robbed graves to get them. Morton measured the skulls and used the numbers to rank the races of people. The idea that skull size shows the intelligence or worth of a group of people is false.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A professor, a fallen stone and a journal" -->
Benjamin Silliman became Yale's professor of chemistry, the study of what things are made of and how they change, in 1802. His job covered natural history too, the study of plants, animals and rocks. He gave his first lectures in 1804.

**The Weston meteorite.** A meteorite, a rock from space, fell at Weston, Connecticut, on December 14, 1807. Silliman and James Kingsley wrote down what was known about the fall. Then they ran chemical tests to learn what the rock was made of. It was the first meteorite fall in the Americas that scientists recorded and studied in this way.

Silliman founded the *American Journal of Science* in 1818. It was the first science journal in the country that lasted, and for decades people called it "Silliman's Journal."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who found induction first?" -->
Michael Faraday in London was the first to publish the discovery of electromagnetic induction, the making of an electric current with a magnet. He published it in 1831, and he keeps the credit. Joseph Henry, in Albany, New York, had found the same effect on his own but published later.

**Self-induction.** Henry has the credit for a related discovery, self-induction, which he published in Silliman's journal in July 1832. When the electric current in a coil of wire changes, the coil makes its own push of electricity against that change. Scientists measure the strength of self-induction in a coil with a unit called the henry, named after him.

Henry was a teacher at the Albany Academy, a school in Albany, and his best years of research there ran from 1826 to 1832. He built the strongest electromagnets of his day. An electromagnet is a magnet made by sending electricity through a coil of wire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Samuel Morton's skulls" -->
Samuel George Morton, a Philadelphia doctor, collected human skulls and used them to rank the races of people. He lived from 1799 to 1851 and belonged to the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, a society of scientists. He prepared a lecture on how skulls differ in 1830 and began to collect them in the early 1830s. When he died in 1851, he owned 867 human skulls.

**Where they came from.** He got the skulls through 138 contacts: scientists, traders, military men and missionaries, people sent by churches to spread their religion. Army surgeons at far-off posts were his most useful suppliers. Some of his collectors robbed graves. About a dozen skulls came from a potter's field in Philadelphia, a burial ground for poor people, where poor Black Philadelphians were buried.

Morton worked as a doctor at the city's almshouse, a public home for poor people. In those same years he himself got the remains, what was left of the bodies, of at least 14 Philadelphians, Black and white. Paul Wolff Mitchell, a researcher for a University of Pennsylvania project, wrote a 2021 report on these remains. It does not name who dug up their bodies. Reporters at WHYY, a Philadelphia news station, wrote the same year that the Philadelphia skulls were likely bought from grave robbers.

A doctor in Havana, José Rodríguez Cisneros, dug up the graves of enslaved Africans in Cuba in 1840 and shipped their skulls to Morton. He wrote that he had got 50 skulls, and other records count 51 or 55. More skulls came from battlefields and from gallows, the frames on which people were hanged. Many of the skulls were those of Native Americans.

**The measuring.** Morton filled each skull with peppercorns, and later with lead shot, which means tiny balls of lead. Then he poured them out and measured how much the skull had held. He believed that a bigger skull meant a more intelligent person. He ranked the races by the average size of their skulls, with white Europeans at the top.

His book *Crania Americana*, Latin for "American skulls," came out in 1839 and made him famous in other countries. The Penn Museum's own account states that Morton is sometimes called the "father of scientific racism." Scientific racism is the use of the look of science to claim that some races are better than others.

**Used to defend slavery.** Two of Morton's followers, the doctor Josiah Nott and George Gliddon, used his work to defend slavery. In 1854 they published a book in Morton's honor called *Types of Mankind*. Nott argued in it that Black people were a separate species, a separate kind of living thing, naturally and forever below white people. His aim was to show that freeing enslaved people would be a disaster for both races. Nott wrote to protect his clients, who were slaveholders.

**Disputed.** Scholars still argue about how carefully Morton measured. Stephen Jay Gould of Harvard wrote in 1978, and again in a 1981 book, that Morton's numbers were biased, meaning his mistakes leaned toward the answer he wanted. In 2011 six scholars led by Jason E. Lewis measured 308 of the 670 skulls Morton had published, and they found his errors small and random. A 2016 paper argued against parts of their work.

**Known:** Morton's main idea, that skull size ranks the intelligence or worth of groups of people, is false, and the argument is only about how carefully he measured.

**The skulls today.** The Penn Museum, at the University of Pennsylvania, holds Morton's skulls. Museum staff took them off a classroom display in 2020. On April 12, 2021, the museum's director, Christopher Woods, apologized for "the unethical possession of human remains in the Morton Collection." Unethical means wrong. He announced a plan to return the remains.

On the order of a Philadelphia court, museum staff buried the skulls of 19 Black Philadelphians from the collection in January 2024. They buried them at Eden Cemetery in Collingdale, Pennsylvania, one of the oldest Black burial grounds near Philadelphia. A group called the Black Philadelphians Descendant Community Group objected. Its members said that the Black community, not the museum, should decide what happened to the remains.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### James Smithson

> **Who:** An English chemist who left his fortune to the United States to found the Smithsonian Institution. He never set foot in the country.
> **When and where:** Died in Genoa, Italy, on June 27, 1829. The Smithsonian was founded in Washington on August 10, 1846.

**More than 100,000 gold coins crossed the ocean to found the Smithsonian.**

James Smithson, an English chemist, paid for the Smithsonian Institution, even though he never set foot in the United States. He died in Genoa, Italy, on June 27, 1829.

His will left his money to his nephew, with a condition. If the nephew died without heirs, meaning children or other family to inherit the money, it was to go to the United States. The will said the money was "to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment (a place set up for a purpose) for the increase & diffusion (spreading) of knowledge."

**Not known:** why Smithson left his fortune to a country he had never seen. He never explained it.

The nephew died without heirs in 1835. In September 1838, more than 100,000 gold coins called sovereigns reached the mint in Philadelphia, where the country's coins are made. Workers there turned them into American coins worth more than $500,000. Members of Congress then argued for eight years about what to do with the money. President James K. Polk signed a law to set up the Smithsonian on August 10, 1846.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="james-smithson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-henry" name="Joseph Henry" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Henry

> **Who:** A teacher at the Albany Academy who discovered self-induction and became the first Secretary, or head, of the Smithsonian Institution.
> **When and where:** Albany, New York, for his research of 1826 to 1832. Princeton from 1832. Washington from 1846.

**About 150 volunteers sent him the weather from where they lived.**

Joseph Henry, the first Secretary, or head, of the Smithsonian Institution, drew the first weather maps of the whole country. From 1849 he ran the Smithsonian Meteorological Project, a study of the weather. About 150 volunteers wrote down the weather where they lived. They sent their reports by mail, and by telegraph at no charge. A telegraph sent messages along a wire as bursts of electricity.

A decade later, about 600 observers were sending reports. The project ran from 1849 to 1870, and it led to the National Weather Service, the weather agency of the United States government today.

**How he got the job.** Henry discovered self-induction while he taught at the Albany Academy, and he became a professor at Princeton in 1832. On December 3, 1846, he won seven of the twelve votes cast by the Smithsonian's Board of Regents, the group that runs it. He held the job for 32 years, until he died in 1878.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-henry" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Charting the winds at sea" -->
Matthew Fontaine Maury made charts, or maps for sailors, of the winds at sea and of the currents, the streams of moving water inside the ocean. He built them from thousands of old ships' logs. A log is the daily record a ship's crew keeps of its position, the wind and the sea.

Maury ran the Navy's Depot of Charts and Instruments, where old logs were stored, from 1842. He went through thousands of them for facts about winds and currents. In 1847 he issued the first Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic. He then collected logs from ship captains around the world, all kept in the same format. Captains who used his charts cut their sailing times.

**He joined the Confederacy.** Virginia's leaders took the state out of the United States in April 1861. Maury then resigned from the US Navy and joined the Confederacy, the group of Southern states that broke away from the United States and fought against it in the Civil War. Before the war, Maury had wanted slavery to spread into the American West and also into Cuba and Brazil.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maria-mitchell" name="Maria Mitchell" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maria Mitchell

> **Who:** A librarian and astronomer on Nantucket who found a comet in 1847. She was the first American and the first woman to win the King of Denmark's gold medal for finding a comet.
> **When and where:** Nantucket, Massachusetts. She found the comet on October 1, 1847.

**From a Nantucket bank roof, she found a comet nobody had charted.**

Maria Mitchell was sweeping the sky with a small telescope on October 1, 1847, moving it slowly across the stars. She was on the roof of the Pacific National Bank building on Nantucket, an island off Massachusetts, where her father worked. She found a comet, a frozen body that travels around the sun, that no chart of the sky showed. It is known as "Miss Mitchell's Comet."

The King of Denmark offered a gold medal for each new comet found with a telescope. Mitchell won it, the first American and the first woman to receive it. An Italian astronomer named de Vico saw the same comet two days after she did.

**Library by day.** Mitchell had been the first librarian of the Nantucket Atheneum, a library on the island, since 1836. For the 20 years she held that job, she read in the library by day. At night she and her father watched the sky from the roof of the bank, where he was in charge.

She became the first woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, a society of scholars, in 1848.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 14 minutes**

Louis Agassiz, the most famous scientist in America, used his fame in these years to push a false claim about race. In 1850 he had seven enslaved people in South Carolina photographed, with their clothes taken off, as "evidence" that the races of people were created separately.

Officials of an Army museum in Washington asked Army doctors, from 1868, to send them the skulls of Native Americans, and scientists there measured the skulls to rank the races. Agassiz attacked Charles Darwin's book *On the Origin of Species*, and the botanist Asa Gray defended it.

Other American scientists measured light, energy and the stars with new care. Albert Michelson measured the speed of light in 1879 to within about 0.05 percent of the value used today. Josiah Willard Gibbs of Yale founded the science of energy in chemical changes with a paper published in 1876 and 1878. Williamina Fleming, first hired as a maid, sorted more than 10,000 stars at Harvard's observatory.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The argument over Darwin's book" -->
Charles Darwin's book *On the Origin of Species* came out in London in November 1859. Darwin argued that species, the different kinds of living things, were not each created separately. He wrote that species change over time, and he called the way they change natural selection.

**How it works.** Some living things have a trait, a feature such as color or size, that helps them survive and have young where they live. They have more young, and the young are likely to have the trait too. In time, the trait spreads through the group.

**Two sides at Harvard.** Copies of the book reached Harvard quickly, and two scientists there took opposite sides. Asa Gray, a botanist, defended Darwin's theory. Gray had arranged for the book to be published in America. Louis Agassiz, who studied animals and was the most famous scientist in America, attacked it. He insisted that every species had been created separately.

The two men argued it out at the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in early 1860, after Agassiz opened his attack there on January 10. Gray kept defending Darwin in essays in the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine. He collected them in 1876 in a book called *Darwiniana*. In it he argued that a person could accept science and keep a religious faith at the same time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Agassiz and the fifteen photographs" -->
Louis Agassiz used his standing as America's most famous scientist to push a racist idea called polygenism, the false claim that the races of people were created separately. In the winter of 1850, Agassiz hired a photographer named Joseph T. Zealy to photograph enslaved people in South Carolina as "evidence" for it.

On a tour of plantations, the large farms worked by enslaved people, Agassiz looked for people who had been born in Africa. He thought that people born in Africa were racially "pure." He examined them himself. He left instructions with Robert W. Gibbes, a doctor in Columbia, South Carolina, to collect photographs as evidence. Gibbes arranged for Zealy to photograph the people Agassiz had examined.

Zealy made 15 daguerreotypes, an early kind of photograph made on a polished metal plate. The pictures show seven people: Renty Taylor, his daughter Delia, Alfred, Drana, Fassena, Jack and Jem.

**Found again.** Elinor Reichlin found the daguerreotypes at Harvard's Peabody Museum in 1976. They are the earliest known photographs of enslaved people in America. In 1977 Reichlin wrote that each person had been posed naked. News reports in 2025 said that Renty and Delia were posed with no shirts on and photographed from several angles. Reichlin's account does not make clear whether Gibbes or Zealy did the posing.

**Who they were.** B. F. Taylor enslaved Renty on his plantation, called Edgehill. Delia was Renty's daughter, born in America to parents from Africa. Jack worked in Taylor's fields as a driver, an enslaved man put in charge of other enslaved workers. F. W. Green of Columbia enslaved Jem. Reichlin's account does not name who enslaved Alfred, Drana and Fassena.

**The lawsuit.** Tamara Lanier, who identifies Renty as her great-great-great-grandfather, sued Harvard over the pictures in 2019. In 2022 the judges of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts, the state's highest court, let her claim for emotional distress, harm to a person's mind and feelings, go forward. Lanier and Harvard's lawyers settled the case on May 28, 2025, and Harvard's leaders agreed to give up the images.

Harvard's leaders handed the pictures over in December 2025, from the Peabody Museum to the International African American Museum in Charleston, South Carolina. Museum staff there unveiled them to the public on March 11, 2026.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did Army doctors send skulls to Washington?" -->
Scientists at the US Army Medical Museum in Washington measured the skulls of Native Americans to rank the races. From the 1860s, Army doctors and other collectors sent them the skulls, and the collectors took many of them from graves and battlefields.

**One man's remains.** The museum got the remains of a Dakota man killed in May 1864 near an Army post in rural Minnesota. Militiamen, part-time citizen soldiers who were fighting the Native people there, shot him twice. One bullet shattered his skull. The other went through his mouth or neck.

Soldiers dragged his body to a nearby fort, where settlers beat it. Someone cut off his scalp, the skin and hair of the top of the head, and carried it away to keep. Someone buried the body in a shallow grave. A few months later, his skeleton was dug up again.

He was about 25 to 35 years old, and nobody recorded his name. The historian Samuel Redman, who told this man's story in 2016, does not name the militiamen, the settlers or the person who dug him up. Alfred Muller, an Army doctor with the title of acting assistant surgeon, examined the man's remains and sent them to the Army Medical Museum. They ended up at the Smithsonian.

**The orders.** Officials in the Army's Surgeon General's Office, which ran the Army's doctors, sent out a letter called Circular No. 2 in 1867. In it they asked Army medical officers to collect skulls, along with Native weapons, clothing, tools, food and medicine. From 1868, officials at the Army Medical Museum asked Army doctors in "Indian country," the lands where Native nations lived, to send them Native American skulls.

The museum's scientists did craniometry, which means measuring skulls. Craniometry was part of a false "racial science." People who did it used the measurements to sort humans into races, then ranked the races by how intelligent they believed each one was. George A. Otis, the museum's curator, the person in charge of its collection, collected and measured hundreds of the skulls.

Redman's history of these collections tells how the collectors got the skulls. They dug some out of graves, sometimes months after the burial, and emptied whole cemeteries. Others came from battlefields, from people who had just been killed there. Redman's account does not say who did the killing. Some skulls arrived at the museum without anyone asking for them.

**Mulberry Creek, 1869.** One of the collectors was B. E. Fryer, the Army surgeon at Fort Harker in Kansas. In late January 1869, US soldiers and Kansas settlers attacked a party of Pawnee men at a farm near Mulberry Creek, in Kansas. James Riding In, a Pawnee historian, calls it a massacre. The Pawnee said the soldiers fired first, and Army officers said the Pawnee did.

Two soldiers were wounded. The soldiers and settlers killed eight or nine Pawnee men. Settlers captured one of the Pawnee men and killed him, and the reason given was that he was "trying to escape." Five men escaped and hid their dead friends' bodies from the soldiers. Three of those five later died of exposure, from cold out in the open. As many as twelve Pawnee men may have died because of the attack.

Fryer sent a civilian to cut off the dead men's heads on January 30. Over the next weeks, Fryer or men he sent searched for the hidden bodies. On March 11, 1869, Fryer shipped 26 Native skulls to Washington, and six of them were Pawnee. He wrote to Otis that two of the skulls were damaged because soldiers "shot into the bodies and heads several times after the fight."

Fryer sent Otis the remains of at least 42 people from 1868 to 1872. They were Cheyenne, Wichita, Caddo, Osage, Kansa and people of other nations. Riding In told what Fryer did in a 1992 article, quoting Fryer's letter to Otis.

**Moved to the Smithsonian.** The museum's collection held about 3,000 skulls by about 1900. Army officials moved the Native remains to the Smithsonian's national museum in 1898 and again in 1904. The only ones they kept back were those of interest for the study of surgery. One record counts 2,000 skulls in the transfer.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colleges and farm research in every state" -->
The Morrill Act became law on July 2, 1862. Under it, members of Congress gave each state 30,000 acres of public land for each of its senators and representatives. The states sold the land and used the money to start colleges that taught farming and the skills of machines and building. These are called land-grant colleges, and through them students in every state could study science.

**Whose land.** The land had been taken from Native nations. The reporters Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone counted about 10.7 million acres of it. US officials had taken it from nearly 250 Native nations, bands and communities, in more than 160 land deals backed by violence. For all of it, US officials paid less than $400,000.

Under the Hatch Act of 1887, members of Congress set aside federal money for an agricultural experiment station in every state, a place where scientists run tests to improve farming. Scientists at the stations did research for the public in every state, including places far from the old college towns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Maria Mitchell's eclipse trip, 1878" -->
Maria Mitchell led a team of women about 2,000 miles by train to Denver in 1878 to watch a total solar eclipse, when the moon blocks all of the sun's light for a short time. The eclipse took place on July 29, 1878. Her team was made up of students and graduates of Vassar College, a college for women. The women ran the trip themselves, and reporters for newspapers across the country followed it.

Mitchell had become a professor at Vassar in 1865. She was the first American woman to be a professor of astronomy.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="josiah-willard-gibbs" name="Josiah Willard Gibbs" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Josiah Willard Gibbs

> **Who:** A Yale physicist whose long paper of 1876 and 1878 founded chemical thermodynamics, the science of energy in chemical changes.
> **When and where:** Yale, in New Haven, Connecticut. Professor from 1871.

**Yale paid him nothing for his first nine years as professor.**

Josiah Willard Gibbs founded chemical thermodynamics. Thermodynamics is the science of heat and energy, and chemical thermodynamics uses its rules to explain what happens to energy when substances change. He published this work in two parts, in 1876 and 1878, as *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances*. Equilibrium means a steady balance, and heterogeneous substances are mixtures of different materials.

The work came out in the *Transactions of the Connecticut Academy*, a journal that was little known, and it was very hard to follow.

**A model in the mail.** Scientists in Europe took notice of Gibbs's work before scientists in America did. In 1873 Gibbs had published shorter papers that described a curved surface in mathematics. In 1874 the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell, a scientist who studied matter and energy, built a model of that surface with his own hands. He shaped it in clay, made copies in plaster and mailed one to Gibbs in New Haven.

**No pay.** Gibbs had earned the first American doctorate in engineering, at Yale in 1863. A doctorate is the highest degree a university gives. He was Yale's professor of mathematical physics from 1871, and for his first nine years in that job he received no pay. He lived on money he had inherited. When leaders of Johns Hopkins University offered him a job in 1880, Yale's leaders began to pay him so that he would stay.

He received the Copley Medal, at that time the top science honor in the world, in 1901.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="josiah-willard-gibbs" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="albert-michelson" name="Albert Michelson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Albert Michelson

> **Who:** An immigrant from Prussia, raised in mining towns in the West, who measured the speed of light to within about 0.05 percent of today's value.
> **When and where:** Annapolis, Maryland, in 1879. Cleveland, Ohio, in 1887.

**His speed of light was off by only 1 part in 2,000.**

Albert Michelson measured the speed of light at Annapolis, in Maryland, in 1879. He got 299,864 kilometers per second, in air, and some books print his figure as 299,910. His result came within about 0.05 percent of the value scientists use today. That is an error of about 1 part in 2,000.

Michelson was born on December 19, 1852, in Strelno, a town in Prussia, a German kingdom. The town is now Strzelno, in Poland. His parents brought him to the United States in 1855, when he was 2. He grew up in mining towns in California and Nevada, and he graduated from the US Naval Academy at Annapolis.

**No ether.** Michelson and the chemist Edward Morley ran the test now called the Michelson-Morley experiment in Cleveland, Ohio, from April to July 1887. Scientists then thought that light traveled as waves through an unseen substance they called the ether. Michelson and Morley found no trace of the ether. A result like this, where the test finds nothing, is called a null result.

Their null result was an early clue for the theory of relativity, a new account of space, time and light that Albert Einstein put forward in 1905. Michelson became the first American to win a Nobel Prize in science, in 1907.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="albert-michelson" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="williamina-fleming" name="Williamina Fleming" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Williamina Fleming

> **Who:** An immigrant from Scotland, hired as a maid in the house of the Harvard College Observatory's director, who went on to classify more than 10,000 stars.
> **When and where:** Harvard College Observatory, from about 1879.

**She started as a maid in the observatory director's house.**

Williamina Fleming, an immigrant from Scotland, took a job in about 1879 as a maid in the house of Edward Pickering. He was the director of the Harvard College Observatory, a building for studying the sky. In 1881 she moved onto the observatory's staff, and she sorted more than 10,000 stars by type for the Draper Catalogue, a list of stars published in 1890.

She was one of the observatory's "computers." At that time a computer was a person whose job was to do calculations and measurements. Fleming studied photographic plates, glass sheets that hold photographs of the night sky. On a plate from 1888 she spotted the Horsehead Nebula, a cloud of gas and dust in space.

Fleming discovered 59 nebulae and 10 novae, stars that suddenly grow much brighter for a time. She also found more than 310 variable stars, stars whose brightness changes. In 1899 she became Curator of Astronomical Photographs, the person in charge of the observatory's photographs of the sky.

**The Chest of 1900.** Harvard's time capsule of 1900, a box of papers sealed to be opened in the future, holds an entry by Fleming. It records that she earned $1,500 a year, while men in similar jobs earned $2,500. She wrote: "Does he ever think that I have a home to keep and a family to take care of as well as the men?"
