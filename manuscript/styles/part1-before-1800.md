## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 35 of 37 · Styles · 10 eras · about 100 minutes**

**From the first colonial dress laws to the CROWN Act, Americans with power made rules about who could wear what, and much of the cloth came from people who were enslaved, held captive or paid by the piece.**

Hannah Lyman was about 16 when the judges of the Hampshire County court in Northampton, Massachusetts, fined her 10 shillings for wearing silk. The year was 1676. Massachusetts lawmakers had passed laws saying who was allowed to wear silk, lace and "great boots." The charge against Hannah said she wore her silk "in a flaunting manner," and that she wore it "not only before, but when she stood presented." She wore silk even while she stood charged before the court.

This chapter follows what Americans wore, and what it was made from, through all ten eras: turkey-feather blankets, wigs, homespun, hoop skirts, blue jeans, nylon stockings, polyester suits and sneakers. As you go, watch two things. Watch who made the rules: Massachusetts judges counting silk hoods, South Carolina lawmakers listing the only cloth enslaved people could wear, a San Francisco sheriff cutting off Chinese prisoners' braids, a wrestling referee in New Jersey. And watch who made the cloth: enslaved people in the cotton fields and indigo vats, Thai workers held behind razor wire in El Monte, women sewing in Bangladesh and Los Angeles.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why did lawmakers and officials make rules about other people's clothes and hair?
- Who made the cloth Americans wore?
- Which new materials changed what Americans wore?

**Five minutes?** Read "Cotton picked in the South, woven in the North" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Wigs in Williamsburg, 1769 to 1775", "The Federal style, 1780 to 1820", "Shirtwaists and the Gibson Girl, 1890s". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 5 minutes**

The people of North America before 1500 dressed in what they could grow, hunt or get by trade. They wore deer hide, rabbit fur and turkey feathers, and cloth woven from cotton and from the fiber of the yucca, a desert plant. Their ornaments were sea shells from the Gulf of Mexico and copper from the country around Lake Superior. Their colors came from plants and from a mineral called red ochre.

No written record names anyone who wove this cloth or wore these beads. Archaeologists, scientists who dig up and study the things people left behind, have found the cloth, the feathers, the beads and the copper. What is known comes from those objects.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many feathers went into one blanket?" -->
A blanket made in the 1200s used about 11,550 turkey feathers, pulled from 4 to 10 turkeys. Its makers wrapped the feathers around more than 590 feet (180 meters) of cord twisted from yucca fiber. The blanket is kept at the Edge of the Cedars State Park Museum in southeastern Utah.

Its makers were Ancestral Puebloan people, the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples. They lived in the Four Corners, the place in the Southwest where Utah, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico meet. Most of their clothing was deer hide, rabbit skin and yucca fiber.

Bill Lipe and Shannon Tushingham, researchers at Washington State University, studied the blanket. In 2020 they reported that the makers probably pulled most of the feathers from live birds. In this region, feather blankets had replaced blankets made from strips of rabbit fur many centuries earlier.

**Cotton.** Cotton came north by trade from Mexico. The oldest farmed cotton found in the Tonto Basin of Arizona dates to about the year 250, and cotton reached the Four Corners around 600. By about 900, Ancestral Puebloan men were growing cotton, spinning it into thread and weaving large cloths and blankets on upright looms, frames that hold threads tight so a weaver can pass other threads over and under them.

The Salado people lived in the Tonto Basin from about 1250 to 1450, and by about 1300 their basin was one of the leading cotton-growing areas of the Southwest. They wove plain cotton cloth and openwork cloth, with small openings left in the weave as a pattern. They made blankets from strips of worn-out cotton cloth. One brightly dyed strap they wove is a tumpline, a band worn across the forehead to carry a load on the back. Their sandals were woven from agave and yucca, two desert plants.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell and copper along the Mississippi" -->
Cahokia was a city of earth mounds in Illinois, a few miles east of the Mississippi River and today's St. Louis. Its people traded for copper from the Lake Superior region and for sea shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Archaeologists call the way of life of Cahokia and the towns like it the Mississippian culture.

Mississippian metalworkers heated copper, hammered it while the heat kept it soft, let it cool, and did the same again and again, a way of working metal called annealing. They made hair ornaments, pieces for headdresses and flat plates.

**The bird-man.** Some plates show a figure that is part bird and part man, pushed out by hammering from the back of the sheet. Artists at Cahokia began making pictures of this bird-man between 1100 and 1300. Mississippian copper plates were made from about 800 to 1600, and they have been found from Florida and Georgia to Oklahoma and Wisconsin.

At Cahokia, archaeologists found a copper workshop just north of a mound called Mound 34. It was a building about 18 by 14 feet (5.5 by 4.4 meters). Inside were raw copper nuggets, scraps of worked copper and stone tools for working it. Gregory Perino first found signs of the workshop in 1956. Later its exact spot was lost, and archaeologists found it again in 1999.

**A bed of shell beads.** People at Cahokia buried a man and a woman in the 1000s on a bed of shell disc beads laid out in the shape of a bird. The beads were made from sea shells. The magazine *Archaeology* gives the count as 20,000 beads, and another published count gives about 10,000.

Melvin Fowler dug up this burial in a mound called Mound 72 between 1967 and 1971, and he described both bodies at the center as men. In 2016 a team led by Thomas Emerson of the Illinois State Archaeological Survey studied the bones again. They found that the two were a man and a woman, with other pairs of a man and a woman buried around them.

Mound 72 holds about 272 people, buried at different times over about a century. Archaeologists judge that almost 62 percent of them were sacrifices, people whom others at Cahokia killed on purpose as part of the burial ceremonies. No written record names them or the people who killed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors from plants and earth" -->
Red ochre is a mineral made of iron and oxygen, and it can be brown, red or yellow. Accounts written after Europeans arrived say that the Karankawa people of the Texas coast painted symbols on their faces with ochre, using their fingers or the stalks of plants. The Karankawa got their ochre by trade from inland. At a burial site called Mitchell Ridge, red ochre stains the bones of 18 people.

Native people of North America decorated clothing with porcupine quills, the stiff, hollow spines that cover a porcupine, before Europeans arrived. The Huron, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Cree and Gwich'in were among the nations that did quillwork. Men hunted the porcupines, and women did the work. They flattened the quills with an awl, a pointed tool, or with a piece of bone, then dyed them and sewed them onto moccasins, pouches, baby carriers and clothing.

**The dyes.** Red came from the bark of the tamarack tree, from spruce cones and from berries, and black from walnuts and wild grapes. For yellow, quillworkers used wild sunflower, pine bark or willow root. Blueberries and larkspur, a flower, made blue. Writers at the Penn Museum in Philadelphia list these dye plants. The art is much older than the quilled pieces in the Penn Museum. Among the oldest there are pieces that George Catlin collected between 1832 and 1839.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 5 minutes**

The first written descriptions of what Native people wore come from the Spanish, French and English explorers and soldiers who arrived in the 1500s. They wrote down deerskin, cloth of bark fiber and feathers, and ornaments of pearls. Some of them took what they found. Hernando de Soto's soldiers dug up Native graves at the town of Cofitachequi in 1540 to take the pearls buried there. A French group led by René de Laudonnière built Fort Caroline in Florida in 1564.

De Soto's men also found Spanish beads and a dagger at Cofitachequi, left from a Spanish colony started on the coast in 1526. Spanish colonists drove churro sheep into New Mexico in 1598, onto the land of the Pueblo peoples. Wool from those sheep went into Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for about 300 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth and pearls at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
Hernando de Soto's army reached Cofitachequi, a Native town beside a river in the Southeast, in May 1540. A Portuguese man who marched with the army wrote an account of the march, printed in Portugal in 1557. His name is not known, so he is called the Gentleman of Elvas, after the Portuguese town of Elvas. He described what the people of the town wore and what they kept.

A woman ruled the town. No record gives her own name, and the Spanish called her the Lady of Cofitachequi. She crossed the river by canoe to meet de Soto and gave him mantles, or cloaks, and deerskins. Then she took a long string of pearls from her own neck and put it around his. He gave her a gold ring set with a ruby.

Elvas wrote that the town's storehouses held mantles woven from yarn made of tree bark. Other mantles were made of feathers colored white, green, red and yellow, and people wore them in winter. The storehouses also held deerskins with many panels drawn on them, and some of the deerskins had been made into leggings, stockings and shoes. Elvas wrote that everyone in the town wore shoes and clothes.

**The graves.** De Soto was looking for treasure. According to Elvas, the ruler told him to search the town's graves for pearls, because she had seen how much the Spaniards valued them. De Soto's men dug up the graves. They took the pearls, and small figures of babies and birds made from pearls.

The accounts give different amounts. Richard Hakluyt's English translation of Elvas says the men found "fourteen rows of pearls." Other translations give 14 arrobas, about 350 pounds, since an arroba is a Spanish weight of about 25 pounds. An editor's note in a later printing gives 392 pounds, and other accounts give about 200 pounds.

In the same town, de Soto's men found a dagger and beads. They came from a colony that Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón had started in 1526 on the coast of today's South Carolina or Georgia. Ayllón died there, and the surviving colonists left within about two months.

De Soto then took the ruler with him as a prisoner, guarded by enslaved women. On the march she told her guards she needed to relieve herself. She walked into the woods and escaped, carrying off a cane box full of pearls that had not yet been drilled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What an Algonquian leader wore, 1585" -->
The English painter John White painted Native people who spoke Algonquian languages, a large family of related languages, in 1585. Thomas Hariot wrote the words that went with the pictures. One painting shows a leader of one of these peoples, dressed for a great hunt or a feast. He wears an apron of animal skin with a fringe, and a tail hangs down behind it. His quiver, the case that holds his arrows, is tied to the tail.

Feathers decorate his hair. One side of his head is shaved. Hariot wrote that the shaving kept the hair from tangling in the bowstring.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish clothes in St. Augustine, 1565 to 1600" -->
Spanish soldiers and settlers founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565, on land where the Timucua people lived. Archaeologists from the Florida Museum have dug up many of the things the colonists wore. Rich residents wore costly cloth trimmed with metal lace, buttons, beads and metal ornaments. Buttons were worn mostly by soldiers and the rich.

Most people tied their plain clothes shut with laces. Each lace had a small metal tip, called an aglet, so it could pass through a hole. Many soldiers in the town married Native women.

**A widow's bed.** Juan de Cevadilla was the official in Mexico who collected the pay for the soldiers of St. Augustine. He died suddenly in 1590 and left his widow, Doña Petronilla (Doña is a Spanish title of respect for a woman), a carved bed covered with a thin layer of gold. Over the bed hung a cloth roof, called a canopy, made of taffeta, a smooth silk cloth, from China, with designs stitched into it. He also left her a large amount of costly cloth and clothing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Churro sheep and red from Mexico" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north from Mexico onto the land of the Pueblo peoples in New Mexico in 1598. They came on the road called El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, the Royal Road of the Inland Country, and they brought churro sheep, a Spanish breed. By the 1700s the churros in New Mexico numbered in the hundreds of thousands. For the next 300 years their wool was the base of Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving there.

Before the sheep came, Pueblo weavers worked in cotton on upright looms. The Spanish brought a loom that lay flat on the floor. Weavers in New Mexico later used it to make Rio Grande blankets and serapes, blankets that can be worn as a cloak. After the Spanish brought churro wool and new dyes, wool became the main thread for weaving there, and the old Pueblo cotton weaving declined.

**Cochineal.** Far to the south, in Oaxaca, Puebla and Tlaxcala in Mexico, Native people had long bred a small insect called cochineal to make red dye. As early as the 100s BC they used it to dye cloth and feathers and to color books painted by hand. After Spanish soldiers conquered Mexico, traders carried cochineal around the world. Native Mexicans still did all the skilled work and all the labor of making it. Writers at Harvard's science museums note that they were never given credit for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 10 minutes**

Lawmakers in the English colonies of the 1600s wanted clothes to show who ranked above whom, and in Virginia and Massachusetts they wrote laws to keep it that way. In Massachusetts, a person whose property was worth less than 200 pounds was not allowed to wear silk hoods, gold or silver lace, or boots called "great boots." Judges in the county courts fined people who did. Lists of belongings made after New England colonists died record clothes in red, blue, violet, grey and green, as well as black.

In New Mexico, Spanish soldiers and colonists demanded a woven cotton blanket from each Pueblo household every year. This was tribute, a payment that conquerors force a conquered people to make.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia taxes clothing, 1619" -->
The General Assembly of Virginia, the colony's lawmaking body, met for the first time on July 30, 1619. At that meeting its members passed a law "against excesse in apparel," meaning too much spending on clothes. They voted that each man's share of all public costs, collected through the church, would be set by his clothes. An unmarried man paid according to his own clothes. A married man paid according to his own clothes and his wife's.

A Virginia resolution of 1621 states the aim to "supress excess in cloaths." Under it, men who held no office in the colony could not wear gold in their clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-ring-styles" name="Mary Ring" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Ring

> **Who:** A widow of Plymouth Colony whose list of belongings shows the colonists there dressed in red, violet and blue.
> **When and where:** Leiden in Holland, then Plymouth Colony, 1629 to 1631.

**Her red petticoat was worth 16 shillings.**

Mary Ring owned two petticoats, the skirts women wore, one red and one violet. She also owned two violet waistcoats, which were vests or short jackets, and three blue aprons. When she died, everything she owned was listed and given a value, in a list called a probate inventory. Hers gives values in pounds, shillings and pence, the English money of the time. Only one piece of her clothing was worth more than the red petticoat: a hooded cloak called a huke.

The inventory also lists a black kirtle, a kind of gown, and a waistcoat of "mingled coloured" cloth. It lists an apron in murrey, a dark purplish red, and pieces of blue cloth and red cloth. She owned blue stockings, white Irish stockings and a piece of grey kersey, a rough wool.

She also owned six ruffs, stiff pleated collars, and seven smocks, long linen shirts worn under the other clothes. She had three linen caps, two hats and two pairs of shoes. Her will left green cloth "to make a coat." Caleb Johnson wrote notes on her list for the Plymouth Colony Archive Project. He points out that it shows the English colonists of Plymouth wore violet, red, blue and grey as well as black and white.

**Who she was.** Mary Ring was the widow of William Ring, who died in Leiden, in Holland, in the 1620s. She came to Plymouth in 1629 or 1630 with her children Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. The English had built Plymouth in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a town of the Wampanoag people. About 2,000 Wampanoag had lived in the area. Before 1620 a wave of sickness, now called the Great Dying, killed most of the people of Patuxet, and the few who lived left to join other Wampanoag towns.

She died on July 15 or 19, 1631. She signed her own will, and she owned books. Governor William Bradford and Edward Winslow owed her money when she died.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-ring-styles" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Did the Puritans dress only in black?" -->
The Puritan colonists of Massachusetts wore many colors, which they called "sad" colors, meaning serious rather than unhappy. A list from 1638 names some of them: liver color, tawny, russet, purple, French green, orange and "deer colour." The Puritans were English Protestants who wanted their leaders to strip the Church of England of the customs it still shared with the Catholic Church.

The historian David Hackett Fischer writes that black cloth was kept for the ruling elders, the men who led the church, and for the families who governed the colony. A portrait of Governor John Winthrop, painted in about 1629, shows him in black velvet with slashed sleeves, cut so that the cloth underneath showed through.

Lists of belongings and bills from the colony record "pinck hose," or pink stockings, "green sleeves," and "a Scarlet petticoat with Silver Lace." Fischer writes that Winthrop's own niece, Mary Downing, was scolded for wearing a little lace. His sentence about her does not say who scolded her.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts dress laws, 1634 to 1682" -->
The General Court of Massachusetts Bay was the colony's body of lawmakers and magistrates, its chief officials and judges. Its members passed their first sumptuary law, a law that limits what people may wear according to their rank, in 1634. It banned "new fashions, or long hair," silver and gold hatbands and belts, cloth woven with gold thread, and lace.

It also limited the slashes in a sleeve. The magazine *Atlas Obscura* gives the limit as two slashes in a sleeve, and the historian David Hackett Fischer gives it as one. A person caught wearing these things had to give them up. A law of 1639 banned more kinds of lace and banned short sleeves.

**"Utter detestation."** The members of the General Court wrote in 1651 that they felt "utter detestation," or hatred, when "men or weomen of meane condition," meaning people of low rank, dressed like gentlemen. They named gold or silver lace, gold or silver buttons, laces tied at the knees and "great boots." For women of that rank they named silk hoods and silk scarves. Anyone whose property was worth less than 200 pounds owed a fine of 10 shillings each time he or she wore them.

Magistrates, officers, soldiers and their families did not have to obey this law. Neither did people who had once been rich and had lost their money. The lawmakers gave the selectmen, the elected men who ran each town, the power to tax people who dressed above their rank.

**In court.** Judges in the county courts enforced the law. Judges in Essex County heard 18 cases in 1653, and they let 12 of the people go because they were worth more than 200 pounds. Ten years later, in 1663, the lawmakers made the law cover children and servants too, and barred tailors from making the forbidden clothes. A first offense brought a warning, a second a fine of 20 shillings, and later offenses up to 40 shillings.

Seven married women of Northampton faced charges of wearing silk at the Hampshire County court in 1673. The judges found all but one of them not guilty. At the same court, 9 married women of Springfield faced the same charge, along with 9 of Hadley, 7 of Hatfield and 3 of Westfield.

The five towns of Northampton, Hadley, Hatfield, Westfield and Springfield were themselves charged at the county court between about 1680 and 1682. Their selectmen had failed to tax residents who wore silk "beyond their ranks." James Russell Trumbull, who wrote the history of Northampton, reports that nothing more was heard of these charges.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hannah-lyman-1676" name="Hannah Lyman" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hannah Lyman

> **Who:** A Northampton girl of about 16 who was fined for wearing silk, and who wore it even while she stood charged before the court.
> **When and where:** Northampton, Massachusetts, 1676.

**Charged with wearing silk, she wore silk to court.**

The judges of the Hampshire County court in Massachusetts fined Hannah Lyman of Northampton 10 shillings for wearing silk in 1676. She was about 16 years old, and her father, Richard Lyman, had died. The charge said she wore silk "in a flaunting manner, in an offensive way and garbe, not only before, but when she stood presented" (flaunting means showing off, garbe meant clothing, and to stand presented was to stand charged before the court). She wore silk even then.

**Whose land.** Northampton stood on the land of the Nonotuck people. John Pynchon got a deed, a paper that hands over land, from seven Nonotuck men, including Chickwalloppe, on September 24, 1653. He paid 100 fathoms of wampum, strings of shell beads, and 10 wool coats. A fathom is 6 feet. The historian Margaret Bruchac writes that the Nonotuck signers probably understood the deed as an agreement to share the land, and that they probably expected to keep their own right to hunt, fish and plant there.

**The war.** King Philip's War was fought in 1675 and 1676. Metacom, a leader of the Wampanoag whom the English called King Philip, led several Native nations against the New England colonies. About 9,000 people died in the war, and by some counts about half of all the Native people of New England died. A Native man fighting for the colonists shot Metacom dead on August 12, 1676. The colonists sold hundreds of Native captives into slavery.

During the war the Northampton minister Solomon Stoddard asked the colony's leaders to act against "that intolerable pride in clothes and hair." In November 1675 the members of the General Court passed a law listing the sins they said were common in the colony.

**The court.** Twenty-three people stood charged at the Hampshire County court in Northampton on March 27, 1676, "for wearing silk in a flaunting manner and for long hair and other extravagances" (extravagances meant spending too much on show). Six were married women. Six were unmarried, and the town's historian thought they were probably young girls. The rest were young men.

The judges warned them and made each one pay court fees of 2 shillings 6 pence. The wife of Samuel Holton had been charged before, so the judges fined her 10 shillings. Hannah Lyman was charged at a later session of the same court. That March the county judges also fined 45 people from Springfield, Westfield, Hadley and Hatfield for silk and long hair: 11 married women, 15 unmarried women and 19 young men.

Counts of the Northampton cases differ. The town's historian, James Russell Trumbull, gives the numbers above. The magazine *American Heritage* and the historian David Hackett Fischer write that Hannah Lyman was one of 36 young women of Northampton charged that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hannah-lyman-1676" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blankets taken as tribute in New Mexico" -->
Some Spanish colonists in New Mexico, called encomenderos, held the right to collect tribute from Pueblo households after 1598. Researchers for the Beyond Origins of New Mexico Families project report that by the 1660s each household owed one fanega of corn in the spring, a fanega being a Spanish measure of grain. In the fall each household owed one woven cotton blanket, called a manta, or one hide. The Spanish king allowed no more than 35 encomenderos at a time.

Ginés de Herrera Horta was a Spanish legal officer in Oñate's colony. In 1601 he testified that soldiers demanded a blanket each year from each household. When people said they had no blankets to give, the soldiers took the blankets off the women who were wearing them and left the women naked. The Spanish records of these seizures give no names for the women whose blankets were taken.

Most of the Pueblo peoples rose up against the Spanish colonists in 1680, in the Pueblo Revolt, led by Po'pay of San Juan Pueblo. They killed about 400 Spaniards and forced about 2,000 settlers to flee New Mexico for about 12 years. Among the Pueblo people's complaints was the tribute of labor, ground corn and cloth that the colonists had forced them to hand over.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 7 minutes**

In the English colonies between 1700 and 1750, a wig showed that a man ranked near the top, and coarse cloth showed that a person was enslaved. The historian Thomas Ford calls the wig a badge of rank. A traveler in Maryland in the 1740s wrote that only a few men of the highest rank wore wigs, and that other men wore caps.

South Carolina's lawmakers wrote the coarse cloth into law in 1740. They named cheap fabrics and barred enslaved people from wearing anything finer. They ordered constables, local officers who kept order, and anyone else who found finer clothes on an enslaved person to take them away and keep them. In the 1740s South Carolina planters began growing indigo, a plant that gives a blue dye, in large amounts and shipping the dye to England. Enslaved people did the work of turning the leaves into dye.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who wore a wig?" -->
Only a few men of "the very elevated Sort" wore wigs, a traveler in Maryland wrote in the 1740s. Ordinary men wore caps of wool or yarn. Better-off men wore caps of white linen or cotton, even on journeys of 50 miles.

A wig of this time was called a peruke. A wigmaker built it from rows of hair sewn onto a cap of net and ribbon, using human hair and also hair from horses, cows and goats. The historian Thomas Ford writes that a wigmaker preferred to measure a customer whose head was shaved, so the wig would fit. He calls the wig "an important badge of social rank."

Some rich men, including Councillor Robert Carter, a member of the governor's council, and Dr. John Sequeira, had enslaved men trained to shave them and to dress their hair. Ford writes that rich planters may sometimes have dressed their enslaved coachmen and house servants in white wigs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1740 clothing law in South Carolina" -->
Enslaved people in South Carolina rose up against their enslavers in the Stono Rebellion in September 1739. On May 10, 1740, the lawmakers of the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. Its full name was An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing Negroes and Other Slaves in this Province, and it used "Negroes," the lawmakers' word for Black people. Section 40 of the act was about clothes.

**What Section 40 said.** It began by claiming that "many of the slaves in this Province wear clothes much above the condition of slaves." The lawmakers said enslaved people got those clothes by "sinister and evil methods," meaning wrong and wicked ones. The section barred any owner from letting an enslaved person "have or wear any sort of apparel whatsoever, finer, other, or greater value" than the cloths it named. Servants in livery, a uniform the master chose, did not fall under this rule.

The list read: "Negro cloth, duffels, kerseys, osnabrigs, blue linen, check linen or coarse garlix, or calicoes, checked cottons, or Scotch plaids." Many of those cloths were coarse, which means rough and thick. Calicoes and checked cottons were cotton cloths. Garlix was a linen, and the law calls it "coarse."

Negro cloth was a coarse cloth made for enslaved people's clothes. Osnaburg was a coarse, plain cloth first woven from flax in the German city of Osnabrück. Kersey was a thick, coarse wool, and duffel was a heavy wool used for blankets and coats. An earlier South Carolina act, passed in 1735, had already named cheap cloth for enslaved people's clothing.

**Who took the clothes.** The law required "all and every constable and other persons" to seize finer clothes from any enslaved person wearing them. The person who seized the clothes could keep them "to his or their own use." An owner who thought the clothes had been taken wrongly could take the matter to a justice of the peace, a local judge. The surviving records do not show how often anyone seized clothes under this law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-gambia-1745" name="Jack" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack

> **Who:** A man taken from Gambia and enslaved in Virginia, who ran away within five weeks of his sale.
> **When and where:** Hanover County, Virginia, 1745.

**Within five weeks of his sale, Jack ran away.**

Slave traders brought Jack and another man from Gambia, in West Africa, on the ship *Ranger*. They sold the two men at Newcastle, Virginia, on September 5, 1745. Neither man spoke English, and both were nearly 6 feet tall.

Almost everything known about Jack comes from an advertisement that his owner, Margaret Arbuthnott of Hanover County, placed in the *Virginia Gazette* in October 1745 to get him back. The advertisement does not name the traders. Arbuthnott described Jack as having a scar over his right eyebrow. The other man, whose name she did not give, had what she described as "3 small Strokes on each Side of his Face."

The two men ran away in new jackets and in breeches, short pants that ended at the knee. Both were made of napped cotton, brushed so that its surface was soft and fuzzy, with no buttons and no buttonholes. The men also wore shirts of osnaburg and felt hats.

Arbuthnott accused the two men of taking a damask tablecloth, its pattern woven into the cloth, along with fine Scottish linen, checked cloth, a white linen shirt and a silk handkerchief. She offered a reward to anyone who caught them and brought them back.

The record does not say whether Jack was caught.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo blue, 1739 to 1750" -->
Indigo seeds came to South Carolina with the first English settlers in 1670. In the late 1730s and early 1740s, hundreds of South Carolina planters tried out new crops, and indigo was one of them.

**Eliza Lucas.** Eliza Lucas began trying to grow indigo in 1739 at Wappoo Plantation, on Wappoo Creek west of the Ashley River, near Charleston. She was 17 and managed the plantation and the 20 people enslaved on it. She worked first with an indigo maker from the island of Montserrat. She had the most success with an indigo maker of African descent whom her father hired from the French West Indies. In 1744 she used her crop to make seed and shared it with other planters.

The historian Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library writes that her part has been greatly exaggerated in recent times. Many of her neighbors were following the same steps. Her husband, Charles Pinckney, whom she married in 1744, published her methods in articles in 1744 and 1745 under the name "Agricola."

**The bounty.** South Carolina's lawmakers voted in 1744 to pay planters a bounty, a reward from the government, of 1 shilling for each pound of indigo. They canceled it in 1746. Members of the British Parliament, the lawmakers in London, voted a bounty of 6 pence a pound in 1748, starting in 1749.

Planters began shipping large amounts of indigo to England in 1747. Exports rose from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 and 1746 to about 130,000 pounds in 1748, about 26 times as much.

**Who did the work.** Enslaved people of African descent did almost all of the work. They planted and tended the indigo. They built the vats, big tubs for making dye, and filled them with water and cut leaves. After the leaves soaked, the workers beat the liquid with paddles or with buckets that had no bottoms. They added lime, made from burnt oyster shells, so that the blue settled out. Then workers scooped the blue mud into bags, dried it and cut it into cubes.

Charles Woodmason wrote in 1755 that 15 enslaved workers could plant and tend 50 acres of indigo, and that it took 25 skilled workers to turn that crop into dye. The accounts of indigo making from these years do not name the enslaved workers who made the dye.

One enslaved man who built the vats is named. He was Quash, a carpenter whom the Lucas family held in slavery at Wappoo. In 1744 George Lucas, Eliza's father, wrote that "Quashe" should build a wooden indigo works at the family's Garden Hill plantation. Eliza told her father that Quash had spent 33 days building indigo vats at other Lucas plantations. The minister Alexander Garden baptized him on April 16, 1746. The historian Tiffany Momon, who wrote about his life, calls him John "Quash" Williams.

South Carolina's indigo was shipped to England for British cloth makers. South Carolinians bought most of their own cloth from England, already dyed.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 8 minutes**

Colonists who opposed British taxes turned their clothes into a protest. From the Stamp Act tax of 1765 until the Revolution, they agreed again and again to boycott, or refuse to buy, British goods, including cloth. Delegates at the First Continental Congress, a meeting of leaders from the colonies, voted in 1774 to ban British goods in every colony from December 1.

During the boycott of 1768 and 1769, a colonist who wore homespun, cloth spun and woven in America, often at home, showed that he or she backed the boycott. Women held public spinning meetings. Harvard's graduating class of 1768 voted not to wear imported British clothes at their graduation.

George Washington took the oath as the first president in 1789 in a suit of brown wool woven in Connecticut. Furniture makers in the new country copied a style of sharp edges, legs that grew thinner toward the floor, and inlaid eagles, now called the Federal style. In Spanish New Orleans in 1786, the governor ordered Black women and women of mixed race to stop wearing feathers and jewels in their hair and to wear it plain or covered with a cloth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homespun as a protest, 1765 to 1776" -->
During the boycotts of British goods in the 1760s, Boston merchants opened spinning schools again. In Hartford, members of a society offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of wool cloth in a year. Newspaper printers ran reports of spinning meetings in Massachusetts, Connecticut and Rhode Island. In Newport one spinner was a woman of 70 who had never spun a thread before. Only six of the newspaper stories called the spinners "Daughters of Liberty."

**A day at the wheel.** Women met at their minister's house in Brookfield, Massachusetts, at 5 in the morning one day in August 1769. A report of the meeting said the best young spinner at the linen wheel, a spinning wheel for linen thread, spun 70 knots of yarn, a knot being a measured length.

One married woman did her morning housework and made cheese first. Then she rode more than 2 miles carrying her own spinning wheel, spun 53 knots between 9 and 7, and went home to milk the cows.

The seniors at Harvard College voted in the autumn of 1767 not to drink tea or wear imported British clothes at their commencement, the ceremony at which students graduate. They did it to protest the Townshend taxes on goods from Britain. The printed sheets for the 1768 commencement, which listed the statements the graduates would argue for, were on paper made in New England. The next year's seniors did the same.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charity Clarke

> **Who:** A young New Yorker who wrote to her cousin in London that American women would spin, weave and dress in their own cloth rather than buy British clothes.
> **When and where:** Her father's estate called Chelsea, on Manhattan, 1768 to 1774.

**She promised "a fighting army of Amazons" armed with spinning wheels.**

Charity Clarke was 21 or 22 when she wrote to her cousin in London in 1769. If the English did not give Americans the liberty they asked for, she told him, she would lead "a fighting army of Amazons," the women warriors of old Greek stories. She would gather "a number of ladies armed with spinning wheels." The historian Alan Singer dates the letter June 1769, and other accounts date it December 8, 1769.

Her ladies would have helpers "who shall all learn to weave and keep sheep," and they would all be "clothed with the work of our hands." They would be "no more slave to fashion and ceremony."

Her cousin was Joseph Jekyll, a lawyer in London, and she wrote to him between 1768 and 1774. Columbia University holds the letters today. She wrote to him again in 1774: "Though this body is not clad with silken garments, these limbs are armed with strength."

She was born in New York on June 28, 1747, and grew up on her father's estate, called Chelsea, on the island of Manhattan. She married the Rev. Benjamin Moore of Trinity Church in 1778. Their son, Clement Clarke Moore, wrote the poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charity-clarke" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs in Williamsburg, 1769 to 1775" -->
Edward Charlton came to Williamsburg, Virginia, from London and became the town's leading barber and wigmaker. In 1769 eight barbers and wigmakers worked in the town. Charlton's account book for 1769 to about 1775 shows that in one year he sold sixty brown wigs of a style called the "dress bob." They cost 43 shillings each.

For shaving a man and dressing his wig for a year, Charlton usually charged 2 pounds 3 shillings. Peter Pelham paid 1 pound 5 shillings, and Speaker Randolph, the leader of the colony's lawmakers, paid 4 pounds.

Wigmakers needed hair. George Long of Petersburg, another Virginia town, advertised that he would pay one shilling an ounce for human hair.

Wigs were going out of fashion in England, and Charlton retired shortly before the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo's peak and end" -->
South Carolina planters shipped more than one million pounds of indigo to England in 1775, more than seven times the 130,000 pounds of 1748. Enslaved workers did almost all of the work of making it. The British bounty on indigo ran out in 1777, during the war with Britain. Some planters grew indigo again after the war ended in 1783. In the 1790s most of them gave up indigo, and many turned to cotton.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did a governor ban feathers in women's hair?" -->
Governor Esteban Rodríguez Miró of Spanish New Orleans ordered Black women and women of mixed race to stop wearing feathers and jewels in their hair in 1786. The historian Virginia Meacham Gould writes that he aimed the rule at women who dressed "too elegantly" or "competed too freely with white women for status."

The rule was part of a set of rules Miró issued on June 2, 1786, called the *bando de buen gobierno*, the order of good government. It said the women could "no longer have feathers nor jewelry in their hair." They had to wear their hair plain or cover it with a *pañuelo*, a head cloth. People later called such a cloth a tignon.

Gould found little written evidence that officials enforced the rule, and no record that anyone was punished under it. Portraits painted in New Orleans in the early 1800s show free Black women and women of mixed race wearing head wraps tied in many different styles. Gould reads the head wraps in these portraits as a mark of distinction.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### George Washington

> **Who:** The first president, who took the oath of office in a suit made from American wool.
> **When and where:** Mount Vernon, Virginia, and New York City, January to April 1789.

**He took the oath in brown wool woven in Connecticut.**

George Washington took the oath as the first president of the United States on April 30, 1789, in New York. His suit was brown wool cloth made in Connecticut. Three weeks before, on April 8, he wrote: "I shall allways take a peculiar pleasure in giving every proper encouragement in my power to the manufactures of my Country" (manufactures meant goods made by hand or by machine).

**Finding the cloth.** In January 1789 a writer in *The Federal Gazette* had urged American leaders to wear clothing made in America. He signed himself "a Philadelphia mechanic," meaning a skilled worker who made things. The Hartford Woolen Manufactory in Connecticut had just begun making cloth. A merchant in Philadelphia could not get the cloth for Washington, so Henry Knox, then the secretary of war, dealt with the Hartford factory himself. The cloth reached Alexandria, Virginia, in early April. Washington left Mount Vernon for New York on April 16.

The staff at Mount Vernon believe that Cavan Boa made the coat, waistcoat and breeches. Boa was Washington's indentured tailor, a servant bound to work for him for a set number of years. Kathrin Breitt Brown makes copies of old clothes for Mount Vernon. She calls the coat "well-made but hastily finished," and she thinks Boa probably had help. Washington asked for six large engraved buttons.

In England the color of the cloth was called London brown. Americans called it Congress brown. The coat came to the collection at Mount Vernon in 1933.

Washington did not wear a wig. He powdered his own hair.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Federal style, 1780 to 1820" -->
American cabinetmakers, the skilled makers of fine furniture, made pieces in a style now called Federal from about 1780 to 1820. Federal pieces had legs that grew thinner toward the floor, and sharp edges. Makers covered them with veneers, thin sheets of fine wood laid over the surface. They inlaid pictures of eagles, urns and bellflowers, setting small pieces of different wood into the surface.

The main wood was mahogany, and makers also used satinwood, maple and birch. American makers copied designs from English pattern books by George Hepplewhite and Thomas Sheraton. Makers included John and Thomas Seymour in Boston and Duncan Phyfe in New York.
