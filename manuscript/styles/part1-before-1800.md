## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The people of North America before 1500 made their clothes and ornaments from plants, animals and minerals found near home. They also used goods they got by trade. They wore deer hide, rabbit fur and turkey feathers, and cloth woven from yucca fiber and cotton. They made ornaments from sea shells that came from the Gulf of Mexico and from copper that came from the country around Lake Superior. Their colors came from plants and from a mineral called red ochre.

No written record names any of the people who wove this cloth or wore these beads. What is known comes from the objects themselves. Archaeologists, scientists who dig up and study the things people left behind, have found the cloth, the feathers, the beads and the copper.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Feathers, fiber and cotton in the Southwest" -->
The Four Corners is the place in the Southwest where Utah, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico meet. There the Ancestral Puebloan people, the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples, made most of their clothing from deer hide, rabbit skin and fiber from the yucca, a desert plant. They also made blankets of turkey feathers. A feather blanket made in the 1200s is kept at the Edge of the Cedars State Park Museum in southeastern Utah. Its maker wrapped about 11,550 turkey feathers around more than 180 meters (590 feet) of cord twisted from yucca fiber.

The feathers came from 4 to 10 turkeys. Bill Lipe and Shannon Tushingham, researchers at Washington State University, studied the blanket. In 2020 they reported that the makers probably pulled most of the feathers from live birds. In this region, feather blankets had replaced blankets made from strips of rabbit fur many centuries before this one was made.

Cotton came north by trade from Mexico. The oldest farmed cotton found in the Tonto Basin of Arizona dates to about the year 250. Cotton reached the Four Corners around 600. By about 900, Ancestral Puebloan men were weaving large cloths and blankets on upright looms. A loom is a frame that holds threads tight so a weaver can pass other threads over and under them. The men grew the cotton, spun it into thread and wove it.

The Salado people lived in the Tonto Basin from about 1250 to 1450. By about 1300 their basin was one of the leading cotton-growing areas of the Southwest. They wove plain cotton cloth and openwork cloth, with small openings left in the weave as a pattern. They also made blankets from strips of worn-out cotton cloth. One brightly dyed strap they wove is a tumpline, a band worn across the forehead to carry a load on the back. They wove their sandals from the fibers of two desert plants, agave and yucca.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shell and copper along the Mississippi" -->
Cahokia was a city of earth mounds in Illinois, a few miles east of the Mississippi River and today's St. Louis. Its people traded for copper from the Lake Superior region and for sea shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Archaeologists call the way of life of Cahokia and the towns like it the Mississippian culture.

Mississippian metalworkers heated copper, hammered it while it was soft, and did the same again and again. This way of working metal is called annealing. They made hair ornaments, pieces for headdresses and flat plates. Some plates show a figure that is part bird and part man, pushed out by hammering from the back of the sheet. Pictures of this bird-man began at Cahokia between 1100 and 1300. Mississippian copper plates were made from about 800 to 1600. They have been found from Florida and Georgia to Oklahoma and Wisconsin.

At Cahokia, archaeologists found a copper workshop just north of a mound called Mound 34. It was a building about 5.5 by 4.4 meters, or about 18 by 14 feet. Inside were raw copper nuggets, scraps of worked copper and stone tools for working it. Gregory Perino first found signs of the workshop in 1956. Later its exact spot was lost, and archaeologists found it again in 1999.

In the 1000s, people at Cahokia buried a man and a woman on a bed of shell disc beads. They laid the beads out in the shape of a bird. The beads were made from sea shells. The magazine *Archaeology* gives the count as 20,000 beads, and another published count gives about 10,000. Melvin Fowler dug up this burial in a mound called Mound 72 between 1967 and 1971, and he described both central bodies as men. In 2016 a team led by Thomas Emerson of the Illinois State Archaeological Survey studied the bones again. They found that the two were a man and a woman, with other pairs of a man and a woman buried around them.

Mound 72 holds about 272 people, buried at different times over about a century. Archaeologists judge that almost 62 percent of them were sacrifices, people whom others at Cahokia killed on purpose as part of the burial ceremonies. No written record names them or the people who killed them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors from plants and earth" -->
Red ochre is iron oxide, a mineral made of iron and oxygen. It can be brown, red or yellow. Accounts written after Europeans arrived say that the Karankawa people of the Texas coast painted symbols on their faces with ochre, using their fingers or the stalks of plants. The Karankawa got their ochre by trade from inland. At a burial site called Mitchell Ridge, red ochre stains the bones of 18 people.

Porcupine quillwork is an art that Native people of North America practiced before Europeans arrived. A quill is one of the stiff, hollow spines that cover a porcupine. The Huron, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Cree and Gwich'in were among the nations that did quillwork. Men hunted the porcupines, and women did the work. They flattened the quills with an awl, a pointed tool, or with a piece of bone, then dyed them and sewed them onto moccasins, pouches, baby carriers and clothing.

Red came from the bark of the tamarack tree, from spruce cones and from berries, and black from walnuts and wild grapes. For yellow, quillworkers used wild sunflower, pine bark or willow root. Blueberries and larkspur, a flower, made blue. Writers at the Penn Museum in Philadelphia list these dye plants. The quilled pieces in museums are much younger than the art. Among the oldest in the Penn Museum's collection are pieces that George Catlin collected between 1832 and 1839.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Spanish, French and English explorers and soldiers came to this land in the 1500s. A French group led by René de Laudonnière built Fort Caroline in Florida in 1564. Spanish and English newcomers wrote down what the Native people they met were wearing: deerskin, cloth of bark fiber and feathers, with ornaments of pearls. In 1540 the soldiers of Hernando de Soto dug up Native graves at the town of Cofitachequi to take the pearls buried there.

The Spanish also brought new materials. Beads and a dagger from a Spanish colony had reached Cofitachequi before de Soto did. In 1598 Spanish colonists drove churro sheep into New Mexico, onto the land of the Pueblo peoples. Wool from those sheep went into Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving for about 300 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth and pearls at Cofitachequi, 1540" -->
In May 1540 Hernando de Soto's army reached Cofitachequi, a Native town beside a river in the Southeast. A Portuguese man who marched with the army wrote an account of the march, printed in Portugal in 1557. His name is not known. He is called the Gentleman of Elvas, after the Portuguese town of Elvas. He described what the people of the town wore and what they kept.

A woman ruled the town. No record gives her own name, and the Spanish called her the Lady of Cofitachequi. She crossed the river by canoe to meet de Soto and gave him mantles, which are cloaks, and deerskins. Then she took a long string of pearls from her own neck and put it around his. He gave her a gold ring set with a ruby.

Elvas wrote that the town's storehouses held mantles woven from yarn made of tree bark. Other mantles were made of feathers colored white, green, red and yellow, and people wore them in winter. The storehouses also held deerskins with many panels drawn on them, and some of the deerskins had been made into leggings, stockings and shoes. Elvas wrote that everyone in the town wore shoes and clothes.

De Soto was looking for treasure. According to Elvas, the ruler told him to search the town's graves for pearls. She had seen how much the Spaniards valued them. De Soto's men dug up the graves. They took the pearls, and small figures of babies and birds made from pearls. The writers who tell this story give different amounts. Richard Hakluyt's English translation of Elvas says the men found "fourteen rows of pearls," and an editor's note in a later printing gives 392 pounds. Other translations give 14 arrobas. An arroba is a Spanish weight of about 25 pounds, so 14 arrobas is about 350 pounds. Other accounts give about 200 pounds.

In the same town, de Soto's men found a dagger and beads from the colony that Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón had started on the coast of today's South Carolina or Georgia in 1526. Ayllón died there, and the surviving colonists left within about two months.

De Soto then took the ruler with him as a prisoner, guarded by enslaved women. On the march she told her guards she needed to relieve herself. She walked into the woods and escaped. She carried off a cane box full of pearls that had not yet been drilled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What a Secotan leader wore, 1585" -->
In 1585 the English painter John White painted Native people who spoke Algonquian languages, a large family of related languages. Thomas Hariot wrote the words that went with the pictures. One painting shows a leader of the Secotan, one of these peoples. He wears an apron of animal skin with a fringe, and a tail hangs down behind it. His quiver, the case that holds his arrows, is tied to the tail.

Feathers decorate his hair. One side of his head is shaved. Hariot wrote that the shaving kept the hair from tangling in the bowstring.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Spanish clothes in St. Augustine, 1565 to 1600" -->
Spanish soldiers and settlers founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565, on land where the Timucua people lived. Archaeologists from the Florida Museum have dug up many of the things the colonists wore. Rich residents wore costly cloth trimmed with metal lace, buttons, beads and metal ornaments. Buttons were worn mostly by soldiers and the rich.

Most people tied their plain clothes shut with laces. Each lace was tipped with a small metal end, called an aglet, so it could pass through a hole. Many soldiers in the town married Native women.

Juan de Cevadilla was the official in Mexico who collected the pay for the soldiers of St. Augustine. He died suddenly in 1590. His widow was Doña Petronilla. Doña is a Spanish title of respect for a woman. He left her a carved bed covered with a thin layer of gold, with a canopy of embroidered taffeta from China. Taffeta is a smooth silk cloth. He also left her a large amount of costly cloth and clothing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Churro sheep and red from Mexico" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north from Mexico onto the land of the Pueblo peoples in New Mexico in 1598. They came on the road called El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, the Royal Road of the Inland Country. They brought churro sheep, a Spanish breed. By the 1700s the churros in New Mexico numbered in the hundreds of thousands. For the next 300 years their wool was the base of Native, Spanish and Mexican weaving there.

Before the sheep came, Pueblo weavers worked in cotton on upright looms. The Spanish brought a loom that lay flat on the floor. Weavers in New Mexico later used it to make Rio Grande blankets and serapes, a kind of blanket that can be worn as a cloak. After the Spanish brought churro wool and new dyes, Pueblo weavers wove less and less cotton.

Far to the south, in Oaxaca, Puebla and Tlaxcala in Mexico, Native people had long bred a small insect called cochineal to make red dye. As early as the 100s BC they used it to dye cloth and feathers and to color books painted by hand. After Spanish soldiers conquered Mexico, traders carried cochineal around the world. Native Mexicans still did all the skilled work and all the labor of making it. Writers at Harvard's science museums note that they were never given credit for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the English colonies of the 1600s, lawmakers in Virginia and Massachusetts wrote laws about who could wear what. In Massachusetts, a person whose property was worth less than 200 pounds was not allowed to wear silk hoods, gold or silver lace, or big boots called "great boots." Judges in the county courts fined people who did.

Lists of belongings made after New England colonists died record clothes in red, blue, violet, grey and green, as well as black.

In New Mexico, Spanish soldiers and colonists demanded a woven cotton blanket from each Pueblo household every year. This was tribute, a payment that conquerors force a conquered people to make.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Virginia taxes clothing, 1619" -->
The General Assembly of Virginia, the colony's lawmaking body, met for the first time on July 30, 1619. At that meeting its members passed a law "against excesse in apparell," meaning too much spending on clothes. They voted that each man's share of all public costs, collected through the church, would be set by his clothes. An unmarried man paid according to his own clothes. A married man paid according to his own clothes and his wife's.

A Virginia resolution of 1621 states the aim to "supress excess in cloaths." Under it, men who held no office in the colony could not wear gold in their clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="mary-ring-styles" name="Mary Ring" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Mary Ring

> **Who:** A widow of Plymouth Colony. The list of her belongings made after her death records each piece of her clothing and its color.
> **When and where:** Leiden in Holland, then Plymouth Colony, 1629 to 1631.

Mary Ring owned a red petticoat, a violet petticoat, two violet waistcoats and three blue aprons. A petticoat was a skirt, and a waistcoat was a vest or short jacket. A list of her belongings, made after she died, records these clothes and many more. Caleb Johnson wrote notes on her list for the Plymouth Colony Archive Project. He points out that the list shows the English colonists of Plymouth did not dress only in black and white.

Mary Ring was the widow of William Ring, who died in Leiden, in Holland, in the 1620s. She came to Plymouth in 1629 or 1630 with her children Elizabeth, Susanna and Andrew. The English had built Plymouth in 1620 on the site of Patuxet, a town of the Wampanoag people. About 2,000 Wampanoag had lived in the area. Before 1620 a wave of sickness, now called the Great Dying, killed most of the people of Patuxet. The few who lived left to join other Wampanoag towns.

Mary Ring died on July 15 or 19, 1631. When a colonist died, everything the person owned was listed and given a value. This list is called a probate inventory. Mary Ring's inventory gives values in pounds, shillings and pence, the English money of the time. Her red petticoat was worth 16 shillings, the highest value of any piece of her clothing except a hooded cloak called a huke.

The inventory also lists a black kirtle, which is a kind of gown, and a waistcoat of "mingled coloured" cloth. It lists an apron in murrey, a dark purplish red, and pieces of blue cloth and red cloth. She owned blue stockings, white Irish stockings and a piece of grey kersey, which is a rough wool. She also owned six ruffs, which were stiff, pleated collars, and seven smocks, which were long linen shirts worn under the other clothes. She had three linen caps, two hats and two pairs of shoes. Her will left green cloth "to make a coat."

She signed her own will, and she owned books. Governor William Bradford and Edward Winslow owed her money when she died.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="mary-ring-styles" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Colors in a Puritan closet" -->
The Puritan colonists of Massachusetts wore colors they called "sad" colors. A list from 1638 names some of them: liver color, tawny, russet, purple, French green, orange and "deer colour." Black cloth was kept for the ruling elders of the church and the families who governed the colony. A portrait of Governor John Winthrop, painted in about 1629, shows him in black velvet with slashed sleeves.

Lists of belongings and bills from the colony record "pinck hose," "green sleeves," and "a Scarlet petticoat with Silver Lace." Hose were stockings. The historian David Hackett Fischer writes that Winthrop's own niece, Mary Downing, was scolded for wearing a little lace. He does not name the person who scolded her.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Massachusetts dress laws, 1634 to 1682" -->
A law that limits what people may wear, or spend on clothes, according to their rank is called a sumptuary law. The General Court of Massachusetts Bay was the colony's body of lawmakers and magistrates, the colony's chief officials and judges. Its members passed their first sumptuary law in 1634. It banned "new fashions, or long hair," silver and gold hatbands and belts, cloth woven with gold thread, and lace. It also limited slashes, cuts in a sleeve that showed the cloth underneath. The magazine *Atlas Obscura* gives the limit as two slashes in a sleeve, and the historian David Hackett Fischer gives it as one. A person caught wearing these things had to give them up. A law of 1639 banned more kinds of lace and banned short sleeves.

In 1651 the members of the General Court wrote that they felt "utter detestation," which means hatred, when "men or weomen of meane condition" dressed like gentlemen. Mean condition meant low rank. The lawmakers named gold or silver lace, gold or silver buttons, laces tied at the knees and "great boots." For women of that rank they named silk hoods and silk scarves. Anyone whose property was worth less than 200 pounds owed a fine of 10 shillings each time he or she wore them.

Magistrates, officers, soldiers and their families did not have to obey this law. Neither did people who had once been rich and had lost their money. The lawmakers told the selectmen, the elected men who ran each town, to tax people who dressed above their rank.

Judges in the county courts enforced the law. In 1653 judges in Essex County heard 18 cases. They let 12 of the people go, because they were worth more than 200 pounds. Ten years later, in 1663, the lawmakers made the law cover children and servants too. They also barred tailors from making the forbidden clothes. A first offense brought a warning, a second a fine of 20 shillings, and later offenses up to 40 shillings.

Seven married women of Northampton faced charges of wearing silk at the Hampshire County court in 1673. The judges found all but one of them not guilty. At the same court, 9 married women of Springfield faced the same charge, along with 9 of Hadley, 7 of Hatfield and 3 of Westfield.

Between about 1680 and 1682, the five towns of Northampton, Hadley, Hatfield, Westfield and Springfield were charged at the county court. Their selectmen had failed to tax residents who wore silk "beyond their ranks." James Russell Trumbull, who wrote the history of Northampton, reports that nothing more was heard of these charges.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="hannah-lyman-1676" name="Hannah Lyman" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Hannah Lyman

> **Who:** A Northampton girl of about 16 who was fined for wearing silk, and who wore it again to the court where she was charged.
> **When and where:** Northampton, Massachusetts, 1676.

In 1676 the judges of the Hampshire County court in Massachusetts fined Hannah Lyman of Northampton 10 shillings for wearing silk. She was about 16 years old. She was charged with wearing silk "in a flaunting manner, in an offensive way and garbe, not only before, but when she stood presented." Flaunting means showing off, and garbe meant clothing. The charge meant she had worn the silk again to the court where she was on trial. Her father, Richard Lyman, had died.

Northampton stood on the land of the Nonotuck people. On September 24, 1653, John Pynchon got a deed to that land from seven Nonotuck men, including Chickwalloppe. A deed is a paper that hands over land. He paid 100 fathoms of wampum, which are strings of shell beads, and 10 wool coats. A fathom is 6 feet. The historian Margaret Bruchac writes that the Nonotuck signers probably understood the deed as an agreement to share the land. She writes that they probably expected to keep their own right to hunt, fish and plant there.

King Philip's War was fought in 1675 and 1676. Metacom, a leader of the Wampanoag whom the English called King Philip, led several Native nations against the New England colonies. About 9,000 people died, and the colonists sold hundreds of Native captives into slavery. During the war the Northampton minister Solomon Stoddard asked the colony's leaders to act against "that intolerable pride in clothes and hair." In November 1675 the members of the General Court passed a law listing the sins of the colony and their punishments.

On March 27, 1676, at the Hampshire County court in Northampton, 23 people were charged "for wearing silk in a flaunting manner and for long hair and other extravagances." Extravagances meant spending too much on show. Six were married women. Six were unmarried, and the town's historian thought they were probably young girls. The rest were young men. The judges warned them and made each one pay court fees of 2 shillings 6 pence. The wife of Samuel Holton had been charged before, so the judges fined her 10 shillings. Hannah Lyman was charged at a later session of the same court.

That March the county judges also fined 45 people from Springfield, Westfield, Hadley and Hatfield for silk and long hair. They were 11 married women, 15 unmarried women and 19 young men. Counts of the Northampton cases differ. The town's historian, James Russell Trumbull, gives the numbers above. The magazine *American Heritage* and the historian David Hackett Fischer write that Hannah Lyman was one of 36 young women of Northampton charged that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="hannah-lyman-1676" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Blankets taken as tribute in New Mexico" -->
After 1598 some Spanish colonists in New Mexico, called encomenderos, held the right to collect tribute from Pueblo households. A study of these encomenderos by the Beyond Origins of New Mexico Families project reports that by the 1660s each household owed one fanega of corn in the spring. A fanega was a Spanish measure of grain. In the fall each household owed one woven cotton blanket, called a manta, or one hide. The Spanish king allowed no more than 35 encomenderos at a time.

Ginés de Herrera Horta was a Spanish legal officer in Oñate's colony. In 1601 he testified that soldiers demanded a blanket each year from each household. When people said they had no blankets to give, the soldiers took the blankets off the women who were wearing them and left the women naked. The Spanish records of these seizures give no names for the women whose blankets were taken.

In 1680 most of the Pueblo peoples rose up against the Spanish colonists in the Pueblo Revolt, led by Po'pay of San Juan Pueblo. They killed about 400 Spaniards and forced about 2,000 settlers to flee New Mexico for about 12 years. Among the Pueblo people's complaints was the tribute of labor, ground corn and cloth that the colonists had forced them to hand over.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
What a man wore on his head showed his rank in the English colonies between 1700 and 1750. The historian Thomas Ford calls the wig a badge of rank. A traveler in Maryland in the 1740s wrote that only a few men of the highest rank wore wigs, and that other men wore caps.

Enslavers dressed the people they enslaved in coarse, cheap cloth. In 1740 the lawmakers of South Carolina made that a law. They listed the only fabrics enslaved people were allowed to wear. They ordered constables, local officers who kept order, and anyone else who found finer clothes on an enslaved person to take them away and keep them. In the same years, South Carolina planters began growing indigo, a plant that gives a blue dye, to sell in England. Enslaved people did the work of turning the leaves into dye.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs and caps" -->
A wig of this time was called a peruke. A wigmaker built it from rows of hair sewn onto a cap of net and ribbon. Wigmakers used human hair and also hair from horses, cows and goats. The historian Thomas Ford writes that a wigmaker preferred to measure a customer whose head was shaved, so the wig would fit. He calls the wig "an important badge of social rank."

A traveler in Maryland in the 1740s wrote that only a few men of "the very elevated Sort" wore wigs. Ordinary men wore caps of wool or yarn. Better-off men wore caps of white linen or cotton, even on journeys of 50 miles.

Some rich men, including Councillor Robert Carter, a member of the governor's council, and Dr. John Sequeira, had enslaved men trained to shave them and to dress their hair. Ford writes that rich planters may sometimes have dressed their enslaved coachmen and house servants in white wigs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1740 clothing law in South Carolina" -->
In September 1739, enslaved people in South Carolina rose up against their enslavers in the Stono Rebellion. On May 10, 1740, the lawmakers of the South Carolina assembly passed the Negro Act. Its full name was An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing Negroes and Other Slaves in this Province. Section 40 of the act was about clothes.

Section 40 began: "whereas, many of the slaves in this Province wear clothes much above the condition of slaves, for the procuring whereof they use sinister and evil methods." It barred any owner from letting an enslaved person "have or wear any sort of apparel whatsoever, finer, other, or greater value than Negro cloth, duffels, kerseys, osnabrigs, blue linen, check linen or coarse garlix, or calicoes, checked cottons, or Scotch plaids." In plain words, the lawmakers claimed that enslaved people were getting clothes too fine for enslaved people by wrong and wicked means. Servants in livery, a uniform the master chose, did not fall under this rule.

The fabrics on that list were rough and cheap. Calicoes and checked cottons were cotton cloths, and garlix was a linen. Negro cloth was a coarse cloth made for enslaved people's clothes. Osnaburg was a coarse, plain cloth first woven from flax in the German city of Osnabrück. Kersey was a thick, coarse wool, and duffel was a heavy wool used for blankets and coats. An earlier South Carolina act, passed in 1735, had already named cheap cloth for enslaved people's clothing.

The law required "all and every constable and other persons" to seize finer clothes from any enslaved person wearing them. A constable was a local officer who kept order. The person who seized the clothes could keep them "to his or their own use." An owner who thought the clothes had been taken wrongly could take the matter to a justice of the peace, a local judge. The surviving records do not show how often anyone seized clothes under this law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jack-gambia-1745" name="Jack" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Jack

> **Who:** A man taken from Gambia and enslaved in Virginia, who ran away within five weeks of his sale.
> **When and where:** Hanover County, Virginia, 1745.

Jack and another man ran away wearing the new clothes their owner had given them: jackets and breeches of napped cotton with no buttons and no buttonholes, shirts of osnaburg, and felt hats. Napped cloth has been brushed so that its surface is soft and fuzzy. Almost everything known about him comes from an advertisement that his owner, Margaret Arbuthnott of Hanover County, placed in the *Virginia Gazette* in October 1745 to get him back.

Slave traders had brought Jack and another man from Gambia, in West Africa, on the ship *Ranger*. On September 5, 1745, the traders sold them at Newcastle, Virginia. The advertisement does not name the traders. Neither man spoke English. Both were nearly 6 feet tall. Arbuthnott described Jack as having a scar over his right eyebrow. The other man, whose name the advertisement does not give, had what Arbuthnott described as "3 small Strokes on each Side of his Face."

Arbuthnott accused the two men of taking a damask tablecloth, fine Scottish linen, checked cloth, a white linen shirt and a silk handkerchief. Damask is cloth woven with a pattern that shows in the weave. She offered a reward to anyone who caught them and brought them back.

The record does not say whether Jack was caught.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jack-gambia-1745" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo blue, 1739 to 1750" -->
Indigo seeds came to South Carolina with the first English settlers in 1670. In the late 1730s and early 1740s, hundreds of South Carolina planters tried out new crops, and indigo was one of them. From 1739, Eliza Lucas tried growing indigo at Wappoo Plantation, on Wappoo Creek west of the Ashley River, near Charleston. She was 17 and managed the plantation and the 20 people enslaved on it. She worked first with an indigo maker from the island of Montserrat. She had the most success with an indigo maker of African descent whom her father hired from the French West Indies. In 1744 she used her crop to make seed and shared it with other planters.

The historian Nic Butler of the Charleston County Public Library writes that her part has been greatly exaggerated in recent times. Many of her neighbors were following the same steps. Her husband, Charles Pinckney, whom she married in 1744, printed her methods in newspaper articles in 1744 and 1745 under the name "Agricola."

South Carolina's lawmakers voted to pay planters a bounty, a reward from the government, of 1 shilling for each pound of indigo in 1744. They canceled it in 1746. In 1748 members of the British Parliament voted a bounty of 6 pence a pound, starting in 1749. Planters began shipping large amounts of indigo to England in 1747. Exports rose from about 5,000 pounds in 1745 and 1746 to about 130,000 pounds in 1748.

Enslaved people of African descent did almost all of the work. They planted and tended the indigo, built the vats, the big tubs for making dye, and filled them with water and cut leaves. After the leaves soaked, the workers beat the liquid with paddles or with buckets that had no bottoms. They added lime, made from burnt oyster shells, so that the blue settled out. Then workers scooped the blue mud into bags, dried it and cut it into cubes.

Charles Woodmason wrote in 1755 that 15 enslaved workers could plant and tend 50 acres of indigo, and that it took 25 skilled workers to turn that crop into dye. The accounts of indigo making from these years do not name the enslaved workers who made the dye. One enslaved man who built the vats is named. He was Quash, a carpenter whom the Lucas family held in slavery at Wappoo. In 1744 George Lucas, Eliza's father, wrote that "Quashe" should build a wooden indigo works at the family's Garden Hill plantation. Eliza told her father that Quash had spent 33 days building indigo vats at other Lucas plantations. In 1746 the minister Alexander Garden baptized him, and he took the name John Williams.

South Carolina's indigo was shipped to England for British cloth makers. South Carolinians bought most of their own cloth from England, already dyed.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From the Stamp Act tax of 1765 until the Revolution, colonists who opposed British taxes agreed again and again to stop buying British goods, including cloth. A refusal like this is called a boycott. In 1774 the First Continental Congress, a meeting of leaders from the colonies, banned British goods in every colony from December 1. Colonists wore homespun, cloth spun and woven in America, often at home, to show that they backed the boycott. Women held public spinning meetings. Harvard's graduating class of 1768 voted not to wear imported British clothes at their graduation.

In 1789 George Washington took the oath as the first president in a suit of brown wool woven in Connecticut. Furniture makers in the new country copied a style of sharp edges, legs that grew thinner toward the floor, and inlaid eagles. It is now called the Federal style. In Spanish New Orleans in 1786, the governor ordered Black women and women of mixed race to stop wearing feathers and jewels in their hair and to wear it plain or covered with a cloth.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Homespun as a protest, 1765 to 1776" -->
During the boycotts of British goods in the 1760s, Boston merchants opened spinning schools again. In Hartford, members of a society offered a prize of 12 pounds to whoever made the most yards of wool cloth in a year. Newspaper printers ran reports of spinning meetings in Massachusetts, Connecticut and Rhode Island. In Newport one spinner was a woman of 70 who had never spun a thread before. Only six of the newspaper stories called the spinners "Daughters of Liberty."

In August 1769 women met at their minister's house in Brookfield, Massachusetts, at 5 in the morning and spun until 7 at night. A report of the meeting said the best young spinner at the linen wheel, a spinning wheel for linen thread, spun 70 knots of yarn. A knot was a measured length of yarn. One married woman did her morning housework and made cheese first. Then she rode more than 2 miles carrying her own spinning wheel, spun 53 knots between 9 and 7, and went home to milk the cows.

In the autumn of 1767, the seniors at Harvard College voted not to drink tea or wear imported British clothes at their commencement, the ceremony at which students graduate. They did it to protest the Townshend taxes on goods from Britain. The printed sheets for the 1768 commencement, which listed the statements the graduates would argue for, were on paper made in New England. The next year's seniors did the same.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Charity Clarke

> **Who:** A young New Yorker who wrote to her cousin in London that American women would spin, weave and dress in their own cloth rather than buy British clothes.
> **When and where:** Her father's estate called Chelsea, on Manhattan, 1768 to 1774.

In 1769, when she was 21 or 22, Charity Clarke wrote to her cousin in London about what she would do if the English did not give Americans the liberty they asked for. The historian Alan Singer dates the letter June 1769, and other accounts date it December 8, 1769. She would lead "a fighting army of Amazons" and gather "a number of ladies armed with spinning wheels." In old Greek stories the Amazons were a nation of women warriors. Her ladies would have helpers "who shall all learn to weave and keep sheep," and they would all be "clothed with the work of our hands." They would be "no more slave to fashion and ceremony."

Charity Clarke was born in New York on June 28, 1747. She grew up on her father's estate, called Chelsea, on the island of Manhattan. Between 1768 and 1774 she wrote letters to her cousin Joseph Jekyll, a lawyer in London. Columbia University holds the letters today.

In 1774 she wrote to him again: "Though this body is not clad with silken garments, these limbs are armed with strength."

In 1778 she married the Rev. Benjamin Moore of Trinity Church. Their son, Clement Clarke Moore, wrote the poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charity-clarke" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wigs in Williamsburg, 1769 to 1775" -->
Edward Charlton came to Williamsburg, Virginia, from London and became the town's leading barber and wigmaker. His account book for 1769 to about 1775 shows that he sold sixty brown wigs of a style called the "dress bob" in one year, at 43 shillings each. For shaving a man and dressing his wig for a year, Charlton usually charged 2 pounds 3 shillings. Peter Pelham paid 1 pound 5 shillings, and Speaker Randolph, the leader of the colony's lawmakers, paid 4 pounds. In 1769 eight barbers and wigmakers worked in Williamsburg.

Wigmakers needed hair. George Long of Petersburg, another Virginia town, advertised that he would pay one shilling an ounce for human hair. The date of his advertisement is not known.

Wigs were going out of fashion in England, and Charlton retired shortly before the Revolution.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indigo's peak and end" -->
In 1775 South Carolina planters shipped more than one million pounds of indigo to England. Enslaved workers did almost all of the work of making it. The British bounty on indigo ran out in 1777, during the war with Britain. Some planters grew indigo again after the war ended in 1783. In the 1790s most of them gave up indigo, and many turned to cotton.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tignon order in New Orleans, 1786" -->
New Orleans was a Spanish colony in 1786. On June 2 of that year, Governor Esteban Rodríguez Miró issued a set of rules called the *bando de buen gobierno*, the order of good government. One rule was about the hair of Black women and women of mixed race. It said they could "no longer have feathers nor jewelry in their hair." They had to wear their hair plain or cover it with a *pañuelo*, a head cloth. People later called such a cloth a tignon.

The historian Virginia Meacham Gould writes that Miró aimed the rule at women who dressed "too elegantly" or "competed too freely with white women for status." She found little written evidence that officials enforced the rule, and no record that anyone was punished under it.

Portraits painted in New Orleans in the early 1800s show free women of color wearing head wraps tied in many different styles. Gould reads the head wraps in these portraits as a mark of distinction.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### George Washington

> **Who:** The first president, who took the oath of office in a suit made from American wool.
> **When and where:** Mount Vernon, Virginia, and New York City, January to April 1789.

George Washington took the oath as the first president of the United States on April 30, 1789, in New York, wearing a suit of brown wool cloth made in Connecticut. He chose American cloth on purpose. On April 8, 1789, he wrote: "I shall allways take a peculiar pleasure in giving every proper encouragement in my power to the manufactures of my Country." Manufactures meant goods made by hand or by machine.

In January 1789 a writer who signed himself "a Philadelphia mechanic" had urged American leaders, in *The Federal Gazette*, to wear clothing made in America. A mechanic then meant a skilled worker who made things. The Hartford Woolen Manufactory in Connecticut had just begun making cloth. A merchant in Philadelphia could not get the cloth for Washington. Henry Knox, then the secretary of war, dealt with the Hartford factory himself. The cloth reached Alexandria, Virginia, in early April. Washington left Mount Vernon for New York on April 16.

The staff at Mount Vernon believe that Cavan Boa made the coat, waistcoat and breeches. Boa was Washington's indentured tailor, a servant bound to work for him for a set number of years. Kathrin Breitt Brown makes copies of old clothes for Mount Vernon. She calls the coat "well-made but hastily finished," and she thinks Boa probably had help. Washington asked for six large engraved buttons.

In England the color of the cloth was called London brown. Americans called it Congress brown. The coat came to the collection at Mount Vernon in 1933.

Washington did not wear a wig. He powdered his own hair.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-washington-styles" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Federal style, 1780 to 1820" -->
From about 1780 to 1820, American cabinetmakers made furniture in a style now called Federal. A cabinetmaker is a skilled maker of fine furniture. Federal pieces had legs that grew thinner toward the floor, and sharp edges. Makers covered them with veneers, thin sheets of fine wood laid over the surface. They inlaid pictures of eagles, urns and bellflowers, setting small pieces of different wood into the surface.

The main wood was mahogany, and makers also used satinwood, maple and birch. American makers copied designs from English pattern books by George Hepplewhite and Thomas Sheraton. Makers included John and Thomas Seymour in Boston and Duncan Phyfe in New York.
