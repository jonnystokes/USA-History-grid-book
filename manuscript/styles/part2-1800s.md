## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The cotton spun in American mills between 1800 and 1850 came from Southern plantations, where enslaved people grew and picked it. Workers in New England mills spun and wove that cotton into cloth. Workers in some mills in Rhode Island and in Lowell, Massachusetts, also wove a rough, cheap cloth that planters bought to dress the people they enslaved.

Fashionable women wore high-waisted white cotton dresses in the early 1800s. Later they wore tight corsets, stiff garments laced around the body, and wide skirts held out by layers of petticoats, skirts worn under the outer skirt. Men stopped powdering their hair and cut it short. Some still wore knee breeches, pants that ended at the knee, and by the 1840s men wore narrow trousers. A government report in 1811 found that two-thirds of all the clothes Americans wore were made at home. People who could pay for it had a tailor or a seamstress make their clothes. The first shops that sold clothes already made stood near the docks, and their customers were sailors and other working men.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What fashionable people wore, 1800 to 1850" -->
In the first ten years of the 1800s, the fashionable dress for a woman had its waistline just under the breasts. It was made of muslin, a thin cotton cloth, and white was the most fashionable color. White dresses were hard to keep clean, and cleaning them cost money. For every day, women wore printed cottons and patterned silks, and wool in cold weather.

Men in the same years stopped powdering their hair and cut it short. They gave up embroidered silk and lace for plain wool coats in dark colors. Some still wore knee breeches. Others wore pantaloons, tight pants that reached the calf or the ankle.

In the early 1830s women's sleeves puffed out wide from the shoulder. This shape was called the gigot, or leg-of-mutton, sleeve, because it looked like a leg of mutton, which is sheep meat. Dressmakers made skirts wider, and women wore more and more petticoats under them. A petticoat is a skirt worn under another skirt. Under the dress, a woman wore a corset, a stiff garment laced tight around the body from the chest to the hips.

By the 1840s a fashionable corset ran from a woman's breasts down over her hips. Heavy cords and strips of whalebone stiffened it. Whalebone is a stiff, bendable material from the mouths of some whales. A long, flat piece of steel, wood or whalebone, called a busk, ran down the front. Sleeves were cut so tight that she often could not lift her arms higher than her shoulders. By the end of the decade some women wore six or seven petticoats. The frock coat was the standard coat for men, worn with narrow trousers and vests of brightly patterned silk.

*Godey's Lady's Book* was a magazine for women, published in Philadelphia from 1830. Each issue opened with a fashion plate, a picture of the latest dresses colored by hand. Louis Godey, the publisher, said that coloring the plates cost $8,000 in 1859. Sarah Josepha Hale edited the magazine from 1837 to 1877. About 10,000 copies of each issue went out in 1837, about 40,000 two years later and about 150,000 by 1860.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton picked in the South, woven in the North" -->
The owners and workers of the cotton mills in Lowell, Massachusetts, depended on cotton grown in the South by enslaved people. US Senator Charles Sumner called the tie between Southern planters and New England mill owners an "unholy union." On one side, he said, stood "the cotton planters and fleshmongers of Louisiana and Mississippi." On the other stood "the cotton spinners and traffickers of New England." A fleshmonger was a person who traded in human beings, and a trafficker was a trader.

In Lowell, workers at the Merrimack Company printed calico, cotton cloth printed with colored patterns. The company's owners hired skilled printers from England. The head printer they hired in 1825 was paid more than the company's treasurer, the officer in charge of its money.

Solomon Northup described how the cotton was picked. Northup was a free Black man, born in Minerva, New York. In 1841 two white men who called themselves Merrill Brown and Abram Hamilton took him to Washington, drugged him and sold him into slavery. Slave traders sold him in Louisiana, and enslavers there held him until January 1853. For almost ten years he picked cotton on the plantation of Edwin Epps, on Bayou Boeuf.

Northup wrote that the pickers had to be in the field as soon as it was light. They stayed until it was too dark to see, with 10 or 15 minutes at noon to eat cold bacon. At night each picker's basket was weighed. An ordinary day's work was 200 pounds of cotton. Epps and his drivers, the men who ran the work in the field, whipped anyone who brought in less. The more a picker came up short, the more lashes he or she got. A lash is one blow of the whip.

Epps set each picker's daily amount by what that person had shown he or she could pick. Northup called a woman named Patsey "the most remarkable cotton picker on Bayou Boeuf." He wrote that 500 pounds a day was not unusual for her, and that she "would surely have been beaten if she failed to produce twice as much" as the 200 pounds of an ordinary day's work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth made for enslaved people's clothes" -->
Planters called the rough fabric they bought for enslaved people's clothes "negro cloth" or "slave cloth." The names covered several coarse fabrics. Two of them were osnaburg, a coarse plain cloth, and kersey, a thick, coarse wool. Both were on the list in South Carolina's 1740 law, which barred enslaved people from wearing anything finer. Another was linsey-woolsey, a cheap cloth of wool mixed with cotton or linen. Jeans was a cotton and wool cloth woven in a twill, a weave that makes thin diagonal lines.

The Hazard family owned a woolen mill in Peace Dale, Rhode Island, where workers made negro cloth for Southern planters. In the 1820s Isaac Peace Hazard traveled through the South taking orders from plantation owners. By 1845 the owners of 17 of Rhode Island's 40 textile mills had made negro cloth their main product. Workers in the Lowell mills made it too.

Southern buyers told the Hazards what they wanted. One wrote in 1824 that the cloth "would not suit servants unless it was blue." By "servants" he meant enslaved people. A complaint of the time described clothes "as thin as baize, threads not beat close together, others split all to pieces." Baize is a thin wool cloth.

Enslavers handed out clothing on set days. On Colonel Edward Lloyd's plantation in Maryland it came once a year. In Charleston, the people Mary Pringle enslaved as house servants got a summer share and a winter share. In the 1850s Stephen Duncan owned the Carlisle plantation in Mississippi. There each man got enough cloth for three shirts, two pairs of summer pants and one pair of winter pants. He also got a coat and two pairs of shoes. Each child got four slips, short loose dresses. John Potter's rice plantation in South Carolina used 1,800 yards of cloth a year for the 400 people he enslaved. That came to about 4.5 yards for each person.

Harriet Jacobs was enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in the household of Dr. James Norcom. In her book she calls him Dr. Flint. She wrote: "I have a vivid recollection of the linsey-woolsey dress given me every winter by Mrs. Flint. How I hated it! It was one of the badges of slavery." Her grandmother's work paid for the rest of her clothes.

Before the slave trader Theophilus Freeman sold people, he dressed them in new clothes. In 1841 he held Solomon Northup and other captives in his slave pen in New Orleans, a building where traders locked up enslaved people until they were sold. Northup wrote that the traders made the captives wash and made the men shave. Then they gave each person "a new suit each, cheap, but clean." The men got a hat, a coat, a shirt, pants and shoes, and the women a calico dress and a handkerchief to tie around the head.

### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A man enslaved in Maryland who described the clothes he was given as a child, and who escaped to freedom dressed as a sailor.
> **When and where:** Talbot County and Baltimore, Maryland, the 1820s to 1838.

As a child, Frederick Douglass had nothing to wear but one coarse linen shirt that reached to his knees. It came from the clothing that Colonel Edward Lloyd and his overseers handed out each year. He had no shoes, stockings, jacket or trousers, "in hottest summer and coldest winter." He had no bed. On the coldest nights he took a bag used for carrying corn to the mill, crawled into it and slept on the cold, damp clay floor. He wrote: "My feet have been so cracked with the frost, that the pen with which I am writing might be laid in the gashes."

Douglass was born enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland. As a child he lived on the home plantation of Colonel Edward Lloyd. Lloyd enslaved people on several farms, and they came to the home plantation for their food and their yearly clothing.

Each enslaved adult got two coarse linen shirts, one pair of linen trousers, one jacket, one pair of winter trousers "made of coarse negro cloth," one pair of stockings and one pair of shoes a year. Douglass wrote that all of it "could not have cost more than seven dollars." Children too young to work in the fields got only two coarse linen shirts a year. "When these failed them," he wrote, "they went naked until the next allowance-day."

When he was about seven or eight, his owners sent him to Baltimore. Before he left, Lucretia Auld, the daughter of his old master, told him he could have a pair of trousers if he scrubbed the dirt off his skin first. He wrote: "The thought of owning a pair of trousers was great indeed!"

On September 3, 1838, Douglass escaped from Baltimore by train. He wrote: "I had on a red shirt and a tarpaulin hat, and a black cravat tied in sailor fashion carelessly and loosely about my neck." A tarpaulin hat was a sailor's waterproof hat, and a cravat was a cloth tied around the neck. He carried a paper called a sailor's protection, borrowed from a friend who was a sailor. It described a free American sailor, and Douglass used it in place of papers showing he was free.

Anna Murray, a free Black woman who worked as a laundress, helped him. She married him in New York that same month. According to *The Frederick Douglass Encyclopedia*, her laundry work gave her access to sailors' clothing, and she got some of it for him. Other accounts say she sewed the sailor's outfit herself. She also gave him part of her savings, and she sold one of her feather beds to add to it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-douglass-styles" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first ready-made clothes" -->
The first clothes sold ready to wear in America were sold in the early 1800s at shops called slop shops. They stood on the streets next to the docks of Boston, New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore, and of smaller whaling and fishing towns. The people who bought these clothes were sailors and other working men. The clothes came in one size, and a sailor heading out to sea made them fit as best he could.

Between 1840 and 1860, more and more clothing makers sewed cheap clothes for planters to give to enslaved people. Many New York firms made what they called "Negro clothing." A planter who bought these clothes did not have to use the time of enslaved workers to sew them.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the second half of the 1800s, Americans began sewing clothes on machines and wearing cloth colored with man-made dyes. Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851. From 1856 his partner, Edward Clark, let families pay for one a few dollars a month. In 1856 a chemistry student in London made the first man-made dye to be sold, a purple, from chemicals in coal tar. In 1873 a tailor in Reno and a merchant in San Francisco got a patent for work pants with metal rivets, small metal pins that hold cloth together. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the right to be the only one to make an invention for a set number of years. The riveted pants were later called blue jeans.

In 1851 a few leaders of the fight for women's rights wore short skirts over trousers, and men mocked them for it. In 1876 members of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors ordered that every man in the county jail have his hair cut to one inch. Sheriff Matthew Nunan used the order to cut off Chinese prisoners' long braids, and a federal judge later found that the order was meant only for the Chinese. From 1879, the staff at the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania cut Native children's hair and took away their clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bloomer costume, 1851 to 1854" -->
In February 1851, Elizabeth Smith Miller visited Amelia Bloomer in Seneca Falls, New York, wearing a dress that reached the knee, over trousers. It was sometimes called the Turkish dress. Bloomer began wearing it too. She printed articles and pictures of it in her newspaper, *The Lily*. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, a leader of the fight for women's rights, also wore it. People named the outfit bloomers, after Amelia Bloomer, the woman best known for wearing it and speaking up for it.

Bloomer wrote that "letters came pouring in upon me by the hundreds from women all over the country" asking about the dress. The number of copies of *The Lily* sent out each month rose from 500 to 4,000, by the count of the National Park Service. Another count gives 3,000.

Men mocked the women who wore it and called them "Amazons," after the women warriors of old Greek stories. Leaders of some churches refused to let young women who wore it become members. By about 1854, Stanton and other leaders had gone back to long skirts and corsets. They feared that the mockery drew attention away from their fight for the right to vote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoops, sewing machines and paper patterns" -->
In 1856 women began to wear the cage crinoline, a frame of steel hoops that held a skirt out wide. With it, women no longer needed the heavy stacks of petticoats, sometimes as many as seven, that they had worn to get the same shape.

Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851. He used the lockstitch, a stitch that locks two threads together, which Elias Howe had invented. Sewing a man's frock coat by hand took almost 17 hours. By machine it took two and a half.

In 1856 a Singer machine cost about $100. That September, Edward Clark, Singer's partner, began to sell machines on installments, which means a buyer paid a little each month. The magazine *American Heritage* gives the terms as $5 a month. Earlier in 1856 Clark had offered $50 off a new Singer for any old sewing machine, even a worthless one.

In 1863 Ebenezer Butterick and his wife, Ellen Augusta Pollard Butterick, of Sterling, Massachusetts, began selling sewing patterns cut from tissue paper. A pattern is a set of paper shapes that a person who sews pins to cloth and cuts around. The idea is said to have come from Ellen Butterick's annoyance with patterns that came in only one size. The Buttericks' patterns came in a range of standard sizes. Members of the family cut and folded the first ones at home. They sold patterns for men's and boys' clothes first, and added women's dresses in 1866.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Elizabeth Keckley

> **Who:** A dressmaker, born enslaved, who bought her freedom with money from her sewing and made Mary Todd Lincoln's dresses.
> **When and where:** Virginia, St. Louis and Washington, D.C., 1818 to 1868.

Elizabeth Keckley paid for her own freedom with money she earned by sewing, after her customers lent her the price. Later she made the dresses of Mary Todd Lincoln, the wife of President Abraham Lincoln.

Keckley was born enslaved in February 1818 at Dinwiddie Court House, Virginia. The Burwell family enslaved her and her mother, Agnes. Her father was Armistead Burwell, of the family that enslaved her. Agnes made clothes for 82 people: the 12 members of the Burwell family and the 70 people they enslaved. As Keckley grew up, she helped her mother make the clothes.

Her old master sent her to work for his eldest son, Robert Burwell, a minister, and his wife. When she was nearly 18, she moved with them to Hillsboro, North Carolina, where Robert Burwell led a church. A schoolmaster named Bingham had promised Mrs. Burwell that he would "subdue," or crush, what he called Keckley's "stubborn pride." He whipped her. When she asked him why, he hit her with a chair and knocked her to the floor.

While she lived in Hillsboro, a white man raped her again and again for four years. Rape means forcing a person to have sex. Keckley wrote that the man "had base designs upon me" and "persecuted me for four years." She refused to name him. "I spare the world his name," she wrote. She gave birth to his son, George, the only child she ever had.

In St. Louis, the Garland family who owned her lived on the money her sewing brought in. She wrote: "With my needle I kept bread in the mouths of seventeen persons for two years and five months." She wrote that "the best ladies in St. Louis" were her customers.

Her owner agreed to free her and George for $1,200. One of her customers, Mrs. Le Bourgeois, raised the money among Keckley's other customers. Keckley and her son were freed on November 15, 1855. She took the money only as a loan, and she paid back every cent from her sewing.

She moved to Washington in 1860 and later employed 20 seamstresses. Abraham Lincoln took office in March 1861. On the Tuesday morning after, she went to the White House. Three other dressmakers were already waiting, and Mary Todd Lincoln chose Keckley. She wrote that she made "fifteen or sixteen dresses" for Mrs. Lincoln that spring and summer. Her customers also included Varina Davis, the wife of Jefferson Davis.

In the summer of 1862, people freed from slavery were coming to Washington from Maryland and Virginia. In August 1862 Keckley and other Black residents of Washington started the Contraband Relief Association to help them. During the Civil War, Union soldiers used the word "contraband" for people who had escaped slavery. The association gave out food, shelter, clothing and medical care. In its first year its members handed out 5,150 pieces of clothing. In 1868 she published a book about her life, *Behind the Scenes, or, Thirty Years a Slave, and Four Years in the White House*. The Smithsonian holds a purple velvet gown she made for Mrs. Lincoln. She died in Washington in May 1907.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-keckley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New colors from coal tar, 1856 on" -->
Every dye before 1856 came from plants, insects, shellfish or minerals. Indigo blue came from a plant, cochineal red from an insect and red ochre from a mineral. In 1856 William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old student at the Royal College of Chemistry in London, was on his Easter break. He was trying to make quinine, a drug for the disease malaria. He worked with chemicals that come from coal tar, a thick black liquid made from coal. Instead of quinine he made a purple dye, now called mauve or mauveine. It was the first man-made dye sold for use. In 1857 he opened a factory near London.

By 1859 chemists were making other new colors from coal tar, such as fuchsine, a deep crimson red. These are called aniline dyes, after aniline, one of the coal-tar chemicals used to make them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis

> **Who:** A tailor in Reno who put metal rivets on work pants, and the San Francisco merchant who shared the patent with him.
> **When and where:** Reno, Nevada, and San Francisco, California, 1870 to 1875.

Jacob Davis put copper rivets, small metal pins that hold cloth together, on work pants so that the seams would not tear. On May 20, 1873, he and Levi Strauss's company got a patent for the idea. A patent is a government paper that gives an inventor the right to be the only one to make an invention for a set number of years. The riveted pants were later called blue jeans.

Davis was born Jacob Youphes in Riga, in the Russian Empire, on May 14, 1831. He came to New York in 1854 and changed his name. By 1869 he ran a tailor shop in Reno, Nevada.

A woman asked Davis to make strong work pants for her husband, a woodcutter. Davis had been using copper rivets on horse blankets. He put rivets at the corners of the pockets, where the seams tore. In a court statement, Davis dated this to January 1871. Other accounts date it to December 1870.

Levi Strauss was born in Buttenheim, Bavaria, on February 26, 1829. He came to the United States in 1847 and reached San Francisco in 1854. There he sold cloth and clothing to stores as Levi Strauss & Co.

The patent, number 139,121, covered "a metal rivet or eyelet at each edge of the pocket opening to prevent the ripping of the seam." Davis and Levi Strauss & Co. shared it. In January 1874 they took A. B. Elfelt and others to court over the patent. On February 10, 1875, the judge decided for them. They had asked for $20,000 and were awarded $2,000.

Davis ran the San Francisco factory that made the riveted pants. Strauss died on September 26, 1902, and Davis died in 1908.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="levi-strauss-jacob-davis" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cutting Chinese men's braids in San Francisco, 1876 to 1879" -->
Chinese men in the 1800s shaved the front of the head and wore the rest of their hair in one long braid called a queue. Justice Stephen Field later wrote that Chinese men saw the loss of the queue as a disgrace that brought "misfortune and suffering after death."

In 1873 members of San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, the men who made the city's laws, voted for an order to cut prisoners' hair. Mayor William Alvord vetoed, or rejected, the hair-cutting part.

In April 1876 California's lawmakers passed a law about lodging houses, buildings that rent out rooms. It made it a crime to sleep in a room with less than 500 cubic feet of air for each person, about the space of a room 8 feet long, 8 feet wide and 8 feet high. Officials used the law against Chinese residents. On June 14, 1876, members of the Board of Supervisors voted that every man jailed in the county have his hair cut to one inch from the scalp. Field later wrote: "The ordinance was intended only for the Chinese in San Francisco." An ordinance is a city law.

In April 1878 a court found Ho Ah Kow guilty under the lodging law and fined him $10. His sentence was five days in the county jail if he did not pay. He did not pay, and he went to jail. Sheriff Matthew Nunan cut off his queue. Nunan and his deputies, the officers who worked under him, cut off other men's queues too. The records do not say how many.

Ho Ah Kow sued Nunan. On July 7, 1879, Justice Field ruled that the hair order went against the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution. That amendment promises every person the equal protection of the laws, which means the same treatment under the law. Field wrote that the order was not meant to stop prisoners from escaping. Its purpose, he wrote, was "merely to aggravate the severity of his confinement." That means its purpose was to make the prisoner's time in jail harder to bear.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uniforms and short hair at Carlisle, 1879 on" -->
Richard Henry Pratt, an Army officer, opened the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1879 for Native children. The staff cut the children's hair and took away their clothes. They dressed the boys in military uniforms and the girls in Victorian dresses, the long dresses of the time.

Carlisle's photographer, John N. Choate, took pictures of students when they arrived. He photographed the same students again after they had been at the school for a while. His "before and after" pictures show a student in the Native clothes he or she arrived in, then the same student in a school uniform. One pair has the caption "Tom Torlino, Navajo. As he entered the school in 1882. As he appeared three years later."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shirtwaists and the Gibson Girl, 1890s" -->
In the 1890s women wore a separate skirt and a shirtwaist, a blouse cut like a man's shirt. Women who worked for pay wore it every day. Sleeves puffed out wide again and were biggest around 1895. Most working men wore the sack suit, a loose jacket with pants.

About 50,000 people rode bicycles in 1885, and about 10 million in 1896, by one estimate. Some women rode in bloomers, but most wore shorter skirts.

Charles Dana Gibson lived from 1867 to 1944. He drew for *Life*, *Collier's Weekly*, *Harper's Weekly* and other magazines. In the 1890s he drew a young woman whom people called the Gibson Girl. In the words of the Library of Congress, she went after "higher education, romance, marriage, physical well-being, and individuality" with more freedom than young women had had before. According to an exhibition of Gibson's drawings at the Library of Congress, from the 1890s until World War I she "set the standard for beauty, fashion, and manners."
