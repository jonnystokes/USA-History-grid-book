## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The cotton that American mills spun between 1800 and 1850 came from Southern plantations, where enslaved people grew and picked it. Mills in New England spun and wove that cotton into cloth. Some mills in Rhode Island and in Lowell, Massachusetts, also wove a rough, cheap cloth that planters bought to dress the people they enslaved.

Fashionable women wore high-waisted white cotton dresses in the early 1800s. Later they wore tight corsets and wide skirts held out by layers of petticoats. Men gave up knee breeches and powdered hair for long trousers and short hair. Most clothes were still sewn at home or by a tailor. The first shops that sold clothes already made stood near the docks, and their customers were sailors.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="What fashionable people wore, 1800 to 1850" -->
In the first ten years of the 1800s, the fashionable dress for a woman had its waistline just under the bust. It was made of muslin, a thin cotton cloth, and white was the favorite color. White dresses were hard to keep clean, and cleaning them cost money. For every day, women wore printed cottons and patterned silks, and wool in cold weather.

Men in the same years stopped powdering their hair and cut it short. They gave up the embroidered silk of the 1700s for plain wool coats in dark colors. Some still wore knee breeches. Others wore pantaloons, tight pants that reached the calf or the ankle.

In the early 1830s women's sleeves puffed out wide from the shoulder. This shape was called the gigot, or leg-of-mutton, sleeve. Skirts grew wide, and women wore more and more petticoats under them. A petticoat is a skirt worn under another skirt. Under the dress, a woman wore a corset, a stiff garment laced tight around the body from the chest to the hips.

By the 1840s a fashionable corset ran from a woman's breasts down over her hips. Cords and strips of whalebone stiffened it. A flat piece of steel, wood or whalebone, called a busk, ran down the front, so the woman could not bend at the waist. Sleeves were cut so tight that she often could not lift her arms higher than her shoulders. By the end of the decade some women wore six or seven petticoats. Men wore long frock coats, narrow trousers and vests of brightly patterned silk.

Many women learned about new fashions from *Godey's Lady's Book*, a magazine published in Philadelphia from 1830. Each issue opened with a fashion plate, a picture of the latest dresses colored by hand. Louis Godey, the publisher, said that coloring the plates cost $8,000 in 1859. Sarah Josepha Hale edited the magazine from 1837 to 1877. It had about 10,000 subscribers in 1837, about 40,000 two years later and about 150,000 by 1860.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cotton picked in the South, woven in the North" -->
The owners and workers of the cotton mills in Lowell, Massachusetts, depended on cotton grown in the South by enslaved people. Charles Sumner, a senator from Massachusetts, called the tie between Southern planters and New England mill owners an "unholy union ... between the cotton planters and fleshmongers of Louisiana and Mississippi and the cotton spinners and traffickers of New England." A fleshmonger was a person who traded in human beings.

In Lowell, the Merrimack Company printed calico, cotton cloth printed with colored patterns. Its owners hired skilled printers from England. The head printer they hired in 1825 was paid more than the company's treasurer.

Solomon Northup described how the cotton was picked. Northup was a free Black man from New York. Kidnappers seized him in Washington in 1841 and sold him into slavery in Louisiana. He was held there until January 1853. He picked cotton on the plantation of Edwin Epps, on Bayou Boeuf.

Northup wrote that the pickers had to be in the field as soon as it was light. They stayed until it was too dark to see, with 10 or 15 minutes at noon to eat cold bacon. At night each picker's basket was weighed. An ordinary day's work was 200 pounds of cotton. Epps and his drivers, the men who ran the work in the field, whipped anyone who brought in less. Northup counted the punishment in lashes. A lash is one blow of the whip.

Epps set each picker's daily amount by what that person had shown he or she could pick. A woman named Patsey was the fastest picker on Bayou Boeuf, and she often picked 500 pounds a day. Northup wrote that Patsey "would surely have been beaten if she failed to produce twice as much" as the 200 pounds he was held to.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cloth made for enslaved people's clothes" -->
Planters called the rough fabric they bought for enslaved people's clothes "negro cloth" or "slave cloth." The names covered several coarse fabrics. Two of them, osnaburg and kersey, were already on the list in South Carolina's 1740 law. Others were linsey-woolsey, a cheap cloth of wool mixed with cotton or linen, and jeans, a cotton and wool cloth woven in a twill, a weave that makes thin diagonal lines.

The Hazard family owned a woolen mill in Peace Dale, Rhode Island, that made negro cloth for Southern planters. In the 1820s Isaac Peace Hazard traveled through the South taking orders from plantation owners. By 1845, 17 of Rhode Island's 40 textile mills made negro cloth their main product. The mills in Lowell made it too.

Southern buyers told the Hazards what they wanted. One wrote in 1824 that the cloth "would not suit servants unless it was blue." Enslaved people showed one visitor clothes "as thin as baize, threads not beat close together, others split all to pieces." Baize is a thin wool cloth.

Enslavers handed out clothing on set days. On some plantations it came once a year, and on others in a summer share and a winter share. In the 1850s, on Stephen Duncan's Carlisle plantation in Mississippi, each man got enough cloth for three shirts, two pairs of summer pants and one pair of winter pants. He also got a coat and two pairs of shoes. Each child got four slips, short loose dresses. John Potter bought 1,800 yards of cloth a year for the 400 people he enslaved on his rice plantation in South Carolina. That came to about 4.5 yards for each person.

Harriet Jacobs was enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in the household of Dr. James Norcom. In her book she calls him Dr. Flint. She wrote: "I have a vivid recollection of the linsey-woolsey dress given me every winter by Mrs. Flint. How I hated it! It was one of the badges of slavery." Her grandmother's work paid for the rest of her clothes.

Slave traders dressed people in new clothes before they sold them. In 1841 Theophilus Freeman held Solomon Northup and other captives in his slave pen in New Orleans. Northup wrote that the traders made the captives wash and made the men shave. Then they gave each person "a new suit each, cheap, but clean." Each man got a hat, a coat, a shirt, pants and shoes. Each woman got a calico dress and a handkerchief to tie around her head.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Frederick Douglass

> **Who:** A man enslaved in Maryland who described the clothes he was given as a child, and who escaped to freedom dressed as a sailor.
> **When and where:** Talbot County and Baltimore, Maryland, the 1820s to 1838.

As a child, Frederick Douglass was given one coarse linen shirt that reached to his knees, and nothing else to wear. He had no shoes, stockings, jacket or trousers, summer or winter. On the coldest nights he slept inside a sack for corn on a clay floor. He wrote: "My feet have been so cracked with the frost, that the pen with which I am writing might be laid in the gashes."

Douglass was born enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland. As a child he lived on the home plantation of Colonel Edward Lloyd. Lloyd enslaved people on several farms, and they came to the home plantation for their food and their yearly clothing.

Each enslaved adult got two coarse linen shirts, one pair of linen trousers, one jacket, one pair of winter trousers "made of coarse negro cloth," one pair of stockings and one pair of shoes a year. Douglass wrote that all of it "could not have cost more than seven dollars." Children too young to work in the fields got only two coarse linen shirts a year. "When these failed them," he wrote, "they went naked until the next allowance-day."

When he was about seven or eight, his owners sent him to Baltimore. Before he left, Lucretia Auld, the daughter of his old master, told him he could have a pair of trousers if he scrubbed the dirt off his skin first. He wrote: "The thought of owning a pair of trousers was great indeed!"

On September 3, 1838, Douglass escaped from Baltimore by train. He wrote: "I had on a red shirt and a tarpaulin hat, and a black cravat tied in sailor fashion carelessly and loosely about my neck." A tarpaulin hat was a sailor's waterproof hat, and a cravat was a cloth tied around the neck. He carried a paper called a sailor's protection, borrowed from a friend who was a sailor. It described a free American sailor, and Douglass used it in place of papers showing he was free.

Anna Murray, a free Black woman in Baltimore, helped him. She married him in New York that same month. According to *The Frederick Douglass Encyclopedia*, her work as a laundress gave her access to sailors' clothing, and she got some of it for him. She also gave him part of her savings, and she sold one of her feather beds to add to it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-douglass-styles" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first ready-made clothes" -->
The first clothes sold ready to wear in America were sold in the early 1800s at shops called slop shops. They stood on the streets next to the wharves of Boston, New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore, and of smaller whaling and fishing towns. The people who bought these clothes were sailors.

Between 1840 and 1860, making cheap clothes for enslaved people grew into an industry of its own. A planter who bought ready-made clothes did not have to take enslaved women away from other work to sew them.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the second half of the 1800s, machines and chemistry changed what Americans wore and what colors they could buy. Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851. From 1856 his partner, Edward Clark, let families pay for one a few dollars a month. In 1856 a chemistry student in London made the first man-made dye to be sold, a purple, from chemicals in coal tar. In 1873 a tailor in Reno and a merchant in San Francisco got a patent for work pants with metal rivets, the pants later called blue jeans.

Some Americans used clothes and hair to force other people into line. In 1851 a few leaders of the fight for women's rights wore short skirts over trousers, and men mocked them for it. In 1876 San Francisco's leaders passed an order that let the sheriff cut off Chinese prisoners' long braids. From 1879, the staff at the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania cut Native children's hair and took away their clothes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Bloomer costume, 1851 to 1854" -->
In February 1851, Elizabeth Smith Miller visited Amelia Bloomer in Seneca Falls, New York, wearing a short dress over loose trousers gathered at the ankle. It was sometimes called the Turkish dress. Bloomer began wearing it too. She printed articles and pictures of it in her newspaper, *The Lily*. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, a leader of the fight for women's rights, also wore it. People named the outfit the Bloomer, after Amelia Bloomer.

Bloomer wrote that "letters came pouring in upon me by the hundreds from women all over the country" asking about the dress. The number of copies of *The Lily* sent out each month rose from 500 to 4,000, by the count of the National Park Service. Another count gives 3,000.

Men mocked the women who wore it and called them "Amazons." Some women who wore it were refused membership in their churches. By about 1854, Stanton and other leaders had gone back to long skirts and corsets. They feared that the mockery drew attention away from their fight for the right to vote.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoops, sewing machines and paper patterns" -->
In 1856 women began to wear the cage crinoline, a frame of steel hoops that held a skirt out wide. It took the place of the stacks of petticoats women had worn to get the same shape.

Isaac Singer began making sewing machines in 1851. He used the lockstitch, a stitch that locks two threads together, which Elias Howe had invented. Sewing a man's frock coat by hand took almost 17 hours. By machine it took two and a half.

In 1856 a Singer machine cost about $100. In September 1856 Edward Clark, Singer's partner, began to sell machines on installments, which means a buyer paid a little each month. The magazine *American Heritage* gives the terms as $5 a month. Clark also let buyers trade in old machines of other makes as part of the price.

In 1863 Ebenezer Butterick and his wife, Ellen Augusta Pollard Butterick, of Sterling, Massachusetts, began selling sewing patterns cut from tissue paper. A pattern is a set of paper shapes that a sewer pins to cloth and cuts around. Before the Buttericks, a pattern came in only one size. Theirs came in a range of standard sizes. Members of the family cut and folded the first ones at home. They sold patterns for men's and boys' clothes first, and added women's dresses in 1866.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Elizabeth Keckley

> **Who:** A dressmaker, born enslaved, who bought her freedom with money from her sewing and made Mary Todd Lincoln's dresses.
> **When and where:** Virginia, St. Louis and Washington, D.C., 1818 to 1868.

Elizabeth Keckley bought her own freedom with money she earned by sewing. Later she made the dresses of Mary Todd Lincoln, the wife of President Abraham Lincoln.

Keckley was born enslaved in February 1818 at Dinwiddie Court House, Virginia. The Burwell family enslaved her and her mother, Agnes. Agnes made clothes for 82 people: the 12 members of the Burwell family and the 70 people they enslaved. Keckley learned to sew by helping her.

When she was nearly 18, she was taken to Hillsboro, North Carolina, and enslaved in the house of Mrs. Burwell. A schoolmaster named Bingham had promised Mrs. Burwell that he would break what he called Keckley's "stubborn pride." He whipped her. When she asked him why, he hit her with a chair and knocked her to the floor. She wrote that for four years after that, a white man forced her to have sex with him. She refused to name him. "I spare the world his name," she wrote. She gave birth to his son, George.

In St. Louis, the Garland family who owned her lived on the money her sewing brought in. She wrote: "With my needle I kept bread in the mouths of seventeen persons for two years and five months." The richest women in the city became her customers.

Her owner agreed to free her and George for $1,200. One of her customers, Mrs. Le Bourgeois, collected the money from other customers as a loan. Keckley and her son were freed on November 15, 1855. She paid back every cent from her sewing.

She moved to Washington in 1860 and later employed 20 seamstresses. Abraham Lincoln took office in March 1861. On the Tuesday morning after, she went to the White House. Three other dressmakers were already waiting, and Mary Todd Lincoln chose Keckley. She wrote that she made "fifteen or sixteen dresses" for Mrs. Lincoln that spring and summer. Her customers also included Varina Davis, the wife of Jefferson Davis.

In August 1862 she started the Contraband Relief Association. It helped Black people who had escaped slavery and come to Washington. In its first year its members handed out 5,150 pieces of clothing. In 1868 she published a book about her life, *Behind the Scenes, or, Thirty Years a Slave, and Four Years in the White House*. The Smithsonian holds a purple velvet gown she made for Mrs. Lincoln. She died in Washington in May 1907.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="elizabeth-keckley" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New colors from coal tar, 1856 on" -->
Every dye before 1856 came from plants, insects, shellfish or minerals, like the indigo, the cochineal and the red ochre of earlier centuries. In 1856 William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old student in London, was trying to make quinine, a drug for the disease malaria. He worked with chemicals that come from coal tar, a thick black liquid made from coal. Instead of quinine he made a purple dye, which he called mauveine. It was the first man-made dye sold for use. In 1857 he opened a factory near London.

By 1859 chemists had made other new colors from coal tar, such as fuchsine, a deep crimson-pink. These are called aniline dyes, after aniline, one of the coal-tar chemicals used to make them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis

> **Who:** A tailor in Reno who put metal rivets on work pants, and the San Francisco merchant who shared the patent with him.
> **When and where:** Reno, Nevada, and San Francisco, California, 1870 to 1875.

Jacob Davis put copper rivets on work pants so that the seams would not tear. On May 20, 1873, he and Levi Strauss's company got a patent for the idea. The riveted pants were later called blue jeans.

Davis was born Jacob Youphes in Riga, in the Russian Empire, on May 14, 1831. He came to New York in 1854 and changed his name. By 1869 he ran a tailor shop in Reno, Nevada.

A woman asked Davis to make strong work pants for her husband, a woodcutter. Davis had been using copper rivets on horse blankets. He put rivets at the corners of the pockets and at the bottom of the fly, where the seams tore. In a court statement, Davis dated this to January 1871. Other accounts date it to December 1870.

Levi Strauss was born in Buttenheim, Bavaria, on February 26, 1829. He came to the United States in 1847 and reached San Francisco in 1854. There he sold cloth and clothing to stores as Levi Strauss & Co.

The U.S. Patent Office granted patent number 139,121 for "a metal rivet or eyelet at each edge of the pocket opening to prevent the ripping of the seam." Davis and Levi Strauss & Co. shared it. In January 1874 they sued a company that copied the rivets. On February 10, 1875, the judge decided for them. They had asked for $20,000 and were awarded $2,000.

Davis ran the San Francisco factory that made the riveted pants. Strauss died on September 26, 1902, and Davis died in 1908.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="levi-strauss-jacob-davis" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cutting Chinese men's braids in San Francisco, 1876 to 1879" -->
Chinese men in the 1800s shaved the front of the head and wore the rest of their hair in one long braid called a queue. Losing the queue was a disgrace. Many believed it brought "misfortune and suffering after death."

In 1873 San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, the men who made the city's laws, passed an order to cut prisoners' hair. Mayor William Alvord vetoed, or rejected, the hair-cutting part.

In April 1876 California's lawmakers made it a crime to sleep in a room with less than 500 cubic feet of air for each person. Officials used the law against Chinese people living in crowded boardinghouses. On June 14, 1876, the Board of Supervisors ordered that every man jailed in the county have his hair cut to one inch from the scalp. Justice Stephen Field later found that the order was "directed against the Chinese only."

In April 1878 Ho Ah Kow was convicted under the lodging law and fined $10. He could not pay, so he was jailed for five days. Sheriff Matthew Nunan cut off his queue. Nunan and his deputies cut off other men's queues too. The records do not say how many.

Ho Ah Kow sued Nunan. On July 7, 1879, Justice Field ruled that the hair order broke the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution, because it punished Chinese prisoners more harshly than others. Field wrote that the order was not meant to stop prisoners from escaping. Its purpose, he wrote, was "merely to aggravate the severity of his confinement," which means to make the prisoner's time in jail harder to bear.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Uniforms and short hair at Carlisle, 1879 on" -->
Richard Henry Pratt, an Army officer, opened the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1879. The staff cut the children's hair and took away their clothes. They dressed the boys in military uniforms and the girls in Victorian dresses, the long dresses of the time.

Carlisle's photographer, John N. Choate, took pictures of students when they arrived. He photographed the same students again after they had been at the school for a while. School staff printed the pairs side by side. Each pair set a student in the clothes he or she arrived in beside the same student in a school uniform. One pair has the caption "Tom Torlino, Navajo. As he entered the school in 1882. As he appeared three years later."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Shirtwaists and the Gibson Girl, 1890s" -->
In the 1890s women wore a separate skirt and a shirtwaist, a blouse cut like a man's shirt. Women who worked in offices and stores wore it every day. Sleeves puffed out wide again and were biggest around 1895. Most working men wore the sack suit, a loose jacket with pants.

About 50,000 people rode bicycles in 1885, and about 10 million in 1896, by one estimate. Some women rode in bloomers, but most wore shorter skirts.

Charles Dana Gibson lived from 1867 to 1944. He drew for *Life*, *Collier's Weekly*, *Harper's Weekly* and other magazines. In the 1890s he drew a tall, slim young woman who played sports and went to college. People called her the Gibson Girl. According to an exhibition of Gibson's drawings at the Library of Congress, from the 1890s until World War I she "set the standard for beauty, fashion, and manners."
