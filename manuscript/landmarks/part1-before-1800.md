## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The oldest landmarks in the United States were built by Native people, hundreds and even thousands of years before any European arrived. They piled up mounds of earth, and one of them covers more ground than the Great Pyramid in Egypt. In the Southwest they built stone houses with hundreds of rooms and set whole villages under cliffs.

No one wrote down the names of the people who built these places. In the Southwest, the builders' descendants, among them the Pueblo peoples, still live in the same region.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Monks Mound and the mound builders" -->
The largest structure made of earth in North and South America is Monks Mound, at Cahokia in what is now Illinois. A mound is a hill that people build by piling up earth. People built Monks Mound in stages, mostly between about the years 1050 and 1200. It stands about 100 feet tall, and its base covers about 14 acres. The base of the Great Pyramid of Giza, in Egypt, covers about 13 acres. The pyramid is far taller, so the mound beats it only in the ground it covers.

Poverty Point, in Louisiana, is much older. People built it between about 1700 BC and 1100 BC. BC means "before Christ," and it counts years backward from the year 1 of the calendar used today. The builders lived by hunting, fishing and gathering wild food. They raised five mounds and six curved ridges of earth, set one inside another around a wide open space in the middle. It is the largest earthwork of its age north of Mexico. An earthwork is anything people build by moving earth. UNESCO is an agency of the United Nations, and its World Heritage List names places chosen for their importance to all people. On June 22, 2014, members of UNESCO's World Heritage Committee, meeting in Doha, Qatar, added Poverty Point to that list. It was the 1,001st place on the list and the 22nd in the United States.

Serpent Mound, in Ohio, is a snake shaped out of earth, about a quarter of a mile long. It is the largest snake-shaped mound in the world. Nothing found inside it shows who built it. Archaeologists, scientists who study the past from the things people left behind, have tested it with radiocarbon dating. That method measures the age of material that was once alive. Tests done in 1991 gave a date of about the year 1070, which matches a people archaeologists call the Fort Ancient culture. Tests done in 2014 gave a date of about 300 BC, which matches a people they call the Adena culture. Archaeologists' later work has found evidence for both dates. They now think it possible that people built the mound in one period and rebuilt it in another.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone buildings of the Southwest" -->
In Chaco Canyon, in what is now New Mexico, people built large stone buildings called great houses between about 850 and 1150. The largest, Pueblo Bonito, rose four stories high. It held about 600 to 650 rooms, or more. The counts differ because archaeologists cannot count the rooms on the upper floors directly. Some counts include a careful guess at those rooms, and some do not.

At Mesa Verde, in what is now Colorado, people built Cliff Palace between about 1190 and 1280. They set it under an overhang, a place where the rock of the cliff sticks out above like a roof. Cliff Palace has about 150 rooms and 23 kivas. A kiva is a room used for religious ceremonies. It is the largest cliff dwelling in North America.

The builders' descendants are the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest. The National Park Service lists 26 tribes with a special tie to Mesa Verde. Among them are the Hopi Tribe, the 19 Pueblos of New Mexico, including Zuni, Taos and Acoma, and the Navajo Nation. The Ute Mountain Ute, the Southern Ute, the Northern Ute and the Jicarilla Apache are also on the list.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Hopewell earthworks of Ohio" -->
About 2,000 years ago, Native people in southern Ohio built long walls of earth in the shapes of circles and eight-sided figures. Archaeologists call these people the Hopewell culture. Hopewell is a name archaeologists gave them, and it is not the name of any nation. A National Park Service article states that they built the earthworks during a period of about 400 years. An article in the Smithsonian's *American Indian* magazine dates that period from about the year 1 to about the year 400. A report on WVXU, a public radio station in Cincinnati, gives a longer span, from 200 BC to the year 500.

The Octagon Earthworks, in Newark, Ohio, is a large circle of earth joined to an eight-sided wall. An octagon is a shape with eight sides. Together the two walls surround nearly 70 acres. The walls line up with the points where the moon rises, in a pattern that repeats every 18.6 years.

In 1910 the Moundbuilders Country Club began leasing the Octagon and used it as a golf course. To lease land is to rent it. The Ohio Historical Society has owned the land since 1933. It is a group set up under state law, and it is not run to make money. It is now called the Ohio History Connection, and for decades its leaders kept leasing the site to the club.

On September 19, 2023, the members of UNESCO's World Heritage Committee voted to add eight Hopewell earthwork sites to the World Heritage List. The Octagon was one of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="glenna-wallace" name="Glenna Wallace" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Glenna Wallace

> **Who:** Chief of the Eastern Shawnee Tribe of Oklahoma from 2006 to 2026, the first woman to be the tribe's chief.
> **When and where:** The Octagon Earthworks, Newark, Ohio, from the late 2000s to 2025.

Glenna Wallace worked for years to get a golf course off the Octagon Earthworks. She was born in about 1939 in Ottawa County, Oklahoma. She taught and worked at Crowder College in Neosho, Missouri, for 38 years. In 2006 the Eastern Shawnee elected her chief. She was the first woman to hold that job.

Her people once lived in western Ohio, near a place called Lewistown, on land they shared with a band of Seneca. On July 20, 1831, the band's leaders signed a treaty with James B. Gardiner, a commissioner, or special official, sent by the United States. A treaty is a written agreement between nations. In it the band gave up about 48 square miles of land near Lewistown, plus 8,960 acres of a reservation dating from 1818. A reservation is land set aside for a Native nation. In return Gardiner promised, for the United States, 60,000 acres west of the Mississippi River. Under the treaty, US officials had to move the band there. In 1832 US officials forced the people out of Ohio. A profile of Wallace by the Ohio History Connection states that they walked about 800 miles and that more than 15 percent of them died on the way. No record of the march names the officials who led it. In December 1832 US officials granted the band a 60,000-acre reservation in what is now Ottawa County, Oklahoma.

Wallace first visited the Octagon Earthworks in the late 2000s. An article in the Smithsonian's *American Indian* magazine gives the year as 2007, and other accounts give 2009. While she was there, a golfer shouted at her, "Out of the way! You don't belong here!" She decided to get the golf course off the earthworks and to win World Heritage status for them.

Leaders of the Ohio History Connection went to court to end the club's lease. They used eminent domain, a legal power to take property for public use as long as the owner is paid. On December 7, 2022, the justices of the Ohio Supreme Court ruled 5 to 1 that they could do it.

In 2023 Wallace gave the main speech in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, when the members of the World Heritage Committee voted to add the Hopewell earthworks to the list. In August 2024 the club and the Ohio History Connection settled the case. Wallace said, "At last. So many people have worked so long for this to become reality." The club ran the golf course until the end of 2024. The Octagon opened to the public on January 1, 2025. Wallace retired as chief in 2026, after 20 years.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Nothing that Europeans built here in the 1500s still stands. The Spanish built their first forts in Florida of wood and earth. Those forts burned many times, and the Spanish rebuilt them again and again. A mission was a settlement run by Catholic priests, where Native people lived and were taught the Catholic religion. Spanish mission buildings of this century, in Florida and in the Southwest, survive only as remains in the ground that archaeologists dig up. Native mounds and stone buildings of earlier centuries, such as Monks Mound and Cliff Palace, were still standing, and they still stand today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine's founding site" -->
St. Augustine, Florida, began in a Native town. On September 8, 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed at Seloy, a town of the Timucua people. The Spanish made their first settlement in Seloy's village. People have lived in St. Augustine without a break ever since. Of all the cities that Europeans founded on the mainland of the United States, it is the oldest that people never left.

In 1586 Francis Drake and his men burned the town. The only landmark left from this century is the place where the Spanish settled. Nothing they built there in the 1500s survives above ground. The stone fort that still stands in St. Augustine, about a mile south of Seloy, was begun in 1672.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1500s" -->

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Three buildings from the 1600s are among the oldest buildings put up by Europeans that still stand in the United States. One is a Spanish stone fort in Florida. Another is a chapel in Santa Fe, New Mexico, made of adobe, or sun-dried mud brick. The third is a wooden meetinghouse in Massachusetts. Spanish officials forced Native men from their towns to work on the fort in Florida, cutting its stone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A fort built of shell stone" -->
Spanish officials ordered a stone fort built at St. Augustine after pirates attacked the town. On the night of May 28, 1668, about a hundred English pirates came ashore at midnight and looted St. Augustine. They killed more than 50 Spaniards. The pirates said they would come back and take the town's wooden fort. Queen Mariana of Spain then named Manuel de Cendoya governor of Florida. She sent him to the viceroy of New Spain, the king's highest official in Mexico. There he was to collect 12,000 pesos, which were Spanish coins, to begin a fort of stone.

Workers broke ground on October 2, 1672. Cendoya laid the first foundation stone on November 9. The main work was finished in about 1687, under Governor Juan Cabrera. Governor Laureano de Torres had the last stones set on the fort's water defenses in 1695 or 1696. Two histories written for the National Park Service give different years. Albert Manucy, who wrote the first one in 1942, gives 1696, and a 1993 park handbook gives August 1695. By 1696 the fort had cost close to 100,000 pesos. It is called the Castillo de San Marcos, and it is the oldest masonry fort in the continental United States. Masonry means built of stone or brick.

The fort is made of coquina, a soft stone made of packed seashells. Workers cut the coquina from quarries on Anastasia Island, across the bay from the town. A quarry is a pit where stone is cut out of the ground. The workers cut grooves in the rock with picks and axes, then broke the blocks loose with bars and wedges. The largest blocks were 2 feet thick and 4 feet long. Manucy wrote that six strong men "could hardly lift" one of them. Oxen dragged the blocks to a wharf, and rafts carried them across the bay.

The stones are held together with mortar, a paste that hardens. Workers made lime for the mortar by burning oyster shells in kilns, or very hot ovens. By the spring of 1672 they had stored about 7,000 bushels of it. The walls they built are 14 feet thick at the base and about 9 feet thick at the top.

English soldiers attacked the fort twice and failed both times. In November 1702, Governor James Moore of Carolina came with an army, and Colonel Robert Daniel was at his side. Almost 1,500 people crowded into the fort for safety. The siege lasted about two months. A siege is an attack in which an army surrounds a place and tries to force the people inside to give up. The English cannonballs did not shatter the walls. The shell stone crushed inward where each ball hit, so the balls sank into the wall or bounced off.

When a Spanish force arrived to help, the English set fire to their own ships and retreated. As they left, Daniel's troops and their Native allies burned the rest of St. Augustine to the ground. The fort and the people inside it survived. In 1740 General James Oglethorpe of Georgia besieged the fort for 27 days, and he failed too. The fort is now the Castillo de San Marcos National Monument.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who cut the stone" -->
Spanish officials forced Native men to work on the Castillo. At times up to 300 Native people were working for the Spanish king in St. Augustine, on the fort and in the corn fields. The men came from three nations: the Guale of what is now Georgia, the Timucua of eastern Florida and the Apalache of western Florida. Officials drafted them for labor in their home towns. A draft is an order that forces people to serve. Some of the men had to walk about 200 miles to reach St. Augustine. Manucy wrote that "many of them served unwillingly."

A drafted man had to choose between bringing his family with him or leaving his wife and children behind in the village to feed themselves. Officials drafted even some chiefs. They kept men working past the end of their terms. One chief was forced to work on the fort for more than three years without once going home. Some governors used Native men as their own servants. Spanish officials had sentenced the Apalache, years before, to work on the forts as a punishment for rebelling.

The Native laborers were paid 1 real a day, plus a daily share of corn. A real was a Spanish silver coin. Manucy counted it as worth 12 and a half cents, and the 1993 park handbook puts it at about 20 cents. Spanish master masons on the same job earned 20 reales a day.

The work needed about 150 laborers, and officials found it hard to get even 100. In the first winter, a sickness the Spanish called El Contagio, "the contagion," spread among the Native laborers. The crew shrank to almost no one. The records do not count how many men died. The payrolls, the lists of workers and their pay, counted the Guale, Timucua and Apalache men who cut the stone. The payrolls give their nations and their numbers, but not their names.

Spanish officials also put convicts to work on the fort. These were men sentenced to work there for a set number of years. One Spaniard caught smuggling English goods got 6 years of labor on the fort. His term would double if he ran away. Enslaved Black people owned by the Spanish king worked there too. In 1687 Spanish officials added eighteen of the king's slaves to the work crew.

That same year, eleven people escaped slavery in Carolina and reached St. Augustine. They were eight men, two women and a nursing baby. Governor Diego de Quiroga y Losada gave them shelter. He had them baptized in the Catholic Church, a ceremony with water that makes a person a member of the church. He hired the men as paid workers, and six of them worked on the Castillo. In 1693 King Charles II of Spain ordered that enslaved people who escaped from the English colonies and became Catholic would be free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-collins-castillo" name="John Collins" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### John Collins

> **Who:** An English prisoner who burned lime and ran the stone quarry for the Castillo de San Marcos.
> **When and where:** St. Augustine, Florida, from 1670 for 19 years or more.

John Collins was an English mason, a builder in stone, whom the Spanish held as a prisoner and put to work on the Castillo. In 1670 a ship was sailing for the new English colony at Charleston, in Carolina. It landed by accident at the Spanish mission of Santa Catalina, near the Savannah River. The Spanish there captured two Englishmen from the ship, William Carr and John Rivers. A small boat came from Charleston to get them back, and Joseph Bailey and John Collins carried a message ashore. The Spanish held them too and sent all four men to St. Augustine as prisoners.

Governor Manuel de Cendoya put the prisoners to work on the fort. On the Spanish payroll, Collins's name appears as Juan Calens. According to Manucy's history, Collins could burn more oyster shells into lime in a week than Spanish workmen could in two. The lime went into the mortar that held the coquina blocks together. As a prisoner he was paid 8 reales a day. A master workman was owed 20.

Collins rose from master of the lime kilns to master of the quarry. Later he ran the dugout boats, the food stores and the convict workers. At last he held the job of pilot, guiding ships between St. Augustine and Charleston. Officials of the Spanish king recognized his work after he had spent 19 years or more in the town. The records do not say when he was born or died, or whether he ever went home.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-collins-castillo" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The oldest churches" -->
San Miguel Chapel in Santa Fe, New Mexico, is often called the oldest church in the country. Its site and its foundations are that old, but most of the walls standing today date from a rebuilding in 1710. The chapel is built of adobe, bricks made of mud and straw and dried in the sun. It was built around 1620 as a church for the soldiers, servants and laborers of the town's Spanish officials, and it was in use before 1626.

The chapel was partly destroyed in 1680, when the Pueblo people of northern New Mexico rose up against Spanish rule. Spanish officials had been collecting tribute, payments of goods, from the Pueblos. They had also stopped the Pueblo people from practicing their own religion. A religious leader named Popé planned the uprising from Taos. In the uprising, called the Pueblo Revolt, the Pueblo people drove the Spanish out of the region. The Spanish stayed out until 1692, when Diego de Vargas led them back. The records do not say who damaged the chapel. In 1710 Governor José Chacón had it rebuilt on the old foundations.

The Old Ship Meetinghouse in Hingham, Massachusetts, was built in 1681. The Puritans, a group of English Protestants who settled in New England, met in buildings like this one for church. It is the only Puritan meetinghouse from the 1600s still standing in the country. It is also the oldest church building in the United States that has been in use without a break. Its roof rests on a frame of heavy timbers, left open to view from inside. The frame reminded the people who worshiped there of the hull, or body, of a ship turned upside down, and the building was named for it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1700s, colonists put up some of the first public buildings that are still landmarks today. The first lighthouse in what became the United States was lit in 1716, at the mouth of Boston Harbor. Boston's Old North Church went up in 1723. In 1732 builders in Philadelphia began a statehouse for Pennsylvania, a building where the colony's lawmakers would meet. It is now called Independence Hall. In Texas, Spanish priests ran a mission, a settlement where Native people lived and were taught the Catholic religion. There, at the place later known as the Alamo, the first stone of a stone church was laid in 1744.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first lighthouse" -->
Boston Light stands on Little Brewster Island, at the entrance to Boston Harbor. A lighthouse is a tower with a bright light at the top that warns ships away from rocks and guides them into harbor. Boston Light was first lit on September 16, 1716. It was the first lighthouse in what became the United States.

British forces blew up the tower in 1776. It was rebuilt in 1783, and the 1783 tower is the one that stands today.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-worthylake" name="George Worthylake" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Worthylake

> **Who:** The first keeper of Boston Light, the first lighthouse in what became the United States.
> **When and where:** Little Brewster Island, Boston Harbor, 1716 to 1718.

George Worthylake became the first keeper of Boston Light in 1716. He was paid £50 a year, 50 British pounds, to keep the light burning from sundown to sunrise. Worthylake lived on the island with his wife Ann and his daughter Ruth. He earned extra money guiding ships in and out of the harbor, and he kept a flock of sheep on Great Brewster Island, next door.

On November 3, 1718, the family had gone into Boston for a church sermon. Just after noon, on the way back to the island, their canoe tipped over. Worthylake, Ann and Ruth all drowned. Shadwell, a man Worthylake enslaved, drowned with them. So did George Cutler, a servant, and John Edge, a friend of the family.

Benjamin Franklin was 12 years old and working for his older brother, a printer in Boston. His brother had him write a ballad about the drowning called "The Lighthouse Tragedy." A ballad is a poem that tells a story and can be sung. Once it was printed, Franklin's brother sent him around the town to sell copies. Franklin later called his own verses "wretched stuff," and no copy survives. A few days after the Worthylakes died, the next keeper, Robert Saunders, drowned too.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-worthylake" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A steeple and a statehouse" -->
Old North Church in Boston, also called Christ Church, was built in 1723. It is the oldest church building still standing in Boston. Its steeple, the tall pointed tower on top, was finished in 1740 and is 191 feet high. On April 18, 1775, the church's sexton, or caretaker, Robert Newman, and Captain John Pulling Jr. hung two lanterns in the steeple. The lanterns were a signal, worked out by Paul Revere, that British troops were moving by water.

Work on the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia began in 1732 and ended in 1753, 21 years later. A statehouse is the building where a colony's or a state's lawmakers meet. Andrew Hamilton designed it with Edmund Woolley, a master carpenter. Workers added its tower from 1750 to 1753. In 1753 a bell was hung in the new tower. Words on the bell read "Proclaim liberty throughout all the land." The building is now called Independence Hall.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The mission church later called the Alamo" -->
Father Antonio de San Buenaventura y Olivares, a Spanish priest, founded Mission San Antonio de Valero in San Antonio, Texas, on May 1, 1718. Soldiers sent by Governor Martín de Alarcón came with him. A mission was a settlement run by Catholic priests, where Native people lived and were taught the Catholic religion. The people who lived at this mission came from more than a hundred Native groups. Among them were the Payaya, the Pamaya, the Xarame, the Apache and the Karankawa. Many came to the San Antonio missions for protection from their enemies and for a steadier supply of food, because diseases brought by Europeans had already killed many of their people. As a rule, the Spanish forced no one to come in. Once people had joined a Texas mission, though, priests and soldiers used force to keep them. Soldiers went out to bring back people, and whole groups, who left. In 1739 an epidemic of smallpox and measles struck the mission. An epidemic is an outbreak of a disease that spreads to many people at the same time. In 1745 the mission had 311 residents.

The cornerstone of a stone church was laid on May 8, 1744. A cornerstone is the first stone set at a corner of a new building. That church fell down in the 1750s. Work on the church that stands today began in 1758. The missionaries never finished it. The bell towers and the dome they planned were never built. The records do not say who laid the church's stones.

In 1793 Spanish officials closed the mission and divided its lands among the Native people and other residents who were still there. The old mission church is known today as the Alamo.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Enslaved people worked at every stage of building the first buildings of the new national capital. Leaders of the United States began building the capital beside the Potomac River in the 1790s. Its two main buildings were the President's House and the Capitol. The officials in charge rented enslaved men from their owners, and the owners collected the pay. Two houses of the time that are landmarks today, Mount Vernon and Monticello, were also built by enslaved workers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Raising the capital's buildings" -->
The land chosen for the capital had been home to the Nacotchtank people. In 1608 the English captain John Smith traveled up the Potomac River and found their villages there. European settlers brought diseases that killed many of the Nacotchtank. In colonial times most of their groups joined other Native peoples, and some moved away.

The President's House, later called the White House, was designed by James Hoban, an architect born in Ireland. Its cornerstone was laid on October 13, 1792. A cornerstone is the first stone set at a corner of a new building, often in a ceremony. On September 18, 1793, George Washington laid the cornerstone of the Capitol, the building where Congress meets. Neither building was done by 1800. When John Adams moved into the President's House in November 1800, it was still unfinished. The Capitol had only one wing.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Enslaved men who built the capital" -->
Enslaved people worked at every stage of building the President's House and the Capitol. They quarried sandstone, cut timber, made bricks, sawed boards, did carpentry and hauled loads. On December 2, 1791, the three commissioners in charge of the new District of Columbia bought a sandstone quarry for $6,000. It was on Aquia Creek, in Stafford County, Virginia. A quarry is a pit where stone is cut out of the ground. At the Government Island quarry, forty Black men worked beside free workers.

William C. Allen, a historian in the office of the Architect of the Capitol, studied the Capitol's payrolls for Congress in 2005. His report states that the commissioners rented enslaved men from their owners by the year. They called this "Negro hire." The Capitol's accounts list 385 payments for it between February 11, 1795, and May 17, 1801. The money went to the owners. At first an owner got $60 a year for each man. A white laborer with no special skills earned $70 a year. In 1797 the commissioners raised the owners' rate to $70. When a man missed work, the commissioners cut his owner's pay.

Two of the commissioners, Gustavus Scott and William Thornton, rented their own slaves to the project. Thornton also designed the Capitol. James Hoban was paid for enslaved men too, and so was the commissioners' treasurer, William Deakins. Other owners who were paid included Middleton Belt, who oversaw the Capitol's laborers, and a land developer named Thomas Law. Many Maryland slaveholders rented out men too, and several of them were women, such as Ann Digges and five women of the Brent family.

The commissioners told the overseers to keep the rented men at work "from sunrise to sunset," six days a week. The commissioners housed the men in huts. They gave them rations, a set share of food and drink, of salt meat and whiskey. Allen found that the commissioners' overseers probably kept the enslaved sawyers, men who sawed logs into boards, at work seven days a week. For work at night, on Sundays and on holidays, the men themselves got small extra wages.

Only a few of the men's names were written down, and only their first names. The payments name Dick and Tom, rented from Ann Digges in 1795. They also name Nace, Gerrard, David, Charles, Harry, Gabe and Sil, rented from the Brent women in April 1795. Allen wrote that "virtually nothing is known of their lives."

In 1793 a man named Jacob ran away from the work, and his owner offered a $6 reward for him. The historian Bob Arnebeck counts at least 200 known enslaved laborers on the Capitol and the President's House together. Richard Baker, the Senate Historian, and Kenneth Kato of the House history office introduced Allen's report. They wrote that no one will ever know how many enslaved people helped to build the Capitol.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="white-house-enslaved-carpenters" name="Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel

> **Who:** Five enslaved carpenters who helped build the White House. They are known by name from the payroll for May 1795.
> **When and where:** The President's House building site, Washington, May 1795.

Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry and Daniel were enslaved carpenters who worked on the President's House in May 1795. The carpenters' payroll for that month lists them by their first names only. James Hoban, the architect of the building, held four of them in slavery. His assistant, Pierce Purcell, held the fifth.

The payroll records how many days the men worked that month. Peter and Ben each worked 28 days. Daniel worked 25 days, and Harry worked 9. Hoban signed for their wages. The pay went to the men's owners and not to the men. The payroll does not give their last names.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="white-house-enslaved-carpenters" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Landmark houses" -->
Mount Vernon, George Washington's home in Virginia, was built and enlarged by enslaved workers. Monticello, Thomas Jefferson's home in Virginia, was too. Jefferson began building Monticello in 1769 and left it unfinished in 1784. From 1796 to about 1809 he redesigned it and had it rebuilt. Enslaved workers did much of the building and made much of its fine woodwork.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-jefferson-landmarks" name="Thomas Jefferson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Jefferson

> **Who:** The owner of Monticello, who designed his own house and worked on the design for the Virginia State Capitol.
> **When and where:** Monticello, Virginia, 1769 to about 1809. The design for the Virginia State Capitol in Richmond, 1785.

Thomas Jefferson designed his own house at Monticello, and with a French draftsman he made the design for the Virginia State Capitol. He drew the plans for Monticello himself, and the house was under construction from 1769 to about 1809, a span of 40 years. The people he enslaved did much of the building.

In 1785 the Directors of Public Buildings of Virginia asked Jefferson for a design for a new state capitol in Richmond. Jefferson was then the United States minister to France, which means he represented the country to the French government. A French draftsman, Charles-Louis Clérisseau, persuaded him to model the outside of the building on the Maison Carrée. That is an ancient Roman temple in the city of Nîmes, in France. Clérisseau drew the final plans. A plaster model reached Richmond late in 1786 to guide the workmen, and Samuel Dobie built the capitol.

The Virginia General Assembly is the state's group of lawmakers. Its history of the building states that the capitol "introduced the Classical Revival temple style to the modern world." Classical Revival buildings copy the look of the temples of ancient Greece and Rome. The same history states that the capitol "has inspired countless public buildings for over 200 years."

At Monticello, the fine woodwork of the rebuilt rooms was made by enslaved craftsmen. One of them was John Hemmings, a joiner, or maker of fine woodwork.
