## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Several of the best-known structures in the country went up between 1913 and 1937. They include the Lincoln Memorial, Mount Rushmore, the Empire State Building, the Golden Gate Bridge and Hoover Dam. Workers died on most of these jobs. Five died on the Empire State Building and 11 on the Golden Gate Bridge. At Hoover Dam the official count is 96, and it leaves out 42 more deaths that the records blamed on pneumonia. Mount Rushmore was carved into a mountain in the Black Hills of South Dakota. Members of Congress took that land from the Sioux in 1877, breaking a treaty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Woolworth Building" -->
The Woolworth Building in New York was finished in 1913. At 792 feet, it was then the tallest building in the world. Frank W. Woolworth paid its whole cost of $13.5 million himself, in cash. He had made his fortune from stores that sold goods for five and ten cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Lincoln Memorial" -->
The Lincoln Memorial in Washington honors Abraham Lincoln. At its opening in 1922, a white Marine sent Black guests to a separate section, and Chief Justice William Howard Taft told the only Black speaker to cut his speech. Work began on February 12, 1914, Lincoln's 105th birthday. Joseph Blackburn broke the ground. He was a member of the Lincoln Memorial Commission, the group in charge of the building, and he had been an officer in the Confederate army. The architect Henry Bacon designed the building. The sculptor Daniel Chester French made the huge seated statue of Lincoln inside.

About 50,000 people came to the dedication on May 30, 1922. The crowd was segregated, which means Black people and white people were kept apart. The Black guests' section was roped off, and 21 of them left in protest.

Robert Russa Moton was the only Black speaker. A week before, on May 23, Taft sent him a telegram. Taft was the president of the Lincoln Memorial Commission. He wrote, "Shall have to ask you to cut five hundred words." Taft added, "I am sure you wish to avoid any insinuation of attempt to make the occasion one for propaganda." An insinuation is a hint. Moton made the cuts. One passage he removed said that the work Lincoln died for was "still unfinished."

In 1939 Marian Anderson, a famous African American singer, sang from the memorial's steps. Constitution Hall in Washington belonged to the Daughters of the American Revolution, and the group's rule allowed only white performers there. Eleanor Roosevelt, the president's wife, quit the group in protest. Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, got President Franklin D. Roosevelt's approval for a concert at the memorial. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson sang from its steps to about 75,000 people of all races.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mount Rushmore and the Black Hills" -->
Mount Rushmore is carved on land that US officials promised to the Sioux in a treaty and that members of Congress later took. The Lakota, one of the Sioux peoples, call the mountain Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers. It stands in the Black Hills of South Dakota.

In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, US officials set aside a large reservation for the Sioux, and the Black Hills were part of it. The treaty says the land was "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux. It also says no part of it could be given up unless three-fourths of the adult Sioux men signed.

On July 2, 1874, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led close to 1,000 soldiers and teamsters into the Black Hills. Lieutenant General Philip Sheridan had sent them. By the middle of August they had found gold. In August 1876, members of Congress passed a law that cut off the Sioux's food. Under it, officials would give the Sioux no more food rations until they gave up the Black Hills. The Sioux then depended on those rations to eat.

In September 1876 a group of officials led by George Manypenny brought the Sioux a paper that was already written. It gave up the Black Hills. Only about one in ten of the adult Sioux men signed it, far fewer than the three-fourths the treaty required. On February 28, 1877, members of Congress made that paper a law anyway. With it they took the Black Hills.

In 1980 the Supreme Court decided the case *United States v. Sioux Nation*. Justice Harry Blackmun wrote for the court that the taking had broken the treaty. The justices upheld an award of $17.1 million for the land, plus 5 percent interest for every year since 1877. In 1980 the total came to between about $102 million and $106 million. The Sioux nations have never taken the money. They say the Black Hills were never for sale. The money sits in a federal account whose balance is kept secret. Published estimates put it at more than $1 billion. In 2025 Frank Star Comes Out, president of the Oglala Sioux Tribe, said, "We will never sell out our holy lands, the Black Hills, to the United States for monetary compensation."

Workers carved the mountain from October 4, 1927, to October 31, 1941. The National Park Service counts nearly 400 men and women who worked there, among them drillers and blacksmiths. They blasted off about 90 percent of the rock with dynamite, about 450,000 tons in all. Then drillers made rows of holes close together, a method called honeycombing, and smoothed the faces with hand tools. Workers were lowered down the 500-foot face on steel cables, sitting in bosun chairs, which are seats hung from a cable. No worker died in the 14 years of carving. Today the memorial's staff consult with 21 Native nations connected to the site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gutzon-borglum" name="Gutzon Borglum" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gutzon Borglum

> **Who:** The sculptor who designed Mount Rushmore. Before that, he carved a Confederate memorial at Stone Mountain, Georgia, a project the Ku Klux Klan helped pay for.
> **When and where:** Stone Mountain, Georgia, from 1923. The Black Hills of South Dakota, 1927 to 1941.

Gutzon Borglum worked closely with the Ku Klux Klan before he carved Mount Rushmore. Klan members preached white supremacy, the belief that white people should rule over everyone else. They were also against Catholics, Jews and immigrants. The first Klan had been put down by federal officials in the early 1870s. On Thanksgiving night in 1915, William J. Simmons and 16 other men climbed Stone Mountain in Georgia. They burned a cross there and announced that the Klan was born again.

At Stone Mountain, Borglum designed a carving of three Confederate leaders on horseback, Robert E. Lee, Stonewall Jackson and Jefferson Davis. Klan money helped pay for it. Work began in 1923, and Borglum unveiled the head of Lee in 1924. Historians have found that Borglum went to Klan meetings and took a deep part in Klan politics. No record proves he was a member.

Borglum quarreled with the businessmen who ran the Stone Mountain project, and they fired him. He then destroyed his models so no one else could use his design. That made the directors angry. A warrant was issued for his arrest, and he fled Georgia.

In 1927 Borglum began Mount Rushmore. He directed the carving for 14 years. He died in Chicago on March 6, 1941, after surgery, a few days before his 74th birthday. His son, Lincoln Borglum, finished the work that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gutzon-borglum" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nick-clifford" name="Nick Clifford" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nick Clifford

> **Who:** The youngest worker on Mount Rushmore, and the last of its carvers to die.
> **When and where:** Mount Rushmore, South Dakota, 1938 to 1940. Died in 2019, at 98.

Donald "Nick" Clifford was 17 when he was hired at Mount Rushmore in 1938. He was the youngest worker on the job. The Borglums hired him partly because they wanted him for the crew's baseball team, where he pitched.

Clifford earned 55 cents an hour. He ran the winch, a machine that winds a cable, which carried men up the face of the mountain. He also drilled holes for the dynamite. He worked there until 1940.

Clifford lived longer than any other carver. He died on November 23, 2019, at the age of 98.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nick-clifford" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Empire State Building and the Mohawk ironworkers" -->
The Empire State Building in New York opened on May 1, 1931. It took about 410 days from the first steel to the opening. Up to 3,400 workers were on the job each day. During the fastest stretch the steel frame rose about four and a half floors a week. The building has 102 stories and stands 1,250 feet tall, or 1,454 feet to the tip of today's antenna. It was the tallest building in the world until 1971, when the World Trade Center passed it.

Five workers died building it. That is the official count. A story that hundreds died is false. The photographer Lewis Hine took pictures of the men working on the steel for his book *Men at Work*.

Some of the riveting crews were Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake, a Mohawk community in Canada. Ironworkers put up the steel frames of buildings and bridges. Kahnawake men had done this work since the 1880s.

Many Kahnawake men had died on an earlier job, in 1907. On August 29 of that year, the south half of the Quebec Bridge fell while it was being built across the St. Lawrence River in Canada. The Canadian Encyclopedia counts 75 workers killed. *The Eastern Door*, the Kahnawake newspaper, counts 76. Thirty-three of the dead were Kahnawake men. A Royal Commission, a group the Canadian government named to find the cause, blamed two engineers. Theodore Cooper, the consulting engineer, had made mistakes in the design and had lengthened the bridge to save money. John Deans, the chief engineer, kept the crews working after Cooper warned of danger.

After 1907, the women of Kahnawake made sure that the men of one family did not all work on the same job at the same time. *The Eastern Door* gives the rule this way: "If a lady had seven sons, they're not all going to the same job." Crosses made from the bridge's steel stand in the village cemetery. In 2007 a monument went up with a picture of each man who was killed and a 40-foot steel model of the bridge.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Golden Gate Bridge" -->
The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco opened in May 1937. On May 27, the first day, about 200,000 people walked across it. Eleven workers died building it. One died in October 1936. Ten died on February 17, 1937, when a falling work platform tore through the safety net under the bridge.

Joseph Strauss was the chief engineer. The structural engineer Charles Ellis did the detailed design and thousands of calculations from 1929 to 1931. Strauss fired Ellis before building began, and Ellis got no public credit for decades. In 2007 a report by the Golden Gate Bridge District, which runs the bridge, gave Ellis the main credit for the design.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-strauss" name="Joseph Strauss" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Strauss

> **Who:** The chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge, who put a safety net under the whole job.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in May 1937.

Joseph Strauss required every worker on the Golden Gate Bridge to wear a hard hat. He also spent about $130,000 on a safety net that hung under the bridge while it was built. It was the first large safety net used on an American bridge job.

The net caught 19 men who fell. They called themselves the Halfway to Hell Club.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-strauss" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="al-zampa" name="Al Zampa" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Al Zampa

> **Who:** An ironworker who fell from the Golden Gate Bridge and lived. A bridge in California is named for him.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, October 19, 1936. Born in Crockett, California.

Alfred Zampa was an ironworker from Crockett, California. On October 19, 1936, he slipped on wet steel and fell into the safety net. He was near the shore, where the net sagged down onto the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back.

Zampa spent twelve weeks in the hospital and two years in a back brace. Then he went back to ironwork. He was one of the first members of the Halfway to Hell Club and later spoke for it.

In 2003 members of the California legislature named the new bridge over the Carquinez Strait the Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="al-zampa" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoover Dam" -->
By the official count, 96 men died building Hoover Dam. The real number is higher and not known. The Bureau of Reclamation, the federal agency in charge of the dam, made that count. It covers deaths from falls, rock slides, machinery and drowning. It leaves out deaths from heat, heart trouble and pneumonia, a sickness of the lungs.

The main builder was a company called Six Companies Inc. The records list 42 more workers who died of pneumonia. Workers said these men really died of carbon monoxide poisoning, and that Six Companies called the deaths pneumonia to avoid paying money to the families. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. The workers blamed the gasoline trucks and machines running inside the diversion tunnels, which were dug to carry the river around the building site. The tunnels often reached 140 degrees and filled with thick exhaust. In nearby Boulder City, no one who did not work on the dam was recorded as dying of pneumonia in those years.

The first death on the official list came on December 20, 1922. J. G. Tierney drowned during the surveys for the dam. The last came on December 20, 1935. His son, Patrick Tierney, an electrician's helper, fell from one of the dam's intake towers.

A story says that men are buried inside the dam's concrete. It is false. Workers poured the dam as many separate blocks, in shallow layers, with crews standing over each pour. A body could not have been left inside. President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated the dam on September 30, 1935. Its generators began making electricity in 1936.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Vietnam Veterans Memorial, dedicated in Washington in 1982, is a black stone wall set into the ground. It carries the names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War. Earlier, in the 1960s and 1970s, builders put up a steel arch in St. Louis and two towers in New York, one of which was briefly the tallest building in the world. While the arch was going up, Black workers were kept off the job, and two men climbed it in protest. Workers also restored older landmarks, including the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Space Needle" -->
The Space Needle in Seattle was built in about 400 days for a world's fair called the Century 21 Exposition. It opened on April 21, 1962, the first day of the fair. The tower is 605 feet tall, with a restaurant on top that turns around.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gateway Arch" -->
The Gateway Arch in St. Louis is 630 feet tall and 630 feet wide, and its outside is stainless steel. The architect Eero Saarinen won the contest to design it, and the winner was announced in February 1948. Saarinen died in 1961, before building began. Workers built the arch from February 12, 1963, to October 28, 1965. Insurance companies had predicted 13 deaths, and no worker died.

Black workers were kept out of the construction jobs on the arch, a federal monument. The accounts of the protest do not name who did the hiring. On July 14, 1964, two activists, Percy Green and Richard Daly, climbed 125 feet up a construction ladder on the unfinished north leg of the arch to protest. The Gateway Arch Park Foundation says they stayed up for 6 hours. St. Louis Public Radio says Green was up there for more than four hours. Both men were arrested. Green later helped start a St. Louis group called ACTION, which fought for fair hiring.

In 1966 two Black plumbers, Elijah Smith and Oliver Parker, were hired to work on bathrooms in the arch's visitor center. White workers walked off the job in protest. Lawyers in the US Justice Department then filed a lawsuit under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Title VII is the part of that law that forbids treating workers unfairly because of their race. It was the department's first lawsuit under Title VII charging a "pattern or practice," which means unfair treatment by race as a regular habit. The unions of the St. Louis building trades signed an agreement to end all their practices that treated Black workers unfairly.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harold-mccomber" name="Harold McComber" movie="High Steel (1965)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harold McComber

> **Who:** A Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake who built skyscrapers in New York.
> **When and where:** Manhattan and Kahnawake, filmed in 1965.
> **Movie:** *High Steel* (1965), a short documentary by the National Film Board of Canada, directed by Don Owen. It is about McComber's working life.

Harold McComber was a Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake. Like many Kahnawake men, he worked on the steel frames of skyscrapers in Manhattan. In 1965 the director Don Owen made a documentary film about his working life, called *High Steel*. The National Film Board of Canada produced it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harold-mccomber" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The World Trade Center" -->
The World Trade Center in New York had its ribbon-cutting on April 4, 1973. The architect Minoru Yamasaki designed its two towers, each 110 stories tall. The North Tower stood 1,368 feet, which made it the tallest building in the world. It held that title for only a few weeks. Then the steel frame of the Sears Tower in Chicago rose higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philippe-petit" name="Philippe Petit" movie="Man on Wire (2008)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Philippe Petit

> **Who:** A French high-wire walker who walked on a cable between the two towers of the World Trade Center.
> **When and where:** New York, August 7, 1974.
> **Movie:** *Man on Wire* (2008), directed by James Marsh. It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about the walk.

On August 7, 1974, Philippe Petit walked on a wire strung between the tops of the Twin Towers, about 1,350 feet above the ground. He and his friends had smuggled a cable up to the roofs and strung it from one tower to the other. Petit crossed eight times in 45 minutes.

Afterward he was arrested. The charges against him were dropped. In return, he put on a show for children in Central Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philippe-petit" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Vietnam Veterans Memorial" -->
The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington is two long walls of polished black granite set into the ground of the National Mall. Cut into the stone are the names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War, in the order they died. It was dedicated on November 13, 1982, and it became the most visited memorial in Washington.

Critics attacked the design before it was built. One veteran called it "a black gash of shame." Another critic said that burying the dead "in a black stone sarcophagus," a stone coffin, "is like spitting on their graves." Patrick Buchanan and Congressman Henry Hyde led the opposition. The members of the US Commission of Fine Arts, a federal board that reviews designs in Washington, kept the wall. They also added a statue of three soldiers by Frederick Hart nearby, in 1984. Hart's design had won third place in the contest for the memorial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maya-lin" name="Maya Lin" movie="Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maya Lin

> **Who:** The 21-year-old college student whose design won the contest for the Vietnam Veterans Memorial.
> **When and where:** Yale University and Washington, 1981 to 1982.
> **Movie:** *Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision* (1994). It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about Lin.

Maya Lin was a 21-year-old architecture student at Yale University when she won the contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial. She had first drawn the design for a class. She was born in Ohio, where her parents were professors. They had escaped from mainland China as young adults.

The contest was the biggest design competition held in the United States up to then. The judges looked at the entries without knowing who had made them. They chose entry number 1026 over 1,420 others. In May 1981 officials came to Lin's college room to tell her she had won the $20,000 first prize.

People who attacked the wall used Lin's age and her Chinese family background as reasons to change the design or drop it. At the dedication in 1982, no one spoke her name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maya-lin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Restoring the old landmarks" -->
From 1984 to 1986, workers restored the Statue of Liberty for its 100th birthday. They took down its original 1886 torch in 1984. It is now in the statue's museum. In its place they put a copy made of copper and covered in gold leaf, thin sheets of gold. The statue was dedicated again on July 4, 1986.

On May 11, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed Proclamation 3656, which made Ellis Island part of the Statue of Liberty National Monument. Ellis Island's main building had opened in 1900. Workers restored it, and in 1990 it opened as a museum of immigration.

On the West Coast, Angel Island's barracks had held immigrants from 1910. In 1970 more than 200 poems were found carved into the barracks walls by Chinese immigrants who had been held there. After the poems were found, people campaigned to save the barracks. In 1997 Angel Island Immigration Station was named a National Historic Landmark.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="landmarks" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have built memorials to people who were killed or enslaved: the victims of the September 11 attacks, the Black Americans murdered by lynch mobs, and the people held in slavery. In the same years, officials and work crews took down hundreds of monuments to the Confederacy, the Southern states that fought to keep slavery in the Civil War. In April 2025 the Southern Poverty Law Center, an advocacy group, counted 415 Confederate memorials removed, moved or renamed since 2015. By its count, 2,086 were still standing. In 2025 federal officials began putting some of the removed monuments back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ground Zero rebuilt" -->
The National September 11 Memorial in New York stands where the Twin Towers stood. On September 11, 2001, hijackers took over four airliners. They crashed two of them into the North Tower and the South Tower, and fires from the crashes made both towers collapse. The attacks killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 hijackers, by the count of the 9/11 Memorial and Museum.

The memorial opened on September 11, 2011, ten years after the attacks. It has two pools set into the footprints of the two towers. Each pool covers nearly an acre. Water falls 30 feet into each pool and then drops another 20 feet. Around the edges, bronze panels carry the names of 2,983 people. They are the people killed on September 11, 2001, and the six people killed in an earlier bombing of the towers in 1993.

The design, called *Reflecting Absence*, won a contest in 2004 against 5,201 entries from 63 countries. The museum under the memorial opened on May 21, 2014. On November 3, 2014, One World Trade Center opened nearby. It stands 1,776 feet to the tip of its spire. The height was chosen to match 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="michael-arad" name="Michael Arad" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Michael Arad

> **Who:** The architect who designed the National September 11 Memorial.
> **When and where:** New York. He won the design contest in 2004, and the memorial opened in 2011.

Michael Arad was 34 years old and working as an architect for a New York City government agency when he entered the contest for the memorial. In his first sketches, he pictured two empty spaces cut into the surface of the Hudson River. His finished design put two pools where the towers had stood.

Arad worked with the landscape architect Peter Walker, who plans outdoor spaces such as parks. Their entry was chosen from 5,201 in 2004.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="michael-arad" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New memorials in Washington" -->
In Washington, new memorials were built for Black Americans, among them the enslaved workers who helped build the Capitol. On February 28, 2012, members of Congress unveiled a marker in Emancipation Hall, the great hall of the Capitol Visitor Center. Emancipation means freeing people from slavery. The marker is a block of Aquia Creek sandstone taken from the Capitol's East Front, which was built from 1824 to 1826. Its plaque says the stone "was quarried by laborers, including enslaved African Americans, and commemorates their important role in building the Capitol."

The Martin Luther King, Jr. Memorial was dedicated on October 16, 2011. It was the first memorial to an African American in the area of the National Mall. At its center is a 30-foot carving called the Stone of Hope.

The National Museum of African American History and Culture opened on September 24, 2016, beside the Washington Monument. It is part of the Smithsonian. The architect David Adjaye led its design. The outside of the building is a bronze-colored shape in three tiers, called the corona, which draws on the art of the Yoruba, an African people. The pattern of its metal screen copies ironwork made by enslaved and free Black craftsmen in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Memorials to lynching and slavery" -->
The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, in Montgomery, Alabama, is a memorial to Black people who were lynched. Lynching is a killing by a mob, without a trial. The lawyer Bryan Stevenson founded the Equal Justice Initiative (EJI), a legal group that does not make a profit. EJI built the memorial and opened it on April 26, 2018, with a museum called the Legacy Museum.

The memorial holds 805 steel blocks, each six feet tall, hanging from above. Each one stands for a county in the United States where EJI found a record of a lynching. EJI's study *Lynching in America* counts almost 4,400 lynchings of Black people from 1877 to 1950. A copy of each county's block stands at the site. EJI offers each copy to groups in that county to set up at home. A team called MASS Design Group designed the memorial, and it cost about $20 million, paid by private foundations.

In March 2024 EJI opened the Freedom Monument Sculpture Park nearby, on 17 acres with nearly 50 sculptures. Its National Monument to Freedom carries 122,000 last names that formerly enslaved people recorded in the 1870 census. That census was the first time people who had been enslaved could claim a last name that the government would recognize. CBS News reported in 2024 that the monument stands 43 feet tall.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Confederate monuments coming down and going back up" -->
In 2018 the Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) reported that members of the United Daughters of the Confederacy had put up hundreds of Confederate statues at courthouses and other public places since the Civil War. The SPLC found two main periods of building. The first began around 1900, while Southern states were passing Jim Crow laws, the laws that kept Black and white people apart and took away Black people's rights. The second ran from the early 1950s through the 1960s, when segregationists, people who wanted to keep the races apart, fought the civil rights movement.

Hundreds of these monuments came down after three killings. On June 17, 2015, a 21-year-old white man named Dylann Roof sat in on a Bible study at the Emanuel A.M.E. Church in Charleston, South Carolina. After about an hour he opened fire and killed nine Black worshippers. Roof had posted pictures of himself with Confederate flags on his website.

In Charlottesville, Virginia, Paul Goodloe McIntire paid for a statue of the Confederate general Robert E. Lee, and it was dedicated in 1924. On February 6, 2017, the city council voted 3 to 2 to remove it. White supremacists then organized a rally called Unite the Right around the statue. On August 12, 2017, James Alex Fields Jr. drove his car into a crowd of people protesting against the rally. He killed Heather Heyer, a 32-year-old paralegal, and injured 19 others. City workers took the statue down on July 10, 2021. The city gave it to the Jefferson School African American Heritage Center, and in October 2023 the statue was melted down at a foundry.

On May 25, 2020, a Minneapolis police officer murdered George Floyd, and protests followed. The SPLC counted at least 168 Confederate symbols removed in 2020 alone. In June 2020 Virginia Governor Ralph Northam ordered the removal of Richmond's Lee statue, a 12-ton monument put up in 1890. After the Supreme Court of Virginia cleared the way, a crew led by the contractor Devon Henry took it down on September 8, 2021, ABC News reported that day.

Of the 2,086 Confederate memorials that the SPLC counted still standing in public places in April 2025, 685 were monuments. The SPLC's Rivka Maizlish called them "the results of a mass propaganda campaign."

In 2025 federal officials began putting some monuments back. In June 2020, protesters had pulled down a statue of the Confederate general Albert Pike in Washington and set it on fire. In October 2025 National Park Service workers put it back up. They cited a historic preservation law and an executive order from President Donald Trump on restoring removed monuments.

The Confederate Memorial at Arlington National Cemetery was put up in 1914 by the United Daughters of the Confederacy. The sculptor, Moses Ezekiel, had fought for the Confederacy. The memorial shows "an enslaved man following his owner to war." It also shows an enslaved woman as a "mammy," a name white Southerners used for enslaved women made to care for white children, holding a white officer's child. A commission set up by Congress found that it gave "highly sanitized depictions of slavery," pictures of slavery with the cruelty cleaned out. On that commission's advice, the memorial was taken down in December 2023. In August 2025 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced that it would return in 2027, after repairs costing about $10 million, according to the newspaper *Stars and Stripes*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Exhibits about slavery at the President's House" -->
In 2026, National Park Service workers took down an exhibit about slavery in Philadelphia, and a federal judge ordered it put back. The President's House site marks where George Washington lived while he was president. Its exhibit panels told the lives of nine people Washington held in slavery there. The workers removed the panels in January 2026, following President Trump's executive order called "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." The City of Philadelphia sued. On February 16, 2026, US District Judge Cynthia Rufe ordered the panels restored while the case went on, and workers began putting them back three days later. The administration appealed. By July 2026, judges of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals had upheld the administration's power to replace the exhibit.

After the Osage Nation got back the rest of Sugarloaf Mound in 2025, Andrea Hunter, the Osage Nation's Historic Preservation Officer, said, "It has been a long 17-year journey, and I am thrilled to finally see the entire mound come under Osage Nation control." She said her office must now "remove all the structures from the properties and stabilize the mound." Osage leaders plan to build a center there to teach visitors about the mound.

In 2009 workers finished restoring the barracks of the Angel Island Immigration Station in California. On February 22, 2018, President Donald Trump signed a law that renamed the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial in St. Louis, the grounds of the Gateway Arch, as Gateway Arch National Park. It is the smallest national park in the country.
