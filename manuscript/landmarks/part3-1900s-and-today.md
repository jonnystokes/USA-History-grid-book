## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Many large structures in the country went up between 1913 and 1937. Among them were the Lincoln Memorial, Mount Rushmore, the Empire State Building, the Golden Gate Bridge and Hoover Dam. Workers died on most of these jobs. Five died on the Empire State Building and 11 on the Golden Gate Bridge. At Hoover Dam the official count is 96. It does not include 42 more deaths that were listed as pneumonia. Mount Rushmore was carved into a mountain in the Black Hills of South Dakota. Members of Congress took that land from the Sioux in 1877, breaking a treaty, a written agreement between nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Woolworth Building" -->
The Woolworth Building in New York was finished in 1913. At 792 feet, it was then the tallest building in the world. Frank W. Woolworth paid its whole cost of $13.5 million himself, in cash. He had made his fortune from stores that sold goods for five and ten cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Lincoln Memorial" -->
The Lincoln Memorial in Washington honors Abraham Lincoln. At its opening in 1922, Black guests were made to sit apart from white guests. The only Black speaker was told to cut his speech. Work began on February 12, 1914, Lincoln's 105th birthday. Joseph Blackburn broke the ground. He was a member of the Lincoln Memorial Commission, the group in charge of the building. He had also been an officer in the Confederate army, the army of the Southern states that fought to keep slavery in the Civil War. The architect Henry Bacon designed the building. The sculptor Daniel Chester French made the seated statue of Lincoln inside.

About 50,000 people came to the dedication on May 30, 1922. The crowd was segregated, which means Black people and white people were kept apart. A white Marine harshly ordered the Black guests into a section roped off from the rest. Twenty-one of them left in protest.

Robert Russa Moton was the only Black speaker. A week before, on May 23, William Howard Taft sent him a telegram. Taft was the Chief Justice, the head of the US Supreme Court, and he was also the president of the Lincoln Memorial Commission. He wrote, "Shall have to ask you to cut five hundred words." Taft added, "I am sure you wish to avoid any insinuation of attempt to make the occasion one for propaganda." An insinuation is a hint. Propaganda means messages made to push people toward one side. Moton made the cuts. One passage he removed said that the work Lincoln died for was "still unfinished."

In 1939 Marian Anderson, a famous African American singer, sang from the memorial's steps. Constitution Hall in Washington belonged to the Daughters of the American Revolution, and the group's rule allowed only white performers there. Eleanor Roosevelt, the president's wife, quit the group in protest. Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, got President Franklin D. Roosevelt's approval for a concert at the memorial. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson sang from its steps to about 75,000 people of all races.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mount Rushmore and the Black Hills" -->
Mount Rushmore is carved on land that US officials promised to the Sioux in a treaty and that members of Congress later took. The Lakota, one of the Sioux peoples, call the mountain Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers. It stands in the Black Hills of South Dakota.

In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, US officials set aside a large reservation for the Sioux, and the Black Hills were part of it. The treaty says the land was "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux. It also says no part of it could be given up unless three-fourths of the adult Sioux men signed.

On July 2, 1874, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led close to 1,000 soldiers and teamsters, men who drove wagons, into the Black Hills. Lieutenant General Philip Sheridan had sent them. By the middle of August they had found gold. In August 1876, members of Congress passed a law saying no more money would be spent on food for the Sioux until they gave up the Black Hills. Under it, officials would give the Sioux no more food rations. The Sioux then depended on those rations to eat.

In September 1876 a group of officials led by George Manypenny brought the Sioux a paper that was already written. It gave up the Black Hills. Only about one in ten of the adult Sioux men signed it, far fewer than the three-fourths the treaty required. On February 28, 1877, members of Congress made that paper a law anyway. With it they took the Black Hills.

In 1980 the justices of the Supreme Court decided the case *United States v. Sioux Nation*. Justice Harry Blackmun wrote for the court that the taking had broken the treaty. The justices agreed with an earlier award of $17.1 million for the land, plus 5 percent interest for every year since 1877. In 1980 the total came to between about $102 million and $106 million.

The Sioux nations have never taken the money. They say the Black Hills were never for sale. The money sits in a federal account held by the US Interior Department, and officials keep its balance sealed. Published estimates put it at more than $1 billion. In 2025 Frank Star Comes Out, president of the Oglala Sioux Tribe, said, "We will never sell out our holy lands, the Black Hills, to the United States for monetary compensation."

Workers carved the mountain from October 4, 1927, to October 31, 1941. The National Park Service counts nearly 400 men and women who worked there, among them drillers and blacksmiths. They did about 90 percent of the carving with dynamite. In all, they removed about 450,000 tons of rock. Then drillers made rows of holes close together, a method called honeycombing, and smoothed the faces with hand tools. Workers were lowered down the 500-foot face on steel cables, sitting in bosun chairs, which are seats hung from a cable. No worker died in the 14 years of carving. Today the memorial's staff consult with 21 Native nations connected to the site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gutzon-borglum" name="Gutzon Borglum" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gutzon Borglum

> **Who:** The sculptor who designed Mount Rushmore. Before that, he carved a Confederate memorial at Stone Mountain, Georgia, a project the Ku Klux Klan helped pay for.
> **When and where:** Stone Mountain, Georgia, from 1923. The Black Hills of South Dakota, 1927 to 1941.

Before he carved Mount Rushmore, Gutzon Borglum went to meetings of the Ku Klux Klan and took a deep part in Klan politics, according to reports in the Washington Post and Smithsonian magazine. No record proves he was a member. Confederate veterans had started the first Klan in Pulaski, Tennessee, in December 1865. Its members whipped, burned the homes of and murdered freed Black people and the people who helped them. Federal officials put that Klan down in the early 1870s.

On Thanksgiving night in 1915, William J. Simmons and 16 other men climbed Stone Mountain in Georgia. They burned a cross there and announced that the Klan was born again. Members of the new Klan preached white supremacy, the belief that white people should rule over everyone else. They were also against Catholics, Jews and immigrants.

At Stone Mountain, Borglum designed a carving of three Confederate leaders on horseback, Robert E. Lee, Stonewall Jackson and Jefferson Davis. Klan money helped pay for it. Work began in 1923, and Borglum unveiled the head of Lee in 1924.

Borglum quarreled with the businessmen who ran the Stone Mountain project, and they fired him. He then destroyed his models to protect his design. That made the directors angry. A warrant, a court paper ordering his arrest, was issued, and he fled Georgia.

In 1927 Borglum began Mount Rushmore. He directed the carving from then until he died in 1941. He died in Chicago on March 6, 1941, after surgery, a few days before his 74th birthday. His son, Lincoln Borglum, finished the work that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gutzon-borglum" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nick-clifford" name="Nick Clifford" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nick Clifford

> **Who:** The youngest worker on Mount Rushmore, and the last of its carvers to die.
> **When and where:** Mount Rushmore, South Dakota, 1938 to 1940. Died in 2019, at 98.

Donald "Nick" Clifford was 17 when he was hired at Mount Rushmore in 1938. He was the youngest worker on the job. The Borglums hired him partly because they wanted him for the crew's baseball team, where he pitched.

Clifford earned 55 cents an hour. He ran the winch, a machine that winds a cable, which carried men up the face of the mountain. He also drilled holes for the dynamite. He worked there until 1940.

Clifford was the last of the carvers to die. He died on November 23, 2019, at the age of 98.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nick-clifford" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Empire State Building and the Mohawk ironworkers" -->
The Empire State Building in New York opened on May 1, 1931. It took about 410 days from the first steel to the opening. Up to 3,400 workers were on the job each day. During the fastest stretch the steel frame rose about four and a half floors a week. The building has 102 stories and stands 1,250 feet tall, or 1,454 feet to the tip of today's antenna. It was the tallest building in the world until 1970, when the steel frame of the World Trade Center's North Tower rose higher.

Five workers died building it. That is the official count. A story that hundreds died is false. The photographer Lewis Hine took pictures of the men working on the steel for his book *Men at Work*.

Some of the riveting crews were Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake, a Mohawk community in Canada. Riveters joined steel beams with rivets, hot metal pins that lock the beams together. Ironworkers put up the steel frames of buildings and bridges. Kahnawake men had done this work since the 1880s.

Kahnawake ironworkers had died on an earlier job, in 1907. On August 29 of that year, the south half of the Quebec Bridge fell while it was being built across the St. Lawrence River in Canada. The Canadian Encyclopedia counts 75 workers killed. *The Eastern Door*, the Kahnawake newspaper, counts 76. Thirty-three of the dead were Kahnawake men. A Royal Commission, a group the Canadian government named to find the cause, blamed two engineers. Theodore Cooper, the consulting engineer, an expert brought in to oversee the design, had made mistakes in the design and had lengthened the bridge to save money. John Deans, the chief engineer, kept the crews working after Cooper warned of danger.

After 1907, the women of Kahnawake made sure that the men of one family did not all work on the same job at the same time. *The Eastern Door* gives the rule this way: "If a lady had seven sons, they're not all going to the same job." Crosses made from the bridge's steel stand in the village cemetery. In 2007 a monument went up with a picture of each man who was killed and a 40-foot steel model of the bridge.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Golden Gate Bridge" -->
The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco opened in May 1937. On May 27, the first day, about 200,000 people walked across it. Eleven workers died building it. One died in October 1936. Ten died on February 17, 1937, when a falling work platform tore through the safety net under the bridge.

Joseph Strauss was the chief engineer. The structural engineer Charles Ellis did the detailed design and thousands of calculations from 1929 to 1931. Strauss fired Ellis before building began, and Ellis got no public credit for decades. In a 2007 report, officials of the Golden Gate Bridge District gave Ellis the main credit for the design.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-strauss" name="Joseph Strauss" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Strauss

> **Who:** The chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge, who hung a safety net under the bridge while it was built.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in May 1937.

Joseph Strauss required hard hats on the Golden Gate Bridge job. He also spent about $130,000 on a safety net that hung under the bridge while it was built. It was the first large safety net used on an American bridge job.

The net caught 19 men who fell. They called themselves the Halfway to Hell Club.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-strauss" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="al-zampa" name="Al Zampa" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Al Zampa

> **Who:** An ironworker who fell from the Golden Gate Bridge and lived. A bridge in California is named for him.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, October 19, 1936. Born in Crockett, California.

Alfred Zampa was an ironworker from Crockett, California. On October 19, 1936, he slipped on wet steel and fell into the safety net. He was near the shore, where the net sagged down onto the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back.

Zampa spent twelve weeks in the hospital and two years in a back brace. Then he went back to ironwork. He was one of the first members of the Halfway to Hell Club and later its unofficial spokesman, the man who spoke for the club without holding any title.

In 2003 California lawmakers named the new bridge over the Carquinez Strait the Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="al-zampa" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Hoover Dam" -->
By the official count, 96 men died building Hoover Dam. The count comes from the Bureau of Reclamation, the federal agency that runs the dam. It covers deaths from falls, rock slides, machinery and drowning. It leaves out deaths from heat, heart trouble and pneumonia, a sickness of the lungs. The exact number of deaths is not known.

The main builder was a company called Six Companies Inc. The records list 42 more workers who died of pneumonia. Workers said these men really died of carbon monoxide poisoning. They said Six Companies called the deaths pneumonia to avoid paying compensation claims, the money owed when a worker is hurt or killed on the job. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. The workers blamed the gasoline-powered trucks running inside the diversion tunnels, which were dug to carry the river around the building site. The tunnels often reached 140 degrees Fahrenheit and filled with thick exhaust. In nearby Boulder City, no one who did not work on the dam was recorded as dying of pneumonia in those years.

The first deaths on the official list came in 1922, during the surveys for the dam. On December 20 of that year J. G. Tierney drowned, and Harold Connelly also died that year. The last death on the list came 13 years to the day after Tierney's. On December 20, 1935, his son, Patrick Tierney, an electrician's helper, fell from one of the dam's intake towers.

A story says that men are buried inside the dam's concrete. It is false. Workers poured the dam as many separate blocks, in shallow layers, with crews standing over each pour. A body could not have been left inside. President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated the dam on September 30, 1935. Its generators began making electricity in 1936.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Vietnam Veterans Memorial, dedicated in Washington in 1982, is a black stone wall set into the ground. The names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War are cut into it. Earlier, in the 1960s and 1970s, builders put up a steel arch in St. Louis and two towers in New York, one of which was for a few years the tallest building in the world. While the arch was going up, Black workers were kept out of the building trades unions and so off the job, Justice Department lawyers later charged in court. Two men climbed the arch in protest. Workers also restored older landmarks, including the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Space Needle" -->
The Space Needle in Seattle was built in about 400 days for a world's fair called the Century 21 Exposition. It opened on April 21, 1962, the first day of the fair. The tower is 605 feet tall, with a restaurant on top that turns around.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gateway Arch" -->
The Gateway Arch in St. Louis is 630 feet tall and 630 feet wide, and its outside is stainless steel. The architect Eero Saarinen won the contest to design it, and the winner was announced in February 1948. Saarinen died in 1961, before building began. Workers built the arch from February 12, 1963, to October 28, 1965. The insurers' estimates had called for 13 deaths, and no worker died.

Black workers were kept out of the construction jobs on the arch, a federal monument. Unions are groups that workers join to bargain together for jobs and pay. In St. Louis, the building trades unions, the unions of carpenters, plumbers, ironworkers and other builders, had almost all white members. Justice Department lawyers later charged in court that these unions kept Black men out of their membership and so out of the jobs.

On July 14, 1964, Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed a 125-foot construction ladder on the unfinished north leg of the arch. The two men were activists, people who campaign for a cause, and they climbed to protest. The Gateway Arch Park Foundation says they stayed up for 6 hours. St. Louis Public Radio says Green was up there for more than four hours. Police arrested both men. Green later helped start the Action Committee to Improve Opportunities for Negroes, called ACTION, a St. Louis group that worked for fair hiring.

In 1966 two Black plumbers, Elijah Smith and Oliver Parker, were hired to work on bathrooms in the arch's visitor center. White union workers walked off the job to protest the hiring of the two Black plumbers. Lawyers in the US Justice Department then sued the St. Louis Building and Construction Trades Council and five of its unions under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Title VII is the part of that law that forbids employers and unions to treat workers unfairly because of their race, color, religion, sex or national origin. It was the department's first lawsuit under Title VII charging a "pattern or practice," which means unfair treatment by race as a regular habit. The St. Louis building trades unions signed an agreement to end "all practices that were discriminatory," meaning every rule and habit that kept Black workers out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harold-mccomber" name="Harold McComber" movie="High Steel (1965)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harold McComber

> **Who:** A Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake who built skyscrapers in New York.
> **When and where:** Manhattan and Kahnawake, filmed in 1965.
> **Movie:** *High Steel* (1965), a documentary by the National Film Board of Canada, directed by Don Owen. It is about McComber's working life.

Harold McComber was a Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake. Like many Kahnawake men, he worked on the steel frames of skyscrapers in Manhattan. In 1965 the director Don Owen made a documentary film about his working life, called *High Steel*. The National Film Board of Canada produced it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harold-mccomber" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The World Trade Center" -->
The World Trade Center in New York had its ribbon-cutting on April 4, 1973. The architect Minoru Yamasaki designed its two towers, each 110 stories tall. The North Tower stood 1,368 feet. Its steel frame had passed the height of the Empire State Building in 1970, which made it the tallest building in the world. It kept that title until May 1973, about a month after the ribbon-cutting, when the steel frame of the Sears Tower in Chicago rose higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philippe-petit" name="Philippe Petit" movie="Man on Wire (2008)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Philippe Petit

> **Who:** A French high-wire walker who walked on a cable between the two towers of the World Trade Center.
> **When and where:** New York, August 7, 1974.
> **Movie:** *Man on Wire* (2008), directed by James Marsh. It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about the walk.

On August 7, 1974, Philippe Petit walked on a wire strung between the tops of the Twin Towers, about 1,350 feet above the ground. He and his friends had smuggled a cable up to the roofs and strung it from one tower to the other. Petit crossed eight times in 45 minutes.

Afterward he was arrested. The charges against him were dropped. In return, he put on a show for children in Central Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philippe-petit" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Vietnam Veterans Memorial" -->
The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington is two long walls of polished black granite set into the ground of the National Mall. The names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War are cut into the stone, in the order they died. It was dedicated on November 13, 1982, and it became the most visited memorial in Washington.

Critics attacked the design before it was built. One veteran called it "a black gash of shame." Another critic said that burying the dead "in a black stone sarcophagus," a stone coffin, "is like spitting on their graves." Opponents of the design included Patrick Buchanan and Congressman Henry Hyde. The members of the US Commission of Fine Arts, a federal board that reviews designs in Washington, kept the wall. They also added a statue of three soldiers by Frederick Hart nearby, in 1984. Hart's design had won third place in the contest for the memorial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maya-lin" name="Maya Lin" movie="Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maya Lin

> **Who:** The 21-year-old college student whose design won the contest for the Vietnam Veterans Memorial.
> **When and where:** Yale University and Washington, 1981 to 1982.
> **Movie:** *Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision* (1994). It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about Lin.

Maya Lin was a 21-year-old architecture student at Yale University when she won the contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial. She had first drawn the design for a class. She was born in Ohio, where her parents were professors. They had escaped from mainland China as young adults.

The contest was the biggest design competition held in the United States up to then. The judges looked at the entries without knowing who had made them. They chose entry number 1026 over 1,420 others. In May 1981 officials came to Lin's college room to tell her she had won the $20,000 first prize.

People who attacked the wall used Lin's age and her Chinese family background as reasons to change the design or drop it. At the dedication in 1982, no one spoke her name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maya-lin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Restoring the old landmarks" -->
From 1984 to 1986, workers restored the Statue of Liberty for its 100th birthday. They took down its original 1886 torch in 1984. It is now in the statue's museum. In its place they put a copy made of copper and covered in gold leaf, thin sheets of gold. The statue was dedicated again on July 4, 1986.

Ellis Island, in New York Harbor, was where the government had checked arriving immigrants. On May 11, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed Proclamation 3656, a formal order from the president, which made Ellis Island part of the Statue of Liberty National Monument. Ellis Island's main building had opened in 1900. Workers restored it, and in 1990 it opened as a museum of immigration.

On the West Coast, from 1910, US officials held immigrants in barracks on Angel Island, in San Francisco Bay. Chinese immigrants held there had carved more than 200 poems into the barracks walls, and in 1970 the poems were found again. After that, people campaigned to save the barracks. In 1997 Angel Island Immigration Station became a National Historic Landmark.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, Americans have built memorials to people who were killed or enslaved: the victims of the September 11 attacks, the Black Americans murdered by lynch mobs, and the people held in slavery. In the same years, officials and work crews took down hundreds of monuments to the Confederacy, the Southern states that fought to keep slavery in the Civil War. In April 2025 researchers at the Southern Poverty Law Center, a group that campaigns for civil rights, counted 415 Confederate memorials removed, moved or renamed since 2015. By its count, 2,086 were still standing. In 2025 federal officials began putting some of the removed monuments back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ground Zero rebuilt" -->
The National September 11 Memorial in New York stands where the Twin Towers stood. On September 11, 2001, hijackers took over four airliners. They crashed two of them into the North Tower and the South Tower, and fires from the crashes made both towers collapse. They crashed a third into the Pentagon, the headquarters of the US military, near Washington. The fourth, Flight 93, also crashed. The attacks killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 hijackers, by the count of the 9/11 Memorial and Museum. Of those, 2,753 were killed in New York, 184 at the Pentagon and 40 on Flight 93.

The memorial opened on September 11, 2011, ten years after the attacks. It has two pools set into the footprints of the two towers. Each pool covers nearly an acre. Water falls 30 feet into each pool and then drops another 20 feet. Around the edges, bronze panels carry the names of 2,983 people. They are the people killed on September 11, 2001, and the six people killed in an earlier bombing of the towers in 1993.

The design, called *Reflecting Absence*, won a contest in 2004 against 5,201 entries from 63 countries. The museum under the memorial opened on May 21, 2014. On November 3, 2014, One World Trade Center opened nearby. It stands 1,776 feet to the tip of its spire. The height was chosen to match 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="michael-arad" name="Michael Arad" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Michael Arad

> **Who:** The architect who designed the National September 11 Memorial.
> **When and where:** New York. He won the design contest in 2004, and the memorial opened in 2011.

Michael Arad was 34 years old and working as an architect for a New York City government agency when he entered the contest for the memorial. In his first sketches, he pictured two empty spaces cut into the surface of the Hudson River. His finished design put two pools where the towers had stood.

Arad worked with the landscape architect Peter Walker, who plans outdoor spaces such as parks. In 2004 the contest judges chose their entry from 5,201.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="michael-arad" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New memorials in Washington" -->
In Washington, new memorials were built for Black Americans, among them the enslaved workers who helped build the Capitol. On February 28, 2012, members of Congress unveiled a marker in Emancipation Hall, the great hall of the Capitol Visitor Center. Emancipation means freeing people from slavery. The marker is a block of Aquia Creek sandstone taken from the Capitol's East Front, which was built from 1824 to 1826. Its plaque says the stone "was quarried by laborers, including enslaved African Americans, and commemorates their important role in building the Capitol." Quarried means cut out of the ground.

The Martin Luther King, Jr. Memorial was dedicated on October 16, 2011. It was the first memorial to an African American in the area of the National Mall. At its center is a 30-foot carving called the Stone of Hope.

The National Museum of African American History and Culture opened on September 24, 2016, beside the Washington Monument. It is part of the Smithsonian. The architect David Adjaye led its design. He based the building's outside, a bronze-colored shape in three levels called the corona, on the art of the Yoruba. The Yoruba are an African people. The pattern of its metal screen copies ironwork made by enslaved and free Black craftsmen in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Memorials to lynching and slavery" -->
The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, in Montgomery, Alabama, is a memorial to Black people who were lynched. Lynching is a killing by a mob, without a trial. The lawyer Bryan Stevenson founded the Equal Justice Initiative (EJI), a legal group that does not make a profit. EJI built the memorial and opened it on April 26, 2018, with a museum called the Legacy Museum.

The memorial holds 805 steel blocks, each six feet tall, hanging from above. Each one stands for a county in the United States where EJI found a record of a lynching. EJI's study *Lynching in America* counts almost 4,400 lynchings of Black people from 1877 to 1950. A copy of each county's block stands at the site. EJI offers each copy to groups in that county to set up at home. A team called MASS Design Group designed the memorial, and it cost about $20 million, paid by private foundations.

In March 2024 EJI opened the Freedom Monument Sculpture Park nearby, on 17 acres with nearly 50 sculptures. Its National Monument to Freedom lists more than 122,000 last names taken from the 1870 census. Nearly five million Black people took those names. The 1870 census was the first time people who had been enslaved could claim a last name that the government would recognize. The monument stands 43 feet tall and 155 feet long, and EJI dedicated it on Juneteenth, June 19, 2024.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Confederate monuments coming down and going back up" -->
In 2018 the Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) reported that members of the United Daughters of the Confederacy had put up hundreds of Confederate statues at courthouses and other public places since the Civil War. The SPLC found two main periods of building. The first began around 1900, while lawmakers in Southern states were passing Jim Crow laws. Lawmakers wrote these laws to force Black and white people apart and to take away Black people's rights. The second ran from the early 1950s through the 1960s, when segregationists, people who wanted to keep the races apart, fought the civil rights movement.

Hundreds of these monuments came down after people were killed in three places: Charleston, Charlottesville and Minneapolis. On June 17, 2015, a 21-year-old white man named Dylann Roof sat in on a Bible study at the Emanuel A.M.E. Church in Charleston, South Carolina. After about an hour he opened fire and killed nine Black worshippers. Roof had posted pictures of himself with Confederate flags on his website, and he had written racist views there.

In Charlottesville, Virginia, Paul Goodloe McIntire paid for a statue of the Confederate general Robert E. Lee, and it was dedicated in 1924. On February 6, 2017, the members of the city council voted 3 to 2 to remove it. White supremacists then organized a rally called Unite the Right around the statue. On August 12, 2017, James Alex Fields Jr. drove his car into a crowd of people protesting against the rally. He killed Heather Heyer, a 32-year-old paralegal, and injured many others. Counts of the injured run from 19 to 29.

City workers took the Charlottesville statue down on July 10, 2021. City officials gave it to the Jefferson School African American Heritage Center. In October 2023 the center's Swords into Plowshares project had the statue melted down at a foundry.

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd by kneeling on his neck, back and shoulder for about ten minutes. In 2021 a jury convicted Chauvin of murder. Protests followed Floyd's death. The SPLC counted at least 168 Confederate symbols removed in 2020 alone. In June 2020 Virginia Governor Ralph Northam ordered the removal of Richmond's Lee statue, a 12-ton monument put up in 1890. The justices of the Supreme Court of Virginia then refused to block the removal in two lawsuits. ABC News reported that a crew led by the contractor Devon Henry took the statue down on September 8, 2021.

In April 2025 the SPLC counted 2,086 Confederate memorials still standing in public places. In its count, memorials include place names and other symbols as well as monuments such as statues, and 685 of the 2,086 were monuments. Rivka Maizlish of the SPLC called the memorials "the results of a mass propaganda campaign."

In 2025 federal officials began putting some monuments back. In June 2020, protesters had pulled down a statue of the Confederate general Albert Pike in Washington and set it on fire. In October 2025 National Park Service workers put it back up. They cited a historic preservation law and an executive order, a written command from the president, that President Donald Trump had signed on restoring removed monuments.

The Confederate Memorial at Arlington National Cemetery was put up in 1914 by the United Daughters of the Confederacy. The sculptor, Moses Ezekiel, had fought for the Confederacy. The memorial shows "an enslaved man following his owner to war." It also shows an enslaved woman as a "mammy," holding a white officer's child. The "mammy" was a made-up picture of an enslaved Black woman who cared for a white family's children and seemed happy in slavery. The picture hid the truth that many such women were kept from their own children. A commission set up by Congress found that the memorial showed "highly sanitized depictions of slavery," pictures of slavery with the cruelty cleaned out. On that commission's advice, the memorial was taken down in December 2023. In August 2025 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced that it would return in 2027. The newspaper *Stars and Stripes* reported that the repairs would cost about $10 million.

In 2026, National Park Service workers took down an exhibit about slavery in Philadelphia, and a federal judge ordered it put back. The President's House site marks where George Washington lived while he was president. Its exhibit panels described the lives of nine people Washington held in slavery there. The workers removed the panels in January 2026, following President Trump's executive order called "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." Lawyers for the City of Philadelphia sued. On February 16, 2026, US District Judge Cynthia Rufe ordered the panels restored while the case went on, and workers began putting them back three days later. The administration appealed, which means it asked a higher court to change the ruling. By July 2026, judges of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals had agreed that the administration had the power to replace the exhibit.

Sugarloaf Mound, on the south side of St. Louis, is the only Native mound left in that city. Native people built about 40 mounds there between about the years 600 and 1300, and as the city grew, people dug away all the others. In 1928 someone built a house on top of Sugarloaf Mound. The Osage Nation bought that house and about a third of the mound in 2009, and tore the house down in 2017. In 2025, working with an arts group called Counterpublic, the Osage Nation got the last two pieces of the property transferred to it. Afterward Andrea Hunter, the Osage Nation's Historic Preservation Officer, said, "It has been a long 17-year journey, and I am thrilled to finally see the entire mound come under Osage Nation control." She said her office must now "remove all the structures from the properties and stabilize the mound." Osage leaders plan to build a center there to teach visitors about the mound.

In 2009 workers finished restoring the barracks of the Angel Island Immigration Station in California. On February 22, 2018, President Donald Trump signed a law that gave the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial in St. Louis, the grounds of the Gateway Arch, a new name, Gateway Arch National Park. It is the smallest national park in the country.
