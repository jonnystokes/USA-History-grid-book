## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 14 minutes**

Workers died building three of the giant landmarks that went up between 1913 and 1941. Five died on the Empire State Building and 11 on the Golden Gate Bridge. At Hoover Dam the official count is 96, and it leaves out 42 more deaths that were listed as pneumonia.

The Lincoln Memorial and Mount Rushmore went up in the same years. Mount Rushmore was carved into a mountain in the Black Hills of South Dakota, on land that members of Congress took from the Sioux in 1877, breaking a treaty, a written agreement between nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Woolworth Building" -->
Frank W. Woolworth paid the whole $13.5 million cost of the Woolworth Building in New York himself, in cash. Workers finished it in 1913. At 792 feet, it was then the tallest building in the world. Woolworth had made his fortune from stores that sold goods for five and ten cents.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A roped-off section at the Lincoln Memorial" -->
At the opening of the Lincoln Memorial in Washington in 1922, a white Marine ordered Black guests into a roped-off section, apart from white guests. William Howard Taft, who led the group in charge of the memorial, told the only Black speaker to cut his speech.

**The building.** Work on the memorial to Abraham Lincoln began on February 12, 1914, Lincoln's 105th birthday. Joseph Blackburn, a member of the Lincoln Memorial Commission, the group in charge of the building, broke the ground. He had been an officer in the Confederate army, the army of the Southern states that fought to keep slavery in the Civil War. The architect Henry Bacon designed the building, and the sculptor Daniel Chester French made the seated statue of Lincoln inside.

About 50,000 people came to the dedication on May 30, 1922. The crowd was segregated, which means Black people and white people were kept apart. According to an account in Smithsonian magazine, the Marine was rough and rude as he sent the Black guests to their section. Twenty-one of them left in protest.

**The telegram.** Robert Russa Moton was the only Black speaker. A week before, on May 23, Taft sent him a telegram, a message sent by wire. Taft was the Chief Justice, the head of the US Supreme Court, and also the president of the Lincoln Memorial Commission. He wrote, "Shall have to ask you to cut five hundred words."

Taft added, "I am sure you wish to avoid any insinuation (hint) of attempt to make the occasion one for propaganda (messages meant to push people to one side)." Moton made the cuts. One passage he removed said that the work Lincoln died for was "still unfinished."

**A concert on the steps.** Marian Anderson, a famous African American singer, sang from the memorial's steps in 1939, after the Daughters of the American Revolution refused to let her sing in Constitution Hall. The group owned the hall, in Washington, and its rule allowed only white performers there. Eleanor Roosevelt, the president's wife, quit the group in protest.

Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, got President Franklin D. Roosevelt's approval for a concert at the memorial. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson sang from its steps to about 75,000 people of all races.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Whose land is Mount Rushmore on?" -->
Mount Rushmore is carved on land that US officials promised to the Sioux in a treaty and that members of Congress later took. The Lakota, one of the Sioux peoples, call the mountain Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers. It stands in the Black Hills of South Dakota.

**The treaty.** In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, US officials set aside a large reservation for the Sioux, and the Black Hills were part of it. The treaty states that the land was "for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux, theirs alone to live on and use. It also states that no part of it could be given up unless three-fourths of the adult Sioux men signed.

Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led close to 1,000 soldiers and teamsters, men who drove wagons, into the Black Hills on July 2, 1874. Lieutenant General Philip Sheridan had sent them, and by the middle of August they had found gold.

**The gold rush.** Hundreds of miners rushed into the hills once the news spread. At first soldiers tried to keep them out of the land the treaty had set apart for the Sioux. In late 1875 President Ulysses S. Grant ordered the Army to leave the miners alone.

**The food law.** Members of Congress then set out to get the hills for the United States. In August 1876 they passed a law saying no more money would be spent on food for the Sioux until they gave up the Black Hills. Under it, officials would give the Sioux no more food rations, and the Sioux then depended on those rations to eat.

A group of officials led by George Manypenny brought the Sioux a paper in September 1876. It was already written, and it stated that the Sioux gave up the Black Hills. Only about one in ten of the adult Sioux men signed it, far fewer than the three-fourths the treaty required. Members of Congress made that paper a law anyway on February 28, 1877, and with it they took the Black Hills.

The judges of the US Supreme Court, who are called justices, decided the case *United States v. Sioux Nation* in 1980. Justice Harry Blackmun wrote for the court that the taking had broken the treaty. The justices agreed with an earlier award of $17.1 million for the land, plus interest, extra money of 5 percent for every year since 1877. The total came to about $106 million.

The Sioux nations have never taken the money. They say the Black Hills were never for sale. The money sits in a federal account held by the US Interior Department, and officials keep its balance secret. South Dakota Public Broadcasting put it at about $2 billion in 2022. Frank Star Comes Out, president of the Oglala Sioux Tribe, has said, "We will never sell out our holy lands, the Black Hills, to the United States for monetary compensation (payment in money)."

**The carving.** Workers carved the mountain from October 4, 1927, to October 31, 1941. According to the National Park Service, nearly 400 men and women worked there, among them drillers and blacksmiths. They did about 90 percent of the carving with dynamite and removed about 450,000 tons of rock in all.

Then drillers made rows of holes close together, a method called honeycombing, and smoothed the faces with hand tools. Workers were lowered down the 500-foot face on steel cables, sitting in bosun chairs, seats hung from a cable. No worker died in the 14 years of carving. Today the memorial's staff consult with 21 Native nations connected to the site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="gutzon-borglum" name="Gutzon Borglum" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Gutzon Borglum

> **Who:** The sculptor who designed Mount Rushmore, after starting a Confederate carving in Georgia that the Ku Klux Klan helped pay for.
> **When and where:** Stone Mountain, Georgia, from 1923. The Black Hills of South Dakota, 1927 to 1941.

**Klan money helped pay for Borglum's carving at Stone Mountain.**

Before he carved Mount Rushmore, Gutzon Borglum went to meetings of the Ku Klux Klan and took a deep part in Klan politics, according to reports in the Washington Post and Smithsonian magazine. No record proves he was a member.

**The Klan.** Confederate veterans had started the first Klan in Pulaski, Tennessee, in December 1865. Its members whipped, burned the homes of and murdered freed Black people and the people who helped them. Federal officials put that Klan down in the early 1870s.

William J. Simmons and 16 other men climbed Stone Mountain in Georgia on Thanksgiving night in 1915. They burned a cross there and announced that the Klan was born again. Members of the new Klan preached white supremacy, the belief that white people should rule over everyone else. They were also against Catholics and Jews, and they wanted fewer immigrants let into the country.

At Stone Mountain, Borglum designed a carving of three Confederate leaders on horseback, Robert E. Lee, Stonewall Jackson and Jefferson Davis. Klan money helped pay for it. Work began in 1923, and Borglum unveiled the head of Lee in 1924.

Borglum quarreled with the businessmen who ran the Stone Mountain project, and they fired him. He then destroyed his models to protect his design, which made the directors angry. A warrant, a court paper ordering his arrest, was issued, and he fled Georgia.

Borglum began Mount Rushmore in 1927 and directed the carving until he died on March 6, 1941. His son, Lincoln Borglum, finished the work that year.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="gutzon-borglum" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="nick-clifford" name="Nick Clifford" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Nick Clifford

> **Who:** The youngest worker on Mount Rushmore, and the last of its carvers to die.
> **When and where:** Mount Rushmore, South Dakota, 1938 to 1940. Died in 2019, at 98.

**The Borglums hired the 17-year-old partly to pitch for their baseball team.**

Donald "Nick" Clifford was 17 when he was hired at Mount Rushmore in 1938, the youngest worker on the job. The Borglums wanted him for the crew's baseball team, where he pitched.

Clifford earned 55 cents an hour. He ran the winch, a machine that winds a cable, and the cable carried men up the face of the mountain. He also drilled holes for the dynamite. He worked there until 1940.

When Clifford died on November 23, 2019, at the age of 98, he was the last of the carvers.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="nick-clifford" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Empire State Building and the Mohawk ironworkers" -->
Five workers died building the Empire State Building in New York, by the official count. A story that hundreds died is false.

Builders took about 410 days to put up the building, from the first steel to the opening on May 1, 1931. Up to 3,400 workers were on the job each day, and during the fastest stretch the steel frame rose about four and a half floors a week. The photographer Lewis Hine took pictures of the men working on the steel for his book *Men at Work*.

The building has 102 stories and stands 1,250 feet tall, or 1,454 feet to the tip of today's antenna. It was the tallest building in the world until 1970, when the steel frame of the World Trade Center's North Tower rose higher.

**High steel.** Some of the riveting crews were ironworkers from Kahnawake, a community of the Mohawk Nation in Canada. Ironworkers put up the steel frames of buildings and bridges, and riveters joined the steel beams with rivets, hot metal pins that lock the beams together. Kahnawake men had done this work since the 1880s.

**The Quebec Bridge.** Kahnawake ironworkers had died on an earlier job. On August 29, 1907, the south half of the Quebec Bridge fell while it was being built across the St. Lawrence River in Canada. James H. Marsh, writing in the Canadian Encyclopedia, gives 75 workers killed, and Eve Cable, writing in *The Eastern Door*, the Kahnawake newspaper, gives 76. Thirty-three of the dead were Kahnawake men.

A Royal Commission, a group set up to find the cause, blamed two engineers. Theodore Cooper, an expert brought in to oversee the design, had made mistakes in it and had lengthened the bridge to save money. John Deans, the chief engineer, kept the crews working after Cooper warned of danger.

**The women's rule.** The women of Kahnawake made sure after 1907 that the men of one family did not all work on the same job at the same time. *The Eastern Door* gives the rule this way: "If a lady had seven sons, they're not all going to the same job." Crosses made from the bridge's steel stand in the village cemetery. In 2007 a monument went up with a picture of each man who was killed and a 40-foot steel model of the bridge.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Golden Gate Bridge" -->
Eleven workers died building the Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco. One died in October 1936, and ten died on February 17, 1937, when a falling work platform tore through the safety net under the bridge. The bridge opened in May 1937, and on May 27, the first day, about 200,000 people walked across it.

**Who designed it.** Joseph Strauss was the chief engineer. Charles Ellis, the engineer who worked out how the bridge's structure would hold its weight, did the detailed design and thousands of calculations from 1929 to 1931. Strauss fired Ellis before building began, and Ellis got no public credit for decades. In a 2007 report, officials of the Golden Gate Bridge District gave Ellis the main credit for the design.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-strauss" name="Joseph Strauss" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Strauss

> **Who:** The chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge, who hung a safety net under the bridge while it was built.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, which opened in May 1937.

**A $130,000 net under the bridge caught 19 falling men.**

Joseph Strauss required hard hats on the Golden Gate Bridge job. He also spent about $130,000 on a safety net that hung under the bridge while it was built. It was the first large safety net used on an American bridge job.

The net caught 19 men who fell. They called themselves the Halfway to Hell Club.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-strauss" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="al-zampa" name="Al Zampa" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Al Zampa

> **Who:** An ironworker who fell from the Golden Gate Bridge and lived, and who later had a bridge named for him.
> **When and where:** The Golden Gate Bridge, October 19, 1936. From Crockett, California.

**Zampa fell into the net where it sagged down onto the rocks.**

Alfred Zampa was an ironworker from Crockett, California. On October 19, 1936, he slipped on wet steel and fell into the safety net. He was near the shore, where the net sagged down onto the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back.

Zampa spent twelve weeks in the hospital and two years in a back brace. Then he went back to ironwork. He was one of the first members of the Halfway to Hell Club and later its unofficial spokesman, the man who spoke for the club without holding any title.

California lawmakers named the new bridge over the Carquinez Strait the Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge in 2003.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="al-zampa" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many men died building Hoover Dam?" -->
By the official count, 96 men died building Hoover Dam. The count comes from the Bureau of Reclamation, the federal office that runs the dam. It includes deaths from falls, rock slides, machinery and drowning, and it leaves out deaths from heat, heart trouble and pneumonia, a sickness of the lungs. The exact number of deaths is not known.

**The 42.** The main builder was a company called Six Companies Inc. The records list 42 more workers who died of pneumonia. Workers said these men really died of carbon monoxide poisoning. They said officials of Six Companies listed the deaths as pneumonia so the company would not have to pay compensation claims, the payments owed when a worker is hurt or killed on the job.

Carbon monoxide is a gas with no color or smell that comes from burning fuel. When a person breathes it in, it stops the blood from carrying oxygen. The workers blamed the gasoline-powered vehicles running inside the diversion tunnels, which were dug to carry the river around the building site. The tunnels often reached 140 degrees Fahrenheit and filled with thick exhaust. In nearby Boulder City, no one who did not work on the dam was recorded as dying of pneumonia in those years.

**Father and son.** The first deaths on the official list came in 1922, during the surveys for the dam. J. G. Tierney drowned on December 20 of that year, and Harold Connelly also died that year. The last death on the list came 13 years to the day after Tierney's. On December 20, 1935, his son, Patrick Tierney, an electrician's helper, fell from one of the dam's intake towers, the towers that take in water.

People tell a story that men are buried inside the dam's concrete. It is false. Workers poured the dam as many separate blocks, in shallow layers, with crews standing over each pour, so a body could not have been left inside. President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated the dam on September 30, 1935, and its generators began making electricity in 1936.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 8 minutes**

Americans in these years fought over who was allowed to build a landmark and over what a memorial to the dead should look like. In St. Louis, Justice Department lawyers later charged in court that leaders and members of the building trades unions kept Black workers out of the unions, and so off the job building the Gateway Arch. Two men climbed the unfinished arch in protest.

In Washington, critics attacked the design of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, a black stone wall set into the ground. The names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War are cut into it, and it was dedicated in 1982. Builders also put up two towers in New York, one of them for a few years the tallest building in the world, and workers restored older landmarks, including the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Space Needle" -->
Builders put up the Space Needle in Seattle in about 400 days for a world's fair called the Century 21 Exposition. It opened on April 21, 1962, the first day of the fair. The tower is 605 feet tall, with a restaurant on top that turns around.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Gateway Arch and the men kept off the job" -->
The Gateway Arch in St. Louis is 630 feet tall and 630 feet wide, and its outside is stainless steel. The architect Eero Saarinen won the contest to design it, and the winner was announced in February 1948. Saarinen died in 1961, before building began. Workers built the arch from February 12, 1963, to October 28, 1965. Insurers had predicted 13 deaths, and no worker died.

Black workers were kept out of the building jobs on the arch, a federal monument. In St. Louis, the building trades unions, groups that carpenters, plumbers, ironworkers and other builders join to bargain together for jobs and pay, had almost all white members. Justice Department lawyers later charged in court that the leaders and members of these unions kept Black men out of the unions and so out of the jobs.

**The climb.** Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed a 125-foot construction ladder on the unfinished north leg of the arch on July 14, 1964. The two men were activists, people who campaign for a cause, and they climbed to protest. An article by the Gateway Arch Park Foundation states that they stayed up for 6 hours, and a St. Louis Public Radio report states that Green was up there for more than four hours. Police arrested both men.

Green later helped start ACTION, a St. Louis group that worked for fair hiring. ACTION stood for the Action Committee to Improve Opportunities for Negroes, a word of that time for Black people.

Two Black plumbers, Elijah Smith and Oliver Parker, were hired to work on bathrooms in the arch's visitor center. White union workers walked off the job on January 7, 1966, to protest the hiring of the two men.

**The lawsuit.** Lawyers in the US Justice Department sued the St. Louis Building and Construction Trades Council and five of its unions about a month later, on February 4, 1966. They charged the unions under the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which made it against the law for unions and employers to treat workers unfairly because of their race.

Officials of several of the unions settled the case. They signed an agreement to end "all practices that were discriminatory," meaning every rule and habit that kept Black workers out, and to judge everyone who applied by the same written standards. Two unions, the sheet metal workers and the electricians, fought the case in court. A judge ruled for them in 1968. In 1969 appeals judges overturned that ruling, found that the two unions' rules still kept Black workers out, and ordered the rules changed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="harold-mccomber" name="Harold McComber" movie="High Steel (1965)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Harold McComber

> **Who:** A Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake, in Canada, who left home to work on the steel frames of Manhattan skyscrapers.
> **When and where:** Manhattan and Kahnawake, filmed in 1965.
> **Movie:** *High Steel* (1965), a documentary by the National Film Board of Canada, directed by Don Owen. It is about McComber's working life.

**McComber's sons grew up without him while he built New York skyscrapers.**

Harold McComber was a Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake, in Quebec. Like many Kahnawake men, he left home to work on the steel frames of skyscrapers in Manhattan.

The director Don Owen built his 13-minute documentary *High Steel* around McComber's working life, and the National Film Board of Canada produced it in 1965. The film shows McComber and the men he worked with on the steel high above New York City. It also shows the quieter life of their community at home. To film the men at work, the camera crew reached the site by crossing on a ladder from the building next door.

In the film, McComber is sorry that his sons have had to grow up without their father. The film also tells how Kahnawake men first earned their name for steelwork, on a railway bridge built in the late 1800s.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="harold-mccomber" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The World Trade Center" -->
The architect Minoru Yamasaki designed the two towers of the World Trade Center in New York, each 110 stories tall. The ribbon-cutting was on April 4, 1973. The North Tower stood 1,368 feet. Its steel frame had passed the height of the Empire State Building in 1970, which made it the tallest building in the world. It kept that title until May 1973, about a month after the ribbon-cutting, when the steel frame of the Sears Tower in Chicago rose higher.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philippe-petit" name="Philippe Petit" movie="Man on Wire (2008)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Philippe Petit

> **Who:** A French high-wire walker who walked on a cable between the two towers of the World Trade Center.
> **When and where:** New York, August 7, 1974.
> **Movie:** *Man on Wire* (2008), directed by James Marsh. It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about the walk.

**Petit crossed between the towers eight times in 45 minutes.**

Philippe Petit walked on a wire strung between the tops of the Twin Towers, about 1,350 feet above the ground, on August 7, 1974. He and his friends had smuggled a cable up to the roofs and strung it from one tower to the other. Petit crossed eight times in 45 minutes.

Afterward police arrested him. The charges against him were dropped. In return, he put on a show for children in Central Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philippe-petit" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Vietnam Veterans Memorial and its critics" -->
Two long walls of polished black granite, set into the ground of the National Mall in Washington, form the Vietnam Veterans Memorial. The names of more than 58,000 Americans who died in the Vietnam War are cut into the stone, in the order they died. It was dedicated on November 13, 1982, and it became the most visited memorial in Washington.

**The attacks.** Critics attacked the design before it was built. One veteran called it "a black gash (a deep cut) of shame." Another critic said that burying the dead "in a black stone sarcophagus," a stone coffin, "is like spitting on their graves." Opponents of the design included the radio host Patrick Buchanan and Congressman Henry Hyde. They campaigned to make the wall white and to add an eight-foot statue of soldiers.

The members of the US Commission of Fine Arts, a federal board that reviews designs in Washington, kept the wall. They also added a statue of three soldiers by Frederick Hart nearby, in 1984. Hart's design had won third place in the contest for the memorial.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="maya-lin" name="Maya Lin" movie="Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision (1994)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Maya Lin

> **Who:** The 21-year-old college student whose design won the contest for the Vietnam Veterans Memorial.
> **When and where:** Yale University and Washington, 1981 to 1982.
> **Movie:** *Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision* (1994). It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature and is about Lin.

**Entry number 1026 beat 1,420 others.**

Maya Lin was a 21-year-old architecture student at Yale University when she won the contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial. She had first drawn the design for a class. She was born in Ohio, where her parents were professors. They had escaped from mainland China as young adults.

**The contest.** It was the biggest design competition held in the United States up to then. The judges looked at the entries without knowing who had made them, and they chose entry number 1026 over 1,420 others. In May 1981 officials came to Lin's college room to tell her she had won the $20,000 first prize.

**The critics.** Lin argued against the campaign to make her wall white and add a statue, and she said Congressman Henry Hyde was "drawing mustaches on other people's portraits." Some of the wall's critics also used her age and her Chinese family background as reasons to change the design or drop it. The speakers at the dedication in 1982 did not mention her.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="maya-lin" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Restoring the old landmarks" -->
Workers restored the Statue of Liberty for its 100th birthday from 1984 to 1986. They took down its original 1886 torch in 1984, and it is now in the statue's museum. In its place they put a copy made of copper and covered in gold leaf, thin sheets of gold. The statue was dedicated again on July 4, 1986.

Ellis Island, in New York Harbor, was where federal officials checked arriving immigrants. On May 11, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed Proclamation 3656, a formal order from the president, under which Ellis Island became part of the Statue of Liberty National Monument. Ellis Island's main building had opened in 1900. Workers restored it, and in 1990 it opened as a museum of immigration.

On the West Coast, from 1910, US officials held immigrants in barracks on Angel Island, in San Francisco Bay. Chinese immigrants held there had carved more than 200 poems into the barracks walls, and in 1970 the poems were found again. After that, people campaigned to save the barracks. In 1997 Angel Island Immigration Station became a National Historic Landmark.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 12 minutes**

Memorials to people who were killed or enslaved went up after 2000: to the victims of the September 11 attacks, to the Black Americans murdered by mobs without a trial, and to the people held in slavery. In the same years, officials and work crews took down, moved or renamed hundreds of memorials to the Confederacy, the Southern states that fought to keep slavery in the Civil War.

These memorials include statues and place names. Researchers at the Southern Poverty Law Center, a group that campaigns for civil rights, counted 415 of them removed, moved or renamed between 2015 and April 2025, and 2,086 still standing. In 2025 federal officials began putting some of the removed monuments back.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ground Zero rebuilt" -->
The National September 11 Memorial in New York stands where the Twin Towers stood. On September 11, 2001, hijackers took over four airliners. They crashed two of them into the North Tower and the South Tower, and fires from the crashes made both towers collapse. They crashed a third into the Pentagon, the headquarters of the US military, near Washington, and the fourth, Flight 93, also crashed.

**The dead.** The hijackers killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 hijackers themselves, according to the 9/11 Memorial and Museum. Of those, 2,753 were killed in New York, 184 at the Pentagon and 40 on Flight 93.

The memorial opened on September 11, 2011, ten years after the attacks. It has two pools set into the footprints of the two towers. Each pool covers nearly an acre. Water falls 30 feet into each pool and then drops another 20 feet. Around the edges, bronze panels carry the names of 2,983 people. They are the people killed on September 11, 2001, and the six people killed in an earlier bombing of the towers in 1993.

The design, called *Reflecting Absence*, won a contest in 2004 that drew 5,201 entries from 63 countries. The museum under the memorial opened on May 21, 2014. On November 3, 2014, One World Trade Center opened nearby. It stands 1,776 feet to the tip of its spire. The height was chosen to match 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="michael-arad" name="Michael Arad" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Michael Arad

> **Who:** The architect, then 34 and working for New York City, whose design for the National September 11 Memorial was chosen from 5,201 entries.
> **When and where:** New York. He won the design contest in 2004, and the memorial opened in 2011.

**His first sketches showed two empty spaces in the Hudson River.**

Michael Arad was 34 years old and working as an architect for a New York City government office when he entered the contest for the memorial. In his first sketches, he pictured two empty spaces cut into the surface of the Hudson River. His finished design put two pools where the towers had stood.

Arad worked with the landscape architect Peter Walker, who plans outdoor spaces such as parks. The contest judges chose their entry from 5,201 in 2004.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="michael-arad" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New memorials in Washington" -->
New memorials to Black Americans went up in Washington after 2000. The Martin Luther King, Jr. Memorial was dedicated on October 16, 2011, the first memorial to an African American in the area of the National Mall. At its center is a 30-foot carving called the Stone of Hope.

**The Capitol's builders.** Members of Congress unveiled a marker to the enslaved workers who helped build the Capitol on February 28, 2012, in Emancipation Hall, the great hall of the Capitol Visitor Center. The marker is a block of Aquia Creek sandstone taken from the Capitol's East Front, which was built from 1824 to 1826. Its plaque states that the stone "was quarried by laborers, including enslaved African Americans, and commemorates (honors) their important role in building the Capitol."

The National Museum of African American History and Culture opened on September 24, 2016, beside the Washington Monument. It is part of the Smithsonian. The architect David Adjaye led its design. He based the building's outside, a bronze-colored shape in three levels called the corona, on the art of the Yoruba, an African people. The pattern of its metal screen is copied from ironwork made by enslaved and free Black craftsmen in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Memorials to lynching and slavery" -->
The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, in Montgomery, Alabama, is a memorial to Black people who were lynched, killed by a mob without a trial. The lawyer Bryan Stevenson founded the Equal Justice Initiative (EJI), a legal group that does not make a profit. EJI built the memorial and opened it on April 26, 2018, with a museum called the Legacy Museum.

**805 blocks.** The memorial holds 805 steel blocks, each six feet tall, hanging from above. Each one stands for a county in the United States where EJI researchers found a record of a lynching. In their study *Lynching in America*, EJI researchers counted almost 4,400 lynchings of Black people from 1877 to 1950.

A copy of each county's block stands at the site, and EJI offers each copy to groups in that county to set up at home. A team called MASS Design Group designed the memorial, and it cost about $20 million, paid by private foundations.

**122,000 names.** EJI opened the Freedom Monument Sculpture Park nearby in March 2024, on 17 acres with nearly 50 sculptures. Its National Monument to Freedom lists more than 122,000 last names taken from the 1870 census, the government's count of everyone in the country that year. That census was the first time people who had been enslaved could claim a last name that the government would recognize. In it, nearly five million Black people took these names as their own.

The monument stands 43 feet tall and 155 feet long, and EJI dedicated it on Juneteenth, June 19, 2024. Juneteenth marks June 19, 1865, the day Major General Gordon Granger of the US Army reached Galveston, Texas, with the news that enslaved African Americans were free.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Confederate monuments coming down and going back up" -->
**Who put them up.** Members of the United Daughters of the Confederacy had put up hundreds of Confederate statues at courthouses and other public places since the Civil War, researchers at the Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) reported in 2018. The researchers found two main periods of building.

The first began around 1900, while lawmakers in Southern states were passing Jim Crow laws, which they wrote to force Black and white people apart and to take away Black people's rights. The second ran from the early 1950s through the 1960s, when segregationists, people who wanted to keep the races apart, fought the civil rights movement.

Hundreds of these memorials came down or were renamed after people were killed in three places: Charleston, Charlottesville and Minneapolis.

**Charleston.** A 21-year-old white man named Dylann Roof sat in on a Bible study at the Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston, South Carolina, on June 17, 2015. After about an hour he opened fire and killed nine Black worshippers. Roof had posted pictures of himself with Confederate flags on his website, and he had written racist views there. A federal jury found Roof guilty of all 33 federal charges against him on December 15, 2016, and the federal court sentenced him to death on January 10, 2017.

The Confederate battle flag had flown at the South Carolina State House, in Columbia, since 1961. After the murders, state lawmakers voted to take it down, and Governor Nikki Haley signed the law on July 9, 2015. The next day an honor guard of the state Highway Patrol lowered the flag, and it went to the Confederate Relic Room at the state museum.

**Charlottesville.** Paul Goodloe McIntire paid for a statue of the Confederate general Robert E. Lee in Charlottesville, Virginia, and it was dedicated in 1924. The members of the city council voted 3 to 2 on February 6, 2017, to remove it. City workers took the statue down on July 10, 2021, and city officials gave it to the Jefferson School African American Heritage Center. In October 2023 the staff of the center's Swords into Plowshares project had the statue melted down at a foundry.

**The rally.** White supremacists organized a rally called Unite the Right around the statue for August 2017, six months after the council's vote. On August 12 James Alex Fields Jr. drove his car into a crowd of people protesting against the rally. He killed Heather Heyer, a 32-year-old paralegal, a worker who helps lawyers, and injured many others. Counts of the injured run from 19 to 29.

A Virginia jury found Fields guilty in December 2018 of first-degree murder, the most serious kind of murder, done on purpose. In 2019 he pleaded guilty to 29 federal hate crimes, crimes against people chosen because of their race, religion or a similar trait, and a federal judge sentenced him to life in prison.

**Minneapolis.** Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd on May 25, 2020, by kneeling on his neck, back and shoulder for about ten minutes. A jury convicted Chauvin of murder in 2021. Protests followed Floyd's murder, and SPLC researchers counted at least 168 Confederate symbols removed in 2020 alone.

Virginia Governor Ralph Northam ordered the removal of Richmond's Lee statue, a 12-ton monument put up in 1890, in June 2020. The judges of the Supreme Court of Virginia then refused to block the removal in two lawsuits. A crew led by Devon Henry, who owned the construction company doing the work, took the statue down on September 8, 2021, ABC News reporters wrote.

SPLC researchers counted 2,086 Confederate memorials still standing in public places in April 2025. In their count, memorials include place names and other symbols as well as monuments such as statues, and 685 of the 2,086 were monuments. Rivka Maizlish of the SPLC called the memorials "the results of a mass propaganda campaign," meaning a huge effort to push one side of the story.

**Going back up.** Federal officials began putting some monuments back in 2025. Protesters had pulled down a statue of the Confederate general Albert Pike in Washington in June 2020 and set it on fire. National Park Service workers put it back up in October 2025. They cited a historic preservation law and an executive order, a written command from the president, that President Donald Trump had signed on restoring removed monuments.

Members of the United Daughters of the Confederacy put up the Confederate Memorial at Arlington National Cemetery in 1914. The sculptor, Moses Ezekiel, had fought for the Confederacy. The memorial shows "an enslaved man following his owner to war." It also shows an enslaved woman as a "mammy," holding a white officer's child.

The "mammy" was a made-up picture of an enslaved Black woman who cared for a white family's children and seemed happy in slavery. The picture hid the truth that enslavers separated these women from their own children. A commission set up by Congress found that the memorial showed "highly sanitized depictions of slavery," pictures of slavery with the cruelty cleaned out.

On that commission's advice, the memorial was taken down in December 2023. *Stars and Stripes*, the newspaper that reported it, does not say who did the work. In August 2025 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced that it would return in 2027, and *Stars and Stripes* reported that the repairs would cost about $10 million.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who took down the slavery exhibit in Philadelphia?" -->
National Park Service workers took down an exhibit about slavery in Philadelphia in 2026. A federal judge ordered it put back, but appeals judges later let officials of the Trump administration replace it with panels of their own. The President's House site marks where George Washington lived while he was president. Its exhibit panels described the lives of nine people Washington held in slavery there.

**The court case.** The workers removed the panels in January 2026, following President Trump's executive order called "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." Lawyers for the City of Philadelphia sued. On February 16, 2026, US District Judge Cynthia Rufe ordered the panels restored while the case went on, and workers began putting them back three days later.

Lawyers for the federal government appealed, which means they asked a higher court to change the ruling. On June 18, 2026, judges of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that the city had no right to decide what the exhibit showed. On July 15 officials of the Trump administration replaced the old panels with their own.

The new panels still describe the nine enslaved people, but they leave out some of the old panels' detail, such as a map of the routes of the slave trade and a timeline of slavery. As of September 2026, the administration's panels were in place, and the city's case was still going on in the lower court.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Old landmarks, new names and owners" -->
Sugarloaf Mound, on the south side of St. Louis, is the only one left of the roughly 40 mounds that Native people built in the city. People dug away all the others as the city grew, Big Mound and its 32 burials among them, as 1850 to 1900 describes. Someone built a house on top of Sugarloaf Mound in 1928.

**Back to the Osage.** The Osage Nation bought that house and about a third of the mound in 2009, and had the house torn down in 2017. In 2025, working with an arts group called Counterpublic, the Osage Nation got the last two pieces of the property transferred to it. The Osage Nation's announcement describes Sugarloaf Mound as a sacred Osage site.

Afterward Andrea Hunter, the Osage Nation's Historic Preservation Officer, said, "It has been a long 17-year journey, and I am thrilled to finally see the entire mound come under Osage Nation control." She said her office must now "remove all the structures from the properties and stabilize (make firm) the mound." Osage leaders plan to build a center there to teach visitors about the mound.

**New names.** Workers finished restoring the barracks of the Angel Island Immigration Station in California in 2009. On February 22, 2018, President Donald Trump signed a law under which the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial in St. Louis, the grounds of the Gateway Arch, became Gateway Arch National Park. It is the smallest national park in the country.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- Who built the White House and the Capitol, and who was paid for their work? Enslaved men did much of the work, from quarrying the stone to sawing boards and hauling loads. The officials in charge rented them from their owners by the year, and the owners collected the pay.
- How many workers died building Hoover Dam? By the official count, 96. The records list 42 more as dying of pneumonia, deaths that workers blamed on carbon monoxide in the tunnels, so the exact number is not known.
- What has happened to the Confederate monuments since 2015? By April 2025 officials and work crews had taken down, moved or renamed 415 Confederate memorials, from statues to place names, hundreds of them after the killings in Charleston, Charlottesville and Minneapolis. In 2025 federal officials began putting some back.

**What changed:** No one wrote down who built the oldest landmarks here, and since 2000 Americans have built memorials to the enslaved and the murdered and fought over monuments to the Confederacy.

**Five dates to keep**
- October 2, 1672: Workers break ground for the Castillo de San Marcos.
- September 18, 1793: George Washington lays the cornerstone of the Capitol.
- February 28, 1877: Members of Congress take the Black Hills from the Sioux.
- May 24, 1883: The Brooklyn Bridge opens, after at least 21 workers died building it.
- June 17, 2015: Dylann Roof kills nine Black worshippers in Charleston.

**Not known:** Nobody knows how many enslaved people helped to build the Capitol, and two congressional historians wrote that no one ever will.

**Where to go:** The Octagon Earthworks in Newark, Ohio, a golf course from 1910 to 2024, opened to the public on January 1, 2025.
