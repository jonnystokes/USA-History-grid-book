## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans began building monuments to the Revolution and to George Washington. A monument is something built to help people remember a person or an event. Two of them were tall stone pillars: the Bunker Hill Monument near Boston and the Washington Monument in the capital. The people raising both ran out of money and had to stop building. Workers finished the Bunker Hill Monument in 1842. Work on the Washington Monument stopped in 1854 at about 150 feet, and the stump stood unfinished for 25 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two obelisks" -->
Both monuments are obelisks. An obelisk is a tall stone pillar with four sides that narrows toward a pointed top.

The Bunker Hill Monument marks the Battle of Bunker Hill, fought on June 17, 1775. It stands on Breed's Hill in Charlestown, Massachusetts. In 1823 a group of leading citizens of the Boston area formed the Bunker Hill Monument Association to build it.

A cornerstone is the first stone set at a corner of a new building, often in a ceremony. Daniel Webster and the Marquis de Lafayette, a French general, led the ceremony that laid this one on June 17, 1825. That day was the 50th anniversary of the battle. About 40 men who had fought in the battle came, along with about 190 other men who had fought in the Revolutionary War.

The association bought a granite quarry, a pit where stone is cut out of the ground, in Quincy, Massachusetts. Horses pulled the granite blocks three miles to the Neponset River on a railway. A National Park Service article describes it as the first horse-drawn railway built to carry goods as a business. Ships then carried the blocks to Charlestown.

In December 1828, after 19 months of work, the association's money ran out and the work stopped. To pay off its debts, the association sold 10 acres of the old battlefield in 1839. Sarah Josepha Hale organized a Ladies' Fair at Quincy Market in Boston in 1840. The money taken in at the fair came to more than $30,000, about a quarter of the monument's cost. Workers finished the 221-foot granite obelisk in July 1842. It was dedicated, or opened with a public ceremony, on June 17, 1843, with President John Tyler there.

The Washington Monument was begun in the capital on July 4, 1848. About 20,000 people watched members of the Washington National Monument Society lay the cornerstone. By 1854 the society's money was gone, and work stopped at about 150 feet.

That same year, in March 1854, members of the Know-Nothing movement stole a block of marble meant for the monument. Pope Pius IX, the head of the Catholic Church, had given it. The Know-Nothings were people in a political movement who were against Catholics. By the usual account, the thieves threw the stone into the Potomac River. It was never found. From 1855 to 1858 Know-Nothings took control of the monument society. The stump of the monument stood unfinished until 1879.

From 1847 to 1855, workers in Washington put up the Smithsonian Institution Building, built of red sandstone. The architect James Renwick Jr. designed it. Its cornerstone was laid on May 1, 1847. People call it the Castle.

### John Hemmings

> **Who:** An enslaved joiner who made the fine woodwork of Monticello's rebuilt rooms.
> **When and where:** Monticello and Poplar Forest, Virginia. Born 1776, died 1833.

John Hemmings was a joiner, a woodworker who makes the fine wooden parts inside a house, such as doors, trim and furniture. Thomas Jefferson held him in slavery at Monticello, Jefferson's home in Virginia. Hemmings was born in 1776. From the age of 17 he trained under white joiners whom Jefferson hired.

When Jefferson rebuilt Monticello, Hemmings made the fine woodwork of the remodeled rooms and much of the furniture. Hemmings also led the woodwork at Poplar Forest, Jefferson's other house in Virginia.

Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people during his life. In his will he freed five of them. Hemmings was one of the five, and he became free in 1826. Jefferson also left him the tools of the joiner's shop. Hemmings died in 1833.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
When workers finished the Washington Monument in 1884, it was the tallest structure in the world, at 555 feet. In the same half century, workers raised a bronze statue onto the new dome of the Capitol. Others hung the Brooklyn Bridge from cables and set up the Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor. At least 21 workers died building the Brooklyn Bridge. As St. Louis grew, between 1850 and 1904, almost all of the Native mounds that had stood there for centuries were dug away.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Statue of Freedom" -->
On December 2, 1863, workers set the last section of a bronze statue called Freedom on top of the new dome of the Capitol. From 1860 to 1862 the statue was cast at the foundry of Clark Mills, a sculptor who had taught himself. A foundry is a workshop where metal is melted and poured into molds. One of the men who cast it was Philip Reid, whom Mills held in slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-reid-landmarks" name="Philip Reid" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Reid

> **Who:** An enslaved foundry worker who helped cast the Statue of Freedom for the dome of the Capitol, and who was free by the day it was raised.
> **When and where:** Clark Mills' foundry, Washington, 1860 to 1863.

Philip Reid worked out how to take apart the plaster model of the Statue of Freedom so it could be cast in bronze. He was born in slavery in Charleston, South Carolina, in about 1820. Clark Mills bought him there. Mills said he paid $1,200 for Reid "because of his evident talent for the business."

An Italian sculptor on the job refused to show how to take the model apart unless he got a raise in pay. Reid worked out how to separate the sections himself, using a pulley and tackle, a set of ropes and wheels for lifting heavy loads.

Reid earned $1.25 a day, but he kept only Sunday's pay. Mills collected the pay for the other six days of each week. On April 16, 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed the District of Columbia Compensated Emancipation Act. Emancipation means setting enslaved people free, and compensated means paid. When Lincoln signed the law, about 3,100 enslaved people in the capital became free. Owners who had stayed loyal to the Union, the side of the United States government in the Civil War, could be paid up to $300 for each person. Reid received his freedom that day. When the last section of Freedom was set on the dome in December 1863, Reid was a free man. He later signed his name Philip Reed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-reid-landmarks" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Brooklyn Bridge" -->
The Brooklyn Bridge, in New York, opened on May 24, 1883. It is a suspension bridge, which means its roadway hangs from cables strung between tall towers. At least 21 workers died building it, and the counts differ. The historian David McCullough counted 21 deaths. C. C. Martin, an assistant engineer on the bridge, told the *Brooklyn Eagle* newspaper that 27 men died in accidents. E. F. Farrington, the master mechanic, the head mechanic on the job, said the number could be as high as 40. Most of the dead were immigrants.

The towers stand on caissons. A caisson is a huge box, open at the bottom, sunk to the bottom of the river. Men worked inside it, digging out the riverbed in air pumped in under pressure to keep the water out. When men came up out of that air too quickly, bubbles of gas formed in their blood. The bubbles caused great pain in the joints. They could leave a man paralyzed, unable to move parts of his body, or kill him. This illness is called caisson disease, or the bends.

Dr. Andrew Smith, the bridge company's doctor, gave it the name caisson disease. Between January 25 and May 31, 1872, he treated 110 cases among the roughly 600 men who worked in the air under pressure. Three of them died of it.

Caisson disease killed John Myers on April 22, 1872, Patrick McKay eight days later, and Daniel Reardon within the month. After those deaths, Washington Roebling, the chief engineer, stopped digging the New York caisson any deeper.

Other workers died too. John French and Thomas Douglas died before the caisson deaths, and Neil Mullen died in 1877. In 1878 a strand of cable snapped at the Manhattan end and threw Thomas Blake and Harry Supple, two men from Newfoundland, to their deaths. Caisson disease also crippled many workers who lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="roeblings" name="John, Washington, and Emily Roebling" movie="Brooklyn Bridge (1981)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John, Washington, and Emily Roebling

> **Who:** The man who designed the Brooklyn Bridge, the son who took charge of building it, and the son's wife, who carried his instructions to the engineers for 11 years.
> **When and where:** New York, 1869 to 1883.
> **Movie:** *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981), Ken Burns' first documentary, narrated by David McCullough. It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. It is about the bridge and the Roeblings.

John A. Roebling designed the Brooklyn Bridge, but he died before building began. In June 1869 he was measuring the site for a tower when a ferry crushed his foot against the posts that held up the dock. Doctors told him the foot should be cut off. He refused and allowed only a water treatment, with cold water dripped steadily onto the wound. His crushed toes were cut off, and tetanus set in. Tetanus is an infection from a wound that makes the muscles tighten and lock. He died on July 22, 1869.

His son, Washington Roebling, took over as chief engineer. He went down into the caissons with the workers and got caisson disease. He collapsed in 1872 and was left partly paralyzed. For the next ten years he directed the work from a room in Brooklyn Heights, watching the bridge through a telescope.

His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, carried his instructions to the engineers for 11 years. She studied cable construction, the forces on a bridge and the mathematics of bridge building. That let her pass on his orders correctly and answer the engineers' questions. Trustees are people chosen to look after a project or a group's money. When the bridge's trustees tried to remove her husband, she defended him before them. Some writers today call her the bridge's "effective chief engineer," meaning she did much of that job in practice. Washington kept the title and made the engineering decisions from his sickroom. Emily was the person the engineers dealt with every day.

When the bridge opened in 1883, Emily Roebling was the first person to cross it by carriage. She carried a live rooster as a sign of victory.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roeblings" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Washington Monument, finished" -->
In 1876 members of Congress voted to have the federal government take over the unfinished Washington Monument. In 1879 Army engineers led by Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Lincoln Casey started work again. The workers finished the monument on December 6, 1884. On its point they placed a cast aluminum tip that weighed 100 ounces. Aluminum then cost about a dollar an ounce, roughly a day's wage for a laborer, and the whole tip cost $225.

The monument was dedicated on February 21, 1885. At 555 feet, it was the tallest structure in the world until the Eiffel Tower was built in Paris in 1889. About 150 feet up, the color of the marble changes. The stone laid after 1879 came from different beds of rock in the quarries than the stone laid from 1848 to 1854. The line shows where the work stopped for 25 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the Statue of Liberty" -->
People in France gave the money for the Statue of Liberty, and Americans had to raise the money for its pedestal, the tall stone base it stands on. Édouard de Laboulaye proposed the statue in 1865. The sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi designed it.

The statue's finished arm and torch were shown at the Centennial Exhibition, a big fair in Philadelphia in 1876. Centennial means a 100th birthday, and the fair marked 100 years since 1776. From 1877 to 1882 the arm stood in Madison Square Park in New York to draw gifts of money for the pedestal. The engineer Gustave Eiffel joined the work in 1879 and designed the iron frame inside the statue. The statue's copper skin is 3/32 of an inch thick. In the words of the National Park Service, that is "about the thickness of two pennies placed together." The copper hangs on the iron frame.

The architect Richard Morris Hunt designed the pedestal, which was built from 1884 to 1886. Money for it came in slowly until 1885. That year Joseph Pulitzer, who published the *New York World* newspaper, ran a campaign in its pages. More than $100,000 came from about 120,000 donors. Most gave less than a dollar, and many of the gifts were coins from schoolchildren. Every donor's name was printed in the paper.

President Grover Cleveland led the dedication ceremony on October 28, 1886. The statue is 151 feet tall. With its pedestal it stands about 305 feet high.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" name="Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi

> **Who:** The French sculptor who designed the Statue of Liberty.
> **When and where:** Paris, 1870 to 1885. New York Harbor, dedicated October 28, 1886.

Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi worked on the Statue of Liberty for more than 15 years, from 1870 to 1886. He was born in 1834 in Colmar, in the Alsace region of France, and he died in 1904.

In 1871 Bartholdi traveled to the United States. In a little under three months he chose Bedloe's Island in New York Harbor as the site and met many of the leading people in America. He and Laboulaye set up committees in both countries to raise money.

The statue's skin was made of thin copper sheets shaped by hammering, a method called repoussé. The arm and torch were finished in 1876, and the head and shoulders in 1878. That year the head and shoulders were shown at the Paris Universal Exposition, a world's fair. The whole statue was put together in Paris between 1881 and 1884.

Then it was taken apart again. The French ship Isère carried it across the Atlantic Ocean in 350 pieces packed in 214 crates. It arrived on June 17, 1885. Bartholdi came back to New York with a group sent from France for the dedication in 1886.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mounds dug away for fill" -->
St. Louis, Missouri, was once called the Mound City. It was built among about 40 mounds, hills of earth that Native people built between about the years 600 and 1300. As the city grew, people dug away most of them. One group of 25 mounds stood along the north riverfront. By the 1850s all of them were gone except part of the largest, Big Mound.

Big Mound stood about 30 to 34 feet high. In 1869 it was dug away completely, and its earth was sold to the North Missouri Railroad to build up the beds for its tracks. The workers who removed it found 32 burials, the graves of people, in two chambers inside it. No surviving record says what became of the bones. An archaeologist is a scientist who studies the past from the things people left behind, and no archaeologist is known to have recorded the digging. The history of the mounds written for the Landmarks Association of St. Louis does not name the men who dug the mound out or the person who sold its earth.

Before the World's Fair of 1904 in Forest Park, two more groups of mounds along the River des Peres were destroyed, 16 mounds in all. That same history does not name who destroyed them.

One mound is left in St. Louis, Sugarloaf Mound, on the south side of the city. In 1928 someone built a house on top of it. The Osage Nation, a Native American nation, bought that house and about a third of the mound in 2009 and tore the house down in 2017. In 2025 the Osage Nation, working with an arts group called Counterpublic, got the rest of the mound back. The Osage Nation's announcement describes Sugarloaf Mound as a sacred Osage site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The largest private house" -->
George W. Vanderbilt had Biltmore House built in Asheville, North Carolina, between 1889 and 1895. It has 250 rooms and covers about 179,000 square feet. It is still the largest privately owned house in the United States. Vanderbilt opened it to his family on Christmas Eve, 1895.
