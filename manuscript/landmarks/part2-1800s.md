## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1800 and 1850, Americans began building monuments to their own short history. The two largest were tall stone pillars: the Bunker Hill Monument near Boston and the Washington Monument in the capital. Work on both stopped when the people raising them ran out of money. The Bunker Hill Monument was finished in 1842. The Washington Monument stood unfinished for 25 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two obelisks" -->
Both monuments are obelisks. An obelisk is a tall stone pillar with four sides that narrows toward a pointed top.

The Bunker Hill Monument marks the Battle of Bunker Hill, fought on June 17, 1775. It stands on Breed's Hill in Charlestown, Massachusetts. In 1823 a group of leading citizens of the Boston area formed the Bunker Hill Monument Association to build it. The cornerstone was laid on June 17, 1825, the 50th anniversary of the battle. About 40 men who had fought in the battle attended. The association bought a granite quarry in Quincy, Massachusetts. Horses pulled the granite blocks three miles to the Neponset River on the first commercial horse-drawn railway, and ships carried them to Charlestown.

In December 1828, after 19 months of work, the association's money ran out and the work stopped. In 1839 its leaders sold 10 acres of the old battlefield to pay off debts. In 1840 Sarah Josepha Hale organized a Ladies' Fair at Quincy Market in Boston. The fair brought in more than $30,000, about a quarter of the monument's cost. Workers finished the 221-foot granite obelisk in July 1842. It was dedicated on June 17, 1843, with President John Tyler there.

The Washington Monument was begun in the capital on July 4, 1848. About 20,000 people watched members of the Washington National Monument Society lay the cornerstone. By 1854 the society's money was gone, and work stopped at about 150 feet.

That same year, in March 1854, members of the Know-Nothing movement stole a block of marble that Pope Pius IX, the head of the Catholic Church, had given for the monument. The Know-Nothings were a political movement that opposed Catholics. By the usual account, the thieves threw the stone into the Potomac River. It was never found. From 1855 to 1858 Know-Nothings took control of the monument society. The stump of the monument stood unfinished until 1879.

In the same years, workers in Washington put up the first building of the Smithsonian Institution, a national museum. The architect James Renwick Jr. designed it. Its cornerstone was laid on May 1, 1847, and it was built of red sandstone between 1847 and 1855. People call it the Castle.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### John Hemmings

> **Who:** An enslaved joiner who made the fine woodwork of Monticello's rebuilt rooms.
> **When and where:** Monticello and Poplar Forest, Virginia. Born 1776, died 1833.

John Hemmings was a joiner, a woodworker who makes fine inside woodwork such as moldings, doors and furniture. Thomas Jefferson held him in slavery at Monticello. Hemmings was born in 1776. From the age of 17 he trained under white joiners whom Jefferson hired.

When Jefferson rebuilt Monticello, Hemmings made the fine woodwork of the remodeled rooms and much of the furniture. Hemmings also led the joinery at Poplar Forest, Jefferson's retreat house in Virginia.

In his will, Jefferson freed only five of the people he enslaved. Hemmings was one of them, and he became free in 1826. Jefferson also left him the tools of the joiner's shop. Hemmings died in 1833.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
When workers finished the Washington Monument in 1884, it was the tallest structure in the world, at 555 feet. In the same half century, workers finished the new dome of the Capitol, hung the Brooklyn Bridge from cables and set up the Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor. At least 21 workers died building the Brooklyn Bridge. Between 1850 and 1904, builders in St. Louis dug away almost all of the Native mounds that had stood there for centuries.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Statue of Freedom" -->
On December 2, 1863, workers set the last section of a bronze statue called Freedom on top of the new dome of the Capitol. From 1860 to 1862 the statue was cast at the foundry of Clark Mills, a sculptor who had taught himself. A foundry is a workshop where metal is melted and poured into molds. One of the men who cast it was Philip Reid, whom Mills held in slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="philip-reid-landmarks" name="Philip Reid" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Philip Reid

> **Who:** An enslaved foundry worker who helped cast the Statue of Freedom for the dome of the Capitol, and who was free by the day it was raised.
> **When and where:** Clark Mills' foundry, Washington, 1860 to 1863.

Philip Reid worked out how to take apart the plaster model of the Statue of Freedom so it could be cast in bronze. He was born in slavery in Charleston, South Carolina, in about 1820. Clark Mills bought him there. Mills said he paid $1,200 for Reid "because of his evident talent for the business."

The plaster model had to be moved from the Capitol to the foundry, and no one knew how to take it apart. An Italian sculptor on the job refused to show how unless he got a raise in pay. Reid found the seams between the sections himself, using a pulley and tackle, a set of ropes and wheels for lifting heavy loads.

Reid earned $1.25 a day, but he kept only Sunday's pay. Mills collected the pay for the other six days of each week. On April 16, 1862, President Abraham Lincoln signed a law that freed the enslaved people of the District of Columbia. When the last section of Freedom was set on the dome in December 1863, Reid was a free man. He later signed his name Philip Reed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="philip-reid-landmarks" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Brooklyn Bridge" -->
The Brooklyn Bridge, in New York, opened on May 24, 1883. It is a suspension bridge, which means its roadway hangs from cables strung between tall towers. At least 21 workers died building it, and the counts differ. The historian David McCullough counted 21 deaths. C. C. Martin, an assistant engineer on the bridge, told the *Brooklyn Eagle* newspaper that 27 men died in accidents. E. F. Farrington, the master mechanic, said the number could be as high as 40. Most of the dead were immigrants.

The towers stand on caissons. A caisson is a huge box, open at the bottom, sunk to the bottom of the river. Men worked inside it, digging out the riverbed in air pumped in under pressure to keep the water out. When men came up out of that air too quickly, many got caisson disease, also called the bends. It could cripple, paralyze or kill. Caisson disease killed John Myers on April 22, 1872. It killed Patrick McKay eight days later, and Daniel Reardon within the month. After those deaths, Washington Roebling, the chief engineer, stopped digging the New York caisson any deeper.

Other workers died in accidents. John French and Thomas Douglas died before the caisson deaths, and Neil Mullen died in 1877. In 1878 a strand of cable snapped at the Manhattan end and threw Thomas Blake and Harry Supple, two men from Newfoundland, to their deaths. Caisson disease also crippled many workers who lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="roeblings" name="John, Washington, and Emily Roebling" movie="Brooklyn Bridge (1981)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John, Washington, and Emily Roebling

> **Who:** The man who designed the Brooklyn Bridge, the son who took charge of building it, and the son's wife, who carried his instructions to the engineers for 11 years.
> **When and where:** New York, 1869 to 1883.
> **Movie:** *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981), Ken Burns' first documentary, narrated by David McCullough. It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. It is about the bridge and the Roeblings.

John A. Roebling designed the Brooklyn Bridge, but he died before building began. In June 1869 he was surveying the site for a tower when a ferry crushed his foot against the pilings of the dock. He refused the usual medical treatment. A few weeks later he died of tetanus, an infection from a wound that makes the muscles tighten and lock.

His son, Washington Roebling, took over as chief engineer. He went down into the caissons with the workers and got caisson disease. He collapsed in 1872 and was left partly paralyzed. For the next ten years he directed the work from a room in Brooklyn Heights, watching the bridge through a telescope.

His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, carried his instructions to the engineers for 11 years. She studied cable construction, the forces on a bridge and the mathematics of bridge building so that she could pass on his orders correctly and answer the engineers' questions. When the bridge's trustees tried to remove her husband, she defended him before them. Some writers today call her the bridge's real chief engineer. Washington kept the title and made the engineering decisions from his sickroom. Emily was the person the engineers dealt with every day.

When the bridge opened in 1883, Emily Roebling was the first person to cross it by carriage. She carried a live rooster as a sign of victory.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="roeblings" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Washington Monument, finished" -->
In 1876 members of Congress voted to have the federal government take over the unfinished Washington Monument. In 1879 Army engineers led by Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Lincoln Casey, of the Army Corps of Engineers, started work again. The workers set the top stone on December 6, 1884. On its point they placed a cast aluminum tip that weighed 100 ounces. Aluminum then cost about a dollar an ounce, roughly a day's wage for a laborer, and the whole tip cost $225.

The monument was dedicated on February 21, 1885. At 555 feet, it was the tallest structure in the world until the Eiffel Tower was built in Paris in 1889. About 150 feet up, the color of the marble changes. The stone laid after 1879 came from different beds of rock in the quarries than the stone laid from 1848 to 1854. The line shows where the work stopped for 25 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building the Statue of Liberty" -->
The Statue of Liberty was paid for with money raised in France, and Americans had to raise the money for its pedestal, the tall stone base it stands on. Édouard de Laboulaye proposed the statue in 1865. The sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi designed it.

The statue's finished right arm and torch were shown at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. From 1877 to 1882 the arm stood in Madison Square Park in New York to draw gifts of money for the pedestal. The engineer Gustave Eiffel joined the work in 1879 and designed the iron skeleton inside the statue. The statue's copper skin is 3/32 of an inch thick. The National Park Service compares it to the thickness of two pennies placed together. The copper hangs on the iron frame.

The architect Richard Morris Hunt designed the pedestal, which was built from 1884 to 1886. Money for it came in slowly until 1885. That year Joseph Pulitzer, who published the *New York World* newspaper, ran a campaign in its pages. More than $100,000 came from about 120,000 donors. Most gave less than a dollar, and many of the gifts were coins from schoolchildren. The paper printed the name of every donor.

President Grover Cleveland presided at the dedication on October 28, 1886. The statue is 151 feet tall. With its pedestal it stands about 305 feet high.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" name="Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi

> **Who:** The French sculptor who designed the Statue of Liberty.
> **When and where:** Paris, 1870 to 1885. New York Harbor, dedicated October 28, 1886.

Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi worked on the Statue of Liberty for more than 15 years, from 1870 to 1886. He was born in 1834 in Colmar, in the Alsace region of France, and he died in 1904. He began designing the statue in 1870.

In 1871 Bartholdi traveled to the United States. In a little under three months he chose Bedloe's Island in New York Harbor as the site and met some of the most influential people in America. He and Laboulaye set up committees in both countries to raise money.

Bartholdi's workers made the statue's skin by repoussé, a method of shaping thin copper sheets by hammering them. The arm and torch were finished in 1876, and the head and shoulders in 1878. That year the head and shoulders were shown at the Paris Universal Exposition, a world's fair. The whole statue was put together in Paris between 1881 and 1884.

Then it was taken apart again. The French ship Isère carried it across the Atlantic Ocean in 350 pieces packed in 214 crates. It arrived on June 17, 1885. Bartholdi came back to New York with a French delegation for the dedication in 1886.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederic-auguste-bartholdi" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mounds dug away for fill" -->
St. Louis, Missouri, was once called the Mound City. It was built among about 40 Native mounds, made between about the years 600 and 1300. As the city grew, builders dug away most of them for their earth. One group of 25 mounds stood along the north riverfront. By the 1850s all of them were gone except part of the largest, Big Mound.

Big Mound stood about 30 to 34 feet high. In 1869 it was dug away completely, and its earth was sold to the North Missouri Railroad to build up the beds for its tracks. The workers who removed it found 32 burials, the graves of people, in two chambers inside it. The records do not name who dug the mound out or who sold its earth.

Before the World's Fair of 1904 in Forest Park, two more groups of mounds along the River des Peres were destroyed, 16 mounds in all. The records do not name the people who destroyed them. One mound is left in St. Louis, Sugarloaf Mound, on the south side of the city. In 1928 someone built a house on top of it. The Osage Nation bought that house and about a third of the mound in 2009 and tore the house down in 2017. In 2025 the Osage Nation, working with an arts group called Counterpublic, got the rest of the mound back. In its announcement, the Osage Nation called Sugarloaf Mound a sacred Osage site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The largest private house" -->
George W. Vanderbilt had Biltmore House built in Asheville, North Carolina, between 1889 and 1895. It has 250 rooms and covers about 179,000 square feet. It is still the largest privately owned house in the United States. Vanderbilt opened it to his family on Christmas Eve, 1895.
