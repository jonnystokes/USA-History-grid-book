## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 11 minutes**

Native leaders founded national organizations of their own in these fifty years, the Society of American Indians in 1911 and the National Congress of American Indians in 1944. Federal boarding schools stayed open the whole time.

In Oklahoma in the early 1920s, killers murdered dozens of Osage people for their shares of the nation's oil money, in the years called the Reign of Terror.

Members of Congress made Native people citizens in 1924 and ended allotment, the cutting of nations' shared land into private pieces, in 1934. Native men fought for the United States in both world wars, and some of them sent messages in Choctaw and Navajo.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Organizing" -->
A group of Native doctors, lawyers and writers met in Columbus, Ohio, on October 12, 1911, and founded the Society of American Indians. It was the first national Native rights organization that Native people built and ran themselves. Its leaders included Dr. Charles Eastman, who was Santee Dakota, and Dr. Carlos Montezuma, who was Yavapai-Apache. Zitkala-Ša edited its journal.

Many of the members had gone through the boarding schools, and some of them used that schooling to argue against federal Indian policy. The Society lasted from 1911 to 1923.

Twelve men and one woman founded the Alaska Native Brotherhood at Sitka in 1912. They wanted citizenship for Alaska Natives. The Alaska Native Sisterhood began in 1915. For the next fifty years, these two groups were the only civil rights organizations Alaska Natives had, the only groups working for their right as citizens to equal treatment under the law.

Eighty delegates from more than fifty tribes and associations met in Denver on November 16, 1944, and founded the National Congress of American Indians. Native nations still use it as one of their main ways to act together.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ironworkers from Kahnawake" -->
Mohawk men from Kahnawake had worked on structural iron, the metal frames that hold up bridges and tall buildings, since the 1880s. Kahnawake crews worked on the riveting gangs that put up the Empire State Building in New York in 1930 and 1931. A riveting gang joined the steel beams together with hot metal pins called rivets.

Kahnawake ironworkers kept building towers in Manhattan for generations. One of them, Harold McComber, is the subject of *High Steel*. Don Owen directed that documentary film for the National Film Board of Canada in 1965.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Osage and the Reign of Terror" -->
Osage people in Oklahoma earned so much from oil in the 1920s that they were among the wealthiest people in the world, counted per person. White guardians and some of the men who married Osage women stole that money, and killers murdered Osage people to get it.

Osage leaders under Chief James Bigheart won their own terms in the Osage Allotment Act, which members of Congress passed in 1906. Under the Dawes Act, federal officials had given the head of a family 160 acres, single people and children less, and opened the rest of a nation's land to white settlers. The Osage divided all of their reservation among their own people instead, 657 acres each for 2,229 people, about four times a family head's 160 acres.

**Headrights.** The Osage kept the minerals under the ground as shared property. Oil companies paid royalties, which are payments for the oil they took out. Each Osage member got an equal share of that money. The share was called a headright.

Members of Congress passed a guardianship law in 1921. Under it, judges in local courts appointed white guardians over the money of many Osage adults. The guardian, not the Osage owner, controlled the money. Guardians stole from the people whose money they controlled. Some of the men who married Osage women stole from their wives.

Osage headright holders died in murders and in suspicious deaths, deaths that might have been murders, between about 1920 and 1925. These years are known as the Reign of Terror. Counts of the dead disagree. Osage Nation officials count more than sixty. Writers at the Oklahoma Historical Society count about 24, and FBI writers say at least two dozen.

**The investigation.** Members of the Osage Tribal Council asked the federal government to send detectives in 1923. Agents of the new Bureau of Investigation, which later became the FBI, took the case. They proved that William Hale, a cattle rancher whom people called "the King of the Osage Hills," had ordered the murders of Anna Brown and members of her family so that he could inherit their oil rights, getting them after they died. Hale's nephew, Ernest Burkhart, was married to Anna's sister Mollie.

**The killings.** Anna Brown was shot on Hale's orders in May 1921. Two months later her mother, Lizzie Q. Kyle, died, and poison was suspected. In January 1923 Hale had Anna's cousin Henry Roan killed for insurance money. In March 1923 a bomb blew up the house of Anna's sister Rita Smith, killing Rita, her husband Bill and their servant, Nettie Brookshire.

**Who went to prison.** Burkhart pleaded guilty in 1926 and was sentenced to life in prison. Hale was found guilty in 1929 and sent to prison for life. Two other men, Kelsie Morrison and John Ramsey, were also sentenced to life. Hale, Ramsey and Burkhart were all later let out on parole, which means freed early under watch.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Citizenship and the end of allotment" -->
Under the Indian Citizenship Act, which became law on June 2, 1924, every Native person born in the United States became a citizen. In some states they still could not vote, because each state's lawmakers wrote that state's voting rules. The Library of Congress records that officials in some states kept Native people from voting until 1957.

A federal survey called the Meriam Report came out in 1928. Its full title was *The Problem of Indian Administration*. It records that under allotment, tribal land had fallen from 137 million acres to 47 million. It also records the poverty and disease Native people lived with after that loss.

The Meriam Report's writers condemned the boarding schools in detail, judging them wrong and showing why. One of the things they condemned was the half-day system, under which school staff made children spend half of every school day on unpaid work that kept the schools running.

The Indian Reorganization Act became law on June 18, 1934. It was written while John Collier was Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the federal official in charge of dealings with Native nations, a job he held from 1933 to 1945. In the act, members of Congress ended allotment and extended the protections on land held in trust, land the United States holds on behalf of a Native nation or person.

Members of Congress also allowed up to $2,000,000 a year in the act for buying land for Native people. Nations could now write constitutions and set up their own governments. After 1934 federal officials began to recognize the governments that nations set up under the act. For the forty-seven years before, since 1887, federal officials had used allotment to break nations up into single landowners.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The schools stay open" -->
Federal boarding schools kept running after 1900. The federal investigators counted the schools that ran between 1819 and 1969, and some kept going after that. Enrollment, the number of children in the schools, kept climbing for decades and reached its peak of about 60,000 children in 1973.

Collier and the officials under him moved toward day schools, where children went home at night, in the 1930s. They allowed some teaching in Native languages, and they closed some boarding schools. Federal officials still took tens of thousands of children from their homes to the schools every year.

One of those children was Chester Nez, a Navajo boy at the Fort Defiance boarding school, who later helped build the Marines' code in Navajo.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why could no enemy read the code?" -->
Code talkers were Native soldiers who sent military messages in their own languages, which the enemy could not understand. The first code talkers were Choctaw.

American soldiers were fighting in the Meuse-Argonne campaign, a long series of battles in France, in the autumn of 1918. German soldiers were tapping American telephone lines. They were also capturing about one in four runners, the soldiers who carried messages on foot. Choctaw soldiers of the 36th Division began passing orders in Choctaw.

They sent the first secure messages, ones the Germans could not read, on October 26, 1918. When Choctaw had no word for a piece of equipment, the men made one up. A machine gun became "little gun shoot fast."

In the Second World War, Marine Corps officers had Navajo speakers do the same work. Twenty-nine Navajo recruits arrived in San Diego on May 5, 1942, and built the code. Between 350 and 420 Navajo served as code talkers, in all six Marine divisions in the Pacific. At Iwo Jima, Major Howard Connor was the 5th Division's signal officer, in charge of its messages. He said: "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima."

No enemy ever broke the code. It stayed classified, meaning officially secret, until 1968. The original twenty-nine received the Congressional Gold Medal, a medal that members of Congress vote to give, on July 26, 2001, and the code talkers who came after them received silver medals.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Jim Thorpe

> **Who:** Sac and Fox athlete and Carlisle student, the first Native American to win an Olympic gold medal.
> **When and where:** Stockholm, July 1912. Olympic officials took his medals in 1913 and named him the sole champion again in 2022.
> **Movie:** *Jim Thorpe – All-American* (1951)

**Olympic officials took his gold medals over two dollars a game.**

Jim Thorpe was a citizen of the Sac and Fox Nation and a student at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, the federal boarding school in Pennsylvania. There he was twice named a football All-American, one of the best college football players in the country.

He won the pentathlon, a contest of five track and field events, on July 7, and the decathlon, a contest of ten, on July 15, at the Stockholm Olympics in 1912. His decathlon score stood as the world record until 1927.

Olympic officials took both medals back in 1913. Thorpe had played minor-league baseball in 1909 and 1910 for a reported two dollars a game, up to $35 a week. The Olympic rules of the time allowed only amateurs, athletes who are not paid to play.

**Restored.** Officials of the International Olympic Committee named him co-champion in October 1982, sharing the titles with Hugo Wieslander in the decathlon and Ferdinand Bie in the pentathlon. On July 15, 2022, 110 years to the day after his decathlon win, they named him the sole champion of both events.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="ira-hayes" name="Ira Hayes" movie="The Outsider (1961)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Ira Hayes

> **Who:** Akimel O'odham (Pima) Marine from the Gila River community in Arizona, one of the six men who raised the second flag on Mount Suribachi.
> **When and where:** Iwo Jima, February 23, 1945. He died in Arizona on January 24, 1955.
> **Movie:** *The Outsider* (1961). Adam Beach also plays him in *Flags of Our Fathers* (2006).

**He raised the flag on Iwo Jima and died of exposure at 32.**

Ira Hayes, from the Gila River Indian Community in Arizona, was a Marine paratrooper, a soldier trained to jump from planes by parachute. He was one of the six men who raised the second American flag on Mount Suribachi, on Iwo Jima, on February 23, 1945. Americans celebrated the photograph of that flag-raising.

Hayes came home to a fame he had not asked for. He and two other men from the photograph raised a flag at a ceremony in Washington on May 9, 1945. The ceremony started a tour to sell war bonds, loans people made to the government to pay for the war. Later that month the Marines ordered Hayes back to his unit.

Americans celebrated the photograph, but Hayes got little help after the war. Police arrested him 52 times for being drunk in public. Years later President Dwight Eisenhower met him and called him a hero.

Hayes was found dead on the morning of January 24, 1955, near an empty mud-brick hut close to his home at Sacaton, in the Gila River community. He had been drinking the night before. The coroner, the official who decides how a person died, gave the cause as alcohol poisoning and exposure, dying from cold or other harsh weather without shelter. He was 32.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ira-hayes" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chester-nez-native-nations" name="Chester Nez" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Chester Nez

> **Who:** Navajo Marine, one of the twenty-nine who built the code. As a boarding-school student, he was punished for speaking the language the code was made from.
> **When and where:** the Fort Defiance boarding school, Arizona, then the Pacific, 1942 to 1945. He died June 4, 2014.

**He outlived the other twenty-eight who built the Navajo code.**

When Chester Nez spoke Navajo at the Fort Defiance boarding school, staff washed his mouth out with soap.

Marine Corps recruiters signed Nez up in 1942. He was one of the twenty-nine who went to San Diego and built the code out of the language.

He wrote about what happened to him in *Code Talker*, a memoir, or book about the writer's own life, published in 2011 with Judith Schiess Avila. It is the only memoir by one of the original twenty-nine. Nez died on June 4, 2014, the last of the twenty-nine still alive.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 15 minutes**

Native nations spent these fifty years fighting a federal plan to end them as nations, and winning back the running of their own affairs. Members of Congress declared in 1953 that the United States should end its recognition of Native nations, which means its dealings with each nation as a government. Twenty-two years later, in 1975, President Ford signed a law under which Native nations could take control of their own schools, clinics and police.

The Menominee, whose nation members of Congress had terminated, ending its recognition, organized until members of Congress restored it. Fishermen in the Northwest kept fishing until a federal judge ruled on their 1850s treaties. A group called Indians of All Tribes took Alcatraz Island and held it for nineteen months. In 1980, in the Sioux Nation's Black Hills case, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that United States officials had broken the 1868 treaty when they took the hills.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Termination and relocation" -->
Members of Congress passed House Concurrent Resolution 108, a formal statement of policy that they voted on, on August 1, 1953. In it they declared that the United States should end its recognition of Native nations, along with the federal services that came with it. The policy's name was termination. Under it, members of Congress and federal officials stopped treating a tribe as a nation with its own government, and the tribe lost its federal services.

Members of Congress ran about sixty termination proceedings, the formal legal steps that ended a nation's recognition, between 1953 and 1970. Those proceedings covered more than a hundred tribes and bands. More than 3 million acres of land stopped being held in trust, which meant the United States no longer held that land on the nations' behalf.

**The Menominee.** The Menominee of Wisconsin ran a profitable timber business. Members of Congress passed the Menominee termination act in 1954. It took effect in 1961, and the Menominee, who had done well before, became poor. Menominee people organized a movement called DRUMS, with Ada Deer among its leaders. They kept pushing until members of Congress reversed the termination. The Menominee Restoration Act became law on December 22, 1973.

Officials of the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the federal agency in charge of Indian affairs, also moved Native people off reservations and into cities in these years. They began this relocation program between about 1948 and 1952, and members of Congress backed it with the Indian Relocation Act of 1956. People who signed up got a one-way ticket and short-term help. About 30,000 people moved in the 1950s, and more than 100,000 had moved by the time the program ended in the 1970s.

Many of them lived in poverty in the cities. They also built lasting Native communities in Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland and San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Denver. The next Native movement started in those cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Alcatraz and Wounded Knee, 1969 to 1973" -->
George Mitchell, Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt and about 200 other people founded the American Indian Movement, known as AIM, in Minneapolis in July 1968. Its members began with street patrols against police abuse. Many Native people had moved to Minneapolis under relocation.

**Alcatraz.** A group calling itself Indians of All Tribes landed on Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay on November 20, 1969. They occupied the island's federal prison, closed since 1963, moving in and holding it. Richard Oakes, who was Mohawk, was among the leaders. In their proclamation, a public statement, they offered to buy the island for "$24 in glass beads and red cloth." They held it for nineteen months, until June 11, 1971. After the occupation began, President Nixon ended termination as federal policy.

**Blue Lake.** Nixon also signed H.R. 471, a law that members of Congress had passed, on December 15, 1970. Under it, Blue Lake and 48,000 acres of sacred land went back to Taos Pueblo.

**Alaska.** A year after Blue Lake, members of Congress settled the land claims of Alaska Natives, ending them by agreement. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act became law on December 18, 1971. Under it, the claims were settled for 44 million acres and $962.5 million.

That is nearly as much land as the 48 million acres all Native nations together held in 1934. The land and money went to twelve regional corporations, later thirteen, and to more than 200 village corporations. These were businesses, and Alaska Natives became their owners, called shareholders.

**Wounded Knee, 1973.** About 200 Oglala Lakota and AIM members occupied the village of Wounded Knee, on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota, in 1973. They chose it because soldiers of the 7th Cavalry had killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people there in 1890. Of the 146 dead whom a burial party put in one grave there, 62 were women and children.

The occupiers held the village for seventy-one days, from February 27 to May 8, against federal marshals, officers who carry out the orders of the national courts, and agents of the FBI, the national police force that looks into federal crimes. The federal forces surrounded the village with armored vehicles.

**Why they came.** The occupiers were protesting against the treaties that United States officials had broken, and against Richard Wilson, the tribal chairman at Pine Ridge since 1972, who was Oglala himself.

His opponents accused him of corruption, of giving the few jobs and benefits to his family and friends, and of using an armed group of his own, the Guardians of the Oglala Nation, known as the GOONs, to attack people who opposed him. Three weeks before the occupation, members of the tribal council had brought charges against him at a hearing to remove him from office, and Wilson avoided a trial.

Both sides fired shots during the siege, the seventy-one days in which federal forces surrounded the village. Early in the siege, someone shot US Marshal Lloyd Grimm, and afterward he could not move his legs. Accounts of the siege do not say who shot him.

Frank Clearwater, an Eastern Cherokee man, was shot in the head on April 17 while he rested in a church during a gunfight with federal forces. He died on April 25. FBI officials released a report on deaths on the Pine Ridge Reservation in 2000. It found that Clearwater was killed by gunfire from federal officers, who were firing back after people shot at them.

A federal agent firing as a sniper, a hidden marksman, shot and killed Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont, an Oglala man, with a bullet through the heart on April 26.

**The charges.** Judge Fred Nichol threw out the charges against the AIM leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means in 1974. He wrote that "the misconduct by the government in this case is so aggravated that a dismissal must be entered in the interests of justice." He meant that government officials had broken the rules so badly that the case had to end.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fish-ins and the Boldt decision" -->
Leaders of the Northwest nations signed treaties with Isaac Stevens, the governor of Washington Territory, in the 1850s. In them they gave up land and kept the right to fish.

The Treaty of Medicine Creek, signed by the Nisqually, the Puyallup and other nations on December 26, 1854, says that "the right of taking fish, at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations, is further secured to said Indians." Those words promised that they could keep fishing at all the places where they had always fished. A century later, Washington State officers were arresting their people for fishing.

Through the 1960s and into the 1970s, the Nisqually, the Puyallup and other nations held fish-ins. At a fish-in, people fished in the open, in front of the state's officers, and let themselves be arrested. They did it to force the treaties into a courtroom. Frank's Landing on the Nisqually River was the center of the fish-ins.

Judge George Boldt heard the treaty case, *United States v. Washington*, in federal court. He ruled on February 12, 1974, that the treaty rights still stood. Under them, the tribes had a right to half the harvestable salmon, the salmon that could be caught each year.

Boldt also ruled that the tribes were co-managers of the fishery, the salmon catch and the waters it comes from, so that tribal governments and the state run it together. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld the ruling in 1979, which means they let it stand.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why do the Lakota refuse more than a billion dollars?" -->
The Lakota have refused the money that Supreme Court justices awarded them for the Black Hills in 1980, which with the interest it has earned is now more than a billion dollars. Lakota leaders say that the Black Hills were never for sale.

United States officials guaranteed the Black Hills of South Dakota to the Lakota in the Fort Laramie treaty of 1868. Members of an Army expedition led by George Custer announced in 1874 that they had found gold there. United States officials ignored the treaty and took the hills.

The Lakota call one mountain in the Black Hills Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers. It is the mountain known in English as Mount Rushmore.

The justices of the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Sioux Nation*, in 1980, that United States officials had broken the treaty when they took the hills. They awarded the Lakota compensation, money paid to make up for a loss.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Self-rule, children taken and women sterilized" -->
President Ford signed the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act in January 1975, self-determination meaning a nation deciding its own affairs. Under the act, a nation could sign a contract with the federal government and run federal programs itself, such as its own schools, clinics and police. The word Education is in the act's name because schools were at the center of it.

**Children taken.** While nations were taking over their own programs, workers at agencies were still taking Native children from their families, as they had for decades before 1978. Writers at the National Indian Child Welfare Association state that 25 to 35 percent of Native children were taken, between one in four and one in three. They also state that 85 percent of them were placed outside their families, even when relatives or members of their tribe could care for them.

Members of Congress passed the Indian Child Welfare Act in 1978. In it they set federal standards for those cases, meant to keep Native children with Native families. They also gave tribal courts the power to decide the cases.

**Sterilized.** Doctors at hospitals of the Indian Health Service, the federal agency that runs hospitals and clinics for Native people, sterilized thousands of Native women in the same years. Sterilization is an operation after which a woman can never become pregnant. The doctors at these hospitals did two kinds of operation. In a tubal ligation, a surgeon cuts or ties the two tubes that carry eggs to the womb. In a hysterectomy, the surgeon cuts out the womb.

Investigators from the General Accounting Office, a federal office, studied 4 of the agency's 12 areas. In 1976 they reported 3,406 sterilizations of Native women there from 1973 to 1976. Writers at the National Library of Medicine state that the women had not given their permission. The investigators' report does not name the doctors. No surviving record says how many of the women had each kind of operation.

Thirty-six of the women were under 21, although a court had ordered a stop to sterilizing women that young. Two years before the report, Dr. Connie Pinkerton-Uri, a Choctaw and Cherokee doctor, had found that one in four Native women had been sterilized without agreeing to it.

**Gaming.** Ten years after the child welfare law, members of Congress set rules for gaming run by Native nations, meaning gambling businesses such as casinos. Their Indian Gaming Regulatory Act became law on October 17, 1988. For some nations gaming became a large source of income, and the amount varies a great deal from nation to nation.

Under the act, tribal gaming falls into three classes of games, and a nation and a state can make a compact, a formal agreement about the games. In the act, members of Congress also set up the National Indian Gaming Commission to watch over tribal gaming.

President George H. W. Bush signed the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, known as NAGPRA, on November 16, 1990. Under it, Native human remains and sacred objects held in museums and federal collections belong to the nations and families they came from, and the people holding them must send them home, which the law's name calls repatriation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Nations take the schools back" -->
Navajo leaders founded a college of their own at Tsaile, Arizona, in 1968. It was called Navajo Community College, and today it is Diné College. It was the first college in the country controlled by a Native nation. Native nations have founded dozens of tribal colleges since.

Senators on a Senate subcommittee on Indian education, a small group of senators assigned to that one subject, published their report in November 1969. The report's title is *Indian Education: A National Tragedy, A National Challenge*. It is known as the Kennedy Report, because Senator Robert F. Kennedy led the work at first and Senator Edward Kennedy finished it.

The report states that federal policy had been "coercive assimilation," forcing a group of people to give up their own language and ways of life, with "disastrous effects." Its authors called for Native people to run Native schools.

Boarding-school enrollment peaked at about 60,000 children in 1973. After that it fell, as the leaders of Native nations took over schools under the 1975 law and other schools closed. A few boarding schools are still open, run by tribes or by the Bureau of Indian Education, the federal office in charge of Indian schools. People who had been children at the schools began publishing accounts of what had happened to them there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="wilma-mankiller" name="Wilma Mankiller" movie="Mankiller (2017)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Wilma Mankiller

> **Who:** the first woman elected Principal Chief of the Cherokee Nation.
> **When and where:** Cherokee Nation, Oklahoma. She was chief from December 14, 1985 to August 14, 1995.
> **Movie:** *Mankiller* (2017)

**Cherokee voters re-elected her in 1991 with 83 percent of the vote.**

Wilma Mankiller was a child when her family moved to San Francisco under the relocation program. She joined the movement during the occupation of Alcatraz by Indians of All Tribes in 1969.

She went back to Oklahoma. There she worked on projects in which Cherokee communities built their own water lines and health clinics, with the people who lived there doing the work.

She became Principal Chief of the Cherokee Nation, the head of the nation's government, on December 14, 1985, when Ross Swimmer left the office. Cherokee voters elected her in her own right in 1987 and re-elected her in 1991. Until August 14, 1995, she led a nation of about 140,000 citizens.

She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, an award given by the president of the United States, in 1998. Mankiller died on April 6, 2010.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="billy-frank-jr-native-nations" name="Billy Frank Jr." movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Billy Frank Jr.

> **Who:** Nisqually fisherman, a leader of the fish-ins, who later chaired the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission.
> **When and where:** Frank's Landing on the Nisqually River, Washington. He was first arrested at fourteen.

**Officers arrested him more than fifty times for fishing the Nisqually.**

Billy Frank Jr. was fourteen, in 1945, the first time officers arrested him for fishing the Nisqually River. He kept fishing through the fish-ins of the 1960s and 1970s, and he went to jail more than fifty times.

After the Boldt decision of 1974, Frank chaired the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission.

He died in May 2014 and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom after his death, in 2015. As of 2026, Washington State officials planned to place his statue in the National Statuary Hall in the United States Capitol.

### Adam Fortunate Eagle

> **Who:** Ojibwe writer. He was a Pipestone boarding-school student who became a lead organizer of the Alcatraz occupation.
> **When and where:** Pipestone Indian Boarding School, Minnesota, 1935 to 1945. San Francisco Bay, 1969 to 1971.

**He called his boarding-school days "a little bit of heaven."**

Adam Fortunate Eagle spent ten years at the Pipestone Indian Boarding School in Minnesota, from 1935 to 1945. In those years John Collier was Commissioner of Indian Affairs, and federal officials were changing some of the schools. Three decades later, Fortunate Eagle was one of the lead organizers of the occupation of Alcatraz.

He published his account of the school, *Pipestone: My Life in an Indian Boarding School*, in 2010. The line about heaven is from that book. In the same book he writes about the harm the boarding-school system did.

Other people who were children at the schools told it differently. Chester Nez was punished with soap for speaking Navajo at Fort Defiance, and Zitkala-Ša's essays of 1900 criticized her school.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 10 minutes**

Native nations govern themselves today. There are 574 federally recognized Native nations in the United States, which means that United States officials deal with each one as a government. There are also nations recognized by their states, and nations still working to win federal recognition. More than 200 Alaska Native villages have their own corporations. Native Hawaiians have no federally recognized government of their own.

Census takers counted 9.7 million people who identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, alone or together with another group, in 2020. That was 2.9 percent of the people in the country, and almost forty times the 250,000 counted in 1900.

The Navajo Nation's land covers more than 27,000 square miles across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. It is the largest reservation in the country. The number of Cherokee Nation citizens passed 450,000 in 2023, counting enrolled citizens, people officially listed as members. Some counts in 2024 and 2025 put it above 475,000.

The National Museum of the American Indian opened on the National Mall in Washington on September 21, 2004. About 25,000 Native people from across North and South America walked in the opening procession.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sovereignty in court and in office" -->
Justices of the Supreme Court ruled on a question of Native sovereignty, a nation's right to govern itself, in 2020. When United States officials forced the Muscogee (Creek) Nation to move to Oklahoma, they promised it a reservation there. The justices decided *McGirt v. Oklahoma*, a case about whether that reservation still existed, on July 9, 2020. Five of the nine justices held that members of Congress had never ended it. Four justices disagreed.

Under the ruling, about 3.25 million acres of eastern Oklahoma, including much of Tulsa, are still reservation land. Because of that, Oklahoma's state officials can no longer put Native people on trial for major crimes there. Those cases go to federal and tribal courts instead. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion, the written decision of the court. Its first sentence is: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise."

The Trail of Tears was the forced removal of the Cherokee and the other Southeastern nations to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma, in the 1830s. In later rulings, judges applied the same reasoning to the reservations of the other nations that United States officials had forced out of the Southeast.

Seven of the nine justices upheld the Indian Child Welfare Act, which means they kept it in force, in *Haaland v. Brackeen* on June 15, 2023. The people challenging the act had claimed that it broke the Constitution. Two justices disagreed with the ruling.

Deb Haaland of Laguna Pueblo and Sharice Davids, who is Ho-Chunk, became the first Native American women elected to Congress in 2018. In 2021 Haaland became the first Native American cabinet secretary, as Secretary of the Interior, the head of the federal department in charge of the government's dealings with Native nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Standing Rock, and land returned" -->
Leaders of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe opposed the Dakota Access Pipeline, a long pipe built to carry oil, in 2016. Its route ran under Lake Oahe, upstream of the reservation. The lake is the reservation's water supply.

People set up camps beginning in April 2016, among them one called Oceti Sakowin. Young people carried the tribe's message on foot. They ran a 500-mile relay to the office of the Army Corps of Engineers, the part of the army whose permission the pipeline needed, in Omaha, and then a 2,000-mile run to Washington, D.C. Thousands of people came to the camps. News reports at the time called the camps the largest gathering of Native nations in more than a century.

North Dakota's governor, Jack Dalrymple, declared an emergency in August 2016 and put the Morton County Sheriff's Office and other police forces under one command. On November 20, 2016, at the Backwater Bridge, officers fired tear gas, rubber bullets and bags filled with lead into a crowd of protesters. They also sprayed the protesters with a fire hose.

**Sophia Wilansky.** A protester named Sophia Wilansky was hit that night. In 2026 federal appeals judges wrote out her account of what happened, in a ruling on her lawsuit. In that account, three officers fired rounds at her as she ducked behind a metal sheet, and one hit her arm. She ran about 30 yards, and as she bent to pick up a piece of plywood, a round fired by Officer Jonathan Moll hit her.

The judges described the round, called an aerial warning munition, as an exploding flash-bang inside a bullet made to hurt rather than kill. "It tore the flesh off her arm, and she fell to the ground," the judges wrote. "Her left hand and forearm are permanently and severely injured." Federal judges threw out her lawsuits and those of the other injured protesters, and no officer was held responsible.

An Army official, the Assistant Secretary for Civil Works, turned down the easement, the legal permission the pipeline needed to cross under the lake, on December 4, 2016. That same week, more than 2,000 United States military veterans arrived to stand with the camps.

**The oil flows.** President Donald Trump ordered the Army to approve the pipeline quickly in January 2017. Army officials granted the easement two weeks later, and oil has moved through the pipeline since that June. A federal judge cancelled the easement in 2020, but the oil kept flowing for about five years without one. Army officials granted the easement again in 2026, and the tribe's leaders then asked the court to end their own appeal.

**Land returned.** Some land has gone back to Native nations. Federal officials took 18,800 acres out of the Flathead Reservation in Montana in 1908 for the National Bison Range, a federal wildlife refuge, land set aside to protect wild animals. Members of Congress passed a law in December 2020 that gave the range back to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, and the tribes took over running it in 2022.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding-school investigation" -->
Secretary Haaland started an investigation of the boarding schools in June 2021. Earlier officials of her own department had run them.

The first volume of the investigators' report came out in May 2022. It identifies 408 federal schools, and marked and unmarked burial sites at 53 of them. The final volume came out on July 30, 2024. It lists 417 federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states or places that were then territories, open from 1819 to 1969. It also lists 1,025 more places with similar aims that fell outside the four rules the investigators used to count federal schools.

The report records what school staff did to the children. Staff cut the children's hair, replaced their clothes with uniforms, gave them English names and forbade them to speak the languages they had grown up speaking. The punishments the report records for speaking those languages include beatings, solitary confinement, which means being locked up alone, holding back food, and washing children's mouths out with soap.

At least 973 children died at the schools. The investigators found at least 74 burial sites at 65 schools. The report states that the real number of deaths is higher than the number the investigators could count.

President Biden apologized on behalf of the United States for the boarding-school system on October 25, 2024. He spoke at the Gila River Indian Community in Arizona, which was Ira Hayes's home community. It was the first formal presidential apology for the system. He called the system "a sin on our soul."

**Coming home.** Children who died at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School are buried in a cemetery at the Carlisle Barracks, an Army post. Army officials have been digging up the children's remains and returning them to their nations.

Army officials returned two children, Little Chief and Horse, to the Northern Arapaho in 2017. When they opened the grave of a third child, Little Plume, they found two other children in it. They found Little Plume and returned him the next year. Nine Rosebud Sioux children went home to South Dakota in July 2021, and Rosebud young people escorted them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools in Native languages" -->
Boarding-school staff punished children for speaking Native languages. In the two world wars, soldiers used two of those languages, Choctaw and Navajo, to send messages the enemy could not read. Today Native nations run schools that teach children in their own languages.

Hawaii's lawmakers banned teaching in Hawaiian in a law dating from 1896. The first Pūnana Leo immersion preschool, a school where teachers give every lesson in the language itself, opened at Kekaha on Kaua'i in 1984. Children there learn arithmetic and reading in Hawaiian instead of learning Hawaiian as one subject. The Pūnana Leo schools were the first immersion preschools in the United States in an Indigenous language, a language of the people who lived in a place first.

The ban on teaching in Hawaiian was lifted in 1986, and the first public immersion classes opened in 1987. According to ʻAha Pūnana Leo, the group that started the Pūnana Leo schools, students can now be taught in Hawaiian from preschool through graduate school, the university study that comes after a first degree.

Wôpanâak, the Wampanoag language of Massachusetts, had no living speakers after the middle of the 1800s. Jessie Little Doe Baird helped found the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, reclamation meaning taking something back, in 1993. She began rebuilding the language from documents written in it.

Wôpanâak is the first American language with no living speakers to come back into living use. The project opened Mukayuhsak Weekuw, the Children's House, in September 2016. It is a school in Mashpee where Wampanoag children are taught mostly in Wôpanâak.

Cherokee, Lakota, Diné and many other nations run immersion schools of their own.

### Jessie Little Doe Baird

> **Who:** Mashpee Wampanoag social worker who became a linguist and brought back a language that no one had spoken since the middle of the 1800s.
> **When and where:** Mashpee, Massachusetts, from 1993.
> **Movie:** *We Still Live Here: Âs Nutayuneân* (2011)

**She rebuilt a language using a Bible printed in 1663.**

By her own account, the work began with a dream about the language.

Among the written records of the language was the Eliot Bible, a Bible printed in Wôpanâak in 1663. Baird used that Bible and the other documents to rebuild the language.

After she helped found the project in 1993, Baird went to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and earned a degree in linguistics, the study of how languages work. Her daughter grew up speaking Wôpanâak. In 2010 the MacArthur Foundation gave Baird a MacArthur Fellowship, a prize for people's work.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- How did Native nations govern themselves and deal with other nations before the United States existed? They ran their own councils and laws, as the Haudenosaunee did under the Great Law of Peace. Their leaders made alliances and treaties and recorded them on wampum belts.
- What did United States officials and soldiers do to take the nations' land and break the nations up? Soldiers forced whole nations west and onto reservations, and killed hundreds of people at a time at Sand Creek, Wounded Knee and other places. Officials cut the nations' land from about 138 million acres to 48 million and took children to boarding schools, where at least 973 died.
- How did Native nations hold on to their governments, their languages and some of their land? They went to court, from *Worcester v. Georgia* in 1832 to *McGirt v. Oklahoma* in 2020. They organized until members of Congress restored the Menominee, and they kept their languages, first in secret and now in schools of their own.

**What changed:** Nations that held nearly the whole continent in the 1600s had lost most of it by 1900, and after 1950 they won back the running of their own governments, schools and courts.

**Five dates to keep**
- 1680: Pueblo fighters drive the Spanish out of New Mexico.
- 1838: Soldiers force the Cherokee west on the Trail of Tears.
- 1887: Allotment begins under the Dawes Act.
- 1974: Judge George Boldt upholds the Northwest fishing treaties.
- 2020: Five justices rule that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation never ended.

**Not known:** whether anyone carried out Oñate's 1599 sentence to cut off the Acoma men's feet, which the Acoma account says happened to 24 men.

**Where to go:** The National Museum of the American Indian, on the National Mall in Washington, D.C.
