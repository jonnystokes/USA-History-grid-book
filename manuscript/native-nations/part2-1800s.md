## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 10 minutes**

East of the Mississippi River, Native leaders tried several ways to keep their nations' land. North of the Ohio River, people from many nations joined a confederacy and fought American armies. In the Southeast, Cherokee leaders wrote a constitution, a written plan for their government and its basic laws, and took their fight to court. In Florida, Seminole fighters went to war against removal.

Two Shawnee brothers, Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa, built the northern confederacy around one idea. Tecumseh said that the land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell any part of it. Soldiers under the American commander William Henry Harrison burned the confederacy's center in 1811, and American soldiers killed Tecumseh in battle in 1813.

The Cherokee changed their nation fast in the eleven years from 1821 to 1832. Sequoyah showed a way to write the Cherokee language in 1821. Cherokee leaders approved their constitution, with three branches of government, in 1827, and the nation began printing a newspaper the next year. Then, in 1832, Chief Justice John Marshall, the head of the Supreme Court of the United States, wrote the court's ruling in the Cherokee Nation's favor.

Members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act in 1830. Under it, United States soldiers forced the nations of the Southeast out of their homes and moved them west. In the Cherokee removal alone, soldiers held about 16,000 people in stockades, pens fenced with upright logs, and more than 4,000 of them died of cold, hunger and disease. In the West, the people of those nations built their governments again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa" -->
A Shawnee man named Tenskwatawa began preaching around 1805 that Native people should return to Native ways of living. People called him the Prophet. His brother Tecumseh took the movement to other nations and asked them to join it. In 1808 the brothers built a town on the Wabash River as the center of a confederacy of many nations. The town was called Prophetstown.

William Henry Harrison, an American governor, was buying Native land through treaties. Native leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Wayne with him in 1809 and sold him land.

**At Vincennes.** Tecumseh answered Harrison at Vincennes in August 1810. The land, he said, "never was divided, but belongs to all for the use of each." He added, "No part has a right to sell, even to each other, much less to strangers." He called the latest sale "bad," because "it was made by a part only." By that argument, the Fort Wayne sale counted for nothing, and so did every sale made by one nation alone.

Harrison marched about a thousand men to Prophetstown on November 7, 1811, while Tecumseh was away asking more nations to join the confederacy, and fought the people there. The fight became known as the Battle of Tippecanoe. On the next day, November 8, Harrison burned the town.

Fighters from the confederacy fought on beside the British in the War of 1812. They fought the Battle of the Thames at Moraviantown, in the British colony of Upper Canada, on October 5, 1813. Their British ally, Major General Henry Procter, left the battlefield in the middle of the fight. British army officers later put Procter on trial for his failures in that campaign. American soldiers killed Tecumseh in the battle. After his death the confederacy broke apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tecumseh" name="Tecumseh" movie="We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tecumseh

> **Who:** Shawnee leader who went from nation to nation arguing that Native land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell it.
> **When and where:** the Wabash River country and the South, 1805 to 1813. Killed in battle at the Thames, October 5, 1813.
> **Movie:** *We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision* (PBS, 2009), a documentary about him and his brother.

**He carried his argument to a Creek council of 5,000 people.**

Tecumseh arrived at the yearly great council of the Creek Nation at Tuckabatchee in October 1811. More than 5,000 Creek people had gathered there. He asked them to join the confederacy of the northern nations.

The Creek leader Big Warrior refused to join him, because he was afraid of losing the support of the United States.

Tecumseh was still away in the South a month later, when Harrison marched on Prophetstown.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tecumseh" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did the Cherokee learn to read in a few years?" -->
A Cherokee man named Sequoyah showed a syllabary he had made in 1821, a set of 86 written characters in which each character stands for a whole syllable, not a single sound. Within a few years most Cherokee people could read and write it. Writers at the website History.com state that by the 1830s a larger share of Cherokee people could read than of the white settlers around them.

Cherokee leaders set up a capital at New Echota in 1825. Two years later they approved a written constitution, modeled on the United States Constitution, with three branches of government. Officials of the state of Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished, ended by law.

The Cherokee began printing the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper, in 1828. They printed it in Cherokee and in English.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sequoyah-native-nations" name="Sequoyah" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sequoyah

> **Who:** Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language, and made a way to write his own. He is the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone, without first being able to read and write another language.
> **When and where:** born in Taskigi, Tennessee, about 1770. First showed his syllabary in 1821. Died 1843 in Mexico.

**He could not read any language, and he wrote one down.**

Sequoyah could not read or write English or any other language. He worked on the problem for about twelve years before he showed his syllabary in 1821.

Seven years later, half of what the nation's newspaper, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, printed was in his characters. He died in 1843, in Mexico.

**Not known:** the year he was born. The years given for it run from 1770 to 1778, and the Library of Congress lists 1770 with a question mark.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sequoyah-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Removal and the Trail of Tears" -->
President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act into law on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could give Native nations land west of the Mississippi River in exchange for their land in the East. Cherokee leaders took their fight with Georgia to the courts.

**The court case.** Georgia's lawmakers had made it a crime for a white person to live in the Cherokee Nation without a license from the state. Samuel Worcester, a missionary who translated parts of the Bible into Cherokee, refused to leave. Georgia guards arrested him and other missionaries in July 1831. They were tried in Georgia that September, and each was sentenced to four years in prison at hard labor, which means forced hard work. The New Georgia Encyclopedia's account of the case does not name the judge.

Chief Justice John Marshall ruled in *Worcester v. Georgia*, in 1832, that the laws of Georgia had no force inside the Cherokee Nation, which was under the protection of the United States. That ruling is still one of the foundations of tribal sovereignty law, the law about a Native nation's right to govern itself. Georgia's officials ignored it. Worcester stayed in prison until January 1833, when he accepted a pardon, an order forgiving the crime, from Georgia's governor.

**The treaty.** Three years after the ruling, in late 1835, a small group of Cherokee men who favored moving signed the Treaty of New Echota with United States officials. The signers gave up all of the nation's land east of the Mississippi for $5 million. Members of the Senate approved the treaty in May 1836, and United States officials treated it as an agreement by the whole nation to move west.

**The roundup.** President Martin Van Buren sent General Winfield Scott with about 7,000 soldiers in May 1838 to round up the Cherokee people who had not moved west. Georgia's militia, its citizen soldiers, joined them. In 1838 and 1839, soldiers came to Cherokee houses in the Southeast and took families out at gunpoint, often with no time to pack. They held about 16,000 Cherokee people in stockades in Tennessee and Alabama.

Then the soldiers forced them to go to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. More than 4,000 of them died of cold, hunger and disease, about one in every four of the people the soldiers had held.

**How many died.** That figure is the traditional estimate, and the counts differ. The National Park Service counts more than a thousand deaths on the trip itself, and perhaps several thousand more from what the removal did to people afterward. Russell Thornton, a scholar at the University of Minnesota, wrote in 1984 that the number of deaths "may have been twice" the traditional figure.

The Cherokee named the removal *Nunna daul Tsuny*, the Trail Where We Cried. In English it is called the Trail of Tears.

United States officials and soldiers forced all five of the large southeastern nations to move to Indian Territory: the Cherokee, the Muscogee (Creek), the Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Seminole. They removed more than 14,000 Choctaw people from 1831 to 1833, and hundreds died. They removed more than 14,500 Creek people in 1836 and 1837. Several hundred Creek people died on the way, and about 3,200 died after they arrived, of disease, hunger and cold.

**Rebuilding.** In Indian Territory, the people of those nations rebuilt their governments, their courts, their newspapers and their schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** a Cherokee woman who was about three years old when soldiers took her family out of their home in Georgia in 1838.
> **When and where:** Georgia to Indian Territory, 1838 and 1839. Interviewed in 1932.

**Ninety-four years later, she told a historian what she remembered.**

Soldiers took Rebecca Neugin's family out of their home in Georgia in 1838, when Rebecca was about three years old. The soldiers held the family in a camp in Tennessee, and in September 1838 the family set out west. Rebecca and the smaller children rode in a wagon, while her mother, her father and her older brother Dick walked the whole way.

The historian Grant Foreman interviewed her in 1932, when she was about 97 years old, and she told him what she remembered of the journey. She was one of the last people still alive who had been on it. She died on July 15, 1932.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Seminole resistance" -->
In Florida many Seminole people refused to leave, and United States soldiers fought them in the Second Seminole War, from 1835 to 1842. It was the longest war between United States forces and a Native nation east of the Mississippi, and the one that cost the most. National Park Service writers give its cost in a count. For every Seminole person soldiers killed, they forced four out of Florida. For every four forced out, three soldiers died and United States officials spent $32,000.

General Thomas Jesup lured the Seminole war leader Osceola to a talk on October 21, 1837, under a white flag of truce, a sign that both sides promise not to attack or seize anyone while they talk. Osceola came in under the flag, and Jesup had him seized. Many people in the United States protested the trick in public at the time. Osceola died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, on January 30, 1838.

Most of the fighting stopped in 1842. A few hundred Seminole people never surrendered and stayed in the Everglades. Their descendants belong to two tribes today. Federal officials, the officials of the national government, recognized the Seminole Tribe of Florida in 1957 and the Miccosukee Tribe in 1962, accepting each tribe as a nation with its own government. Leaders of both tribes point out that the Seminole were never conquered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="osceola-native-nations" name="Osceola" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Osceola

> **Who:** Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida.
> **When and where:** Florida. Seized by General Thomas Jesup under a flag of truce, October 21, 1837. Died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838.

**He was born Billy Powell, in a Creek town in Alabama.**

Osceola was born in 1804 in the Creek town of Talisi, in what is now Alabama. His name at birth was Billy Powell.

He died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie three months after Jesup seized him. Frederick Weedon, the army doctor there, cut off Osceola's head before the burial and put it on display in his drug store.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 22 minutes**

United States officials and soldiers forced one Native nation after another onto reservations, pieces of land set aside for each nation in a treaty or by an order, between 1850 and 1900. Soldiers and officials held the people there, and officials and settlers took the rest of each nation's country.

United States soldiers killed hundreds of Native people at a time in these years. Army officers hanged 38 Dakota men together at Mankato, Minnesota, in 1862. At Bear River in 1863 soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 500 Shoshone people, and at Sand Creek in 1864 about 230 Cheyenne and Arapaho people, most of them women, children and elders.

Cavalry, soldiers on horseback, killed about 177 Piikani people on the Marias River in 1870, by the best estimate. Soldiers at Wounded Knee in 1890 killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people.

Hunters killed off the bison herds that fed the Plains nations, and army officers encouraged the killing. Navajo leaders signed a treaty in 1868 in which United States officials agreed to let their people go home. Members of Congress stopped making treaties with Native nations in 1871. Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho fighters beat George Custer's soldiers at the Little Bighorn in 1876, and the next year the Nez Perce fled toward Canada with the army behind them.

An Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt opened the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations in 1879. Federal officials used Pratt's school as the model for many more. Pratt stated his aim in his own words: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." Federal investigators later counted at least 973 children who died at the federal boarding schools.

Members of Congress passed a law in 1887 under which federal officials cut nations' shared land into private pieces. The officials opened what was left over to white settlers.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wars, from the nations' side" -->
**The Dakota War.** In Minnesota in 1862, United States officials failed to deliver the food and the payments that they had promised the Dakota in treaties. Dakota fighters went to war, and the war lasted six weeks.

**The trials.** Afterward, army officers sitting as military commissions, courts run by the army, tried 392 Dakota men. Some of the trials took less than five minutes. The men had no defense lawyers and no interpreters, the people who translate between two languages. The officers on the commissions sentenced 303 of the men to death by hanging.

President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers read the trial records. Lincoln looked first for men found guilty of rape, forcing a person into sex, and found two. Then he added men found to have taken part in killing people who were not soldiers. He cut the list of men to be executed down to 39. One of the 39 was reprieved, which means his execution was called off.

**Mankato.** Army officers hanged 38 Dakota men together at Mankato on December 26, 1862, while about 4,000 people watched. It is the largest mass execution, the most people put to death at one time, in United States history. Captain William Duley cut the rope. Several members of his family had been killed in a Dakota attack on the Lake Shetek settlement.

The bodies were put in a shallow grave on a sandbar. The Minnesota Historical Society's account does not say who buried them. Before morning, doctors had dug up most of the bodies to use for medical study.

**Hanged by mistake.** Two of the 38 men were hanged by mistake, by the Minnesota Historical Society's account. The first was Wicaŋȟpi Wastedaŋpi. He went by Caske, a common name that means first-born son. Dakota families gave it to a first-born boy, so many men had it. When the name Caske was called for another man on the list, Wicaŋȟpi Wastedaŋpi stepped forward. He was hanged in that man's place.

The second was Wasicuŋ, a young white man whom the Dakota had adopted as a child. The commission had found him not guilty at his trial.

**Bear River.** A month after the hangings, soldiers attacked a Shoshone camp far to the west. Colonel Patrick Edward Connor led them against a winter camp of Northwestern Shoshone people on the Bear River, near what is now Preston, Idaho, on January 29, 1863. Connor had announced that he would take no prisoners. His soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 500 Shoshone people. Afterward army leaders promoted Connor to brigadier general, a higher rank.

**The Long Walk.** Far to the south, in 1863 and 1864, United States soldiers marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo and held them there. This forced march is known as the Long Walk. Thousands of the Navajo died while soldiers held them at Bosque Redondo. In 1868 Navajo leaders negotiated a treaty in which United States officials agreed to let the survivors go home. The Navajo were one of the very few removed nations who got their homeland back.

**Sand Creek.** Colonel John Chivington led about 675 volunteer soldiers against Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp at Sand Creek in Colorado on November 29, 1864. The camp was flying a United States flag and a white flag.

Earlier that month Major Scott Anthony, the commander at nearby Fort Lyon, had told Black Kettle's people to camp at Sand Creek. In his report of November 16 he wrote that he told them "no war would be waged against them." That promise was to hold until his own commander decided what to do. When Chivington arrived, Anthony agreed to the attack.

The attack lasted eight hours. The soldiers killed about 230 people, most of them women, children and elders.

Captain Silas Soule, one of the officers there, refused to let his men fire. He wrote to his mother that he saw "a Massacre of three hundred Indians mostly women and children." In another letter he wrote that it was "hard to see little children on their knees have their brains beat out." Soldiers cut scalps and other body parts from the dead.

Members of a committee of Congress later wrote, using an old word for cowardly and cruel, that Chivington "deliberately planned and executed a foul and dastardly massacre." Chivington left the army and was never put on trial, and no officer or soldier was punished.

A man shot Soule dead on a Denver street in April 1865, about two months after Soule spoke against Chivington before investigators. No one was ever punished for killing him. National Park Service staff look after the place of the massacre today as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="From Red Cloud to Geronimo" -->
**Red Cloud's War.** Gold was found in Montana in 1863, and the Bozeman Trail was opened as a road for white settlers to reach it. The trail ran through Lakota country, and the Lakota had never given permission for it. When soldiers built forts along it to protect the settlers, Lakota fighters went to war, from 1866 to 1868. The war is named for the Lakota leader Red Cloud.

After the war, United States officials and Lakota leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. In it, the officials agreed to close the Bozeman Trail. They also set up the Great Sioux Reservation "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. It covered all of what is now South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the Black Hills.

**The Marias River.** Major Eugene Baker's cavalry came to a camp of Piikani (Piegan Blackfeet) people on the Marias River in Montana on January 23, 1870. His scout, Joe Kipp, told him it was the camp of Heavy Runner's band, a group of families who lived and traveled together, and that the band was at peace with the United States. By one account, Baker answered, "That makes no difference, one band or another of them, they are all Piegans and we will attack them."

By the best estimate, Baker's soldiers killed 37 men, 90 women and 50 children. The army's own count was 173 dead, and the National Parks Conservation Association's figure is more than 200. Neither Baker nor any of his men was ever punished.

**Gold in the Black Hills.** Members of an expedition led by George Custer announced in 1874 that there was gold in the Black Hills, inside the reservation promised to the Lakota in 1868. Federal officials ignored their own treaty. In December 1875 they ordered the winter camps that were off the reservation to come onto it by January 31, 1876. When the camps did not come in, the officials sent the army.

**The Little Bighorn.** George Custer led part of the 7th Cavalry, an army unit of soldiers on horseback, on June 25, 1876. He attacked a camp where Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho people had gathered as allies, meaning they had agreed to fight on the same side. The camp stood by the Little Bighorn River, also called the Greasy Grass. Fighters from the camp killed Custer and about 200 of the soldiers with him in about an hour. Those soldiers made up five companies, smaller units of the 7th Cavalry.

**Crazy Horse.** The Lakota leader Crazy Horse, who had led fighters at the Little Bighorn, surrendered on May 6, 1877, less than a year after the battle. Soldiers were arresting him at Fort Robinson on September 5, 1877. A Lakota man named Little Big Man jumped on his back and held him, and a soldier on guard stabbed him with a bayonet, a blade fixed to the end of a rifle. Crazy Horse died of the wound.

**Who stabbed him.** Most accounts say a soldier on guard stabbed him. The name most often given is Private William Gentles, but only one witness named him, so who the soldier was is disputed. Little Big Man later told a different story, in which Crazy Horse was cut by his own knife.

**The last surrender.** In the Southwest, Geronimo, of the Chiricahua Apache, surrendered on September 4, 1886. He was the last Native leader to formally surrender to the United States. United States officials then held the Chiricahua people as prisoners of war, people an army holds after capturing them in a war, for 27 years.

**By 1886:** Geronimo had surrendered, the last Native leader to do so, and United States officials held most Native nations on reservations. The Black Hills, promised to the Lakota in 1868, had been taken after the gold strike.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" name="Sitting Bull" movie="Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sitting Bull

> **Who:** Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man. The camp that fought at the Greasy Grass gathered around him.
> **When and where:** the northern Plains. Canada from 1877 to 1881. Killed by Indian police at Standing Rock, December 15, 1890.
> **Movie:** *Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart* (2006), a documentary built from his own recorded words.

**Buffalo Bill's show paid him fifty dollars a week.**

The camp on the Greasy Grass in June 1876 formed around Sitting Bull, and Crazy Horse was one of its battle leaders.

Sitting Bull took his people north into Canada in 1877. Hunters were killing off the bison, and by 1881 there were not enough left to feed his people. He surrendered at Fort Buford in July of that year.

He signed a contract with Buffalo Bill's Wild West, a traveling show, at the Standing Rock Agency on June 6, 1885. The show paid him fifty dollars a week for a four-month season, and only he could sell his own photographs and autographs.

Indian police, Native men who worked as police for the agency that ran the reservation, came to arrest him at Standing Rock on December 15, 1890. Indian agents, the federal officials who ran the reservations, were alarmed by the Ghost Dance, a new religious dance spreading across the reservations. The policemen killed Sitting Bull.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chief-joseph" name="Chief Joseph" movie="I Will Fight No More Forever (1975)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Chief Joseph

> **Who:** Nez Perce leader in the flight of 1877, when his people fled toward Canada with the United States Army behind them.
> **When and where:** the Wallowa Valley of Oregon to the Bear Paw Mountains of Montana, 1877. Surrendered October 5, 1877, forty miles from Canada.
> **Movie:** *I Will Fight No More Forever* (1975, television film).

**They stopped forty miles short of Canada.**

Joseph's band of the Nez Perce lived in the Wallowa Valley of northeastern Oregon. General Oliver O. Howard told the Nez Perce chiefs in 1877 that their people had 30 days to move onto the reservation in Idaho, or the army would move them by force. That year about 750 Nez Perce people fled with the army behind them. Only about 200 of them were fighters, and the rest were families.

They traveled about 1,170 miles, the National Park Service's figure for the trail. Some accounts state a longer distance, up to about 1,700 miles.

They surrendered on October 5, 1877, at the foot of the Bear Paw Mountains in Montana, forty miles short of the Canadian border. Lieutenant C. E. S. Wood recorded Joseph's words at the surrender: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."

**The promise.** Colonel Nelson Miles had promised Joseph that his people would be taken back to the reservation in Idaho. General William Tecumseh Sherman, the commanding general of the army, overruled the promise, cancelling it. He wanted the Nez Perce punished as an example to other nations and never allowed to go home. "I believed General Miles, or I never would have surrendered," Joseph said afterward.

United States officials sent his people to Kansas, then to Indian Territory. There, many of them were sick and short of supplies, and their number fell from 410 to 391. Two hundred sixty-eight Nez Perce people left Indian Territory for the Northwest in May 1885. Officials sent Joseph to the Colville reservation in Washington, and he died there, at Nespelem, in 1904. Officials never allowed him back to the Wallowa Valley.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chief-joseph" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison, and the end of treaties" -->
There were roughly 30 million bison on the Plains in the early 1800s. One count in 1884 found 325, about one bison left for every 90,000 there had been. By 1900 there were fewer than a thousand.

Railroads carried hunters into the herds' country. Hunters made hide hunting into a large business, killing bison for their skins. Disease and drought killed others. Army officers encouraged the killing, because the herds were the Plains nations' food and the base of how they made a living, and the bison were part of their religion.

Members of Congress stopped making treaties with Native nations in 1871. They did it with a rider, a short passage attached to a spending bill called the Indian Appropriations Act. Treaties already made stayed in force. After 1871, United States officials dealt with Native nations through acts of Congress and orders from the president instead of treaties.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did the nations lose 90 million acres?" -->
Native nations lost about 90 million acres between 1887 and 1934 through allotment, the cutting of land that a whole nation owned together into private pieces. Federal officials carried it out.

Members of Congress passed the Dawes Act, also called the General Allotment Act, in 1887. Under it, federal officials divided nations' land into separate parcels, or pieces. The head of a family got 160 acres, a single adult or an orphan got 80 acres, and each other child got 40 acres. On land fit only for grazing animals, each person got twice as much. Officials called everything left over "surplus," meaning extra, and opened it to white settlers.

The land Native nations held fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million in 1934. The lost land was about two-thirds of all the land Native nations still had in 1887. The Meriam Report, a federal study published in 1928, states the figures as 137 million and 47 million acres. The loss is about the same by either count.

Allotment went on until members of Congress ended it in 1934.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding schools" -->
Richard Henry Pratt, an Army officer, opened the Carlisle Indian Industrial School at Carlisle, Pennsylvania, on November 1, 1879. It was the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations. Federal officials used Carlisle as the model for the schools that followed.

Pratt stated the purpose himself. In 1892 he spoke to a national conference of charities in Denver. He said that "all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." People often quote the line in a shorter form, "kill the Indian, save the man." Pratt's recorded words are the longer ones, printed in the conference's official report.

Members of Congress passed two laws to get the children into the schools. In 1891 they gave the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the federal official in charge of Native affairs, the power to make and enforce rules forcing Native children to attend school. In 1893 they let federal officials hold back rations, clothing and annuity payments from parents who would not send their children. These were the food, goods and yearly payments that United States officials had promised a nation in its treaties.

Some Indian agents, the federal officials in charge of the reservations, sent reservation police to seize children. Some families sent their children willingly, hoping the schooling would help them. Many families had no real choice.

**Arrival.** When a child arrived, staff at school after school cut the child's hair and replaced the child's clothes with a uniform. They gave the child an English name and forbade the child to speak the language of home. Staff punished children for speaking their own languages. The federal investigation of the schools and survivors' own accounts document the punishments: beatings, solitary confinement, which means locking a child away alone, withholding food, and washing children's mouths out with soap.

Children spent half of each school day doing unpaid work that kept the school running. Pratt also ran what he called the outing system. Under it, he sent students to live and work with white families, in the summers and during school terms.

**The count.** Investigators at the United States Department of the Interior studied the schools and published their findings in 2022 and again on July 30, 2024. They counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states and territories, open between 1819 and 1969. Their first report, in 2022, listed 408. They also found more than a thousand other places that had similar aims.

At least 973 children died at the federal schools. The investigators found at least 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 of the schools. The report states that its count of the dead is low.

Children kept their languages by speaking them where the staff could not hear. At these schools, children made friends with children from other nations. Those friendships were one of the starting points of the Native organizing of the next century. Some students, Zitkala-Ša among them, used the reading and writing they learned at the schools to argue against the schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" name="Sarah Winnemucca" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Winnemucca

> **Who:** Northern Paiute writer, interpreter, and lecturer who argued her people's case in front of audiences across the East.
> **When and where:** Nevada, and the lecture halls of the East, in the 1870s and 1880s.

**"My people were only given one week to get ready in."**

Sarah Winnemucca was working as an interpreter when she learned, just before Christmas 1878, that President Rutherford B. Hayes had ordered her people taken to the Yakama reservation under an army guard. She wrote the line about the one week. Officials were punishing the whole group because a few Paiute men had fought in the Bannock War of 1878.

United States officials forced about 550 Northern Paiute men, women and children to move 350 miles north in 1879, from the Malheur reservation in Oregon to the Yakama reservation. The march took nearly a month through snow and over two mountain ranges. The officials in charge made some of the men walk in leg irons, chains locked around the ankles.

Five children, one woman and an old man died on the road. Their bodies were left beside it because the ground was too cold to dig graves. The Oregon Historical Society's account of the march does not say who decided to leave them. The historian Sally Zanjani estimated that more than one fifth of the group died far from home after they were sent away, mostly of hunger and disease.

**Schurz's promise.** Winnemucca went east and gave lectures about what officials had done to her people. She met with Secretary of the Interior Carl Schurz in Washington in 1880. He gave her a written promise that the Paiute could go back to Malheur, and then he broke it. Her people never returned there. President Chester Arthur ended the Malheur reservation in 1882 and 1883, and white settlers could then claim its land.

She published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims* in 1883. It is the first known autobiography, a book a person writes about their own life, by a Native American woman. She was also the first Native woman known to hold a United States copyright, the legal right to control who copies and sells a piece of writing. In 1884 she asked Congress to give her people back their reservation.

She opened a school of her own for Paiute children near Lovelock, Nevada, in the mid-1880s. Accounts of her life give the year as 1884 or 1885. Staff at the government boarding schools forbade children to speak their own languages, but at her school Winnemucca taught in both Paiute and English. The school ran out of money and closed within a few years.

### Zitkala-Ša

> **Who:** Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and organizer, taken as a child to a boarding school in Indiana.
> **When and where:** the Yankton reservation, South Dakota, and Washington, D.C. Born February 22, 1876. Died January 26, 1938.

**An interpreter promised her all the red apples she wanted.**

Zitkala-Ša wrote about how she left home in her essay "The Big Red Apples." She was in her eighth year, in March, when two white missionaries came to her village on the Yankton reservation. Her mother told her they had come "to take away Indian boys and girls to the East." A young white interpreter told the girl she could pick all the red apples she wanted and ride on "the iron horse," a train. "I had never seen a train, and he knew it," she wrote.

She begged to go. Her mother warned her, "Their words are sweet, but, my child, their deeds are bitter." Then her mother agreed. The missionaries took the girl to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute. The essay does not name them.

The editors of the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine, published a set of her essays about her own life in 1900. One of them was "The School Days of an Indian Girl." In the essays she described her time at the school and criticized it. She was working at Carlisle when she published them. They appeared again in her book *American Indian Stories* in 1921.

She helped start the National Council of American Indians in 1926 and led it until she died in 1938.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee" -->
A Northern Paiute man named Wovoka, from the Walker Lake country of Nevada, had a vision on January 1, 1889. It came during a solar eclipse, when the moon blocked the sun. He taught that if people lived rightly and danced a round dance, in which people move in a circle, the dead would return and the world would be made whole.

On reservations where people were hungry, they took up the dance, which became known as the Ghost Dance. Indian agents who were frightened by it called it the start of an uprising, an armed revolt. Newspaper writers printed stories that said the same.

Indian police killed Sitting Bull at Standing Rock on December 15, 1890, while they were trying to arrest him.

**Wounded Knee.** Soldiers of the 7th Cavalry under Colonel James W. Forsyth were disarming Spotted Elk's band, also called Big Foot's band, at Wounded Knee Creek on December 29, 1890, taking away the people's weapons. During the disarming, a rifle belonging to Black Coyote, a deaf man, went off. By some accounts he fired first, and by others a soldier tried to grab his gun and it went off. The soldiers opened fire.

They fired carbines, which are short rifles, and four Hotchkiss guns, small cannon set on a hill above the camp. The soldiers killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people. The killing is known as the Wounded Knee Massacre.

A burial party put 146 of the dead in one grave at the site in early January 1891. They were 84 men and boys, 44 women and 18 children. Others died later of their wounds, and some bodies were never found, so no count gives the women and children among all the dead.

Twenty-five soldiers also died. Writers for Britannica and History.com say that most of those soldiers were hit by their own side's fire. Twenty soldiers of the 7th Cavalry received the Medal of Honor, the top award the United States military gives for bravery in battle, for what they did that day. Some of the medal statements praise soldiers for driving out "Sioux Indians" who were hiding in a ravine, a narrow, steep valley.

**If you remember one thing:** In these fifty years United States soldiers and officials took most of the land the nations still held, forced the people onto reservations and began taking their children to boarding schools.
**One number:** about 250,000. That is how many Native people census takers counted in 1900, the lowest number on record. Even the lowest estimate for 1500, about 1 million, is four times as many.
**Next:** In the next fifty years Native people built national organizations of their own, while the boarding schools stayed open.
