## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, Native nations east of the Mississippi River tried two ways to keep their land, and both failed. Nations north of the Ohio River joined a confederacy and fought American armies. The Cherokee in the Southeast did not go to war. They built a government with a written constitution and took their fight to court.

Two Shawnee brothers, Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa, built the confederacy, a union of many nations, around one idea. They taught that the land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell any part of it. In 1811 soldiers under the American commander William Henry Harrison burned the confederacy's capital. Tecumseh died in battle in 1813.

Between 1821 and 1832, in eleven years, the Cherokee gained a written form of their own language, a printing press, a newspaper and a written constitution with three branches of government. They also won a ruling in their favor from the Supreme Court of the United States.

In 1830 members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, United States soldiers forced the nations of the Southeast out of their homes and moved them west. Thousands of people died on the way. In the West, the nations that arrived built their governments again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa" -->
Around 1805 a Shawnee man named Tenskwatawa began preaching that Native people should return to Native ways of living. His brother Tecumseh took the movement to other nations and asked them to join it. In 1808 the brothers built a town on the Wabash River as the capital of a confederacy of many nations. The Americans called the town Prophetstown, because Tenskwatawa was known as the Prophet.

Tecumseh went to one nation after another to argue about who owned the land. United States officials were buying land from one nation at a time. Tecumseh said that all the nations owned the land in common, meaning together, so no one nation had the right to sell any of it. By that argument, every sale already made was void, which means it counted for nothing.

On November 7, 1811, while Tecumseh was away recruiting, William Henry Harrison marched about a thousand men to Prophetstown and fought the people there. The fight became known as the Battle of Tippecanoe. On the next day, November 8, Harrison burned the town.

Fighters from the confederacy fought on beside the British in the War of 1812. On October 5, 1813, they fought the Battle of the Thames at Moraviantown, in the British colony of Upper Canada. Their British ally, General Henry Procter, left the battlefield in the middle of the fight. Tecumseh died in that battle. After his death the confederacy broke apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tecumseh" name="Tecumseh" movie="We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tecumseh

> **Who:** Shawnee leader who traveled thousands of miles arguing that Native land was held in common by all the nations, and could only be defended in common.
> **When and where:** the Ohio Country and the South, 1805 to 1813. Died in battle at the Thames, October 5, 1813.
> **Movie:** *We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision* (PBS, 2009), a documentary about him and his brother.

Tecumseh spent years traveling from council to council, across the Ohio Country and down into the South. At each council he made the same argument. Some of the nations he spoke to had bad histories with one another.

He told them that they had all been losing land in the same way, one treaty at a time. The only way to stop it, he said, was for every nation to refuse to let any single nation sign land away.

Enough nations joined him to make Prophetstown the capital of a confederacy. In 1811 Harrison burned the town while Tecumseh was away. Two years later Tecumseh died in Canada, fighting beside the British.

He had spent eight years building the confederacy, and no other leader took his place.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tecumseh" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee renaissance" -->
In 1821 a Cherokee man named Sequoyah showed a syllabary he had made. A syllabary is a set of written characters in which each character stands for a whole syllable, not a single sound. Sequoyah's had 86 characters. Within a few years most Cherokee people could read and write it. History.com states that by the 1830s a larger share of Cherokee people could read than of the white settlers around them.

Cherokee leaders set up a capital at New Echota in 1825. In 1827 they approved a written constitution, a written plan for their government and its basic laws. They modeled it on the United States Constitution, with three branches of government. Officials of the state of Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished, which means ended by law.

In 1828 the Cherokee began printing the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper. They printed it in Cherokee and in English.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sequoyah-native-nations" name="Sequoyah" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sequoyah

> **Who:** Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language, and made a way to write his own. He is the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone, without first being able to read and write another language.
> **When and where:** born in Taskigi, Tennessee, about 1770. First showed his syllabary in 1821. Died 1843 in Mexico.

Sequoyah could not read English. Nobody had written Cherokee down before his syllabary, so there was no Cherokee writing for him to read either. He worked on the problem for about twelve years.

By 1821 he had made 86 characters, one for each syllable in spoken Cherokee. A person who already spoke Cherokee did not have to learn spelling rules to use them. Because of that, Cherokee speakers learned the syllabary fast. Within a few years most of the Nation was reading and writing.

Sources list Sequoyah's birth year as anywhere from 1770 to 1778. The Library of Congress lists it as 1770 with a question mark, which means the year is not certain. He died in 1843, in Mexico.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sequoyah-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Removal" -->
The Indian Removal Act became law on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could make treaties that traded Native nations' land in the East for land west of the Mississippi River.

Cherokee leaders took their fight with Georgia to the courts. In *Worcester v. Georgia*, decided in 1832, Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the laws of Georgia had no force inside the Cherokee Nation. He held that the Nation was a separate community under the protection of the United States. Judges still treat that ruling as one of the foundations of tribal sovereignty law, the law about a Native nation's right to govern itself. Georgia's officials ignored the ruling, and United States officials went ahead with removal anyway.

The word in the act was "removal." For the Cherokee it meant that in 1838 and 1839, soldiers came to Cherokee houses in the Southeast and took families out at gunpoint. The soldiers forced more than 16,000 people to go to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. More than 4,000 of them died. That figure is the traditional estimate, and some scholars argue the real number was higher. People today call the Cherokee removal the Trail of Tears.

United States officials and soldiers forced all five of the large southeastern nations to move to Indian Territory: the Cherokee, the Muscogee (Creek), the Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Seminole. Within a generation, those nations had rebuilt their governments, their courts, their newspapers and their schools there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** a Cherokee woman who was about three years old when soldiers took her family out of their home in Georgia in 1838.
> **When and where:** Georgia to Indian Territory, 1838 and 1839. Interviewed in Oklahoma in 1932.

In 1838 soldiers took Rebecca Neugin's family out of their home in Georgia. Rebecca was about three years old. On the way west she rode in the wagon, while her mother, her father and her older brother walked.

In 1932 the historian Grant Foreman interviewed her, and she told him what she remembered. She was about 97 years old. The interview came 94 years after the removal, and she was one of the last people still alive who had been on it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Seminole resistance" -->
In Florida the Seminole refused to leave, and the Second Seminole War followed, from 1835 to 1842. It was the longest and costliest war between United States forces and a Native nation east of the Mississippi.

On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup invited the Seminole war leader Osceola to a talk under a white flag of truce. Under a flag of truce, both sides promise not to attack or seize anyone while they talk. Osceola came in under the flag, and Jesup had him seized. Newspaper writers and public figures in the United States condemned the trick at the time, meaning they spoke against it in public. Osceola died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, on January 30, 1838.

The fighting died down after 1842. A few hundred Seminole people never surrendered and stayed deep in the Everglades. Their descendants belong to two tribes today. Federal officials, meaning officials of the national government, formally recognized the Seminole Tribe of Florida in 1957 and the Miccosukee Tribe in 1962. That means they accepted each tribe as a nation with its own government. Both tribes point out that the Seminole were never conquered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="osceola-native-nations" name="Osceola" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Osceola

> **Who:** Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida.
> **When and where:** Florida. Seized by General Thomas Jesup under a flag of truce, October 21, 1837. Died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838.

Osceola led the Seminole fight against removal for two years, and United States soldiers never beat him in battle. On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup called him to a talk under a white flag of truce. When Osceola arrived, Jesup had him arrested.

Newspaper writers and public figures in the United States said at the time that the arrest was wrong.

Osceola died three months later, on January 30, 1838, a prisoner at Fort Moultrie. The records do not state the year he was born. The war he had been fighting went on for another four years without him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="osceola-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="native-nations" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
United States officials and soldiers forced Native nations onto reservations in the second half of the 1800s. A reservation is an area of land that United States officials set aside for a nation, most often in a treaty. They required the nation's people to stay on it. Officials and settlers then took the rest of the nation's country.

Hunters killed off the bison herds that fed the Plains nations, and army officers encouraged the killing. Members of Congress ended treaty-making with Native nations in 1871.

In 1879 an Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt opened the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations. Federal means run by the national government of the United States. Federal officials used Pratt's school as the model for many more. Pratt and the officials who followed him meant to take Native children away from their nations and raise them to stop living as Native people.

In 1887 members of Congress passed a law under which federal officials cut nations' shared land into private pieces. The officials sold off what was left over.

Native leaders in these years had to choose among war, flight and treaties. In 1868 Navajo leaders signed a treaty that let their people go home. Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho fighters beat George Custer's soldiers at the Little Bighorn in 1876. The Nez Perce fled toward Canada in 1877.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wars, from the nations' side" -->
In Minnesota in 1862, United States officials failed to deliver the food and the payments that their treaties with the Dakota had promised. The Dakota went to war, and the war lasted six weeks. Afterward, army officers sitting as military commissions tried 392 Dakota men. A military commission is a court run by the army. Some of the trials took less than five minutes. The men had no defense lawyers and no interpreters, the people who translate between two languages.

President Abraham Lincoln read the trial records and cut the list of men to be executed down to 39. One of the 39 was later reprieved, which means his execution was called off. On December 26, 1862, at Mankato, 38 Dakota men were hanged at the same time. The hanging at Mankato is the largest mass execution, the most people put to death at one time, in United States history. The sources do not identify the men who carried it out. Two of the men, it later came out, had been hanged by mistake. One had answered to another man's name, and the commission had found the other not guilty at his trial.

On November 29, 1864, about 675 volunteer soldiers under Colonel John Chivington attacked Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp at Sand Creek in Colorado. The camp was flying a United States flag and a white flag, because its people had been told they were under the army's protection. The attack lasted eight hours. The soldiers killed about 230 people, most of them women, children and elders. The National Park Service keeps the place today as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site. A massacre is the killing of many people who cannot defend themselves.

In 1863 and 1864, United States soldiers marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo and held them there. This forced march is known as the Long Walk. Thousands of the Navajo died while soldiers held them at Bosque Redondo. In 1868 Navajo leaders negotiated a treaty that let the survivors go home. Almost no other nation that United States officials removed from its homeland ever got that homeland back.

After Red Cloud's War, United States officials and Lakota leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. In it, the officials agreed to close the Bozeman Trail, a road through Lakota country. They also set up the Great Sioux Reservation "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. It covered all of what is now South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the Black Hills.

In 1874 members of an expedition led by George Custer announced that there was gold in the Black Hills. Federal officials ignored their own 1868 treaty. In December 1875 they ordered the winter camps that were off the reservation to come onto it by January 31, 1876. When the camps did not come in, the officials sent the army.

On June 25, 1876, Custer led part of the 7th Cavalry, an army unit of soldiers on horseback, against an allied camp of Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho people. The camp stood by the Little Bighorn River, also called the Greasy Grass. Fighters from the camp killed Custer and about 200 of his men, five companies in all, in about an hour.

Crazy Horse, one of the battle leaders at the Little Bighorn, surrendered on May 6, 1877, less than a year after the battle. On September 5, 1877, at Fort Robinson, someone stabbed him to death with a bayonet while he was being arrested. A bayonet is a blade fixed to the end of a rifle. The sources do not identify the men who arrested him or the person who stabbed him. Geronimo, of the Chiricahua Apache, surrendered on September 4, 1886. He was the last Native leader to formally surrender to the United States. United States officials then held his people as prisoners of war for 27 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" name="Sitting Bull" movie="Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sitting Bull

> **Who:** Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man. The camp that fought at the Greasy Grass gathered around him.
> **When and where:** the northern Plains. Canada from 1877 to 1881. Killed by Indian police at Standing Rock, December 15, 1890.
> **Movie:** *Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart* (2006), a documentary built from his own recorded words.

Sitting Bull refused to bring his people onto the reservation. The camp on the Greasy Grass in June 1876 formed around him, and Crazy Horse was one of its battle leaders.

In 1877 he took his people north into Canada. Hunters were killing off the bison, and by 1881 there were not enough left to feed his people. He surrendered at Fort Buford in July of that year.

In 1885 he traveled for one season with Buffalo Bill's Wild West, a traveling show, at fifty dollars a week. Then he went home.

On December 15, 1890, at Standing Rock, Indian police came to arrest him during the alarm over the Ghost Dance, a new religious dance spreading across the reservations. Indian police were Native men who worked as police for the agency, the government office that ran the reservation. The policemen killed Sitting Bull.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chief-joseph" name="Chief Joseph" movie="I Will Fight No More Forever (1975)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Chief Joseph

> **Who:** Nez Perce leader in the flight of 1877. About 750 people, only some 200 of them fighters, fled with the United States Army behind them.
> **When and where:** Oregon to Montana, June to October 1877. Surrendered October 5, 1877, forty miles from Canada.
> **Movie:** *I Will Fight No More Forever* (1975, television film).

In 1877 the Nez Perce left their country rather than let United States officials force them onto a reservation. About 750 people went, and only about 200 of them were fighters. The rest were families.

They traveled about 1,170 miles. That is the National Park Service's figure for the trail. Some accounts state a longer distance, up to about 1,700 miles.

They surrendered on October 5, 1877, forty miles short of the Canadian border. Lieutenant C. E. S. Wood recorded Joseph's words at the surrender: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."

Joseph had been promised that his people could go home. That promise was overruled, which means someone with more power cancelled it. The sources do not state who made the promise or who overruled it. United States officials sent his people to Kansas, then to Indian Territory, then to the Colville reservation in Washington. Joseph died there in 1904. Officials never allowed him back to the Wallowa Valley.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chief-joseph" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison, and the end of treaties" -->
There were roughly 30 million bison on the Plains in the early 1800s. One count in 1884 found 325. By 1900 there were fewer than a thousand.

Railroads carried hunters into the herds' country. Hunters made hide hunting into a large business, killing bison for their skins alone. Disease and drought killed others. Army officers encouraged the killing, because the herds were the Plains nations' food supply, the base of how they made a living and part of their religion. Without the herds, the Plains nations lost the food they had lived on.

Native nations had used treaties to bargain for about two hundred years. In 1871 members of Congress ended treaty-making with Native nations. They did it with a rider, a short passage attached to a spending bill called the Indian Appropriations Act. Few people noticed it at the time. Treaties already made stayed in force. After 1871, United States officials dealt with Native nations through acts of Congress and orders from the president instead of treaties. A nation had no part in signing either one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Allotment" -->
Native nations held their land in common. The whole nation owned the land together, and no single person owned a piece of it. Allotment means cutting land held that way into private pieces.

The Dawes Act, also called the General Allotment Act, became law in 1887. Under it, federal officials divided nations' land into separate parcels, or pieces, and assigned a parcel to each head of a family. They called everything left over "surplus," meaning extra, and sold it to settlers.

The land Native nations held fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934. That is a loss of about 90 million acres in 47 years. The lost land was about two-thirds of all the land Native nations still had in 1887. The Meriam Report, a federal study published in 1928, states the figures as 137 million and 47 million acres. The loss is about the same by either count.

In 1934 members of Congress ended allotment when they passed the Indian Reorganization Act. In the same act they set aside money to buy some land back. They did not return the roughly 90 million acres already lost.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding schools" -->
The Carlisle Indian Industrial School opened on November 1, 1879, at Carlisle, Pennsylvania. Its founder was an Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt. It was the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations. Federal officials used Carlisle as the model for the schools that followed.

Pratt stated the purpose himself. In 1892 he spoke to a national conference of charities in Denver. He said that "all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." People often quote the line in a shorter form, "kill the Indian, save the man." Pratt's recorded words are the longer ones, printed in the conference's official report.

Members of Congress passed two laws to get the children into the schools. In 1891 they gave the Commissioner of Indian Affairs the power to make and enforce rules forcing Native children to attend school. The Commissioner was the federal official in charge of Native affairs. In 1893 they let federal officials withhold rations, clothing and annuity payments from parents who would not send their children. Rations, clothing and annuities were the food, goods and yearly payments that treaties had promised a nation.

Some Indian agents sent reservation police to take children. An Indian agent was the federal official in charge of a reservation. Some families sent their children willingly, hoping the schooling would prepare them for what was coming. Many families had no choice at all.

When a child arrived, staff at school after school cut the child's hair and replaced the child's clothes with a uniform. They gave the child an English name and forbade the child to speak the language of home. Staff punished children for speaking their own languages. The federal investigation of the schools and survivors' own accounts document the punishments: beatings, solitary confinement, withholding food, and washing children's mouths out with soap. Solitary confinement means locking a child away alone.

Children spent half of each school day doing unpaid work that kept the school running, in the laundry, the fields and the kitchens. Pratt also ran what he called the outing system. Under it, he sent students to live and work with white families, in the summers and during school terms.

Investigators at the United States Department of the Interior studied the schools. They published their findings in 2022 and again on July 30, 2024. They counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states and territories, open between 1819 and 1969. Their first report, in 2022, listed 408. They also found more than a thousand other institutions with similar aims. At least 973 children died at the federal schools. The investigators found at least 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 of the schools. The report states that its count of the dead is low.

Children kept their languages by speaking them where the staff could not hear. At these schools, children met children from nations hundreds of miles away, whom they would never otherwise have met. Those friendships were one of the starting points of the Native organizing of the next century. Some students, Zitkala-Ša among them, used what the schools taught them to argue against the schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" name="Sarah Winnemucca" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Winnemucca

> **Who:** Northern Paiute writer, interpreter, and lecturer who argued her people's case in front of audiences across the East.
> **When and where:** Nevada, and the lecture halls of the East, in the 1870s and 1880s.

Sarah Winnemucca worked as an interpreter, a person who puts spoken words from one language into another. Then she went east and gave lectures about how her people were being treated.

In 1883 she published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims*. It is the first known autobiography written by a Native American woman. An autobiography is a book a person writes about their own life. She was also the first Native woman known to hold a United States copyright, the legal right to control who copies and sells a piece of writing.

In the mid-1880s she opened a school of her own for Paiute children near Lovelock, Nevada. Sources list the opening year as either 1884 or 1885. Staff at the government boarding schools forbade children to speak their own languages. At her school, Winnemucca taught in both Paiute and English.

The school ran out of money and closed within a few years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zitkala-sa-native-nations" name="Zitkala-Ša" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Zitkala-Ša

> **Who:** Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and organizer, taken as a child to a mission boarding school in Indiana.
> **When and where:** the Yankton reservation, South Dakota, and Washington, D.C. Born February 22, 1876. Died January 26, 1938.

By her own account, Zitkala-Ša was eight years old when she was taken to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute. The sources do not state who took her.

In 1900 the editors of the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine, published a set of her autobiographical essays, short pieces about her own life. One of them was "The School Days of an Indian Girl." In the essays she described her time at the school and sharply criticized it. She was working at Carlisle when she published them. The essays record a student's own account of life inside a boarding school. In 1921 they appeared again in her book *American Indian Stories*.

In 1926 she co-founded the National Council of American Indians, and she led it until she died in 1938.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zitkala-sa-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee" -->
On January 1, 1889, a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka, from the Walker Lake country of Nevada, had a vision. It came during a solar eclipse, when the moon blocked the sun. He taught that if people lived rightly and danced a round dance, the dead would return and the world would be made whole.

On reservations where people were hungry, they took up the dance, which became known as the Ghost Dance. Indian agents who were frightened by it called it the start of an uprising, meaning an armed revolt. Newspaper writers wrote exaggerated stories that said the same.

Indian police killed Sitting Bull at Standing Rock on December 15, 1890, while they were trying to arrest him during the alarm.

On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the 7th Cavalry were disarming Spotted Elk's band, also called Big Foot's band, at Wounded Knee Creek. Disarming means taking away people's weapons. A band is a group of families who live and travel together. In the middle of the disarming, the soldiers opened fire.

They fired carbines, which are short rifles, and Hotchkiss guns, which are small cannon. The soldiers killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children. Britannica's entry on the killing is titled "Wounded Knee Massacre." Twenty-five soldiers also died. Accounts in Britannica and History.com state that most of those soldiers were hit by their own side's fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1900 census takers counted about 250,000 Native people in the United States. That is the lowest number on record for the Native population.
