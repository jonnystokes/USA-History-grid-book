## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, Native nations east of the Mississippi River tried two ways to keep their land, and both failed. Nations north of the Ohio River joined a confederacy and fought American armies. The Cherokee in the Southeast did not go to war. They built a government with a written constitution and took their fight to court.

Two Shawnee brothers, Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa, built the confederacy, a union of many nations, around one idea. They taught that the land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell any part of it. In 1811 soldiers under the American commander William Henry Harrison burned the confederacy's capital. Tecumseh died in battle in 1813.

Between 1821 and 1832, in eleven years, the Cherokee gained a written form of their own language, a printing press, a newspaper and a written constitution with three branches of government. They also won a ruling in their favor from the Supreme Court of the United States.

In 1830 members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, United States soldiers forced the nations of the Southeast out of their homes and moved them west. Thousands of people died on the way. In the West, the nations that arrived built their governments again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa" -->
Around 1805 a Shawnee man named Tenskwatawa began preaching that Native people should return to Native ways of living. His brother Tecumseh took the movement to other nations and asked them to join it. In 1808 the brothers built a town on the Wabash River as the capital of a confederacy of many nations. The Americans called it Prophetstown, because Tenskwatawa was known as the Prophet.

Tecumseh went to one nation after another to argue about who owned the land. United States officials were buying land from one nation at a time. Tecumseh said that all the nations owned the land in common, meaning together, so no one nation had the right to sell any of it. By that argument, every sale already made was void, which means it counted for nothing.

On November 7, 1811, while Tecumseh was away recruiting, William Henry Harrison marched about a thousand men to Prophetstown and fought the people there. The fight became known as the Battle of Tippecanoe. On the next day, November 8, Harrison burned the town.

Fighters from the confederacy fought on beside the British in the War of 1812. On October 5, 1813, they fought the Battle of the Thames at Moraviantown, in the British colony of Upper Canada. Their British ally, General Henry Procter, left the battlefield in the middle of the fight. Tecumseh died in that battle. After his death the confederacy broke apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tecumseh" name="Tecumseh" movie="We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tecumseh

> **Who:** Shawnee leader who traveled thousands of miles arguing that Native land was held in common by all the nations, and could only be defended in common.
> **When and where:** the Ohio Country and the South, 1805 to 1813. Died in battle at the Thames, October 5, 1813.
> **Movie:** *We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision* (PBS, 2009), a documentary about him and his brother.

Tecumseh spent years traveling from council to council, across the Ohio Country and down into the South. At each council he made the same argument. Some of the nations he spoke to had bad histories with one another.

He told them that they had all been losing land in the same way, one treaty at a time. The only way to stop it, he said, was for every nation to refuse to let any single nation sign land away.

Enough nations joined him to make Prophetstown the capital of a confederacy. In 1811 Harrison burned the town while Tecumseh was away. Two years later Tecumseh died in Canada, fighting beside the British.

He had spent eight years building the confederacy, and no other leader took his place.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tecumseh" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee renaissance" -->
In 1821 a Cherokee man named Sequoyah showed a syllabary he had made. A syllabary is a set of written characters in which each character stands for a whole syllable, not a single sound. Sequoyah's had 86 characters. Within a few years most Cherokee people could read and write it. History.com states that by the 1830s a larger share of Cherokee people could read than of the white settlers around them.

Cherokee leaders set up a capital at New Echota in 1825. In 1827 they approved a written constitution, a written plan for their government and its basic laws. They modeled it on the United States Constitution, with three branches of government. Officials of the state of Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished, which means ended by law.

In 1828 the Cherokee began printing the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper. They printed it in Cherokee and in English.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sequoyah-native-nations" name="Sequoyah" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sequoyah

> **Who:** Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language, and made a way to write his own. He is the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone, without first being able to read and write another language.
> **When and where:** born in Taskigi, Tennessee, about 1770. First showed his syllabary in 1821. Died 1843 in Mexico.

Sequoyah could not read English. Nobody had written Cherokee down before his syllabary, so there was no Cherokee writing for him to read either. He worked on the problem for about twelve years.

By 1821 he had made 86 characters, one for each syllable in spoken Cherokee. A person who already spoke Cherokee did not have to learn spelling rules to use them. Because of that, Cherokee speakers learned the syllabary fast. Within a few years most of the Nation was reading and writing.

Sources give Sequoyah's birth year as anywhere from 1770 to 1778. The Library of Congress lists it as 1770 with a question mark, which means the year is not certain. He died in 1843, in Mexico.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sequoyah-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Removal" -->
The Indian Removal Act became law on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could make treaties that traded Native nations' land in the East for land west of the Mississippi River.

Cherokee leaders took their fight with Georgia to the courts. In *Worcester v. Georgia*, decided in 1832, Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the laws of Georgia had no force inside the Cherokee Nation. He held that the Nation was a separate community under the protection of the United States. Judges still treat that ruling as one of the foundations of tribal sovereignty law, the law about a Native nation's right to govern itself. Georgia's officials ignored the ruling, and federal officials went ahead with removal anyway.

The word in the act was "removal." For the Cherokee it meant that in 1838 and 1839, soldiers came to Cherokee houses in the Southeast and took families out at gunpoint. The soldiers forced more than 16,000 people to go to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. More than 4,000 of them died. That figure is the traditional estimate, and some scholars argue the real number was higher. People today call the Cherokee removal the Trail of Tears.

United States officials and soldiers forced all five of the large southeastern nations to move to Indian Territory: the Cherokee, the Muscogee (Creek), the Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Seminole. Within a generation, those nations had rebuilt their governments, their courts, their newspapers and their schools there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** a Cherokee woman who was about three years old when soldiers took her family out of their home in Georgia in 1838.
> **When and where:** Georgia to Indian Territory, 1838 and 1839. Interviewed in Oklahoma in 1932.

In 1838 soldiers took Rebecca Neugin's family out of their home in Georgia. Rebecca was about three years old. On the way west she rode in the wagon, while her mother, her father and her older brother walked.

In 1932 the historian Grant Foreman interviewed her, and she told him what she remembered. She was about 97 years old. The interview came 94 years after the removal, and she was one of the last people still alive who had been on it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Seminole resistance" -->
In Florida the Seminole refused to leave, and the Second Seminole War followed, from 1835 to 1842. It was the longest and costliest war between United States forces and a Native nation east of the Mississippi.

On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup invited the Seminole war leader Osceola to a talk under a white flag of truce. Under a flag of truce, both sides promise not to attack or seize anyone while they talk. Osceola came in under the flag, and Jesup had him seized. Newspaper writers and public figures in the United States condemned the trick at the time, meaning they spoke against it in public. Osceola died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, on January 30, 1838.

The fighting died down after 1842. A few hundred Seminole people never surrendered and stayed deep in the Everglades. Their descendants belong to two tribes today. Federal officials formally recognized the Seminole Tribe of Florida in 1957 and the Miccosukee Tribe in 1962. That means they accepted each tribe as a nation with its own government. Both tribes point out that the Seminole were never conquered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="osceola-native-nations" name="Osceola" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Osceola

> **Who:** Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida.
> **When and where:** Florida. Seized by General Thomas Jesup under a flag of truce, October 21, 1837. Died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838.

Osceola led the Seminole fight against removal for two years, and United States soldiers never beat him in battle. On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup called him to a talk under a white flag of truce. When Osceola arrived, Jesup had him arrested.

Newspaper writers and public figures in the United States said at the time that the arrest was wrong.

Osceola died three months later, on January 30, 1838, a prisoner at Fort Moultrie. The records do not give the year he was born. The war he had been fighting went on for another four years without him.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1850-1900" order="07" chapter="native-nations" label="1850 to 1900" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
These fifty years are the hardest in this chapter. Nations were confined to reservations. A reservation is land set aside for a nation, usually by treaty, which the nation is then required to stay on while the rest of its country is taken. The bison herds that fed the Plains nations were destroyed. In 1871 Congress stopped making treaties with Native nations at all. In 1887 a law cut nations' shared land into private parcels and sold off what was left over. And beginning in 1879 the government built a system of boarding schools whose purpose was to take Native children away from their nations and raise them as something else.

Told from the nations' side, the same fifty years are a record of decisions: when to fight, when to run, when to sign, and how to keep a people alive.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wars, from the nations' side" -->
In Minnesota in 1862 the United States failed to deliver the food and the payments its treaties with the Dakota had promised. The Dakota went to war, and the war lasted six weeks. Afterward, military commissions — courts run by the army — tried 392 Dakota men. Some of the trials took less than five minutes. The men had no defense lawyers and no interpreters. President Abraham Lincoln read the trial records and cut the list of men to be executed down to 39, and one of those was reprieved. On December 26, 1862, at Mankato, 38 Dakota men were hanged at once. It is the largest mass execution in United States history. It emerged afterward that two of the men had been hanged by mistake: one had answered to another man's name, and one had been found not guilty at his trial.

On November 29, 1864, about 675 volunteer soldiers under Colonel John Chivington attacked Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp at Sand Creek in Colorado. The camp was flying a United States flag and a white flag, because its people had been told they were under the army's protection. The attack lasted eight hours. The soldiers killed about 230 people, most of them women, children, and elders.

In 1863 and 1864 the army marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo and held them there. Thousands died in the confinement. In 1868 Navajo leaders negotiated a treaty that let the survivors go home. Almost no other nation removed from its homeland got it back.

Red Cloud's War closed the Bozeman Trail through Lakota country, and the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868 established the Great Sioux Reservation — all of what is now South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the Black Hills — "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. In 1874 an expedition under George Custer announced that there was gold in the Black Hills. The government did not enforce its own treaty. In December 1875 it ordered the winter camps onto the reservation by January 31, 1876, and when they did not come in, it sent the army.

On June 25, 1876, at the Little Bighorn River — the Greasy Grass — the allied Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho camp destroyed the part of the 7th Cavalry that Custer led against it. About 200 men died with him, five companies, inside about an hour.

The victory did not hold the country. Crazy Horse surrendered on May 6, 1877, and on September 5, 1877, at Fort Robinson, he was bayoneted to death while being arrested. Geronimo, of the Chiricahua Apache, surrendered on September 4, 1886 — the last Native leader to formally surrender to the United States. His people were then held as prisoners of war for 27 years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" name="Sitting Bull" movie="Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sitting Bull

> **Who:** Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man; the camp that fought at the Greasy Grass gathered around him.
> **When and where:** the northern Plains; Canada from 1877 to 1881; killed at Standing Rock, December 15, 1890.
> **Movie:** *Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart* (2006) — a documentary built from his own recorded words.

Sitting Bull refused to bring his people onto the reservation. The camp on the Greasy Grass in June 1876 formed around him, and Crazy Horse was one of its battle leaders.

After the fighting he took his people north into Canada in 1877. The bison were being killed off, and by 1881 there was not enough left to feed them. He surrendered at Fort Buford in July of that year.

In 1885 he traveled for one season with Buffalo Bill's Wild West show, at fifty dollars a week, and then went home.

On December 15, 1890, at Standing Rock, Indian police — Native policemen serving the agency — came to arrest him during the alarm over the Ghost Dance. They killed him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chief-joseph" name="Chief Joseph" movie="I Will Fight No More Forever (1975)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Chief Joseph

> **Who:** Nez Perce leader in the flight of 1877 — about 750 people, only some 200 of them fighters, moving with the United States Army behind them.
> **When and where:** Oregon to Montana, June to October 1877; surrendered October 5, 1877, forty miles from Canada.
> **Movie:** *I Will Fight No More Forever* (1975, television film).

In 1877 the Nez Perce left their country rather than be forced onto a reservation. About 750 people went, and only about 200 of them were fighters. The rest were families.

They covered roughly 1,170 miles. That is the National Park Service's figure for the trail; some accounts give a longer distance.

They surrendered on October 5, 1877, forty miles short of the Canadian border. Lieutenant C. E. S. Wood recorded Joseph's words at the surrender: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."

He had been promised that his people could go home. That promise was overruled. They were sent to Kansas, then to Indian Territory, then to the Colville reservation in Washington. Joseph died there in 1904. He was never allowed back to the Wallowa Valley.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chief-joseph" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison, and the end of treaties" -->
There were roughly 30 million bison on the Plains in the early 1800s. By 1900 there were fewer than a thousand; one count in 1884 found 325.

Several things did it. Railroads carried hunters into the herds' country. Hide-hunting became an industry, killing animals for the skins alone. Disease and drought took some. And the army encouraged the killing, because the herds were the Plains nations' food supply, their economy, and part of their religion.

In 1871 Congress ended treaty-making with Native nations. It was done as a rider — a short passage attached to a spending bill, the Indian Appropriations Act — and it drew little notice. Treaties already made stayed in force. But from then on the United States no longer dealt with Native nations by treaty. What came instead were acts of Congress and orders from the president, which a nation had no part in signing.

Those two things together made the reservation era work as it did. The Plains economy was destroyed, and the means nations had used to bargain for two hundred years was closed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Allotment" -->
Native nations held their land in common: the nation owned it, not individual people. Allotment means cutting land held that way into private pieces.

The Dawes Act, also called the General Allotment Act, became law in 1887. It divided nations' land into separate parcels, assigned a parcel to each head of a family, and declared everything left over "surplus," to be sold to settlers.

Native landholdings fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934. That is about 90 million acres — roughly two-thirds of all the land Native nations still had — gone in 47 years. The federal Meriam Report gives the figures as 137 million and 47 million; the loss is about the same either way.

Congress ended allotment with the Indian Reorganization Act in 1934, and that law put money toward buying some land back. It did not return the roughly 90 million acres already gone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding schools" -->
The Carlisle Indian Industrial School opened on November 1, 1879, at Carlisle, Pennsylvania. Its founder was an Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt. It was the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations, and it became the model for the rest.

Pratt stated the purpose himself. In 1892, in a speech to a national conference of charities in Denver, he said that "all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." The line is often quoted in a shortened form; those are the recorded words.

Getting the children took two laws. In 1891 Congress gave the Commissioner of Indian Affairs the power to make and enforce rules compelling Native children to attend school. In 1893 Congress gave the government the power to withhold rations, clothing, and annuity payments — the food, the goods, and the yearly payments that treaties had promised a nation — from parents who would not send their children. Some agents sent reservation police to take children. Some families sent children willingly, hoping the schooling would arm them for what was coming. Many families had no choice at all.

What happened on arrival was the same at school after school. Staff cut the child's hair. They took the child's clothes and put the child in a uniform. They gave the child an English name. They forbade the child to speak the language of home. The federal investigation of the schools documents the punishments used: beatings, solitary confinement, withholding food, and washing children's mouths out with soap.

Half of each school day was unpaid work that ran the institution — the laundry, the fields, the kitchens. Pratt also ran what he called the outing system, which placed students with white families to work.

The United States Department of the Interior investigated the schools and published its findings in 2022 and again on July 30, 2024. It counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states and territories operating between 1819 and 1969; the first volume had counted 408. It identified more than a thousand further institutions with similar aims. At least 973 children died at the federal schools. There are at least 74 burial sites at 65 of them. The report says its own count of the dead is low.

Children kept their languages by speaking them where the staff could not hear. Children from nations hundreds of miles apart, who would never otherwise have met, met at these schools, and those friendships became one root of the Native organizing of the next century. Some students turned the schooling directly against the policy that had produced it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" name="Sarah Winnemucca" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Winnemucca

> **Who:** Northern Paiute writer, interpreter, and lecturer who argued her people's case in front of audiences across the East.
> **When and where:** Nevada, and the lecture halls of the East, in the 1870s and 1880s.

Sarah Winnemucca worked as an interpreter, and then she went east and lectured on how her people were being treated.

In 1883 she published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims*. It is the first known autobiography written by a Native American woman, and she was the first Native woman known to hold a United States copyright.

In the mid-1880s she opened a school of her own for Paiute children near Lovelock, Nevada. Sources give the opening as either 1884 or 1885. She taught in Paiute and in English — the opposite of what the government schools were built to do.

It closed within a few years, for lack of money.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="zitkala-sa-native-nations" name="Zitkala-Ša" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Zitkala-Ša

> **Who:** Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and organizer, taken as a child to a mission boarding school in Indiana.
> **When and where:** the Yankton reservation, South Dakota, and Washington, D.C.; born February 22, 1876, died January 26, 1938.

By her own account, Zitkala-Ša was eight years old when she was taken to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute.

In 1900 the *Atlantic Monthly* published a set of her autobiographical essays, including "The School Days of an Indian Girl," describing what the school did to her. She was employed at Carlisle at the time she published them. The essays were collected in *American Indian Stories* in 1921.

In 1926 she co-founded the National Council of American Indians, and she led it until she died in 1938.

She is one of the reasons the inside of those schools is on the record in a student's own words.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="zitkala-sa-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee" -->
On January 1, 1889, during a solar eclipse, a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka had a vision. He taught that if people lived rightly and danced a round dance, the dead would return and the world would be made whole.

The Ghost Dance spread across reservations where people were hungry. Frightened Indian agents — the federal officials in charge of reservations — and sensational newspapers reported it as the beginning of an uprising.

Sitting Bull was killed at Standing Rock on December 15, 1890, during the alarm.

On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the 7th Cavalry were disarming Spotted Elk's band, also called Big Foot's band, at Wounded Knee Creek when the soldiers opened fire. They fired with carbines and with Hotchkiss guns, which are small cannon. They killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people, most of them women and children. Twenty-five soldiers died; the accounts say most of them were hit by their own side's fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1900 the United States census counted about 250,000 Native people. That is the lowest figure ever recorded, and it is the number the next century starts from.
