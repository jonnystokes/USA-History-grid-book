## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the first half of the 1800s, Native leaders east of the Mississippi River tried two ways to keep their nations' land, and both failed. North of the Ohio River, people from many nations joined a confederacy, a union of nations that agree to act together, and fought American armies. In the Southeast, Cherokee leaders did not go to war. They wrote a constitution, a written plan for their government and its basic laws, and took their fight to court.

Two Shawnee brothers, Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa, built the northern confederacy around one idea. Tecumseh said that the land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell any part of it. In 1811 soldiers under the American commander William Henry Harrison burned the confederacy's capital. Tecumseh died in battle in 1813.

The Cherokee changed their nation fast in the eleven years from 1821 to 1832. In 1821 Sequoyah showed a way to write the Cherokee language. In 1827 Cherokee leaders approved their constitution, with three branches of government, and in 1828 they began printing a newspaper. In 1832 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled in the Cherokee Nation's favor.

In 1830 members of Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. Under it, United States soldiers forced the nations of the Southeast out of their homes and moved them west. More than 4,000 Cherokee people died in their removal alone. In the West, the people of those nations built their governments again.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa" -->
Around 1805 a Shawnee man named Tenskwatawa began preaching that Native people should return to Native ways of living. His brother Tecumseh took the movement to other nations and asked them to join it. In 1808 the brothers built a town on the Wabash River as the capital of a confederacy of many nations. The town was called Prophetstown, because Tenskwatawa was known as the Prophet.

William Henry Harrison, the governor of Indiana Territory, was buying land from the leaders of one nation or a few nations at a time. A treaty is a written agreement between nations, and Harrison made each sale in a treaty. In August 1810 Tecumseh met Harrison at Vincennes and told him that the land belonged to all the nations in common, meaning together. "No part has a right to sell, even to each other, much less to strangers," Tecumseh said. He called the latest sale "bad," because "it was made by a part only." By that argument, every sale made by one nation alone counted for nothing.

On November 7, 1811, while Tecumseh was away asking more nations to join the confederacy, Harrison marched about a thousand men to Prophetstown and fought the people there. The fight became known as the Battle of Tippecanoe. On the next day, November 8, Harrison burned the town.

Fighters from the confederacy fought on beside the British in the War of 1812. On October 5, 1813, they fought the Battle of the Thames at Moraviantown, in the British colony of Upper Canada. Their British ally, Major General Henry Procter, left the battlefield in the middle of the fight. British army officers later put Procter on trial for his failures in that campaign. Tecumseh died in the battle. After his death the confederacy broke apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="tecumseh" name="Tecumseh" movie="We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision (2009)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Tecumseh

> **Who:** Shawnee leader who went from nation to nation arguing that Native land belonged to all the nations together, so no single nation could sell it.
> **When and where:** the Ohio Country and the South, 1805 to 1813. Died in battle at the Thames, October 5, 1813.
> **Movie:** *We Shall Remain: Tecumseh's Vision* (PBS, 2009), a documentary about him and his brother.

Tecumseh spent years going to the meetings of other nations' leaders, north of the Ohio River and in the South. In October 1811 he spoke at the yearly great council of the Creek Nation at Tuckabatchee, where more than 5,000 Creek people had gathered. The Creek leader Big Warrior refused to join him, because he was afraid of losing the support of the United States.

Tecumseh's argument was that the nations had been losing their land one sale at a time. In 1810 he told William Henry Harrison that the land "never was divided, but belongs to all for the use of each." He added, "Any sale not made by all is not valid." Not valid means it does not count.

Enough nations joined him to make Prophetstown the capital of a confederacy. In 1811 Harrison burned the town while Tecumseh was away. Two years later Tecumseh died in Upper Canada, fighting beside the British, and his confederacy broke apart.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="tecumseh" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Cherokee renaissance" -->
In 1821 a Cherokee man named Sequoyah showed a syllabary he had made. A syllabary is a set of written characters in which each character stands for a whole syllable, not a single sound. Sequoyah's had 86 characters. Within a few years most Cherokee people could read and write it. Writers at the website History.com state that by the 1830s a larger share of Cherokee people could read than of the white settlers around them.

Cherokee leaders set up a capital at New Echota in 1825. In 1827 they approved a written constitution. They modeled it on the United States Constitution, with three branches of government. Officials of the state of Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished, which means ended by law.

In 1828 the Cherokee began printing the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper. They printed it in Cherokee and in English.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sequoyah-native-nations" name="Sequoyah" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sequoyah

> **Who:** Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language, and made a way to write his own. He is the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone, without first being able to read and write another language.
> **When and where:** born in Taskigi, Tennessee, about 1770. First showed his syllabary in 1821. Died 1843 in Mexico.

Sequoyah could not read English. Nobody had written Cherokee down before his syllabary, so there was no Cherokee writing for him to read either. He worked on the problem for about twelve years.

By 1821 he had made 86 characters, one for each syllable in spoken Cherokee. Within a few years most of the Cherokee Nation was reading and writing them.

Sources list Sequoyah's birth year as anywhere from 1770 to 1778. The Library of Congress lists it as 1770 with a question mark, which means the year is not certain. He died in 1843, in Mexico.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sequoyah-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Removal" -->
The Indian Removal Act became law on May 28, 1830. Under it, the president could make treaties that traded Native nations' land in the East for land west of the Mississippi River.

Cherokee leaders took their fight with Georgia to the courts. Georgia's lawmakers had made it a crime for a white person to live in the Cherokee Nation without a license from the state. Samuel Worcester, a minister from Vermont who translated parts of the Bible into Cherokee, refused to ask for one. Georgia officers arrested him and other missionaries, people sent to spread their religion, in 1831. Each of them got four years in prison at hard labor. In *Worcester v. Georgia*, decided in 1832, Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the laws of Georgia had no force inside the Cherokee Nation. He held that the Nation was under the protection of the United States. That ruling is still one of the foundations of tribal sovereignty law, the law about a Native nation's right to govern itself. Georgia's officials ignored the ruling, and United States officials went ahead with removal anyway. Worcester stayed in the Georgia prison at Milledgeville until January 1833. He came out after he accepted a pardon, an order forgiving the crime, from Georgia's governor, Wilson Lumpkin.

In 1838 and 1839, soldiers came to Cherokee houses in the Southeast and took families out at gunpoint. The 1830 act called this "removal." The soldiers forced more than 16,000 people to go to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma. More than 4,000 of them died. That figure is the traditional estimate. In 1984 Russell Thornton, a scholar at the University of Minnesota, wrote that the number of deaths "may have been twice" that figure. The Cherokee named the removal *Nunna daul Tsuny*, the Trail Where We Cried. In English it is called the Trail of Tears.

United States officials and soldiers forced all five of the large southeastern nations to move to Indian Territory: the Cherokee, the Muscogee (Creek), the Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Seminole. In Indian Territory, the people of those nations rebuilt their governments, their courts, their newspapers and their schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** a Cherokee woman who was about three years old when soldiers took her family out of their home in Georgia in 1838.
> **When and where:** Georgia to Indian Territory, 1838 and 1839. Interviewed in 1932.

In 1838 soldiers took Rebecca Neugin's family out of their home in Georgia. Rebecca was about three years old. The soldiers held the family in a camp in Tennessee, and in September 1838 the family set out west. Rebecca and the smaller children rode in a wagon, while her mother, her father and her older brother Dick walked the whole way.

In 1932 the historian Grant Foreman interviewed her, and she told him what she remembered of the journey. She was about 97 years old. The interview came 94 years after the removal, and she was one of the last people still alive who had been on it. She died on July 15, 1932.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Seminole resistance" -->
In Florida many Seminole people refused to leave, and the Second Seminole War followed, from 1835 to 1842. It was the longest war between United States forces and a Native nation east of the Mississippi, and the one that cost the most. National Park Service writers give its cost this way: for every four Seminole people the army sent west, soldiers killed one Seminole, three soldiers died, and the army spent $32,000.

On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup lured the Seminole war leader Osceola to a talk under a white flag of truce. Under a flag of truce, both sides promise not to attack or seize anyone while they talk. Osceola came in under the flag, and Jesup had him seized. Many people in the United States protested the trick in public at the time. Osceola died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, on January 30, 1838.

The war ended in 1842. A few hundred Seminole people never surrendered and stayed in the Everglades. Their descendants belong to two tribes today. Federal officials, meaning officials of the national government, recognized the Seminole Tribe of Florida in 1957 and the Miccosukee Tribe in 1962. That means they accepted each tribe as a nation with its own government. Leaders of both tribes point out that the Seminole were never conquered.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="osceola-native-nations" name="Osceola" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Osceola

> **Who:** Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida.
> **When and where:** Florida. Seized by General Thomas Jesup under a flag of truce, October 21, 1837. Died a prisoner at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838.

Osceola was born in 1804 in the Creek town of Talisi, in what is now Alabama, and he was named Billy Powell at birth. He grew up to be a Seminole war leader in the fight against removal from Florida. On October 21, 1837, General Thomas Jesup lured him to a talk under a white flag of truce. When Osceola arrived, Jesup had him seized.

Many people in the United States said in public at the time that the trick was wrong.

Osceola died three months later, on January 30, 1838, a prisoner at Fort Moultrie. After he died, the army doctor Frederick Weedon cut off his head before the burial and put it on display in his drug store. The Seminole kept fighting until 1842.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="osceola-native-nations" -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
United States officials and soldiers forced Native nations onto reservations in the second half of the 1800s. A reservation is an area of land that United States officials set aside for a nation, in a treaty or by an order. Soldiers and officials forced the nation's people onto it and held them there. Officials and settlers then took the rest of the nation's country.

In these years United States soldiers killed hundreds of Native people at a time. At Bear River in 1863 they killed an estimated 250 to 500 Shoshone people, and at Sand Creek in 1864 about 230 Cheyenne and Arapaho people, most of them women, children and elders. Cavalry soldiers on the Marias River in 1870 killed about 177 Piikani people, by the best estimate, and soldiers at Wounded Knee in 1890 killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people. Army officers also hanged 38 Dakota men at one time at Mankato, Minnesota, in 1862.

Hunters killed off the bison herds that fed the Plains nations, and army officers encouraged the killing. In 1871 members of Congress stopped making treaties with Native nations.

In 1879 an Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt opened the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations. Federal means run by the national government of the United States. Federal officials used Pratt's school as the model for many more. Pratt stated his aim in his own words: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." Federal investigators later counted at least 973 children who died at the federal boarding schools.

In 1887 members of Congress passed a law under which federal officials cut nations' shared land into private pieces. The officials opened what was left over to white settlers.

In 1868 Navajo leaders signed a treaty that let their people go home. Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho fighters beat George Custer's soldiers at the Little Bighorn in 1876. The next year the Nez Perce fled toward Canada with the army behind them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The wars, from the nations' side" -->
In Minnesota in 1862, United States officials failed to deliver the food and the payments that their treaties with the Dakota had promised. Dakota fighters went to war, and the war lasted six weeks. Afterward, army officers sitting as military commissions tried 392 Dakota men. A military commission is a court run by the army. Some of the trials took less than five minutes. The men had no defense lawyers and no interpreters, the people who translate between two languages. The commissions sentenced 303 of the men to death by hanging.

President Abraham Lincoln and his lawyers read the trial records. Lincoln looked first for men found guilty of rape, which means forcing a person into sex, and found two. Then he added men found to have taken part in killing people who were not soldiers. He cut the list of men to be executed down to 39. One of the 39 was reprieved, which means his execution was called off. On December 26, 1862, at Mankato, army officers hanged 38 Dakota men together while about 4,000 people watched. The hanging at Mankato is the largest mass execution, the most people put to death at one time, in United States history. Captain William Duley cut the rope. Several members of his family had been killed in a Dakota attack on the Lake Shetek settlement. The bodies went into a shallow grave on a sandbar, and before morning doctors had dug up most of them to use for medical study.

Two of the men had been hanged by mistake. By the Minnesota Historical Society's account, Wicaŋȟpi Wastedaŋpi, who went by the name Caske, stepped forward when the name "Caske" was called. The other, Wasicuŋ, was a young white man whom the Dakota had adopted as a child, and the commission had found him not guilty at his trial.

On January 29, 1863, Colonel Patrick Edward Connor led soldiers against a winter camp of Northwestern Shoshone people on the Bear River, near what is now Preston, Idaho. Connor had announced that he would take no prisoners. His soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 500 Shoshone people. Afterward the army promoted Connor to brigadier general, a higher rank.

In 1863 and 1864, United States soldiers marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to Bosque Redondo and held them there. This forced march is known as the Long Walk. Thousands of the Navajo died while soldiers held them at Bosque Redondo. In 1868 Navajo leaders negotiated a treaty that let the survivors go home. Almost no other nation that United States officials removed from its homeland ever got that homeland back.

Colonel John Chivington led about 675 volunteer soldiers against Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp at Sand Creek in Colorado on November 29, 1864. The camp was flying a United States flag and a white flag. Earlier that month Major Scott Anthony, the commander at nearby Fort Lyon, had told Black Kettle's people to camp at Sand Creek. In his report of November 16 he wrote that he told them "no war would be waged against them" until his own commander decided what to do. When Chivington arrived, Anthony agreed to the attack. The attack lasted eight hours. The soldiers killed about 230 people, most of them women, children and elders. A massacre is the killing of many people who cannot defend themselves. Captain Silas Soule, one of the officers there, refused to let his men fire. He wrote to his mother that he saw "a Massacre of three hundred Indians mostly women and children." In another letter he wrote that he saw small children on their knees beaten to death. Soldiers cut scalps and other body parts from the dead.

A committee of Congress later wrote that Chivington "deliberately planned and executed a foul and dastardly massacre." Dastardly means cowardly and cruel. Chivington left the army and was never put on trial, and no officer or soldier was punished. In April 1865, about two months after Soule spoke against Chivington before investigators, a man shot Soule dead on a Denver street. No one was ever tried for killing him. The National Park Service keeps the place of the massacre today as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site.

Lakota fighters led by Red Cloud fought the army over the Bozeman Trail, a road through Lakota country. That war is called Red Cloud's War. After it, United States officials and Lakota leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. In it, the officials agreed to close the Bozeman Trail. They also set up the Great Sioux Reservation "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. It covered all of what is now South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the Black Hills.

On January 23, 1870, Major Eugene Baker's cavalry came at daybreak to a camp of Piikani (Piegan Blackfeet) people on the Marias River in Montana. His scout, Joe Kipp, told him it was the camp of Heavy Runner's band, which was at peace with the United States. By one account, Baker answered, "That makes no difference, one band or another of them, they are all Piegans and we will attack them." By the best estimate, his soldiers killed 37 men, 90 women and 50 children. The army's own count was 173 dead. Neither Baker nor any of his men was ever punished.

In 1874 members of an expedition led by George Custer announced that there was gold in the Black Hills. Federal officials ignored their own 1868 treaty. In December 1875 they ordered the winter camps that were off the reservation to come onto it by January 31, 1876. When the camps did not come in, the officials sent the army.

George Custer led part of the 7th Cavalry, an army unit of soldiers on horseback, on June 25, 1876. He attacked a camp where Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho people had gathered as allies, meaning they had agreed to fight on the same side. The camp stood by the Little Bighorn River, also called the Greasy Grass. Fighters from the camp killed Custer and about 200 of the soldiers with him in about an hour. Those soldiers made up five companies, smaller units of the 7th Cavalry.

Crazy Horse, one of the battle leaders at the Little Bighorn, surrendered on May 6, 1877, less than a year after the battle. On September 5, 1877, at Fort Robinson, soldiers were taking him to the guardhouse, the fort's jail, to lock him up. When he tried to break away, a Lakota man named Little Big Man jumped on his back, and a soldier on guard stabbed him with a bayonet. A bayonet is a blade fixed to the end of a rifle. Crazy Horse died of the wound. Most accounts name the soldier as Private William Gentles, but only one witness named him, and who he was is disputed. Little Big Man later told a different story, in which Crazy Horse was cut by his own knife.

Geronimo, of the Chiricahua Apache, surrendered on September 4, 1886. He was the last Native leader to formally surrender to the United States. United States officials then held the Chiricahua people as prisoners of war for 27 years. Prisoners of war are people an army holds after capturing them in a war.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" name="Sitting Bull" movie="Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart (2006)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sitting Bull

> **Who:** Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man. The camp that fought at the Greasy Grass gathered around him.
> **When and where:** the northern Plains. Canada from 1877 to 1881. Killed by Indian police at Standing Rock, December 15, 1890.
> **Movie:** *Sitting Bull: A Stone in My Heart* (2006), a documentary built from his own recorded words.

The camp on the Greasy Grass in June 1876 formed around Sitting Bull, and Crazy Horse was one of its battle leaders.

In 1877 Sitting Bull took his people north into Canada. Hunters were killing off the bison, and by 1881 there were not enough left to feed his people. He surrendered at Fort Buford in July of that year.

On June 6, 1885, at the Standing Rock Agency, he signed a contract with Buffalo Bill's Wild West, a traveling show. The show paid him fifty dollars a week for a four-month season, and only he could sell his own photographs and autographs.

On December 15, 1890, at Standing Rock, Indian police came to arrest him while Indian agents were alarmed by the Ghost Dance, a new religious dance spreading across the reservations. Indian police were Native men who worked as police for the agency, the government office that ran the reservation. The policemen killed Sitting Bull.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sitting-bull-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="chief-joseph" name="Chief Joseph" movie="I Will Fight No More Forever (1975)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Chief Joseph

> **Who:** Nez Perce leader in the flight of 1877. About 750 people, only some 200 of them fighters, fled with the United States Army behind them.
> **When and where:** the Wallowa Valley of Oregon to the Bear Paw Mountains of Montana, 1877. Surrendered October 5, 1877, forty miles from Canada.
> **Movie:** *I Will Fight No More Forever* (1975, television film).

Joseph's band of the Nez Perce lived in the Wallowa Valley of northeastern Oregon. In 1877 General Oliver O. Howard told the Nez Perce chiefs that their people had 30 days to move onto the reservation in Idaho, or the army would move them by force. That year about 750 Nez Perce people fled with the army behind them, and only about 200 of them were fighters. The rest were families.

They traveled about 1,170 miles. That is the National Park Service's figure for the trail. Some accounts state a longer distance, up to about 1,700 miles.

They surrendered on October 5, 1877, at the foot of the Bear Paw Mountains in Montana, forty miles short of the Canadian border. Lieutenant C. E. S. Wood recorded Joseph's words at the surrender: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."

Colonel Nelson Miles had promised Joseph that his people would be taken back to the reservation in Idaho. General William Tecumseh Sherman, the commanding general of the army, overruled the promise, which means he cancelled it. He wanted the Nez Perce punished as an example to other nations and never allowed to go home. "I believed General Miles, or I never would have surrendered," Joseph said afterward. United States officials sent his people to Kansas, then to Indian Territory. There, sickness and a lack of supplies cut their number from 410 to 391 in the first months. In May 1885, 268 Nez Perce people left Indian Territory for the Northwest. Officials sent Joseph to the Colville reservation in Washington, and he died there, at Nespelem, in 1904. Officials never allowed him back to the Wallowa Valley.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="chief-joseph" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The bison, and the end of treaties" -->
There were roughly 30 million bison on the Plains in the early 1800s. One count in 1884 found 325. By 1900 there were fewer than a thousand.

Railroads carried hunters into the herds' country. Hunters made hide hunting into a large business, killing bison for their skins. Disease and drought killed others. Army officers encouraged the killing. The herds were the Plains nations' food and the base of how they made a living, and the bison were part of their religion.

In 1871 members of Congress stopped making treaties with Native nations. They did it with a rider, a short passage attached to a spending bill called the Indian Appropriations Act. Treaties already made stayed in force. After 1871, United States officials dealt with Native nations through acts of Congress and orders from the president instead of treaties.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Allotment" -->
Allotment means cutting land that a whole nation owned together into private pieces.

The Dawes Act, also called the General Allotment Act, became law in 1887. Under it, federal officials divided nations' land into separate parcels, or pieces. The head of a family got 160 acres of farmland, a single man got 80 acres, and each child got 40 acres. Officials called everything left over "surplus," meaning extra, and opened it to white settlers.

Between 1887 and 1934, Native nations lost about 90 million acres. The land they held fell from about 138 million acres to 48 million. The lost land was about two-thirds of all the land Native nations still had in 1887. The Meriam Report, a federal study published in 1928, states the figures as 137 million and 47 million acres. The loss is about the same by either count.

In 1934 members of Congress passed the Indian Reorganization Act. In it they ended allotment, and they allowed up to $2,000,000 a year to be spent buying land for Native people.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The boarding schools" -->
The Carlisle Indian Industrial School opened on November 1, 1879, at Carlisle, Pennsylvania. Its founder was an Army officer named Richard Henry Pratt. It was the first federal boarding school for Native children built far from the reservations. Federal officials used Carlisle as the model for the schools that followed.

Pratt stated the purpose himself. In 1892 he spoke to a national conference of charities in Denver. He said that "all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." People often quote the line in a shorter form, "kill the Indian, save the man." Pratt's recorded words are the longer ones, printed in the conference's official report.

Members of Congress passed two laws to get the children into the schools. In 1891 they gave the Commissioner of Indian Affairs the power to make and enforce rules forcing Native children to attend school. The Commissioner was the federal official in charge of Native affairs. In 1893 they let federal officials hold back rations, clothing and annuity payments from parents who would not send their children. Rations, clothing and annuities were the food, goods and yearly payments that treaties had promised a nation.

Some Indian agents sent reservation police to seize children. An Indian agent was the federal official in charge of a reservation. Some families sent their children willingly, hoping the schooling would help them. Many families had no real choice.

When a child arrived, staff at school after school cut the child's hair and replaced the child's clothes with a uniform. They gave the child an English name and forbade the child to speak the language of home. Staff punished children for speaking their own languages. The federal investigation of the schools and survivors' own accounts document the punishments: beatings, solitary confinement, withholding food, and washing children's mouths out with soap. Solitary confinement means locking a child away alone.

Children spent half of each school day doing unpaid work that kept the school running. Pratt also ran what he called the outing system. Under it, he sent students to live and work with white families, in the summers and during school terms.

Investigators at the United States Department of the Interior studied the schools. They published their findings in 2022 and again on July 30, 2024. They counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states and territories, open between 1819 and 1969. Their first report, in 2022, listed 408. They also found more than a thousand other places that had similar aims. At least 973 children died at the federal schools. The investigators found at least 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 of the schools. The report states that its count of the dead is low.

Children kept their languages by speaking them where the staff could not hear. At these schools, children made friends with children from other nations. Those friendships were one of the starting points of the Native organizing of the next century. Some students, Zitkala-Ša among them, used the reading and writing they learned at the schools to argue against the schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" name="Sarah Winnemucca" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Sarah Winnemucca

> **Who:** Northern Paiute writer, interpreter, and lecturer who argued her people's case in front of audiences across the East.
> **When and where:** Nevada, and the lecture halls of the East, in the 1870s and 1880s.

Sarah Winnemucca worked as an interpreter, a person who puts spoken words from one language into another.

In 1879 United States officials forced about 550 Northern Paiute men, women and children to move 350 miles north, from the Malheur reservation in Oregon to the Yakama reservation. The march took nearly a month through snow and over two mountain ranges. The officials in charge made some of the men walk in leg irons, chains locked around the ankles. Five children, one woman and an old man died on the road, and their bodies were left beside it because the ground was too cold to bury them. The historian Sally Zanjani estimated that more than one fifth of the group died in exile, mostly of hunger and disease.

Winnemucca went east and gave lectures about what officials had done to her people. In 1883 she published *Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims*. It is the first known autobiography written by a Native American woman. An autobiography is a book a person writes about their own life. She was also the first Native woman known to hold a United States copyright, the legal right to control who copies and sells a piece of writing.

In the mid-1880s she opened a school of her own for Paiute children near Lovelock, Nevada. Sources list the opening year as either 1884 or 1885. Staff at the government boarding schools forbade children to speak their own languages. At her school, Winnemucca taught in both Paiute and English.

The school ran out of money and closed within a few years.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="sarah-winnemucca-native-nations" -->

### Zitkala-Ša

> **Who:** Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and organizer, taken as a child to a boarding school in Indiana.
> **When and where:** the Yankton reservation, South Dakota, and Washington, D.C. Born February 22, 1876. Died January 26, 1938.

By her own account, Zitkala-Ša was eight years old when she was taken to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute. The records do not name the person who took her.

In 1900 the editors of the *Atlantic Monthly*, a magazine, published a set of her essays about her own life. One of them was "The School Days of an Indian Girl." In the essays she described her time at the school and criticized it. She was working at Carlisle when she published them. In 1921 they appeared again in her book *American Indian Stories*.

In 1926 she helped start the National Council of American Indians, and she led it until she died in 1938.

On January 1, 1889, a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka, from the Walker Lake country of Nevada, had a vision. It came during a solar eclipse, when the moon blocked the sun. He taught that if people lived rightly and danced a round dance, a dance in which people move in a circle, the dead would return and the world would be made whole.

On reservations where people were hungry, they took up the dance, which became known as the Ghost Dance. Indian agents who were frightened by it called it the start of an uprising, meaning an armed revolt. Newspaper writers wrote exaggerated stories that said the same.

Indian police killed Sitting Bull at Standing Rock on December 15, 1890, while they were trying to arrest him.

On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the 7th Cavalry under Colonel James W. Forsyth were disarming Spotted Elk's band, also called Big Foot's band, at Wounded Knee Creek. Disarming means taking away people's weapons. A band is a group of families who live and travel together. During the disarming, a rifle belonging to Black Coyote, a deaf man, went off. By some accounts he fired first, and by others a soldier tried to grab his gun and it went off. The soldiers opened fire.

They fired carbines, which are short rifles, and four Hotchkiss guns, which are small cannon, set on a hill above the camp. The soldiers killed between 250 and 300 Lakota people. In early January 1891 a burial party put 146 of the dead in one grave at the site. They were 84 men and boys, 44 women and 18 children. Others died later of their wounds, and some bodies were never found, so no count gives the women and children among all the dead. The killing is known as the Wounded Knee Massacre. Twenty-five soldiers also died. Writers for Britannica and History.com say that most of those soldiers were hit by their own side's fire. Twenty soldiers of the 7th Cavalry received the Medal of Honor, the army's highest award, for what they did that day.
