## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 2 of 37 · Native Nations · 10 eras · about 100 minutes**

**Native nations were governments long before the United States existed, and through every war, treaty and law aimed at ending them, 574 of them still govern themselves today.**

The Haudenosaunee recorded their first agreement with the Dutch on a belt of shell beads. It is called the Two Row Wampum, or Guswenta. Two purple rows of beads run along a white background. They stand for two boats traveling the same river side by side, with neither one steering the other. Haudenosaunee tradition dates the agreement to 1613. A paper document with the same date also exists, and accounts differ about whether it is genuine.

This chapter follows the nations through ten eras, from the cities and confederacies built before 1500 to the courtrooms and classrooms of today. Watch for the nations acting as governments. Their leaders make alliances, sign treaties, write constitutions, go to war and go to court. Against them, United States officials and soldiers take most of their land through removal at gunpoint, massacres, allotment and boarding schools that took children from their homes. After 1950 the story turns. Nations win back schools, fishing rights and, in 2020, a reservation that five Supreme Court justices said had never ended.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did Native nations govern themselves and deal with other nations before the United States existed?
- What did United States officials and soldiers do to take the nations' land and break the nations up?
- How did Native nations hold on to their governments, their languages and some of their land?

**Five minutes?** Read "Removal and the Trail of Tears" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Comanche power" and "Treaty councils" in 1700 to 1750, and "Ironworkers from Kahnawake" in 1900 to 1950. The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

The nations of this land governed themselves, traded and made war and peace for thousands of years before any ship came from Europe. At Celilo Falls, on the Columbia River, archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study old camps and tools, date the first signs of people to at least 10,000 years ago. Some accounts say 15,000. In those thousands of years, some peoples built cities and later left them.

When Europeans first arrived, people in what are now the United States and Canada spoke more than 300 languages. That is the count of Lyle Campbell, a scholar who studies languages, in Britannica. He sorts them into 57 families of related languages, and 20 of those families were found only in California. The nations built towns and carried goods hundreds of miles to trade. They made agreements with other nations and fought wars against them.

Nobody knows how many people lived here before 1500. Scholars have used different methods and reached different answers. Douglas Ubelaker put the number for the lower 48 states at about 1.85 million in 1976. Henry Dobyns, in 1983, put it at about 18 million for all of North America north of Mexico. Textbooks published between 1988 and 1990 gave numbers from 1 million to 12 million. No number is settled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cities and kingdoms" -->
People built Cahokia, the largest city north of Mexico, near where St. Louis, Missouri, stands today. They lived there from about 700 to about 1400. The city was biggest around 1100, when roughly 10,000 to 20,000 people lived in it. London had about as many people at the same date. Some estimates put as many as 50,000 in the country around the city.

**Why they left.** Cahokia's people had scattered by about 1350, and by about 1400 nobody lived there. Why they left is still argued. The causes put forward are drought, floods, and running short of the wood and food its people needed. No single cause is settled.

Ancestral Puebloan people, the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples (*pueblo* is the Spanish word for town), built large stone buildings called great houses in Chaco Canyon, in what is now New Mexico. After long droughts, people began leaving Chaco around 1140, and by 1200 most of them had gone. They moved into the Pueblo communities of the Southwest.

The Calusa of southwest Florida built a kingdom without farming. Fish and shellfish fed about 20,000 people. Their capital, Calos, stood on mounds of shell at Mound Key. The Calusa kept live fish in pens with walls of shell, called watercourts. Scientists have dated the watercourts to between 1300 and 1400.

The Calusa also collected tribute, regular payments in goods, from other groups. Those groups lived along the coast from south of Tampa Bay down to the Florida Keys.

Traders carried turquoise, seashells, copper and obsidian, a black volcanic glass that chips to a sharp edge, hundreds of miles from nation to nation. Native trails crossed the continent, and later newcomers traveled along them.

The ancestors of the Navajo (Diné) and the Apache moved south toward the Southwest from what is now western Canada and eastern Alaska. Accounts differ about when they arrived. Some date the arrival to the 1200s, and others put it in the 1400s or as late as about 1525.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did five warring nations become one?" -->
Five nations that had been fighting one another, the Mohawk, the Oneida, the Onondaga, the Cayuga and the Seneca, accepted an agreement called the Great Law of Peace, or Gayanesshagowa. Under it they formed one confederacy, the Haudenosaunee, a group of nations that keep their own governments but act together on matters they share. The five nations came together under one Grand Council.

According to Haudenosaunee tradition, two men brought the five warring nations together under the Great Law. They were the Peacemaker and Hiawatha. The Haudenosaunee kept the law in memory and recited it aloud. Instead of writing it on paper, they recorded it on wampum belts, shell beads woven into patterns in which each pattern stands for the terms of an agreement.

Scholars disagree about when the Confederacy began. Barbara Mann and Jerry Fields date the founding to 1142, from an eclipse of the sun described in the founding tradition. Most other estimates place the founding between the middle 1100s and the late 1400s, and many favor the middle or late 1400s.

Writers for the Library of Congress describe the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as one of the oldest democracies, governments in which the people have a say, still existing in the world.

Few records of single persons survive from these centuries.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 9 minutes**

Spanish armies marched inland in the 1500s, and the diseases they carried reached many Native towns before the soldiers did. These expeditions were large armed groups traveling into country new to them. The diseases spread ahead of them and behind them. A town could lose many of its people before any European arrived there, and the diseases killed large shares of whole communities. Some writers call these deaths the Great Dying.

No one can give a single death toll, because no one knows how many people lived here at the start. Estimates for North America north of Mexico run from about 1 million to about 18 million, so a death toll worked out from the low estimate and one worked out from the high estimate differ by millions of people.

United States census takers, the officials who count the people of the country, counted about 250,000 Native people in 1900. Even the lowest estimate for 1500, about 1 million, is four times that count. Disease killed many of the missing people. Over the four centuries after 1500, soldiers, settlers and militias killed many others in wars and massacres and while forcing whole nations off their land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The entradas" -->
Spanish leaders called their armed expeditions into the north entradas. Hernando de Soto led one across the Southeast from 1539 to 1543. Francisco Vázquez de Coronado led another across the Southwest and the southern Plains from 1540 to 1542, with more than 300 armed Europeans and at least 1,200 Native allies.

De Soto sailed from Havana in May 1539 and landed in Florida with more than 620 men and 220 horses. For four years his men marched through the chiefdoms of the Mississippians, the town-building peoples of the Mississippi Valley and the Southeast in these centuries. Each chiefdom was a group of towns under a single ruler.

**What de Soto's men did.** They attacked the people of the towns on their route and forced people to work for the expedition. At the town of Hymahi, de Soto took all of the town's food. Later he burned at least one of its people to death while he questioned them about another town, Cofitachequi. His men also carried disease with them.

De Soto died of a fever in the spring of 1542, and the survivors of his expedition reached Mexico on September 10, 1543. His men were the first Europeans to see those chiefdoms, and almost the last. In the decades after they attacked the towns, forced the people to work and spread disease among them, the chiefdoms broke apart. Over the century that followed, the survivors regrouped and took in refugees, people who had fled their own towns. They formed the new nations and confederacies of the Southeast.

Spanish colonists also brought horses back to North America. Horses had lived here long before, but they had died out thousands of years earlier. Christopher Columbus carried horses on his second voyage, in 1493, and Spanish colonists brought more to the mainland through the 1500s. Coronado's expedition brought horses in 1540, and Juan de Oñate's colonists brought them to New Mexico in 1598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Paquiquineo (Don Luís)

> **Who:** A young man from the Chesapeake Bay country who came home with a Spanish mission in 1570 and then led an attack on it.
> **When and where:** Left the Chesapeake in 1561. Landed back on the James River in September 1570.

**He met the king of Spain, then spent nine years getting home.**

Paquiquineo left the Chesapeake Bay country on a Spanish ship in 1561, as a young man. The Spanish records do not settle whether he went willingly or was taken.

He appeared before King Philip II in Spain. In Mexico City he was baptized, made a member of the Christian church in a ceremony, and given the name of the viceroy there, the man who governed the colony for the king, Don Luís de Velasco.

He came home in September 1570. He landed on the James River as guide and translator for a small Jesuit mission, a group of priests from the Jesuit order of the Catholic church, with helpers, sent to turn people to Christianity. Father Juan Bautista de Segura led this mission.

Paquiquineo led an attack on the mission in February 1571, and the attackers killed the missionaries. They spared the altar boy, Alonso de Olmos, who helped the priests at church services.

**The Spanish answer.** Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, the Spanish governor of Florida, sent soldiers to the James River in August 1572. They captured some of Paquiquineo's people in a fight. The Spanish found several of the captives guilty of killing the missionaries and hanged them from the yardarms, the crossbeams on the masts, of their ship. The Spanish records do not give the names of the men they hanged.

The Powhatan people returned Alonso de Olmos to the Spanish. The soldiers did not find Paquiquineo. Spanish officials sent no more missions to the Chesapeake. English colonists landed at Jamestown, in the same country, 36 years after the mission, in 1607.

**Not known:** what became of Paquiquineo. European records after 1572 do not include him, and no story passed down by word of mouth that can be checked describes him.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paquiquineo" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acoma, 1598 and 1599" -->
Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north from Mexico in 1598 and founded a colony in New Mexico, the country of the Pueblo nations. Acoma Pueblo, the town of the Acoma people, stands on a mesa, a hill with a flat top and steep sides.

**The paper.** Before any fighting, Oñate had gathered the Acoma elders for a ceremony in which they promised to obey the king of Spain. The Spanish wrote it down as the "Act of Obedience and Vassalage by the Indians of Acoma." (A vassal is a person bound to obey a ruler.) From then on the Spanish treated the Acoma as subjects of the king of Spain, bound by Spanish law. Spanish officials later used that paper to argue that their war on Acoma was legal.

Oñate's nephew Juan de Zaldívar came to Acoma in December 1598 with about thirty soldiers. He took some of them up the mesa to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, as reporters for the Rio Grande Sun newspaper told it in 2019, the soldiers raped an Acoma woman, forcing her into sex.

The All Pueblo Council of Governors is a council of the leaders of the Pueblo nations. In a public statement in October 2023, its leaders said that the soldiers "had demanded food and supplies, assaulted an Acoma woman, and forced allegiance to the Spanish crown," which means the soldiers made the Acoma promise loyalty to the king of Spain. Then the Acoma killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men. Accounts differ on the number.

Oñate opened a formal inquiry, an official hearing to find out what had happened, on December 28. He questioned the survivors of Zaldívar's party. He asked the Franciscan friars with him, Catholic religious brothers of the Franciscan order, whether a war against Acoma would be just. The friars answered that he "possessed both the authority and sufficient cause," which meant he had the right to make war and a good enough reason.

**The attack.** Juan de Zaldívar's brother Vicente led about seventy soldiers up the mesa in January 1599. Over two or three days of fighting, his soldiers killed Acoma men, women and children. They took the town and burned much of it.

Nobody counted the dead. The only detailed written story of the fighting by someone who was there is a long poem published in 1610 by Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, a captain who rode with the expedition. He wrote it to praise the expedition.

Estimates of the dead run from about 300 to about 1,500, and others fall between, at about 600, at 600 to 800 and at about 800. Leaders of the All Pueblo Council of Governors use the figure of about 800 Acoma men, women and children today. The National Park Service's figure is about 1,500.

Vicente de Zaldívar's soldiers captured about 500 Acoma men, women and children. One record gives the number put on trial as 507. Oñate tried them at Santo Domingo Pueblo, though some accounts place the trial at San Juan Pueblo.

**The sentences.** Oñate passed sentence on February 12, 1599. He sentenced men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off and to be forced to work for the Spanish without pay for twenty years. Males from twelve to twenty-five, and females over twelve, got the same twenty years of forced work. He sentenced the old people to be handed over as slaves to the Plains Apache.

Oñate sentenced two Hopi men captured in the fighting to have their right hands cut off. Then he had them released to carry word of the punishment to their own people.

Oñate declared the Acoma children under twelve innocent. He still ordered them taken from their parents and handed to the friars. On Oñate's order, about seventy girls under twelve were sent south along the road to Mexico, to convents there, houses of nuns, women who have promised their lives to the Catholic church. Neither the National Park Service's account nor the Pueblo council's statement names the men who took the girls.

The National Park Service's account states that almost certainly none of the girls ever came home. The Pueblo council's 2023 statement gives sixty children taken to Mexico, and it also states that none returned. According to PBS, some scholars believe the children were later sold as slaves.

**The feet.** Oñate's sentence is in the written record. Whether anyone carried out the cutting of feet is disputed. Acoma people have passed down their own account since 1599: Oñate had the right feet of 24 captive men cut off. The New York Times reported that account in 1998. Two historians who worked for years in these records doubt that the feet were cut off. Marc Simmons first wrote that they were, and he later came to doubt it. John Kessell notes that no record describes a one-footed Acoma man.

Spanish officials charged Oñate with thirty crimes. Accounts date the charges to 1612 or 1613 and the judges' decision to 1614. The judges found him guilty of twelve, among them using too much force against Acoma. They banished him from New Mexico for life, ordering him never to return. They also barred him from Mexico City for four years (PBS gives five) and fined him 6,000 ducats, Spanish gold coins.

Oñate had rebuilt a fortune in his family's mines at Zacatecas. Rebecca Carlson, who wrote about his trial in a 2008 university paper, calls the fine unimportant to him. He spent years working to have the decision undone. Accounts differ on whether the king ever pardoned him, which means officially forgave him. Carlson writes that he was pardoned in 1623. Other accounts say the proof of a pardon is unclear.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 11 minutes**

Native nations still held nearly the whole continent through the 1600s. Along the eastern coast, the European settlements were a thin strip. Those settlers needed food and trade from the nations around them, and they stayed only as long as those nations let them. In New Mexico, Spanish colonists had ruled the Pueblo nations by force since 1598. Native leaders chose which newcomers to deal with, the Dutch, the French, the English or the Spanish, and used each group's rivalry with the others to bargain.

Haudenosaunee leaders made alliances with the newcomers and recorded them on wampum belts. In New England, English colonists fought two wars against coastal nations, the Pequot War of 1636 to 1638 and Metacom's war of 1675 and 1676. In the Southwest in 1680, the people of the Pueblo nations rose together and drove the Spanish off their land. They kept the Spanish out for twelve years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who ruled the land where Jamestown stood?" -->
Wahunsenacawh ruled the country where English colonists founded Jamestown in 1607. He had inherited rule over six tribes in eastern Virginia and built them into a group of more than thirty tribes that paid him tribute. His country was called Tsenacommacah. The English called him "the Powhatan." His own title was mamanatowick, or paramount chief, a chief whom other chiefs answer to.

About 15,000 people lived in his country, spread across some 10,000 square miles between the James River and the Potomac. Each tribe there was a people with its own leader, called a weroance, and each weroance paid tribute to Wahunsenacawh.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum diplomacy and the Covenant Chain" -->
Haudenosaunee diplomats, the people who spoke for their nations with other nations, recorded agreements on wampum belts. Speakers kept the words of each agreement in memory, and the pattern of the belt recorded its terms.

The Two Row Wampum, Guswenta, records the Haudenosaunee account of their first agreement with the Dutch. It has two purple rows of beads on a white background. The rows stand for two boats traveling the same river side by side, with neither one steering the other. Haudenosaunee tradition dates the agreement to 1613. A paper document with the same date also exists, and accounts differ about whether it is genuine.

The Haudenosaunee alliance with the English colonies was called the Covenant Chain, from covenant, a serious promise. The oldest surviving record of the Silver Covenant Chain dates from 1677. That year Haudenosaunee leaders and the New York governor, Edmund Andros, made the alliance official at Albany.

**Polishing the chain.** Silver tarnishes, turning dark over time, so a silver chain has to be polished to stay bright. Haudenosaunee and English leaders "polished" the Covenant Chain again and again. They renewed the alliance at councils, with speeches and wampum.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The coastal wars" -->
In Connecticut, English colonists and their Mohegan and Narragansett allies fought the Pequot from 1636 to 1638. Before dawn on May 26, 1637, an English-led force set fire to the Pequot town at Mystic, which was protected by walls. In under an hour they killed hundreds of people, most of them elders, women and children.

The English colonists and their Mohegan and Narragansett allies ended the war with the Treaty of Hartford of 1638, a treaty being a formal written agreement between nations. In it the winners declared that the Pequot nation no longer existed and made it illegal to use the name Pequot. Under the treaty, they took Pequot survivors for themselves or sold them into slavery. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation is in Connecticut today.

**Sold overseas.** Colonists had already begun selling captives during the war. Captain William Peirce sailed the ship *Desire* out of Boston in July 1637 with seventeen Pequot captives, fifteen of them children. Governor John Winthrop of Massachusetts wrote, "We sent fifteen of the boys and two of the women to Bermuda, by Mr. Pierce." Peirce sold them in the Caribbean.

Metacom, the Wampanoag sachem, or leader of the nation, led a coalition of nations, a group that agreed to act together, against the New England colonies. His coalition fought the colonists from 1675 to 1676, nearly forty years after the Pequot War. Colonial soldiers and their Native allies killed Native people, and the coalition's fighters killed colonists.

**The toll.** About 9,000 people died in all, and estimates put Native deaths at roughly half of all the Native people in New England. For the size of the population, writers for Britannica and other histories call it the bloodiest war in American history.

The men who governed the Massachusetts colony ordered on October 13, 1675, that Native people from the praying towns, villages of Native people who had become Christians, be taken to Deer Island in Boston Harbor and kept there. The colonists feared these people would turn against the English and join Metacom. Colonists took the people of Natick to the island first.

**Deer Island.** The National Park Service puts the number held there at about 500 to 1,100, most of them women and children. They had too little food and shelter through a freezing winter. The two accounts of the deaths differ. The National Park Service says that very few of them survived. The Partnership of Historic Bostons, a Boston history group, says that more than half of them died over the winter.

Colonists released the survivors in May 1676. An unknown number had been smuggled off the island and sold as slaves in the West Indies or in Tangier, in North Africa. No surviving record names the men who voted for the island prison, and the National Park Service's account does not name the men who sold the captives. Every year, descendants of the nations in the war hold a gathering on Deer Island to remember them.

Inland, from the middle to the late 1600s, Haudenosaunee warriors fought the Beaver Wars against nations to the west and north. They fought for control of the fur trade, the trade in animal furs with Europeans. More than 1,000 Seneca and Mohawk warriors attacked the two main towns of the Wendat, also called the Huron, in 1649. The warriors destroyed the Wendat nation as a single people.

Some Wendat survivors fled to Quebec, and others went to the Neutral nation. Haudenosaunee warriors defeated the Neutral in 1651. They absorbed the Erie in 1657, taking the Erie survivors into the Haudenosaunee nations. By 1675, the writers of the Canadian Encyclopedia say, they had absorbed the Susquehannock to the south and moved west into the Ohio Valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="metacom-native-nations" name="Metacom (King Philip)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Metacom (King Philip)

> **Who:** Wampanoag sachem, son of Massasoit, who led the coalition war of 1675 and 1676 against the New England colonies. The English called him King Philip.
> **When and where:** Southern New England. Killed at Mount Hope, August 12, 1676.

**"Philip's boy goes now to be sold," a minister wrote in 1677.**

Metacom's father, Massasoit, had made an arrangement with the Plymouth colonists in 1621. In 1675 Metacom took a coalition of nations to war against the New England colonies. The company of the Plymouth captain Benjamin Church captured Metacom's wife and his nine-year-old son in early August 1676.

**Mount Hope.** A Native man named Alderman, who was serving with Church's company, shot Metacom dead at Mount Hope on August 12, 1676. Church wrote his own account of what came next. He ordered that "not one of his bones should be buried," because Metacom had left English dead unburied. Church told a Native man he called his "old Indian Executioner" to cut off Metacom's head and to quarter his body, cutting it into four pieces.

Church gave the head and one of Metacom's hands to Alderman, who showed the hand to colonists who paid him to see it. Plymouth's leaders paid Church's men thirty shillings, a sum in English money, for each enemy they had killed or captured, and they paid the same for Metacom's head. At Plymouth the head was set on a pole. Church's book does not say who put it there. By the account of a later editor of Church's book, it stayed there for more than 24 years.

**The boy.** The leaders of Plymouth Colony asked ministers what to do with Metacom's son. Two ministers, Samuel Arnold and John Cotton, wrote on September 7, 1676, that the children of such enemies could be put to death. Cotton wrote the line about "Philip's boy" in a letter in March 1677. The boy and his mother were sold into slavery. Cotton's letter does not say who sold them. Most accounts say the two were sent to Bermuda, and others say they were sent to the West Indies.

Colonial leaders sold hundreds of other captives into slavery to pay what the war had cost the colonies. On August 4, 1675, the men of Plymouth's council of war ruled that 112 Native men, women and children held at Plymouth were "to be sold," and they named the colony's treasurer to sell them. Church wrote that these people had surrendered because of promises made to them, and that about 160 of them were "sold, and transported out of the Country."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="metacom-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pueblo Revolt" -->
The people of the Pueblo nations along the Rio Grande drove the Spanish out of New Mexico in 1680. They had lived under Spanish rule since Juan de Oñate founded the colony in 1598.

**The arrests.** The Spanish governor, Juan Francisco Treviño, ordered 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested in 1675. The Spanish called their religious practice sorcery, using magic to do harm. Spanish officials sentenced four of the leaders to be hanged. Three were hanged, and the fourth killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the others in public and put them in prison. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be freed, and the Spanish let them go. One of the freed leaders was Po'pay.

The people of many separate towns rose against the Spanish on the same day, August 10, 1680. They spoke Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Tano, Keres and other languages, and the Zuni and the Hopi also promised to help. Pueblo fighters besieged Santa Fe, surrounding the town and cutting it off. They took the town and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. In the rising they killed 401 settlers and 21 Franciscan friars. The Pueblo fighters let most of the Spanish leave, and the Spanish went south.

The Pueblos governed themselves for the next twelve years, through drought, raids and famine, a long shortage of food. Diego de Vargas led Spanish forces back into New Mexico in 1692 to retake it, and Pueblo fighters kept up armed resistance for years after that. Writers of the EBSCO Research Starters history guide call the Pueblo Revolt the most successful uprising by Native people against European rule in North America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pope" name="Po'pay (Popé)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Po'pay (Popé)

> **Who:** A Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who organized the 1680 rising across dozens of towns and several languages.
> **When and where:** New Mexico, August 1680.

**Knotted cords counted down the days to the rising.**

Po'pay had to get the people of dozens of towns to rise on a single day, and the people of those towns did not all speak the same language.

Pueblo accounts say that runners carried knotted cords from town to town. The knots on each cord marked the days left until the rising. Po'pay set the rising for August 11, by one account, or August 13, by another.

**The runners.** The Spanish captured two of the runners, Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua of Tesuque Pueblo, on August 9. Spanish officials tortured them, hurting them on purpose to force them to explain the knotted cords. The Smithsonian's account of the runners does not say how they were hurt. Later the Spanish hanged them. No surviving record names the men who did it.

When Po'pay learned that the runners had been caught, he ordered the rising to begin early. It began on August 10, 1680.

A statue of Po'pay stands in the United States Capitol. It is one of New Mexico's two statues in the Capitol's Statuary Hall collection, which holds statues of two people from each state's history.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pope" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
The Spanish left their horses behind when they left New Mexico in 1680. An estimated 1,500 or more horses passed into Native hands at once. Writers for the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian call it the largest single transfer of horses. According to the traditional account, Native people then traded horses west and north along Native trade routes. The horses reached the Ute, the Navajo, the Apache, the Comanche, the Kiowa, the Shoshone and the nations of the northern Plains.

**A newer finding.** A team led by William Taylor published a study in the journal *Science* in 2023, and its findings do not fit this account. Lakota, Comanche and Pawnee people were among the study's writers, and other Native partners worked on it too. The team used radiocarbon dating on the remains of horses from Wyoming, Kansas and New Mexico. Radiocarbon dating measures a form of carbon that breaks down at a steady rate, so the amount left shows how old a bone is.

The horses had been ridden and had eaten corn that people fed them. They had also worn bridles, straps around a horse's head used to steer it. They were there by the early 1600s, decades before the revolt. Native oral histories, accounts passed down by telling, had said the same thing.

**Known:** Plains nations had horses by the 1700s.
**Not known:** whether most of their horses came from the Spanish herds left in 1680 or had spread north decades earlier.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 4 minutes**

Haudenosaunee leaders bargained with French and British officials for fifty years, because both sides wanted Native allies. Native nations also went on making their own agreements with one another.

The Haudenosaunee accepted the Tuscarora as a member nation in these years, after South Carolina forces had defeated the Tuscarora and colonists had taken their land in North Carolina. On the southern Plains, the Comanche built the strongest power in the region on horses and trade.

The printed speeches of the Onondaga speaker Canasatego, from these years, are among the best records of Native diplomacy, the work of dealing with other nations through talks and agreements, from that century.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Staying neutral" -->
About 1,300 delegates, people sent to speak for their nations, came from more than thirty nations to meet at Montreal on August 4, 1701. There they made peace with the leaders of New France, the French colony in what is now Canada. As part of the same peace, the Haudenosaunee promised to stay neutral. That meant they would fight for neither France nor Britain.

For half a century, Haudenosaunee leaders used their neutrality to bargain with both sides.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did the Five Nations become six?" -->
The Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee became six after the Tuscarora lost a war, and their land, in North Carolina. The Tuscarora moved north and asked to join them.

**The war.** More than 400 Swiss and German settlers, led by Baron Christoph von Graffenried, founded the town of New Bern, North Carolina, on Tuscarora land in 1710. They pushed the people of the Native town of Chattoka off the spot where the Neuse and Trent rivers meet. Slave raiders were also capturing Tuscarora people to sell them as slaves. The State Library of North Carolina's history of the war does not name the raiders.

Tuscarora fighters struck back on September 22, 1711, in attacks they had planned together. The war lasted until 1713. South Carolina colonial forces, with Native allies of their own, defeated the Tuscarora. By that library's count, 1,400 Tuscarora were dead and 1,000 had been captured and enslaved.

About 1,500 Tuscarora survivors then moved north, roughly 500 miles. They asked the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee to sponsor them, which meant to speak for them and accept them. The Oneida took them in. In 1722 the whole Tuscarora nation became the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Since then the Confederacy has had six member nations, and it is also known as the Six Nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Comanche power" -->
The Comanche moved onto the southern Plains in the early 1700s and became the most powerful nation there. Comanchería, the country they controlled, later covered about 240,000 square miles. That is twenty-four times the size of Wahunsenacawh's country in Virginia.

The Comanche built their power on horses. They bred, trained and traded horses, and they controlled the horse trade from the southern Plains to the northern Plains. In the 1740s they traded bison robes and horses to French traders for guns, which were better than the weapons the Comanche could get from the Spanish. Comanche fighters also raided other peoples, making quick attacks to seize goods, horses or people. Comanche leaders made agreements with other nations as well as fighting them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Treaty councils" -->
At treaty councils, Native leaders and colonial officials met to bargain and make agreements. Speakers on both sides made speeches and exchanged wampum.

At Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in June and July of 1744, colonial officials paid the Haudenosaunee to give up their claims to the Shenandoah Valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="canasatego" name="Canasatego" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Canasatego

> **Who:** Onondaga leader and the Haudenosaunee's speaker at treaty councils, whose 1744 speech on union Benjamin Franklin printed.
> **When and where:** Lancaster, Pennsylvania, June and July 1744. He gave the union speech on July 4, 1744.

**On July 4, 1744, he told the colonies to unite.**

Canasatego spoke for the Haudenosaunee Confederacy at treaty councils with colonial officials. He closed the council at Lancaster by advising the colonies to unite, as his own nations had done centuries earlier: "We heartily recommend Union and a good Agreement between you our Brethren... you, as well as we, will become the stronger."

Benjamin Franklin printed the minutes of the council, the written record of what was said there, including the speech.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 7 minutes**

French and British armies fought their wars on Native land after 1750. Then the leaders, soldiers and settlers of a new country, the United States, moved onto that land and fought the nations for it.

When British forces beat the French in 1763, Native nations lost the second empire they had bargained with against Britain. Then the colonists went to war with Britain in the American Revolution. The Haudenosaunee nations took different sides in that war, and their Confederacy split for the first time in centuries. During the war, in 1778, United States officials signed their first treaty with a Native nation. In 1791 fighters from a confederacy of nations in the Ohio Country destroyed a United States army.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pontiac and the Line" -->
French and British commanders both needed Native allies in the French and Indian War, which lasted from 1754 to 1763. A loose confederacy of nations from the Great Lakes, the Illinois Country and the Ohio Country rose against the British in 1763. Under the Odawa leader Pontiac, their fighters took or besieged British forts across the west. Pontiac lifted his siege of Detroit in October 1763, but the fighting went on until 1766.

British officers at Fort Pitt tried to give smallpox, a disease that spreads from person to person and can kill, to the Native fighters around them in 1763. Delaware, Shawnee and Mingo fighters had besieged the fort, where Pittsburgh stands today, and smallpox had broken out among the British soldiers inside.

**The blankets.** The fort's commander, Captain Simeon Ecuyer, and the trader William Trent gave two Delaware men, Turtle's Heart and Mamaltee, two blankets and a handkerchief from the fort's smallpox hospital on June 24, 1763. Trent wrote in his journal, "I hope it will have the desired effect." In July the British commander in North America, General Jeffery Amherst, asked Colonel Henry Bouquet in a letter whether smallpox could be sent among the nations at war. Bouquet answered that he would try to spread it with blankets.

**Whether it worked.** A month after the June meeting, Trent saw the two Delaware men again, and they showed no sign of smallpox. A man named Gershom Hicks later said that about 100 Native people died of smallpox between the spring of 1763 and April 1764. The historian Elizabeth Fenn writes that no one can know whether the attempt to spread smallpox worked.

British officials answered the war by drawing a line. They issued the Royal Proclamation, an official public announcement of an order, on October 7, 1763. In it they forbade colonists to settle west of the Appalachian Mountains, and they set aside the land beyond for the Native nations. Colonists were angry about the line and crossed it anyway. Writers for Encyclopedia Virginia call the Proclamation a foundation of later treaty law, the set of rules for agreements between governments.

Armed settlers from Paxton Township, Pennsylvania, killed the twenty Conestoga people who lived in a small town near Lancaster in December 1763. The Conestoga were unarmed. Fifty-seven men came at daybreak on December 14 and found three men, two women and a boy at home. They shot them, stabbed them and killed them with hatchets.

One of the dead was Shehaes, a very old man who had helped make a treaty with William Penn in 1701. They killed him in his bed. The attackers scalped all six, cutting away the skin and hair from the top of each head, and burned the huts.

**The workhouse.** To keep them safe, Lancaster's officials put the other fourteen Conestoga in the town workhouse, a building where poor people were made to work. Franklin called it "a strong Building." On December 27 about fifty men broke down the workhouse door and killed all fourteen with guns and hatchets, children a year old among them.

The Paxton men claimed the Conestoga had hidden enemy spies. The historian Michael Goode writes that the charge was made up. British soldiers stationed nearby did nothing to stop the killings, and no one was ever put on trial for them. Benjamin Franklin wrote down the names of the dead in 1764.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution splits the Haudenosaunee" -->
Under the Great Law, the Five Nations, and then the Six, had stayed united for centuries. In the American Revolution they split. The Oneida and the Tuscarora sided with the Americans. The Mohawk, the Seneca, the Cayuga and the Onondaga fought with the British. Many of them fought under the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant, whose own name was Thayendanegea. At the Battle of Oriskany, on August 6, 1777, Haudenosaunee men fought on both sides.

George Washington ordered an expedition into Seneca and Cayuga country in 1779. In his written orders, he directed "the total destruction and devastation of their settlements." From July 31 to October 15, General John Sullivan's soldiers burned more than forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and the crops in their fields. Thousands of people fled to the British at Fort Niagara. That winter many of them starved or froze to death there.

Seneca chiefs spoke to Washington directly in 1790. "When your army entered the country of the Six Nations, we called you the Town-destroyer," they said. They told him that "to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale." The name was older than the burning of their towns. Washington had been given the name Conotocaurius, Town Destroyer, in 1753, and his great-grandfather had been given it before him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The treaty system begins" -->
United States officials signed their country's first treaty with a Native nation at Fort Pitt on September 17, 1778. The Lenape leaders White Eyes, John Kill Buck Jr. and Pipe signed for their nation, which the treaty calls the Delaware. In the treaty, the American officials recognized Lenape sovereignty, the right of a nation to govern itself. They also asked the Lenape to let American troops cross their land safely.

**A fourteenth state.** The negotiators raised the idea of a Native fourteenth state, which would join the thirteen and send its own representatives to Congress. No one in Congress ever acted on that idea.

In all, United States officials and Native leaders made 374 treaties that were formally approved, or ratified. The last of them date from the 1860s. The National Archives holds all 374, and its archivists, the people who keep its records, have put every one online. Each of the 374 is an agreement between the United States and a Native nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Northwest Confederacy" -->
North of the Ohio River, the nations built a confederacy to defend their country. Its leaders included Little Turtle of the Miami, Blue Jacket of the Shawnee and Buckongahelas of the Lenape.

About a thousand confederacy fighters fought the army of General Arthur St. Clair on November 4, 1791. About 930 of his soldiers began the fight, and the confederacy's fighters destroyed his army. Counts of the soldiers they killed run from more than 630 to more than 650, and counts of the wounded from 264 to more than 270. Of every 100 soldiers who fought, about 97 were killed or wounded. No battle in the history of the United States Army has had a higher share.

Native casualties, the killed and wounded together, were roughly 100. St. Clair's defeat is still the largest defeat Native forces have ever given a United States army, larger than the Battle of the Little Bighorn, fought 85 years later. After the defeat, members of Congress held the first investigation by Congress in the country's history.

Anthony Wayne led a legion, an army unit of 3,300 men, against the confederacy in 1794. Wayne's legion defeated the confederacy at Fallen Timbers, near where Toledo, Ohio, stands today, on August 20, 1794. The confederacy's leaders had lost the war. At Greenville, on August 3, 1795, they signed a treaty with Wayne, who signed for the United States, and in it they signed away most of Ohio and part of Indiana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="little-turtle" name="Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)

> **Who:** Miami war leader who co-led the confederacy that destroyed St. Clair's army in 1791, then decided the war could not be won and argued for peace.
> **When and where:** The Wabash and Maumee country, in present-day Indiana and Ohio, in the 1780s and 1790s.

**He helped destroy one American army, then signed away most of Ohio.**

Little Turtle was one of the leaders of the confederacy that beat St. Clair on November 4, 1791. Three years later the same confederacy lost at Fallen Timbers, and Little Turtle decided that the war could not be won.

He signed the Treaty of Greenville on August 3, 1795, in which the defeated confederacy's leaders signed away most of Ohio to the United States. He spent his last years arguing against more war with the United States.
