## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Before 1500 many nations lived in the land that is now the United States. A nation is a group of people who govern themselves. Their people spoke about 300 different languages. They built towns and carried goods hundreds of miles to trade. They made agreements with other nations and fought wars against them.

Nobody knows how many people lived here before 1500. Scholars have used different methods and reached different answers. In 1976 Douglas Ubelaker put the number for the lower 48 states at about 1.85 million. In 1983 Henry Dobyns put it at about 18 million for all of North America north of Mexico. Textbooks published between 1988 and 1990 stated numbers from 1 million to 12 million. No number is settled.

People had lived on this land for thousands of years before any ship came from Europe. At Celilo Falls, on the Columbia River, scientists who dig up and study old camps and tools date the first signs of people to at least 10,000 years ago, and some accounts say 15,000. In those thousands of years, some peoples built cities and later left them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cities and kingdoms" -->
Near where St. Louis, Missouri, stands today, people built Cahokia, the largest city north of Mexico. People lived there from about 700 to about 1400. The city was biggest around 1100. Roughly 10,000 to 20,000 people lived in the city then. Some estimates put as many as 50,000 in the country around it. London had about as many people at the same date. Cahokia's people had scattered by about 1350, and by about 1400 nobody lived there. Why they left is still argued. The causes put forward are drought, floods, and running short of the wood and food its people needed. No single cause is settled.

In what is now New Mexico, Ancestral Puebloan people built large stone buildings called great houses in Chaco Canyon. Ancestral Puebloans are the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples. *Pueblo* is the Spanish word for town. After long droughts, people began leaving Chaco around 1140, and by 1200 most of them had gone. They moved into the Pueblo communities of the Southwest.

The Calusa of southwest Florida built a kingdom without farming. Fish and shellfish fed about 20,000 people. Their capital, Calos, stood on mounds of shell at Mound Key. The Calusa built pens with walls of shell, called watercourts. They kept live fish in the pens. Scientists have dated the watercourts to between 1300 and 1400. The Calusa collected tribute, which means regular payments in goods, from other groups. Those groups lived along the coast from south of Tampa Bay down to the Florida Keys.

Traders carried turquoise, seashells, copper and obsidian hundreds of miles from nation to nation. Obsidian is a black glass formed by volcanoes, and it can be chipped to a sharp edge. Native trails crossed the continent, and later newcomers traveled along them.

The ancestors of the Navajo (Diné) and the Apache moved south toward the Southwest. They came from what is now western Canada and eastern Alaska. Accounts differ about when they arrived. Some date the arrival to the 1200s, and others put it in the 1400s or as late as about 1525.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Law of Peace" -->
Five nations had been fighting one another: the Mohawk, the Oneida, the Onondaga, the Cayuga and the Seneca. Their people accepted an agreement called the Great Law of Peace, or Gayanesshagowa, and formed one confederacy, the Haudenosaunee. A confederacy is a group of nations that keep their own governments but agree to act together on matters they share. The five nations came together under one Grand Council.

According to Haudenosaunee tradition, two men brought the five warring nations together under the Great Law. They were the Peacemaker and Hiawatha. The Haudenosaunee kept the law in memory and recited it aloud. They recorded it on wampum belts instead of on paper. A wampum belt is made of shell beads woven into patterns, and each pattern stands for the terms of an agreement.

Scholars disagree about when the Confederacy began. Barbara Mann and Jerry Fields date the founding to 1142. They base that date on an eclipse of the sun described in the founding tradition. Most other estimates place the founding between the middle 1100s and the late 1400s. Many favor the middle or late 1400s.

Writers for the Library of Congress describe the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as one of the oldest democracies in the world that still exist. In a democracy, the people have a say in how they are governed.

Few records of single persons survive from these centuries.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 1500s ships from Europe began to reach these coasts. Spanish expeditions, which were large armed groups traveling into country new to them, marched inland. The Europeans carried diseases, and the diseases spread among the Native nations.

The diseases spread ahead of the Spanish expeditions and behind them. A town could lose many of its people before any European arrived there. The diseases killed large shares of whole communities. Some writers call these deaths the Great Dying.

No one can give a single death toll, because no one knows how many people lived here at the start. Estimates for North America north of Mexico run from about 1 million to about 18 million. A death toll worked out from the low estimate and one worked out from the high estimate differ by millions of people. In 1900, United States census takers, the officials who count the people of the country, counted about 250,000 Native people in the United States. Even the lowest estimate for 1500, about 1 million, is four times that count. Disease killed many of the missing people. Over the four centuries after 1500, wars, killings, and armed men forcing whole nations off their land killed many others.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The entradas" -->
Spanish leaders called their armed expeditions into the north entradas. Hernando de Soto led one across the Southeast from 1539 to 1543. Francisco Vázquez de Coronado led another across the Southwest and the southern Plains from 1540 to 1542. Coronado's force had more than 300 armed Europeans and at least 1,200 Native allies.

De Soto sailed from Havana in May 1539 and landed in Florida with more than 620 men and 220 horses. For four years his men marched through the Southeast. They crossed the lands of the Mississippian chiefdoms. Mississippian is the name now given to the town-building peoples of the Mississippi Valley and the Southeast in these centuries. A chiefdom is a group of towns under a single ruler. De Soto's men attacked the people of the towns on their route and forced people to work for the expedition. At the town of Hymahi, de Soto took all of the town's food. Later he burned at least one of its people to death while he questioned them about another town, Cofitachequi. His men also carried disease with them. De Soto died of a fever in the spring of 1542. The survivors of his expedition reached Mexico on September 10, 1543.

De Soto's men were the first Europeans to see those chiefdoms, and almost the last. In the decades after de Soto's men attacked their towns, forced their people to work and spread disease among them, the chiefdoms broke apart. Over the century that followed, the survivors regrouped and took in refugees, people who had fled their own towns. They formed the new nations and confederacies of the Southeast.

The Spanish also brought horses back to North America. Horses had lived here long before, but they had died out thousands of years earlier. Christopher Columbus carried horses on his second voyage, in 1493. Spanish colonists brought more to the mainland through the 1500s. Coronado's expedition brought horses in 1540, and Juan de Oñate's colonists brought them to New Mexico in 1598.

### Paquiquineo (Don Luís)

> **Who:** A young man from the Chesapeake Bay country who left on a Spanish ship, met the king of Spain, and spent nine years getting home. The records do not say whether he went willingly.
> **When and where:** Left the Chesapeake in 1561. Landed back on the James River in September 1570.

In 1561 a young man named Paquiquineo left the Chesapeake Bay country on a Spanish ship. The Spanish records do not settle whether he went willingly or was taken.

He appeared before King Philip II in Spain. In Mexico City he was baptized, which means he went through the Christian ceremony that makes a person a member of the church. He was given the name of the viceroy there, Don Luís de Velasco. A viceroy governed a Spanish colony for the king.

Paquiquineo spent nine years trying to get back to the Chesapeake. He came back inside a Spanish expedition. In September 1570 he landed on the James River as guide and translator for a small Jesuit mission. A mission was a group of Catholic priests and helpers sent to turn people to Christianity. Jesuits belong to one order, or society, of Catholic priests. Father Juan Bautista de Segura led this one.

In February 1571 Paquiquineo led an attack on the mission, and the attackers killed the missionaries. They spared the altar boy, Alonso de Olmos, a boy who helped the priests at church services.

In August 1572 Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, the Spanish governor of Florida, sent soldiers to the James River. They captured some of Paquiquineo's people in a fight. The Spanish found several of the captives guilty of killing the missionaries and hanged them from the yardarms of their ship. A yardarm is a crossbeam on a ship's mast. The Spanish records do not give the names of the men they hanged. The Powhatan people returned Alonso de Olmos to the Spanish. The soldiers did not find Paquiquineo. European records after 1572 do not include him, and no story passed down by word of mouth that can be checked describes him.

Spanish officials sent no more missions to the Chesapeake. English colonists landed at Jamestown, in the same country, 36 years after the mission, in 1607.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="paquiquineo" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Acoma, 1598 and 1599" -->
In 1598 Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north from Mexico and founded a colony in New Mexico, the country of the Pueblo nations. Acoma Pueblo, the town of the Acoma people, stands on a mesa, a hill with a flat top and steep sides. Before any fighting, Oñate had gathered the Acoma elders for a ceremony in which they promised to obey the king of Spain. The Spanish wrote it down as the "Act of Obedience and Vassalage by the Indians of Acoma." A vassal is a person bound to obey a ruler. From then on the Spanish treated the Acoma as subjects of the king of Spain, bound by Spanish law. Spanish officials later used that paper to argue that their war on Acoma was legal.

In December 1598 Oñate's nephew Juan de Zaldívar came to Acoma with about thirty soldiers. He took some of them up the mesa to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, as reporters for the Rio Grande Sun newspaper told it in 2019, the soldiers raped an Acoma woman. Rape means forcing a person into sex. The All Pueblo Council of Governors is a council of the leaders of the Pueblo nations. In October 2023 its leaders said in a public statement that the soldiers "had demanded food and supplies, assaulted an Acoma woman, and forced allegiance to the Spanish crown." Forcing allegiance to the Spanish crown means the soldiers made the Acoma promise loyalty to the king of Spain. Then the Acoma killed Zaldívar and eleven to fourteen of his men. Accounts differ on the number.

On December 28 Oñate opened a formal inquiry, an official hearing to find out what had happened. He questioned the survivors of Zaldívar's party. He asked the Franciscan friars with him whether a war against Acoma would be just. Friars are Catholic religious brothers, and the Franciscans are one order of them. The friars answered that he "possessed both the authority and sufficient cause," which meant he had the right to make war and a good enough reason. In January 1599 Juan de Zaldívar's brother Vicente led about seventy soldiers up the mesa. Over two or three days of fighting, his soldiers killed Acoma men, women and children. They took the town and burned much of it.

Nobody counted the dead. The only detailed written story of the fighting by someone who was there is a long poem published in 1610 by Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, a captain who rode with the expedition. He wrote it to praise the expedition. Estimates run from about 300 to about 1,500. They include about 300, about 600, 600 to 800, and about 800. Leaders of the All Pueblo Council of Governors use the figure of about 800 Acoma men, women and children today. The National Park Service's figure is about 1,500.

Vicente de Zaldívar's soldiers captured about 500 Acoma men, women and children. One record gives the number put on trial as 507. Oñate tried them at Santo Domingo Pueblo, though some accounts place the trial at San Juan Pueblo. He passed sentence on February 12, 1599. He sentenced men over twenty-five to have one foot cut off and to be forced to work for the Spanish without pay for twenty years. Males from twelve to twenty-five, and females over twelve, got the same twenty years of forced work. He sentenced the old people to be handed over as slaves to the Plains Apache. Oñate's sentence for two Hopi men captured in the fighting was to have their right hands cut off. Then he had them released to carry word of the punishment to their own people.

Oñate declared the Acoma children under twelve innocent. He still ordered them taken from their parents and handed to the friars. On Oñate's order, about seventy girls under twelve were sent south along the road to Mexico, to convents there. Neither the National Park Service's account nor the Pueblo council's statement names the men who took them. A convent is a house where nuns live, women who have promised their lives to the Catholic church. The National Park Service's account states that almost certainly none of the girls ever came home. The Pueblo council's 2023 statement gives sixty children taken to Mexico, and it also states that none returned. According to PBS, some scholars believe the children were later sold as slaves.

Oñate's sentence is in the written record. Whether anyone carried out the cutting of feet is disputed. Acoma people have passed down their own account since 1599: Oñate had the right feet of 24 captive men cut off. The New York Times reported that account in 1998. Two historians who worked for years in these records doubt that the feet were cut off. Marc Simmons first wrote that they were, and he later came to doubt it. John Kessell notes that no record describes a one-footed Acoma man.

Spanish officials charged Oñate with thirty crimes. Accounts date the charges to 1612 or 1613 and the judges' decision to 1614. The judges found him guilty of twelve, among them using too much force against Acoma. They banished him from New Mexico for life, which means they ordered him never to return. They also barred him from Mexico City for four years and fined him 6,000 ducats, a Spanish gold coin. PBS gives the Mexico City term as five years. Oñate had rebuilt a fortune in his family's mines at Zacatecas. Rebecca Carlson, who wrote about his trial in a 2008 university paper, calls the fine unimportant to him. He spent years working to have that decision undone. Accounts differ on whether he was ever pardoned, which means officially forgiven by the king. Carlson writes that he was pardoned in 1623. Other accounts say the proof of a pardon is unclear.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Native nations still held nearly the whole continent through the 1600s. Along the eastern coast, the European settlements were a thin strip. Those settlers needed food and trade from the nations around them, and they stayed only as long as those nations let them. In New Mexico, Spanish colonists had ruled the Pueblo nations by force since 1598. Native leaders chose which newcomers to deal with: the Dutch, the French, the English or the Spanish. They played those newcomers against one another, which means they used each group's rivalry with the others to bargain.

Haudenosaunee leaders made alliances with the newcomers and recorded them on wampum belts. In New England, English colonists fought two wars against coastal nations, the Pequot War of 1636 to 1638 and Metacom's war of 1675 and 1676. In the Southwest in 1680, the people of the Pueblo nations rose together and drove the Spanish off their land. They kept the Spanish out for twelve years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Tsenacommacah" -->
In eastern Virginia, Wahunsenacawh inherited rule over six tribes. He built them into a group of more than thirty tribes that paid him tribute. His country was called Tsenacommacah. The English called him "the Powhatan." His own title was mamanatowick, or paramount chief. A paramount chief is a chief whom other chiefs answer to. About 15,000 people lived in his country, spread across some 10,000 square miles between the James River and the Potomac. A tribe here means a people with its own leader. Each tribe's leader was called a weroance, and each weroance paid tribute to Wahunsenacawh.

English colonists founded Jamestown inside Tsenacommacah in 1607, on land that Wahunsenacawh ruled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Wampum diplomacy and the Covenant Chain" -->
Haudenosaunee diplomats, the people who spoke for their nations with other nations, recorded agreements on wampum belts. Speakers kept the words of each agreement in memory, and the pattern of the belt recorded its terms.

The Two Row Wampum, Guswenta, records the Haudenosaunee account of their first agreement with the Dutch. It has two purple rows of beads on a white background. The rows stand for two boats traveling the same river side by side, with neither one steering the other. Haudenosaunee tradition dates the agreement to 1613. A paper document with the same date also exists, and accounts differ about whether it is genuine.

The Haudenosaunee alliance with the English colonies was called the Covenant Chain. A covenant is a serious promise. The oldest surviving record of the Silver Covenant Chain dates from 1677. That year Haudenosaunee leaders and the New York governor, Edmund Andros, made the alliance official at Albany. Silver tarnishes, which means it turns dark over time, so a silver chain has to be polished to stay bright. Haudenosaunee and English leaders "polished" the Covenant Chain again and again. That meant they renewed the alliance at councils, with speeches and wampum.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The coastal wars" -->
In Connecticut, English colonists and their Mohegan and Narragansett allies fought the Pequot from 1636 to 1638. Before dawn on May 26, 1637, an English-led force set fire to the Pequot town at Mystic, which was protected by walls. In under an hour they killed hundreds of people, most of them elders, women and children.

A treaty is a formal written agreement between nations. In the Treaty of Hartford of 1638, the English colonists and their Mohegan and Narragansett allies declared that the Pequot nation no longer existed. They made it illegal to use the name Pequot. Under the treaty, the winners took Pequot survivors for themselves or sold them into slavery. Colonists had already begun selling captives during the war. In July 1637 Captain William Peirce sailed the ship *Desire* out of Boston with seventeen Pequot captives, fifteen of them children. Governor John Winthrop of Massachusetts wrote, "We sent fifteen of the boys and two of the women to Bermuda, by Mr. Pierce." Peirce sold them in the Caribbean. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation is in Connecticut today.

In 1675, nearly forty years after the Pequot War, Metacom led a coalition of nations against the New England colonies. A coalition is a group that agrees to act together. Metacom was the Wampanoag sachem, and a sachem is the leader of a nation. His coalition fought the colonists from 1675 to 1676. Colonial soldiers and their Native allies killed Native people, and the coalition's fighters killed colonists. About 9,000 people died in all, and estimates put Native deaths at roughly half of all the Native people in New England. For the size of the population, writers for Britannica and other histories call it the bloodiest war in American history.

On October 13, 1675, the men who governed the Massachusetts colony ordered that Native people from the praying towns be taken to Deer Island in Boston Harbor and kept there. The praying towns were villages of Native people who had become Christians. The colonists feared these people would turn against the English and join Metacom. Colonists took the people of Natick to the island first. The National Park Service puts the number held there at about 500 to 1,100, most of them women and children. They had too little food and shelter through a freezing winter. The two accounts of the deaths differ. The National Park Service says that very few of them survived. The Partnership of Historic Bostons, a Boston history group, says that more than half of them died over the winter. Colonists released the survivors in May 1676. An unknown number had been smuggled off the island and sold as slaves in the West Indies or in Tangier, in North Africa. No surviving record names the men who voted for the island prison, and the National Park Service's account does not name the men who sold the captives. Every year, descendants of the nations in the war hold a gathering on Deer Island to remember them.

Inland, from the middle to the late 1600s, Haudenosaunee warriors fought the Beaver Wars against nations to the west and north. They fought for control of the fur trade, the trade in animal furs with Europeans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="metacom-native-nations" name="Metacom (King Philip)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Metacom (King Philip)

> **Who:** Wampanoag sachem, son of Massasoit, who led the coalition war of 1675 and 1676 against the New England colonies. The English called him King Philip.
> **When and where:** Southern New England. Killed at Mount Hope, August 12, 1676.

Metacom was the son of Massasoit, the Wampanoag leader who had made an arrangement with the Plymouth colonists in 1621. In 1675 Metacom led a coalition of nations to war against the New England colonies.

In early August 1676 the company of the Plymouth captain Benjamin Church captured Metacom's wife and his nine-year-old son.

On August 12, 1676, at Mount Hope, a Native man named Alderman, who was serving with Church's company, shot Metacom dead. Church wrote his own account of what came next. He ordered that "not one of his bones should be buried," because Metacom had left English dead unburied. Church told a Native man he called his "old Indian Executioner" to cut off Metacom's head and to quarter his body, which means to cut it into four pieces. Church gave the head and one of Metacom's hands to Alderman. Alderman showed the hand to colonists who paid him to see it. Plymouth's leaders paid Church's men thirty shillings, a sum in English money, for each enemy they had killed or captured, and they paid the same for Metacom's head. At Plymouth the head was set on a pole. Church's book does not say who put it there. By the account of a later editor of Church's book, it stayed there for more than 24 years.

The leaders of Plymouth Colony asked ministers what to do with Metacom's son. On September 7, 1676, two ministers, Samuel Arnold and John Cotton, wrote that the children of such enemies could be put to death. In March 1677 Cotton wrote in a letter, "Philip's boy goes now to be sold." The boy and his mother were sold into slavery. Cotton's letter does not say who sold them. Most accounts say the two were sent to Bermuda. Others say they were sent to the West Indies.

Colonial leaders sold hundreds of other captives into slavery to pay what the war had cost the colonies. On August 4, 1675, Plymouth's council of war ruled that 112 Native men, women and children held at Plymouth were "to be sold." The council named the colony's treasurer to sell them. Church wrote that these people had surrendered because of promises made to them, and that about 160 of them were "sold, and transported out of the Country."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="metacom-native-nations" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pueblo Revolt" -->
The Pueblo nations along the Rio Grande had lived under Spanish rule since Juan de Oñate founded the New Mexico colony in 1598. In 1675 the Spanish governor, Juan Francisco Treviño, ordered 47 Pueblo religious leaders arrested. The Spanish called their religious practice sorcery, which means using magic to do harm. Spanish officials sentenced four of the leaders to be hanged. Three were hanged, and the fourth killed himself. Spanish officials whipped the others in public and put them in prison. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be freed, and the Spanish let them go. One of the freed leaders was Po'pay.

On August 10, 1680, the people of many separate towns rose against the Spanish on the same day. They spoke Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Tano, Keres and other languages, and the Zuni and the Hopi also promised to help. Pueblo fighters besieged Santa Fe, which means they surrounded the town and cut it off. They took the town and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. In the rising they killed 401 settlers and 21 Franciscan friars. The Pueblo fighters let most of the Spanish leave, and the Spanish went south.

The Pueblos governed themselves for the next twelve years. In those years they lived through drought, raids and famine, which is a long shortage of food. Diego de Vargas led Spanish forces back into New Mexico in 1692 to retake it, and Pueblo fighters kept up armed resistance for years after that. Writers of the EBSCO Research Starters history guide call the Pueblo Revolt the most successful uprising by Native people against European rule in North America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pope" name="Po'pay (Popé)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Po'pay (Popé)

> **Who:** A Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who organized the 1680 rising across dozens of towns and several languages.
> **When and where:** New Mexico, August 1680.

Po'pay had to get the people of dozens of towns to rise on a single day. The people of those towns did not all speak the same language.

Pueblo accounts say that runners carried knotted cords from town to town. The knots on each cord marked the days left until the rising. Po'pay set the rising for August 11, by one account, or August 13, by another.

On August 9 the Spanish captured two of the runners, Pedro Omtua and Nicolás Catua of Tesuque Pueblo. Spanish officials tortured them to make them explain the knotted cords. Torture means hurting a person on purpose to force them to talk. The Smithsonian's account of the runners does not say how they were hurt. Later the Spanish hanged them. No surviving record names the men who did it. When Po'pay learned that the runners had been caught, he ordered the rising to begin early. It began on August 10, 1680.

A statue of Po'pay stands in the United States Capitol. It is one of New Mexico's two statues in the Capitol's Statuary Hall collection, which holds statues of two people from each state's history.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pope" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
When the Spanish left New Mexico in 1680, they left their horses behind. An estimated 1,500 or more horses passed into Native hands at once. Writers for the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian call it the largest single transfer of horses. According to the traditional account, Native people then traded horses west and north along Native trade routes. The horses reached the Ute, the Navajo, the Apache, the Comanche, the Kiowa, the Shoshone and the nations of the northern Plains.

In 2023 a team led by William Taylor published a study in the journal *Science*. Its findings do not fit this account. Lakota, Comanche and Pawnee people were among the study's writers, and other Native partners worked on it too. The team used radiocarbon dating on the remains of horses from Wyoming, Kansas and New Mexico. Radiocarbon dating measures a form of carbon that breaks down at a steady rate. The amount left shows how old a bone is. The horses had been ridden and had eaten corn that people fed them. They had also worn bridles, which are straps around a horse's head used to steer it. They were there by the early 1600s, decades before the revolt. Native oral histories, which are accounts passed down by telling, had said the same thing. The timing is not settled.

By either account, Plains nations had horses by the 1700s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
French and British officials both wanted Native allies in the first half of the 1700s. Haudenosaunee leaders bargained with both sides. Native nations also went on making their own agreements with one another.

In these years the Haudenosaunee accepted the Tuscarora as a member nation. South Carolina forces had defeated the Tuscarora, and colonists had taken their land in North Carolina. On the southern Plains, the Comanche built the strongest power in the region on horses and trade.

The printed speeches of the Onondaga speaker Canasatego, from these years, are among the best records of Native diplomacy from that century. Diplomacy means dealing with other nations through talks and agreements.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Neutrality as strategy" -->
On August 4, 1701, about 1,300 delegates from more than thirty nations met at Montreal. Delegates are people sent to speak for a group. There they made peace with the leaders of New France, the French colony in what is now Canada. As part of the same peace, the Haudenosaunee promised to stay neutral. That meant they would fight for neither France nor Britain.

For half a century, Haudenosaunee leaders used their neutrality to bargain with both sides.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tuscarora move north" -->
In 1710 more than 400 Swiss and German settlers, led by Baron Christoph von Graffenried, founded the town of New Bern, North Carolina, on Tuscarora land. They pushed the people of the Native town of Chattoka off the spot where the Neuse and Trent rivers meet. Slave raiders were also capturing Tuscarora people to sell them as slaves. The State Library of North Carolina's history of the war does not name the raiders. On September 22, 1711, Tuscarora fighters struck back in attacks they had planned together. The war lasted until 1713. South Carolina colonial forces, with Native allies of their own, defeated the Tuscarora. By that library's count, 1,400 Tuscarora were dead and 1,000 had been captured and enslaved.

About 1,500 Tuscarora survivors then moved north, roughly 500 miles. They asked the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee to sponsor them, which meant to speak for them and accept them. The Oneida took them in. In 1722 the whole Tuscarora nation became the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Since then the Confederacy has had six member nations, and it is also known as the Six Nations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Comanche power" -->
The Comanche moved onto the southern Plains in the early 1700s and became the most powerful nation there. Comanchería, the country they controlled, later covered about 240,000 square miles.

The Comanche built their power on horses. They bred, trained and traded horses, and they controlled the horse trade from the southern Plains to the northern Plains. In the 1740s they traded bison robes and horses to French traders for guns. The French guns were better than the weapons the Comanche could get from the Spanish. Comanche fighters also raided other peoples. A raid is a quick attack to seize goods, horses or people. Comanche leaders made agreements with other nations as well as fighting them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Treaty councils" -->
A treaty council was a meeting where Native leaders and colonial officials bargained and made agreements. At these councils, speakers on both sides made speeches and exchanged wampum.

At Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in June and July of 1744, colonial officials paid the Haudenosaunee to give up their claims to the Shenandoah Valley.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="canasatego" name="Canasatego" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Canasatego

> **Who:** Onondaga leader and the Haudenosaunee's speaker at treaty councils, whose 1744 speech on union Benjamin Franklin printed.
> **When and where:** Lancaster, Pennsylvania, June and July 1744. He gave the union speech on July 4, 1744.

Canasatego spoke for the Haudenosaunee Confederacy at treaty councils with colonial officials.

On July 4, 1744, he closed the council at Lancaster by advising the colonies to unite, as his own nations had done centuries earlier: "We heartily recommend Union and a good Agreement between you our Brethren... you, as well as we, will become the stronger."

Benjamin Franklin printed the minutes of the council, the written record of what was said there, including the speech.

<!-- hb-time:start id="1750-1800" order="05" chapter="native-nations" label="1750 to 1800" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
After 1750, French and British forces fought their wars on Native land. Then the leaders, soldiers and settlers of a new country, the United States, moved onto that land and fought the nations for it.

British forces beat the French in 1763. An empire is a country that rules other lands and peoples. After 1763, Native nations had no second empire to bargain with against Britain. Then the colonists went to war with Britain in the American Revolution. The Haudenosaunee nations took different sides in that war, and their Confederacy split for the first time in centuries. During the war, in 1778, United States officials signed their first treaty with a Native nation. In 1791 fighters from a confederacy of nations in the Ohio Country destroyed a United States army.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Pontiac and the Line" -->
French and British commanders both needed Native allies in the French and Indian War, which lasted from 1754 to 1763.

In 1763 a loose confederacy of nations from the Great Lakes, the Illinois Country and the Ohio Country rose against the British. Under the Odawa leader Pontiac, their fighters took or besieged British forts across the west. Pontiac lifted his siege of Detroit in October 1763, but the fighting went on until 1766.

At Fort Pitt in 1763, British officers tried to give smallpox to the Native fighters around them. Smallpox is a disease that spreads from person to person and can kill. Delaware, Shawnee and Mingo fighters had besieged the fort, where Pittsburgh stands today, and smallpox had broken out among the British soldiers inside. On June 24, 1763, the fort's commander, Captain Simeon Ecuyer, and the trader William Trent gave two Delaware men, Turtle's Heart and Mamaltee, two blankets and a handkerchief from the fort's smallpox hospital. Trent wrote in his journal, "I hope it will have the desired effect." In July the British commander in North America, General Jeffery Amherst, asked Colonel Henry Bouquet in a letter whether smallpox could be sent among the nations at war. Bouquet answered that he would try to spread it with blankets. A month after the June meeting, Trent saw the two Delaware men again, and they showed no sign of smallpox. A man named Gershom Hicks later said that about 100 Native people died of smallpox between the spring of 1763 and April 1764. The historian Elizabeth Fenn writes that no one can know whether the attempt to spread smallpox worked.

British officials answered the war by drawing a line. They issued the Royal Proclamation on October 7, 1763. A proclamation is an official public announcement of an order. In it they forbade colonists to settle west of the Appalachian Mountains, and they set aside the land beyond for the Native nations. Colonists were angry about the line and crossed it anyway. Writers for Encyclopedia Virginia call the Proclamation a foundation of later treaty law, the set of rules for agreements between governments.

In December 1763 armed settlers from Paxton Township, Pennsylvania, killed the twenty Conestoga people who lived in a small town near Lancaster. The Conestoga were unarmed. On December 14, fifty-seven men came at daybreak and found three men, two women and a boy at home. They shot them, stabbed them and killed them with hatchets. One of the dead was Shehaes, a very old man who had helped make a treaty with William Penn in 1701. They killed him in his bed. The attackers scalped all six, which means they cut away the skin and hair from the top of each head, and burned the huts. Lancaster's officials put the other fourteen Conestoga in the town workhouse to keep them safe. A workhouse was a building where poor people were made to work, and Franklin called this one "a strong Building." On December 27 about fifty men broke down the workhouse door and killed all fourteen with guns and hatchets, children a year old among them. The Paxton men claimed the Conestoga had hidden enemy spies. The historian Michael Goode writes that the charge was made up. British soldiers stationed nearby did nothing to stop the killings, and no one was ever put on trial for them. Benjamin Franklin wrote down the names of the dead in 1764.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Revolution splits the Haudenosaunee" -->
Under the Great Law, the Five Nations, and then the Six, had stayed united for centuries. In the American Revolution they split. The Oneida and the Tuscarora sided with the Americans. The Mohawk, the Seneca, the Cayuga and the Onondaga fought with the British. Many of them fought under the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant, whose own name was Thayendanegea. At the Battle of Oriskany, on August 6, 1777, Haudenosaunee men fought on both sides.

In 1779 George Washington ordered an expedition into Seneca and Cayuga country. In his written orders, Washington directed "the total destruction and devastation of their settlements." From July 31 to October 15, General John Sullivan's soldiers burned more than forty Seneca and Cayuga towns and the crops in their fields. Thousands of people fled to the British at Fort Niagara. That winter many of them starved or froze to death there.

In 1790 Seneca chiefs spoke to Washington directly. "When your army entered the country of the Six Nations, we called you the Town-destroyer," they said, "and to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale." The name was older than the burning of their towns. Washington had been given the name Conotocaurius, Town Destroyer, in 1753, and his great-grandfather had been given it before him.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The treaty system begins" -->
On September 17, 1778, at Fort Pitt, United States officials signed their country's first treaty with a Native nation. The Lenape leaders White Eyes, John Kill Buck Jr. and Pipe signed for their nation. The treaty identifies the Lenape as the Delaware. In the treaty, the American officials recognized Lenape sovereignty, which means the right of a nation to govern itself. They also asked the Lenape to let American troops cross their land safely. The negotiators raised the idea of a Native fourteenth state, which would join the thirteen and send its own representatives to Congress. No one in Congress ever acted on that idea.

In all, United States officials and Native leaders made 374 treaties that were formally approved, or ratified. The last of them date from the 1860s. The National Archives holds all 374, and its archivists, the people who keep its records, have put every one online. Each of the 374 is an agreement between the United States and a Native nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Northwest Confederacy" -->
North of the Ohio River, the nations built a confederacy to defend their country. Its leaders included Little Turtle of the Miami, Blue Jacket of the Shawnee and Buckongahelas of the Lenape.

On November 4, 1791, about a thousand confederacy fighters fought the army of General Arthur St. Clair. About 930 of his soldiers began the fight. The confederacy's fighters destroyed his army. Counts of the soldiers they killed run from more than 630 to more than 650. Counts of the wounded run from 264 to more than 270. The casualty rate, which counts the killed and the wounded together, was about 97 percent of the soldiers who fought. No battle in the history of the U.S. Army has had a higher rate. Native casualties were roughly 100. St. Clair's defeat is still the largest defeat Native forces have ever given a United States army, larger than the Battle of the Little Bighorn, fought 85 years later. After the defeat, members of Congress held the first investigation by Congress in the country's history.

In 1794 Anthony Wayne led a legion, an army unit of 3,300 men, against the confederacy. On August 20, 1794, at Fallen Timbers, near where Toledo, Ohio, stands today, Wayne's legion defeated the confederacy. At Greenville, on August 3, 1795, after that defeat, the confederacy's leaders signed a treaty with Wayne, who signed for the United States. They had lost the war, and in the treaty they signed away most of Ohio and part of Indiana to the United States.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="little-turtle" name="Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)

> **Who:** Miami war leader who co-led the confederacy that destroyed St. Clair's army in 1791, then decided the war could not be won and argued for peace.
> **When and where:** The Wabash and Maumee country, in present-day Indiana and Ohio, in the 1780s and 1790s.

Little Turtle was one of the leaders of the confederacy that beat St. Clair on November 4, 1791.

Three years later the same confederacy lost at Fallen Timbers. Little Turtle decided that the war could not be won. On August 3, 1795, he signed the Treaty of Greenville, in which the defeated confederacy's leaders signed away most of Ohio to the United States. He spent his last years arguing against more war with the United States.
