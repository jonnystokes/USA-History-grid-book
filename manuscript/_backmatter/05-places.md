# Places to See

Museums, sites and archives the chapters name, grouped by state so you can find the ones near you. Then come places in more than one state, places in other countries, online collections, and last, other places whose state the chapters do not give. Places close and exhibits change, so check before you go.

## Alabama

- **16th Street Baptist Church, Birmingham**: where the children's march began, and where a bomb set by Ku Klux Klan members killed four girls in 1963 (Chapter 25)
- **Africatown, Mobile**: the town founded by survivors of the *Clotilda*, the last known slave ship to reach the United States. It has the church and school they started, and Lewis Quarters, one of the first pieces of land they bought (Chapter 24)
- **Edmund Pettus Bridge, Selma**: where state troopers attacked civil rights marchers with clubs and tear gas on Bloody Sunday, March 7, 1965. Television cameras filmed the attack (Chapter 25)
- **Freedom Monument Sculpture Park, Montgomery**: the National Monument to Freedom, with more than 122,000 last names from the 1870 census (Chapter 12)
- **Freedom Monument Sculpture Park, Montgomery**: work by Kehinde Wiley (Chapter 32)
- **National Memorial for Peace and Justice and the Legacy Museum, Montgomery**: 805 steel blocks, one for each county where a lynching was recorded (Chapter 12)

## Alaska

- **Castle Hill, Sitka**: site of the 1867 handover of Alaska, on Tlingit land the Russians took from the Kiksadi clan in 1804 (Chapter 23)

## Arizona

- **Arizona State Museum, Tucson**: the four Ancestral Pueblo flutes from Broken Flute Cave, made between 620 and 670 (Chapter 33)
- **Café Gozhóó, Whiteriver, on the White Mountain Apache Reservation**: Nephi Craig's café built around Apache foods, opened in 2021 (Chapter 15)

## Arkansas

- **Central High School, Little Rock**: where nine Black students entered in 1957 (Chapter 25)
- **Daisy Bates home, Little Rock**: where the nine students met every school day (Chapter 25)

## California

- **50 UN Plaza, San Francisco**: the federal offices disabled protesters occupied in 1977 (Chapter 25)
- **88-Inch Cyclotron, Berkeley**: a later cyclotron at Berkeley that still runs (Chapter 8)
- **Alcatraz Island, San Francisco Bay**: held by Native activists from 1969 to 1971 (Chapter 25)
- **Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge, Carquinez Strait**: the bridge named for the ironworker in 2003 (Chapter 12)
- **Angel Island Immigration Station, San Francisco Bay**: the barracks whose wooden walls carry more than 200 poems carved by Chinese people held there (Chapter 4)
- **Azusa Street plaque, 312 Azusa Street, Los Angeles**: marks the site of the 1906 to 1931 revival (Chapter 29)
- **Beckwourth Riverfront Park, Marysville**: the town's largest park, named in 1996 to recognize the debt Marysville still owed Jim Beckwourth (Chapter 1)
- **Chez Panisse, Berkeley**: Alice Waters's restaurant, opened in 1971 (Chapter 15)
- **Chicano Park, San Diego**: murals on the bridge pillars, first painted in 1973 (Chapter 32)
- **The Edible Schoolyard, Martin Luther King Jr. Middle School, Berkeley**: a school garden where students grow food, cook it and eat it (Chapter 15)
- **Golden Gate Bridge, San Francisco**: the bridge opened in 1937 (Chapter 12)
- **Hetch Hetchy Valley, Yosemite National Park**: under water behind the O'Shaughnessy Dam since 1923 (Chapter 3)
- **LA Plaza, Los Angeles**: the 2012 plaque apologizing for the Mexican Repatriation (Chapter 4)
- **Mount Wilson Observatory**: the Hooker telescope Hubble used (Chapter 9)
- **Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles**: Kara Walker's Unmanned Drone, made from a Stonewall Jackson statue (Chapter 32)
- **Presidio of San Francisco, San Francisco**: the Army post where officers kept Chinese survivors apart in 1906 (Chapter 27)
- **Room 307, Gilman Hall, Berkeley**: the room where plutonium was proved a new element, now a National Historic Landmark (Chapter 13)
- **Temple of the Forest Beneath the Clouds, Weaverville**: Chinese Taoist temple of 1874, still in use, held by California State Parks (Chapter 29)
- **Tule Lake**: the prison camp where Japanese Americans harvested almost 30 crops and most field workers were paid $12 a month (Chapter 15)
- **Yosemite Valley, Yosemite National Park**: the Ahwahneechee homeland, and Wahhoga, the site of the last Native village, where a roundhouse was finished in 2025 (Chapter 3)

## Colorado

- **Cliff Palace, Mesa Verde**: the largest cliff dwelling in North America, built between about 1190 and 1280, with about 150 rooms and 23 kivas, round rooms for ceremonies (Chapter 12)
- **Mesa Verde**: where Ancestral Pueblo potters made black-on-white pottery from about 600 to 1300 (Chapter 32)
- **Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site**: the place of the 1864 massacre, looked after by National Park Service staff (Chapter 2)

## Connecticut

- **Mashantucket Pequot Museum, Mashantucket**: the Pequot Nation's museum and history of the Pequot War (Chapter 22)
- **Mystic Seaport Museum, Mystic**: the Charles W. Morgan, the last wooden whaling ship left in the world, on which Nelson Cole Haley sailed as a harpooner (Chapter 10)

## Florida

- **Anhaica, Tallahassee**: the Apalachee town de Soto's army seized in 1539. Archaeologists have found burned corncobs at the site (Chapter 15)
- **Castillo de San Marcos National Monument (Fort Marion), St. Augustine**: the fort of coquina, a stone made of packed seashells, that Spanish builders began in 1672. Army officers held Native prisoners from the Plains there from 1875 to 1878 (Chapter 12)
- **Fort Mose Historic State Park, near St. Augustine**: the site of Fort Mose, the first free Black town in what is now the United States to be recognized by law (Chapter 24)
- **Fountain of Youth Park, St. Augustine**: the remains of Seloy's Timucua town lie under it (Chapter 7)
- **Mass grave site and state historical marker, West Palm Beach**: where Black victims of the 1928 hurricane were buried, marked in 2003 (Chapter 27)
- **Matanzas Inlet, south of St. Augustine**: where Menéndez's soldiers killed French prisoners who had given up, in 1565 (Chapter 23)
- **Mound Key**: the shell mounds of Calos, the Calusa capital (Chapter 2)
- **Pelican Island**: the first federal bird reservation, named in 1903 (Chapter 3)
- **Plaza de la Constitucion, St. Augustine**: the oldest public square in the country (Chapter 6)
- **St. Augustine**: the oldest town Europeans founded on the US mainland, begun by the Spanish in September 1565 at Seloy, a Timucua village (Chapter 4)
- **St. Augustine National Cemetery, St. Augustine**: the graves of Fort Marion prisoners (Chapter 32)

## Georgia

- **Andersonville**: the Confederate prison camp where almost 13,000 Union soldiers died (Chapter 22)
- **Fort Pulaski National Monument, near Savannah**: a national park site whose exhibit showed a copy of the 1863 photograph of Peter's back, covered in scars from whipping. Peter had escaped slavery in Louisiana. In 2025 newspapers reported that officials ordered the photograph removed, and an Interior Department spokesperson said park sites were not asked to remove it (Chapter 24)
- **Jekyll Island Club, Jekyll Island**: where six men secretly wrote a plan for a central bank in November 1910 (Chapter 17)

## Hawaii

- **Bellows Beach site, Waimānalo, Oʻahu**: the one early home site in Hawaii dated with confidence (Chapter 23)
- **ʻIolani Palace, Honolulu**: where officials of the Republic of Hawaii tried Queen Liliʻuokalani in her throne room in 1895 and held her prisoner in an upstairs bedroom (Chapter 23)
- **Kealakekua Bay, island of Hawaii**: where Captain Cook's sailors watched Hawaiians surf in 1779 (Chapter 36)
- **Mauna Loa**: where the Keeling Curve measurements have run since 1958 (Chapter 9)
- **USS Arizona, Pearl Harbor**: the sunken ship, with more than 900 of its crew still inside (Chapter 22)

## Illinois

- **Cahokia, across the Mississippi from St. Louis**: the largest town north of Mexico before Europeans came. Its remains are now Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, on the World Heritage List. The site has Monks Mound, the Grand Plaza, the burial mound called Mound 72, and the places where the log wall, the Woodhenge circles of sunrise posts and a copper workshop stood (Chapter 6)
- **Chicago History Museum, Chicago**: its Haymarket history counts eight police officers dead and an unknown number in the crowd killed or wounded (Chapter 14)
- **Chicago History Museum, Chicago**: its count of the 163 items on Montgomery Ward's first catalog, a single sheet of paper he mailed from Chicago in 1872 (Chapter 18)
- **Chicago's bungalows, Chicago**: more than 80,000 brick bungalows built between 1910 and the mid-1930s (Chapter 7)
- **Field Museum, Chicago**: covered display cases in two halls under the 2024 NAGPRA rule (Chapter 9)
- **Haymarket Square, Chicago**: the square where the bomb was thrown at the police on May 4, 1886 (Chapter 14)
- **Henry Gerber House, Chicago**: a National Historic Landmark, home of the founder of the Society for Human Rights (Chapter 25)
- **Monks Mound, Cahokia**: the largest structure made of earth in North and South America (Chapter 12)
- **Nauvoo**: the town on the Mississippi River where the Latter-day Saints settled in 1839, on ground where a large Meskwaki village had stood. After a mob killed their founder, Joseph Smith, in 1844, they began leaving for the West in 1846. A state park is there today (Chapter 29)
- **Newberry Library, Chicago**: a research library whose staff write about the Spanish missions in New Mexico (Chapter 29)

## Kentucky

- **Kentucky Horse Park**: Isaac Murphy's grave (Chapter 36)

## Louisiana

- **Congo Square, New Orleans**: the place where enslaved people gathered to sing, dance and play music on Sunday afternoons (Chapter 33)
- **LIGO detector, Livingston**: one of the two detectors that first picked up gravitational waves, on September 14, 2015 (Chapter 9)
- **Poverty Point**: five mounds and six curved ridges built 3,100 to 3,700 years ago, on the World Heritage List (Chapter 12)
- **Tate, Etienne and Prevost Center, New Orleans**: the old McDonogh No. 19 school. On November 14, 1960, Leona Tate, Gail Etienne and Tessie Prevost, all six years old, walked in with federal marshals, among the first Black children in white public schools in Louisiana. The building reopened as the center on May 4, 2022 (Chapter 30)
- **William Frantz Elementary School, New Orleans**: a bronze statue of Ruby Bridges, unveiled on November 14, 2015 (Chapter 30)

## Maine

- **Great Works bridge, Brattle Street, South Berwick**: the rocky gorge below the bridge where the 1634 sawmill stood (Chapter 10)
- **Unity College**: 16 of the Carter White House solar panels, put back up to heat water for the cafeteria (Chapter 10)

## Maryland

- **Catoctin Furnace**: the iron furnace the Johnson brothers built, where at least 271 enslaved African Americans worked, and the cemetery of its African American workers, with burials going back to 1774 (Chapter 13)
- **Clara Barton National Historic Site, Glen Echo**: Clara Barton's house, built partly from the lumber of the Red Cross buildings at Johnstown (Chapter 27)
- **Fort McHenry, Baltimore**: the fort the British shelled for 25 hours in 1814 (Chapter 22)
- **Jefferson Patterson Park and Museum**: its staff say people went on playing chunkey across North America after Cahokia declined around 1500 (Chapter 36)
- **Maryland Center for History and Culture, Baltimore**: Kühn's portrait of Henry Darnall III with the enslaved boy in a collar (Chapter 32)
- **Most Holy Redeemer Cemetery, Baltimore**: Henry Gunther's grave (Chapter 22)
- **Mount Clare Museum House, Baltimore**: the museum whose staff record the history of the Baltimore Iron Works and the enslaved people who worked there (Chapter 13)

## Massachusetts

- **Boston Light, Little Brewster Island, Boston Harbor**: the tower rebuilt in 1783 on the site of the first lighthouse (Chapter 12)
- **Bunker Hill Monument, Charlestown**: the 221-foot granite obelisk on Breed's Hill (Chapter 12)
- **Cole's Hill, Plymouth**: the hill above Plymouth Rock where the statue of Massasoit stands and the National Day of Mourning is held at noon every Thanksgiving. Its plaque comes from the 1998 agreement between the town and the Day of Mourning marchers, after Plymouth police arrested 25 of them in 1997 (Chapter 37)
- **Cornhill, Cape Cod**: where the *Mayflower* men dug up Nauset seed corn in November 1620 (Chapter 15)
- **Deer Island, Boston Harbor**: where colonists held the Christian Native people of the praying towns as prisoners in the winter of 1675 to 1676, and many of them died of hunger and cold. Descendants hold a remembrance there every year (Chapter 30)
- **Faneuil Hall, Boston**: the hall where a 1744 concert raised 205 pounds for the poor of Boston, and where British officers staged the play *Zara* in 1775 (Chapter 33)
- **Harvard Law School, Cambridge**: Feke's portrait of the Royall family (Chapter 32)
- **Lowell**: the mill city the Boston Associates built at Pawtucket Falls on the Merrimack River, on the land of the Pennacook people. Lowell National Historical Park there is about its mills and the slave-grown cotton they spun (Chapter 14)
- **Massachusetts Archives**: Joseph Jenks's patent petition, dated May 10, 1646 (Chapter 8)
- **Massachusetts Historical Society, Boston**: keeps the letters of Abigail and John Adams (Chapter 25)
- **Museum of Fine Arts, Boston**: the two jars Dave made in 1857, one bought from his descendants and one lent by them (Chapter 32)
- **Old North Church, Boston**: the steeple where two lanterns were hung on April 18, 1775 (Chapter 12)
- **Old Ship Meetinghouse, Hingham**: the only Puritan meetinghouse from the 1600s still standing (Chapter 12)
- **Old State House, Boston**: the brick Town House rebuilt after the fire of 1711 (Chapter 27)
- **Phipps Street Burying Ground, Charlestown**: Elizabeth Phillips's gravestone, which says she "brought into this world above 3000 Children" (Chapter 26)
- **Pilgrim Hall Museum, Plymouth**: Loara Standish's sampler (Chapter 32)
- **Pocumtuck Valley Memorial Association, Deerfield**: Deerfield's history museum, which keeps the town's old records of the 1704 raid (Chapter 22)
- **Post Office Square, Plymouth**: a plaque that tells of Metacom, whose head the English put on a pole in that square and left there for 25 years (Chapter 37)
- **Rebecca Nurse's house, Danvers**: her house, which still stands (Chapter 21)
- **Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site, Saugus**: the Hammersmith ironworks, which ran from the 1640s until about 1670, rebuilt on its old site (Chapter 8)
- **Shaw Memorial, Boston Common, Boston**: Saint-Gaudens's memorial to Shaw and the 54th Massachusetts (Chapter 32)
- **Thacher Island, off Cape Ann**: the island where Anthony Thacher and his wife came ashore in 1635 (Chapter 27)
- **Walden Pond, near Concord**: where Thoreau built his cabin and lived from 1845 to 1847. Workers also cut ice from the pond in winter (Chapter 3)
- **West End Museum, Boston**: a history page on Edward Converse's ferry, which it calls the first on that crossing (Chapter 11)
- **West End Museum, Boston**: a history of the Shawmut Peninsula and the Massachusett people, with the count of the 1616 to 1618 epidemic (Chapter 19)
- **Worcester Art Museum, Worcester**: the Freake portrait of Elizabeth and Mary Freake (Chapter 32)

## Minnesota

- **Ingalls dugout site, Plum Creek near Walnut Grove**: the hollow in the creek bank where the Ingalls family's dugout stood, on the former Gordon farm (Chapter 7)
- **University of Minnesota**: holds the Eugenics Record Office records (Chapter 9)
- **University of Minnesota St. Paul campus, St. Paul**: four of the Rangh Court townhomes moved for Interstate 94 (Chapter 6)

## Mississippi

- **Natchez City Cemetery, Natchez**: Milla Granson's grave (Chapter 24)

## Missouri

- **Gateway Arch National Park, St. Louis**: the 630-foot stainless steel arch. On July 14, 1964, Percy Green and Richard Daly climbed 125 feet up its unfinished north leg to protest that Black workers were kept out of the jobs building it (Chapter 12)
- **Missouri State Archives**: records of the 1838 fighting between older Missouri settlers and armed Mormon groups (Chapter 5)
- **Sugarloaf Mound, St. Louis**: the last Native mound in the city, now owned by the Osage Nation (Chapter 12)
- **Truman Library, Independence**: where President Johnson signed Medicare and Medicaid in 1965 (Chapter 26)

## Montana

- **Glacier National Park**: on land federal officials say the Blackfeet sold in 1895. Many Blackfeet say they only leased it (Chapter 3)
- **National Bison Range, Flathead Reservation**: the bison refuge that members of Congress gave back to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes in 2020 (Chapter 3)

## Nebraska

- **Homestead National Historical Park, near Beatrice**: the Freeman School, a one-room brick school used from 1872 to 1967 (Chapter 30)
- **Nebraska State Historical Society**: keeps the letters of Uriah and Mattie Oblinger, a homestead family in Nebraska in the 1870s (Chapter 15)

## Nevada

- **Hoover Dam, near Boulder City**: the dam dedicated in 1935 (Chapter 12)
- **University of Nevada, Las Vegas library, Las Vegas**: keeps a history of Hoover Dam that tells how company doctors called tunnel workers' illness pneumonia (Chapter 14)

## New Hampshire

- **Amoskeag Falls, on the Merrimack River**: Pennacook fishing place where Matthew Patten fished in 1767 (Chapter 15)

## New Jersey

- **Arts Council of Princeton, Princeton**: Nathan Jackson's Raven and Fog Woman pole, in the lobby (Chapter 32)
- **Falls of the Passaic River, Paterson**: where Hamilton's Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures started a factory town (Chapter 16)
- **Nothnagle Log House, Greenwich Township**: a log house built in about 1638 to 1643 that still stands (Chapter 7)
- **Rutgers Oral History Archives**: Donald Lundberg's 2008 interview about shopping under rationing (Chapter 18)

## New Mexico

- **Acoma Pueblo**: the Acoma people's town on its mesa. Spanish soldiers under Vicente de Zaldívar attacked it in January 1599 and killed men, women and children. Juan de Oñate then sentenced the survivors over 12 to twenty years of forced work, the men over 25 to have a foot cut off as well, and the old people to slavery (Chapter 2)
- **Chaco Canyon, northwestern New Mexico**: the stone great houses of the Ancestral Puebloans, begun in the 800s and lined up with the sun and moon, and their planned roads. Casa Rinconada there is a great kiva, a round room for ceremonies, about 63 feet across (Chapter 29)
- **El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro National Historic Trail**: the road from Mexico City to northern New Mexico that Oñate's colonists traveled north in 1598 (Chapter 5)
- **Fajada Butte, Chaco Canyon**: the Sun Dagger slabs and carved spirals (Chapter 9)
- **Gila Wilderness**: the first area in the world officially named a wilderness area (Chapter 3)
- **Palace of the Governors, Santa Fe**: on the plaza, used as a public building without a break for longer than any other building in the United States (Chapter 6)
- **Pueblo Bonito, Chaco Canyon**: the largest great house, four stories high, where jars from about 1000 to 1125 held traces of a drink made from cacao, the bean chocolate comes from (Chapter 12)
- **San Esteban del Rey, Acoma Pueblo**: the oldest European church in New Mexico that still stands, built by Acoma workers (Chapter 32)
- **San Miguel Chapel, Santa Fe**: an adobe chapel on foundations from about 1620 (Chapter 12)
- **Taos Pueblo, Taos**: the North House and the South House, five stories of adobe built between about 1000 and 1450, where people have lived for more than 1,000 years (Chapter 7)
- **Volcanic cliffs west of Albuquerque, Petroglyph National Monument**: about 24,000 to 25,000 petroglyphs, pictures that Native artists cut or pecked into the rock (Chapter 32)
- **White Sands National Park**: human footprints at least 21,000 years old (Chapter 1)

## New York

- **1520 Sedgwick Avenue, the Bronx**: the building city officials named the birthplace of hip-hop (Chapter 33)
- **Arthur Ashe Stadium, New York City**: where the first Fortnite World Cup final was played in 2019 (Chapter 36)
- **Brooklyn Bridge, New York**: the suspension bridge opened in 1883 (Chapter 12)
- **Brooklyn Museum, Brooklyn**: Judy Chicago's The Dinner Party (Chapter 32)
- **Castle Clinton, Battery Park, New York**: the site of Castle Garden, the first official immigrant depot, 1855 to 1890 (Chapter 4)
- **Central Park, New York**: the site of Seneca Village, where about 225 people lived by 1855, most of them African American. City officials forced them out by 1857 to make the park (Chapter 6)
- **Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory**: its archive website publishes the Eugenics Record Office history (Chapter 9)
- **Columbia University**: Charity Clarke's letters to her cousin Joseph Jekyll (Chapter 35)
- **Cooper Union, New York**: the hall where Clara Lemlich called the shirtwaist makers out on strike in 1909 (Chapter 14)
- **East Hampton Library**: Robert Townsend's account books of 1773 to 1785, copied out and published by its staff (Chapter 18)
- **Ellis Island, New York Harbor**: the immigration station where about 12 million immigrants came through from 1892 to 1954. Its main building has been a museum of immigration since 1990, and a statue of Annie Moore, the first immigrant to come through it, stands there (Chapter 4)
- **Empire State Building, New York**: the 102-story building finished in 1931 (Chapter 12)
- **Lewis Latimer House Museum, East Flushing, Queens**: the house Lewis Latimer lived in from 1903 (Chapter 8)
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York**: the Temple of Dendur, in the wing where Nan Goldin's group protested in 2018 and that no longer carries the Sackler name (Chapter 28)
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York**: a signed painting by Prince Demah, and Cole's The Oxbow (Chapter 32)
- **Morgan Library & Museum, New York City**: an exhibition on the Panic of 1907 that describes Morgan's locked-door meeting (Chapter 17)
- **Museum of Modern Art, New York**: the even-numbered panels of Jacob Lawrence's The Migration Series (Chapter 32)
- **National Baseball Hall of Fame, Cooperstown**: Josh Gibson's plaque, which says he hit "almost 800" home runs (Chapter 36)
- **National September 11 Memorial and Museum, New York**: two pools with the names of 2,983 people, the people killed on September 11, 2001, and six killed in a 1993 bombing of the towers. The museum has an exhibit about Mohawk ironworkers called "Skywalkers" (Chapter 12)
- **New York Public Library, New York**: Le Moyne's painting Laudonnière and Athore, 1564 (Chapter 32)
- **One World Trade Center, New York**: the tower 1,776 feet to the tip of its spire (Chapter 12)
- **Onondaga Nation, central New York**: keeps the wampum records of the Haudenosaunee (Chapter 31)
- **St. Patrick's Cathedral, New York**: the crypt where Pierre Toussaint's remains lie (Chapter 4)
- **Statue of Liberty, New York Harbor**: the statue, with Emma Lazarus's poem mounted on a bronze plaque inside its base in 1903, and the original 1886 torch in the statue's museum (Chapter 12)
- **Stonewall National Monument, New York**: Christopher Park, in memory of the 1969 uprising at the Stonewall Inn (Chapter 25)
- **Sun Building, 280 Broadway, New York City**: A. T. Stewart's Marble Palace store building of 1846, which still stands (Chapter 18)
- **Tenement Museum, New York**: the Moores' apartment at 97 Orchard Street, recreated as it was in 1869 (Chapter 6)
- **Tenement Museum, 97 Orchard Street, New York**: the tenement where about 7,000 people lived, with the Baldizzi family's apartment rebuilt from Josephine Baldizzi's memories (Chapter 7)
- **Thomas Cole National Historic Site, Catskill**: Cole's house and studio (Chapter 32)
- **Tompkins Square Park, New York City**: where police beat people with clubs and drove out a crowd of about 7,000 jobless people on January 13, 1874 (Chapter 16)
- **Wall Street and Water Street, New York City**: a sign marking where the city's market for enslaved people stood from 1711 to 1762 (Chapter 18)
- **Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls**: where the 1848 women's rights convention met (Chapter 25)
- **Woolworth Building, New York**: the tallest building in the world when it was finished in 1913 (Chapter 12)

## North Carolina

- **Berry site, Burke County**: the remains of Fort San Juan, built in 1567 (Chapter 22)
- **Biltmore House, Asheville**: the largest privately owned house in the United States (Chapter 12)
- **Roanoke Island**: where John White left 117 colonists in 1587 and found CROATOAN carved on a post in 1590 (Chapter 31)
- **Southern Oral History Program, University of North Carolina**: the 1984 interview with Pearl and Oral Yates about electricity reaching Haywood County (Chapter 10)

## Ohio

- **Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks**: earthen walls and mounds built between about the years 1 and 400, a World Heritage Site (Chapter 29)
- **Miami University**: its librarians keep more than 300 editions of the McGuffey Readers (Chapter 30)
- **Octagon Earthworks, Newark**: earth walls about 2,000 years old that line up with the moonrise, a golf course from 1910 to 2024 and open to the public since January 1, 2025 (Chapter 12)
- **Ohio History Connection, Columbus**: keeps "Buttons," the last confirmed wild passenger pigeon, shot in 1900 (Chapter 3)
- **Serpent Mound**: a snake of earth about a quarter of a mile long (Chapter 12)
- **Taft Museum of Art, Cincinnati**: Duncanson's eight landscape murals in the entrance hall (Chapter 32)

## Oklahoma

- **Greenwood, Tulsa**: the Black district a white mob burned in 1921, which its residents rebuilt (Chapter 17)
- **Greenwood Cultural Center, Tulsa**: where President Biden met the three Greenwood survivors in 2021 (Chapter 25)
- **Oaklawn Cemetery, Tulsa**: where searchers have dug up the remains of 55 people in the search for the massacre's graves (Chapter 25)

## Oregon

- **Crater Lake, southern Oregon**: the lake that filled the collapsed top of Mount Mazama, 1,943 feet deep (Chapter 27)
- **Hells Canyon, Snake River**: where a gang of white horse thieves killed as many as 34 Chinese miners in 1887, with a 2012 stone marker in English, Chinese and Nez Perce (Chapter 4)

## Pennsylvania

- **Amos Humiston monument, Gettysburg**: on North Stratton Street, the only monument on the battlefield to a single enlisted soldier (Chapter 22)
- **Carlisle Indian Industrial School grounds, Carlisle**: a national monument since December 9, 2024, with the Carlisle Barracks cemetery, where many of the children who died at the school are buried (Chapter 30)
- **Eastern State Penitentiary, Philadelphia**: the prison where Charles Williams became Prisoner Number 1 in 1829, now a historic site (Chapter 21)
- **Eden Cemetery, Collingdale**: burial place of 19 Black Philadelphians from the Morton collection (Chapter 9)
- **Franklin Institute, Philadelphia**: a science museum with a history of Franklin's inventions (Chapter 8)
- **Haverford College, Haverford**: keeps the 1688 Germantown protest against slavery (Chapter 25)
- **Historical Society of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia**: Hesselius's portraits of Lapowinsa and Tishcohan (Chapter 32)
- **Hopewell Furnace National Historic Site, French Creek, Berks County**: the charcoal iron furnace that ran from 1771 to 1883, on land federal officials bought in the 1930s. More than 100 woodcutters and about 20 colliers, the men who made the charcoal, worked there in the 1830s (Chapter 10)
- **Independence Hall, Philadelphia**: the Pennsylvania State House, whose planning Andrew Hamilton led, where the delegates signed the Constitution on September 17, 1787, open to visitors as part of a national park (Chapter 20)
- **John Bartram's botanic garden, Kingsessing, on the Schuylkill River**: the oldest botanic garden in America that survives today (Chapter 9)
- **Logan, Franklin, Washington and Rittenhouse squares and City Hall, Philadelphia**: the five squares of Holme's 1682 plan (Chapter 6)
- **Mother Bethel, Sixth and Lombard Streets, Philadelphia**: Richard Allen's church, where he is buried in the basement (Chapter 29)
- **Mütter Museum, Philadelphia**: a museum of medical history (Chapter 26)
- **Penn Museum, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia**: holds Samuel Morton's skull collection (Chapter 9)
- **Penn Museum, Philadelphia**: quilled pieces from the 1830s and later, including pieces George Catlin collected between 1832 and 1839 (Chapter 35)
- **Philadelphia Mint, Philadelphia**: where the last pennies made for everyday use were struck on November 12, 2025 (Chapter 17)
- **Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia**: Augustin de Saint-Aubin's 1777 engraving of Benjamin Franklin in his fur cap (Chapter 23)
- **Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia**: Moses Williams's silhouettes of the Peale family (Chapter 32)
- **President's House site, Philadelphia**: where George Washington lived while he was president, and where Ona Judge, an enslaved woman, walked out to freedom in 1796. Its exhibit panels describe nine people Washington held in slavery there. Officials of the Trump administration replaced the old panels with their own in July 2026 (Chapter 12)
- **Priestley's house, Northumberland**: Joseph Priestley's house, finished in 1798, where American chemists met in 1874, now a National Historic Landmark (Chapter 13)
- **Soldiers' National Cemetery, Gettysburg**: the graves of the Gettysburg dead, 979 of them marked "Unknown" (Chapter 22)
- **Valley Forge**: the winter camp of 1777 to 1778 (Chapter 22)

## Rhode Island

- **Bristol**: the Fourth of July celebration held every year since 1785, which the town calls the oldest in the United States that has never stopped (Chapter 37)
- **Roger Williams National Memorial, Providence**: the memorial Congress voted to set up in 1965 (Chapter 29)

## South Carolina

- **Avery Research Center, Charleston**: the papers of Mamie Garvin Fields (Chapter 30)
- **Charleston County Register of Deeds, Charleston County**: hundreds of colonial land mortgages (Chapter 17)
- **Charleston Museum, Charleston**: two Dave jars that each hold more than 40 gallons (Chapter 32)
- **International African American Museum, Charleston**: the 1850 Zealy daguerreotypes of Renty, Delia and five others, on public view since March 11, 2026 (Chapter 9)
- **International African American Museum, Charleston**: a museum at Gadsden's Wharf, one of the main places where slave ships landed their captives (Chapter 24)
- **South Carolina Department of Archives and History**: thousands of early chattel mortgages, in which enslavers and lenders listed enslaved people as property (Chapter 17)
- **St. Philip's Church, Charleston**: the church whose burning roof Will saved in 1796 (Chapter 27)
- **Sullivan's Island, Charleston**: the harbor island where enslaved Africans were held in pest houses (Chapter 4)

## South Dakota

- **Mount Rushmore, Black Hills**: the carving on the mountain the Lakota call Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers (Chapter 12)
- **South Dakota Cultural Heritage Center**: the La Verendrye lead plate from 1743, found at Pierre in 1913 (Chapter 1)
- **Wounded Knee, Pine Ridge Reservation**: the site of the 1890 massacre and the 1973 occupation (Chapter 2)

## Tennessee

- **Clayborn Temple, Memphis**: the church where police fired tear gas and clubbed marchers on March 28, 1968 (Chapter 14)
- **Dollywood, Pigeon Forge**: the theme park Dolly Parton became a partner in (Chapter 33)
