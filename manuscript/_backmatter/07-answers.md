# Answers to the Chapter Questions

Each chapter opens with three questions. Here they are with their answers, chapter by chapter.

## Chapter 1: Exploration and Discovery

- **How do we know people were living here at least 21,000 years ago?** Human footprints at White Sands, in New Mexico, lie between layers of seeds, pollen and quartz sand, and three different tests date those layers to about 21,000 to 23,000 years ago.
- **What did the explorers do to the people whose land they crossed?** Some traded with them and were fed by them. Others took captives, from the 500 Taino Columbus's men shipped to Spain to the six Inuit Peary brought to New York, and some killed: de Soto's men at Mabila, Coronado's at Arenal, Frémont's on the Sacramento River.
- **Which famous "firsts" are still in doubt?** Peary's North Pole in 1909 and Byrd's flight over it in 1926. Wally Herbert found Peary's records unreliable, and most researchers think Byrd's plane could not carry enough fuel. Older firsts are in doubt too: Spanish slave raiders may have reached Florida before Ponce de León, Ute tradition says Ute ancestors climbed Pikes Peak before Long's men, and nobody knows whether the Norse camp in Newfoundland was Leif's own.

## Chapter 2: Native Nations

- **How did Native nations govern themselves and deal with other nations before the United States existed?** They ran their own councils and laws, as the Haudenosaunee did under the Great Law of Peace. Their leaders made alliances and treaties and recorded them on wampum belts.
- **What did United States officials and soldiers do to take the nations' land and break the nations up?** Soldiers forced whole nations west and onto reservations, and killed hundreds of people at a time at Sand Creek, Wounded Knee and other places. Officials cut the nations' land from about 138 million acres to 48 million and took children to boarding schools, where at least 973 died.
- **How did Native nations hold on to their governments, their languages and some of their land?** They went to court, from Worcester v. Georgia in 1832 to McGirt v. Oklahoma in 2020. They organized until members of Congress restored the Menominee, and they kept their languages, first in secret and now in schools of their own.

## Chapter 3: Land and Environment

- **How did Native nations shape the land before Europeans came?** They burned the eastern forests on purpose, again and again, to keep them open and grow food for game, and they planted corn, beans and squash together to keep the soil good.
- **What happened to the 30 million bison and the billions of passenger pigeons?** Hide hunters, carried by the railroads and helped by army officers who wanted the Plains nations' food gone, left fewer than 1,000 bison by 1900. Netters and shooters killed the pigeons at their nestings, and the last one died in a zoo in 1914.
- **Who was pushed out when parks were made, and who lived next to the waste?** Native people were pushed out: the Ahwahneechee in Yosemite, the Tukudika in Yellowstone, the Blackfeet at Glacier. Studies from 1983 on found waste sites far more often beside Black and poor neighborhoods.

## Chapter 4: Immigration to America

- **Why did people leave home to come here?** Many came for land and work, like the Germans who farmed the Midwest and the Chinese who built the western railroad, or to worship in their own way, like the Puritans. Others ran from danger: the Irish famine, the pogroms against Jews in the Russian Empire, the Nazis, and wars in Vietnam and Sudan.
- **Who did Americans try to keep out, and how?** Lawmakers shut out Chinese workers in 1882 and almost everyone from Asia in 1924, and they cut the number of southern and eastern Europeans with quotas. Mobs burned Catholic churches in Philadelphia and drove Chinese people out of Eureka and Tacoma. Officials pushed hundreds of thousands of people of Mexican descent, many of them citizens, across the border in the 1930s and again in 1954. In 2018 agents took thousands of children from their parents at the border, and in January 2026 officers held more than 71,000 people in detention, the most ever.
- **Who could become a citizen, and how did that change?** In 1790 only "a free white person" could become a citizen. In 1870 members of Congress added people of African birth or descent. In 1898 the Supreme Court ruled in Wong Kim Ark's case that anyone born here is a citizen, and in 1952 members of Congress removed the limit to white people.

## Chapter 5: Migration Across America

- **How did horses reach the Native nations of the Plains?** In the older account, horses spread north through trade after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, when the Spanish left perhaps more than 1,500 of them behind. A 2023 study of horse bones found that many nations had horses by the early 1600s, decades earlier, as the nations' own histories say.
- **How many Cherokee died when soldiers forced them west?** Nobody knows exactly. The count most often given is about 4,000, and estimates run from more than 1,000 on the journey itself to about 8,000 or more.
- **Why did about 6 million Black Americans leave the South between 1910 and 1970?** They left lynch mobs, Jim Crow laws and cotton crops ruined by the boll weevil and floods, and they went toward jobs in Northern and Western cities. In Georgia, one example, lynch mobs killed more than 450 people between 1880 and 1930, and nearly all Black Georgians could not vote.

## Chapter 6: City Building

- **Why do so many American cities have straight streets that cross in a grid?** Town founders chose the grid on purpose, from Spain's town rules of 1573 to Penn's Philadelphia in 1682. The three men who laid a grid over Manhattan in 1811 wrote that square-cornered houses were "the most cheap to build and the most convenient to live in."
- **How did people get clean water into crowded cities?** City leaders paid for big waterworks after epidemics and fires: steam pumps in Philadelphia in 1801, the Croton Aqueduct in New York in 1842, and a canal that turned Chicago's river around in 1900.
- **Who decided which families could live in which neighborhoods?** White owners wrote covenants into their deeds and judges enforced them until 1948, federal officials refused to back home loans where Black families lived, and after 1949 city officials cleared about 2,000 neighborhoods.

## Chapter 7: Home and Family Life

- **Who lived together under one roof, from the longhouse to today?** A longhouse held 30 to 60 relatives, and colonial households held servants, apprentices and enslaved people too. By 2025 the average household held 2.50 people.
- **Who did the work of keeping house, and did stoves, pipes and wires make it lighter?** Women and children did most of it. In Ruth Schwartz Cowan's argument, the machines did not cut a housewife's hours, because families expected more.
- **How did lenders and builders keep Black families out of new neighborhoods, and what did those families do?** FHA officials usually refused to back loans where Black families lived, and William Levitt sold only to white families. The Myers family stayed through weeks of attacks, and about 500 families in the league Clyde Ross helped lead stopped paying together.

## Chapter 8: Technology

- **Why did planters enslave far more people after the cotton gin?** One gin cleaned about 50 pounds of cotton a day, work that took several hundred hours by hand. Planters grew far more cotton and enslaved far more people to grow and pick it, nearly 4 million by 1860.
- **Why could an enslaved inventor not get a patent?** Only the inventor could get a patent, and only by swearing an oath. In 1858 Attorney General Jeremiah Black ruled that an enslaved man was not a citizen and could not swear it, and that the enslaver could not take the patent instead.
- **What went wrong when people trusted a computer's answer?** In 2023 two lawyers cited court decisions that ChatGPT had made up, and a judge ordered them and their law firm to pay a $5,000 penalty. In 2020 Detroit police held Robert Williams for 30 hours after a face-recognition search matched him by mistake.

## Chapter 9: Science

- **How did American scientists learn how far away the stars are?** Henrietta Leavitt showed in 1912 that the blink of a Cepheid star tells how bright it truly is, and so how far away it is. Edwin Hubble used her rule in 1923 to place Andromeda far outside our own galaxy.
- **Why did scientists collect human skulls and brains, and where are those remains now?** Samuel Morton, the Army's museum scientists and Aleš Hrdlička measured them to rank the races, an idea that is false. Morton's skulls are at the Penn Museum, whose staff buried 19 of them in 2024, and the Smithsonian still held at least 255 of the brains in 2023. Many Native American remains have been returned since the laws of 1989 and 1990, but in January 2025 museums still had 90,831 not yet made available for return.
- **Who did the work but was left out of the credit?** Lise Meitner explained fission, and Rosalind Franklin's X-ray pictures showed the shape of DNA. The Nobel Prizes went to Otto Hahn alone in 1944 and to Watson, Crick and Wilkins in 1962.

## Chapter 10: Energy

- **Did kerosene really save the whales?** No. Whaling was shrinking before kerosene came, and in most homes kerosene replaced camphene, after a Civil War tax on alcohol raised camphene's price.
- **How did electricity reach almost every American home?** Edison's Pearl Street station began selling current in 1882, and alternating current, which a transformer could raise or lower, carried power from Niagara Falls to Buffalo by 1896. After 1935 federal loans paid for farmers' own power lines, and by 1950, 94 households in 100 had electricity.
- **Who paid for America's energy with their freedom, their land or their lives?** Enslaved people fed the iron furnaces, at least 271 at Catoctin alone, and about 105,000 coal miners died in accidents from 1900 through 2025. Federal officials made 82,000 people in the Tennessee Valley leave their homes for dams, other dams flooded Kettle Falls and the Fort Berthold farmlands, and Osage people were murdered for their oil money.

## Chapter 11: Transportation

- **How much faster and cheaper did travel get?** On the Erie Canal a ton of goods went from Buffalo to New York City for under $10 instead of about $100 by wagon, and on the first railroad across the country a coast-to-coast trip took a week instead of four months. By 1956, loading a ton of cargo onto a ship cost about 16 cents in containers, against about $5.86 by hand.
- **Who built the new routes, and whose land and homes did they take?** Irish and American-born laborers dug the Erie Canal, enslaved people dug the Dismal Swamp Canal and built most of the Southern railroads, and Chinese workers built the western part of the first railroad across the country. The routes took Native nations' land, and highway officials building federal roads took the homes of more than 475,000 households, many in Black neighborhoods.
- **How did Black travelers fight the laws that made them sit apart?** They refused to move, went to court and stayed off the buses: Ida B. Wells sued a railroad in 1884, Homer Plessy tested a Louisiana law and lost in 1896, Irene Morgan won in the Supreme Court in 1946, and Black riders in Montgomery, Alabama, stayed off the city buses for more than a year in 1955 and 1956. After the Freedom Riders were beaten in Alabama in 1961, federal officials banned separating passengers in bus and train stations.

## Chapter 12: Landmarks

- **Who built the White House and the Capitol, and who was paid for their work?** Enslaved men did much of the work, from quarrying the stone to sawing boards and hauling loads. The officials in charge rented them from their owners by the year, and the owners collected the pay.
- **How many workers died building Hoover Dam?** By the official count, 96. The records list 42 more as dying of pneumonia, deaths that workers blamed on carbon monoxide in the tunnels, so the exact number is not known.
- **What has happened to the Confederate monuments since 2015?** By April 2025 officials and work crews had taken down, moved or renamed 415 Confederate memorials, from statues to place names, hundreds of them after the killings in Charleston, Charlottesville and Minneapolis. In 2025 federal officials began putting some back.

## Chapter 13: The Elements

- **Who lost their land when Americans found gold and copper on it?** The Cherokee lost theirs after gold turned up in Georgia in 1829, when Georgia lawmakers gave it away by lottery and soldiers forced them west in 1838. The Ojibwe bands of Lake Superior gave up the copper country in the Treaty of La Pointe in 1842, and members of Congress took the Black Hills from the Sioux in 1877, after Custer's men found gold there.
- **What did the people in charge say about the radium, lead and uranium that poisoned workers?** Supervisors told the dial painters the paint was safe, the Bayway supervisor said his poisoned men had worked too hard, and Thomas Midgley breathed the vapor of tetraethyl lead in front of reporters to show it was safe. Public Health Service researchers agreed with mine owners not to tell the uranium miners what they found.
- **When did someone in the United States first discover a new element?** In 1940, at Berkeley, California, when teams using Ernest Lawrence's cyclotron made astatine and neptunium, and then plutonium. Every element before that had been found abroad, and Fred Allison's 1931 claims were false.

## Chapter 14: Work and Workers

- **Which workers were not free to leave their work?** Native people forced to work under Spanish rule, indentured servants bound for years, enslaved people, prisoners leased to Southern mines and planters, and the Thai garment workers held behind a fence in El Monte until 1995. Virginia judges sentenced John Punch in 1640 to serve for the rest of his life.
- **What happened when workers went on strike?** Some won, like the Philadelphia printers in 1786 and the Flint autoworkers in 1937. Many times police, hired guards and soldiers broke the strike, and at Lattimer in 1897, Ludlow in 1914 and Chicago in 1937 they killed strikers, their families and people marching with them.
- **What made work less deadly?** Laws written after workers died: more than one way out of every Pennsylvania mine after the Avondale fire, safer couplers after thousands of brakemen died, new factory laws in New York after the Triangle fire, and the 1969 coal mine law after Farmington. Deaths on the job fell from about 38 a day in 1970 to about 14 a day in 2024.

## Chapter 15: Food and Farming

- **How did Native farmers turn a wild grass into corn?** For thousands of years, starting in southern Mexico about 9,000 years ago, they saved seed only from the teosinte plants they chose, until the grass became corn.
- **Who did the hardest farm work, and what did they get for it?** Enslaved people worked the rice and cotton fields for no pay, and sharecroppers got half the crop's value or less and stayed in debt. From 1992 to 2006, crop workers died of heat at nearly 20 times the rate of all American workers.
- **How did Americans come to eat food grown far away by people they never met?** With canning, pond ice and railroad cars cooled with ice, food could be sold far from the farm, and the farms grew fewer, down to 1.9 million in 2022.

## Chapter 16: Economy

- **How did slavery make money for people far from any plantation?** Bankers in London, Amsterdam and New York sold bonds backed by mortgages on enslaved people, Northern companies insured their lives, and Boston mill owners spun the cotton that enslaved people grew. Rhode Island merchants sent out ships that carried more than 100,000 Africans into slavery.
- **What happened to ordinary families when a boom turned into a crash?** Judges jailed debtors in 1819, creditors sold enslaved families apart in 1837, one worker in four had no job by 1933, and lenders took 7.7 million to 10 million homes in the crisis that began in 2007. In the 1930s and after 2007, Black families lost jobs and homes at higher rates than white families.
- **When did the national government start helping people who lost their jobs?** In the 1930s. Before 1933 the federal government paid no unemployment insurance or old-age pensions, and members of Congress set up both in the Social Security Act of 1935, though farm and domestic workers had to wait until 1950 and 1954.

## Chapter 17: Money

- **What has stood behind the dollar, and what stands behind it now?** Silver and gold stood behind it, then gold alone. Since August 15, 1971, only the law and people's trust in the government that prints it have stood behind it.
- **Why did people rush to pull their money out of banks, and who lost when a bank failed?** A bank keeps only part of its money in cash, so the first in line got paid. Until deposit insurance began in 1934, the rest took the loss, about $1.34 billion from 1930 to 1933.
- **How did enslavers and bankers use enslaved people to raise money?** Enslavers pledged them for loans, and in the 1830s Louisiana banks lent out their own paper money against them. When the loans went unpaid, the people were sold.

## Chapter 18: Marketplace and Consumer Life

- **How did colonists pay for goods when they had almost no coins?** They took goods home on credit and paid later in crops, other goods, work and wampum, while the shopkeeper wrote each debt in a ledger.
- **Where were people bought and sold like goods?** At the market New York's aldermen set up on Wall Street in 1711, in newspaper advertisements such as the 277 or more in Franklin's Pennsylvania Gazette, and at a racetrack near Savannah, where Pierce Butler sold between 429 and 436 people in 1859.
- **How much of what Americans buy today is bought online?** About one dollar in six: online sales were 17.1 percent of all retail sales from April to June 2026, by the US Census Bureau's count.

## Chapter 19: Big Business and Its Power

- **How did a few companies come to control whole industries?** Their owners made secret deals with railroads, bought out rivals and joined companies into trusts and mergers. By 1880 Standard Oil refined 90 to 95 percent of the country's oil.
- **What did owners do when their workers went on strike?** They locked workers out and hired armed guards, and officials sent soldiers and court orders. At Homestead in 1892 Frick's Pinkerton men fought the steelworkers, and at Ludlow in 1914 soldiers paid by the mine owners burned the strikers' tent camp.
- **Did breaking up a giant company ever work?** Not clearly. Judges split Standard Oil into 34 companies in 1911, yet Rockefeller was three times as rich by 1913. AT&T gave up its local telephone companies in 1984 in a deal most newspapers called a win for AT&T, the order to split Microsoft was thrown out in 2001, and judges in 2025 and 2026 would not make Google sell off parts of its business.

## Chapter 20: Government, Law, and Politics

- **Who decided who could vote, and how did the answer change?** Each colony's and then each state's lawmakers decided, and the Constitution left it that way. Virginia's burgesses took the vote from free Black men in 1723, New Jersey's lawmakers took it from women in 1807, and Southern delegates took it from Black men again after 1890. Four amendments (1870, 1920, 1964, 1971) and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 took most of that power back from the states.
- **When did judges first cancel a law, and what have they done with that power since?** In 1803, in *Marbury v. Madison*. The justices have used it to allow sterilization, separate railroad cars and the wartime camps, and to strike down school segregation, official prayers, poll taxes and unequal districts.
- **What happens when people in power break the law?** A jury, a court or the Senate decides. In 1614 royal judges found Juan de Oñate, a former governor of New Mexico, guilty of 12 charges and ordered him out of New Mexico for life. A jury found a cabinet secretary guilty of taking a bribe in 1929, and a judge sentenced him to a year in prison. A president resigned in 1974 rather than face a vote. Bill Clinton and Donald Trump were impeached, Trump twice, and the senators found neither guilty. A jury found a senator guilty of bribery in 2024, and he went to prison.

## Chapter 21: Crime, Police, and Justice

- **How did punishment move from the whipping post to the prison cell?** Colonial judges had people whipped, branded and hanged in public, and had their ears cut off. Pennsylvania's lawmakers turned the Walnut Street Jail into the country's first state prison in 1790, and by 2009 American prisons held about 1.62 million people.
- **Who kept order before there were police?** Among the Native nations, families and clans settled killings, and in the colonies settlers took turns on a night watch while white men in the South rode on slave patrols. Full-time police began work in Boston in 1838 and in New York in 1845, and the US attorney general put 34 federal agents to work in 1908.
- **What happened to the people in mobs who killed without a trial?** In almost every case in this chapter, nobody punished them. No one was charged for the Conestoga killings in 1763, for burning Francis McIntosh in 1836 or for burning Henry Smith in 1893, and no white person went to prison for Tulsa in 1921.

## Chapter 22: War and the Military

- **What killed most American soldiers before World War II?** Disease. In the war with Mexico seven of every eight American dead died of something other than enemy fire, and in World War I flu and pneumonia killed more soldiers than the war's deadliest battle did.
- **Who was made to fight, and who could get out of it?** Drafts filled the armies of the Civil War, both world wars and Vietnam. In 1863 a drafted man could pay $300 and go home, and in the Vietnam years college students could put off the draft. Since 1973 everyone who serves has volunteered.
- **Were soldiers who killed people who could not fight back ever punished?** Rarely. No one was charged for Gnadenhutten or tried for Sand Creek, and Calley, the only man convicted for My Lai, went free on parole in 1974.

## Chapter 23: America and the World

- **Who got to choose when this land and its islands changed hands?** Almost never the people who lived there: no Native nation was at Paris in 1783, and no one from Puerto Rico, Guam or the Philippines was at Paris in 1898. Of today's five territories, only the Northern Mariana Islands voted to join, and Hawaii's voters chose statehood in 1959 from a ballot that left out independence.
- **How did a weak new country come to rule islands and overthrow governments?** First it grew: by 1848 it reached the Pacific, and by 1900 its mills made more steel than Britain's and Germany's together. Then its leaders used force. Marines helped overthrow Hawaii's queen in 1893, American leaders took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines after a war with Spain in 1898, and after 1950 CIA officers secretly helped overthrow governments in Iran, Guatemala and Chile.
- **Are the people of the territories American citizens?** Most are, but people born in American Samoa are nationals, not citizens, and no one living in a territory can vote for president.

## Chapter 24: Slavery and Freedom

- **How did a servant in Virginia become a slave for life, and a child a slave from birth?** Virginia's judges and lawmakers did it one ruling at a time. Judges sentenced John Punch to serve for life in 1640, and in 1662 lawmakers ruled that a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life.
- **How did enslaved people fight back?** They worked slowly, ran away, sued, learned to read in secret and rose up with weapons. During the Civil War about half a million freed themselves by reaching the Union army.
- **What happened to the freedom people won in 1865?** Freed people voted, built schools and sent 16 men to Congress, but within twelve years Black Codes, the Klan and the end of Reconstruction took much of it back, until the laws of 1964 and 1965.

## Chapter 25: Rights and Movements

- **How did people without power get the people in power to listen?** They signed petitions, printed pamphlets and spoke to crowds, and later they planned test cases, boycotted, marched, sat in and filmed the police, from the Germantown protest of 1688 to Darnella Frazier's video in 2020.
- **What did organizing cost the people who did it?** Their jobs, their homes and their freedom, and sometimes their bodies and their lives. Lucy Burns was fed by force through a tube, Fannie Lou Hamer was beaten in a Mississippi jail, Medgar Evers was shot in his driveway, and four girls died in a Birmingham church.
- **Can a right that has been won be taken away again?** Yes. New Jersey's lawmakers took the vote from women and free Black men in 1807, members of Congress took Utah women's vote in 1887, and the justices of the Supreme Court cut back the Voting Rights Act in 2013 and 2026 and ended the right to an abortion in 2022.

## Chapter 26: Health, Disease, and Medicine

- **Why did new diseases kill so many Native people after 1492?** The crowd diseases, such as smallpox and measles, had never reached the Americas, so nobody here was immune and a whole town could fall sick at once. Smallpox killed about half of the Cherokee in 1738 and 1739.
- **How did Americans learn to stop smallpox, yellow fever and polio?** Boston doctors tried inoculation in 1721, using a method Onesimus described, and safer vaccination followed in 1800. Army doctors proved in 1900 that mosquitoes spread yellow fever, and after the Salk and Sabin vaccines the last polio case caught inside the country came in 1979.
- **Who was used in medical experiments without agreeing, and what changed because of it?** Enslaved women operated on by J. Marion Sims, the Black men of the Tuskegee study, prisoners and patients in Guatemala, and children at Willowbrook. After the Tuskegee study became public, members of Congress passed the National Research Act in 1974, and a federally paid study on people now needs each person's informed consent.

## Chapter 27: Disasters and Rescue

- **What rules did Americans make after disasters, and did they work?** After many of these disasters someone wrote a rule: Boston's fire wardens in 1711, brick walls in Boston and New Orleans, steamboat inspections, a Weather Bureau in 1890, 36 factory laws after the Triangle fire, a smell in natural gas after New London, and one building code for Florida after Andrew. Some went into use fast: the smell was in natural gas within weeks. Some failed: the 1838 steamboat law had no one to enforce it, a New York law said factory doors must stay unlocked but witnesses said the Triangle owners kept one locked, the *Eastland*'s added lifeboats may have made it more dangerous, and New Orleans's floodwalls broke in 2005. Some are still being tested, such as Kerr County's new flood sirens and FEMA, whose future members of Congress were still debating in late September 2026.
- **Was anyone punished when people's choices killed others?** Rarely. No one was punished for the *Sultana*, the Johnstown club won in court, a jury found the Triangle owners not guilty, and no Pittston official was charged after Buffalo Creek. Barnett Welansky went to prison for the Cocoanut Grove fire, and PG&E pleaded guilty to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter after the Camp Fire.
- **Did everyone get the same help after a disaster?** No. Officials paid out London's money for Charleston in 1740 by the size of each loss, so most went to rich merchants. Armed men forced Black workers to handle Galveston's dead in 1900, and camp leaders forced Black people to work in the 1927 flood. In 2005 police turned back people walking out of New Orleans at gunpoint, and in 2025 orders to leave reached west Altadena hours late.

## Chapter 28: Drugs and Alcohol

- **How long have people here used tobacco, and what did they use it for?** For at least 12,300 years, since the fire at the Wishbone site. Native people offered it in prayer, burning it or smoking it in pipes, and the Algonquian people at Roanoke smoked it to keep the body healthy.
- **What happened when the whole country banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933?** Drinking fell at first to about 30 percent of what it had been, then rose to about 60 to 70 percent. Poisoned factory alcohol killed thousands of drinkers before delegates in Utah ended the ban on December 5, 1933.
- **Who has been punished for drugs, and who has not?** Robert Coles with his red D, the laborer Samuel Caldwell with four years of hard labor, Billie Holiday handcuffed to her hospital bed, and the people sentenced for crack, 91 of every 100 of them Black in 1992, were all punished. Harry Gross never served his jail sentence, and no member of the Sackler family has been charged with a crime.

## Chapter 29: Religion

- **Why did colonists who crossed the ocean to worship freely punish other people for their faith?** The Puritans wanted freedom for their own way of worship, and they ran Massachusetts by it. Their leaders banished Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson and hanged four Quakers in Boston.
- **How did Americans stop paying taxes to support churches?** Baptists and James Madison defeated a new church tax in Virginia, where Jefferson's religious freedom law passed in 1786. Massachusetts ended the last state church tax in 1833.
- **How long did US officials treat Native ceremonies as crimes?** For ninety-five years, from Hiram Price's rules of 1883 until members of Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1978.

## Chapter 30: School and Education

- **When did school become free, and required, for every child?** It came in steps, not on one date. Horace Mann argued for free schools paid for by taxes from 1837, and Massachusetts lawmakers made school a legal duty in 1852. Every state had done the same by 1918, and disabled children won the right to a free public school only in 1975.
- **Which children were kept out of school, and how did they get in?** Lawmakers barred enslaved people from learning to read, and officials separated Black, Chinese and Mexican American children, turned disabled children away and took Native children far from home. Judges first backed keeping them out or apart, in Sarah Roberts's case in 1850, Plessy in 1896 and Martha Lum's case in 1927. Later judges let them in, from the Mendez case in 1946 and Brown in 1954 to the Mills case in 1972, and in 1975 members of Congress passed a law for disabled children and a law that let Native nations run their own schools.
- **Is it still lawful for a teacher to hit a child?** Yes, in 15 states in 2026. The justices of the Supreme Court ruled in 1977 that the Constitution's ban on cruel and unusual punishment does not cover paddling at school, so state lawmakers have banned it one state at a time.

## Chapter 31: News and Communication

- **Did the First Amendment stop the government from punishing people for what they printed?** No. Seven years after it took effect, federal judges jailed editors under the Sedition Act, and during World War I prosecutors won more than 1,000 convictions for words.
- **Who was shut out of carrying and printing the news?** From 1802 to 1865 federal law let only free white persons carry the mail, and white mobs drove Black editors out of Memphis and Wilmington. Black and Cherokee Americans started papers of their own.
- **Why have so many local newspapers closed since 2005?** Advertisers moved their money online, where most of it went to technology companies such as Google and Facebook, so newspapers' advertising money fell by more than four-fifths between 2005 and 2022. Readers moved to phones and feeds too, and by 2025, 212 counties had no local news source.

## Chapter 32: Art

- **How did enslaved artists make art, and who got paid for it?** They painted, cut silhouettes, turned pots and wrote poems while other people held them as property, and the people who held them got the money. Dave's jars were sold for the profit of Lewis J. Miles, and Moses Williams learned to cut silhouettes and build exhibits in Peale's museum while Peale held him enslaved. Henry Barnes took Prince Demah to London for painting lessons, and Dave cut verses into his jars in years when it was a crime for him to learn to read.
- **Who decided what counted as art, and who got to see it?** Mostly people with money or power: the Met's trustees, who kept it shut on Sundays until 1891, a teacher in Santa Fe who taught one "authentic" Native style, and a congressman who called a touring show of modern paintings trash. Artists pushed back, from Oscar Howe's letter in 1958 to Amy Sherald cancelling her show in 2025.
- **Why are museums giving back things they once took?** Diggers took objects and human remains out of Native graves, at Mound City in 1846 and at Spiro in the 1930s, and sold many of the objects. Under a 1990 law, NAGPRA, museums that take federal money must return Native remains and sacred objects, and since 2024 they need a nation's consent even to show them.

## Chapter 33: Music

- **Where did the banjo and the blues come from?** The banjo came from West African instruments, and enslaved Black people played it in the colonies from at least 1736, about a hundred years before a white man played it on a stage. The blues grew out of the work songs and field calls of enslaved people and the songs of Black workers in the South after slavery, between about 1870 and 1900.
- **Why did so many musicians make hit songs and still die poor?** Many were paid a set fee and no royalty, as Bessie Smith was at $125 a side, or someone else put his name on their song, as Don Robey did on "Hound Dog." Others, like Stephen Foster, sold their rights to future payments for money right away.
- **Who tried to stop people from playing their own music, and why?** South Carolina lawmakers banned drums and horns in 1740, saying the sound could call enslaved people together to rise up. Spanish friars burned Pueblo masks in the 1600s to punish Pueblo people for keeping their own religion. Richard Henry Pratt gave Carlisle students brass horns to stop their drumming, at a school whose own paper gave its aim as "the complete transformation of the Indians." Federal rules from 1883 to 1978 punished Native religious dances, which the Secretary of the Interior had called "heathenish."

## Chapter 34: Storytelling Evolution

- **Who tried to stop Americans from acting, and why?** A Virginia court tried three actors in 1665 on one man's complaint, and the record does not say why he objected. Spanish priests burned Pueblo masks to end the ceremonies. Massachusetts lawmakers banned plays, saying they made people less moral and less religious. The delegates to the Continental Congress promised to discourage plays as wasteful, then asked the states to stop them. Later, police arrested Black actors after a rival white manager, by one account, started a riot to destroy their company. Federal officials made Native dances crimes, and missionaries in Alaska called Yup'ik masked dances "heathen rites." Studio heads blacklisted hundreds of film workers over communism.
- **Why did white actors play Black, Asian and Native people for so long?** White audiences paid to laugh at blackface from 1828 on, and producers carried the old minstrel parts into film and radio. The studio heads' own Code and state laws against marriage between races kept actors of color out of leading parts.
- **Who controls a computer copy of an actor's face and voice?** Since the strikes of 2023 and 2024 to 2025, union contracts say a studio or game company must get the performer's consent before it makes or uses one, and the game contract also requires pay. Lawmakers in Tennessee and California passed laws of their own in 2024, and as of September 2026 Congress had passed no national law.

## Chapter 35: Styles

- **Why did lawmakers and officials make rules about other people's clothes and hair?** To keep rank where everyone could see it, and to control the people below them: Massachusetts fined people worth less than 200 pounds for wearing silk, South Carolina held enslaved people to coarse cloth in 1740, and a federal official ordered Native men's hair cut in 1902. Since 2019, CROWN Acts in about 30 states have turned the rule around and forbidden punishing people for natural hair, braids, locs or twists.
- **Who made the cloth Americans wore?** Native weavers first, then enslaved people who grew the cotton and made the indigo, and mill workers in New England. Today almost all of it is sewn in other countries, by workers in places like Bangladesh, and in small Los Angeles shops where workers were paid by the piece until a California law required hourly pay in 2022.
- **Which new materials changed what Americans wore?** Churro wool, brought in 1598, became the main thread for weaving in New Mexico. Dyes made from coal tar, from 1856, gave clothes new colors such as mauve. In the 1920s "artificial silk" let more women dress in the new styles. Nylon made stockings from 1939, and polyester suits from 1951 could be washed and worn without ironing.

## Chapter 36: Sports and Play

- **Who invented baseball?** No one person did. Clubs wrote the rules, and the Knickerbocker club of New York adopted twenty of them in 1845. The Doubleday story rests on one letter from a man who was 5 years old in 1839.
- **Who was kept out of American games, and how did they get in?** The people who ran the games kept out laborers, Black players, girls and Black swimmers, and left enslaved riders' names off the race cards. Some got in through courts and a new law, like Maria Pepe and the girls of Title IX. Jackie Robinson got in when Branch Rickey signed him, and Kathryn Johnston played anyway, under a boy's name.
- **How has children's free time changed?** In the 1830s Lucy Larcom made doll furniture from burrs while the grown-ups left the children alone. Since then more of children's time has gone to lessons and organized sports, and by 2022, 95 of every 100 teenagers had or could use a smartphone.

## Chapter 37: Holidays

- **Who decided when Americans would give thanks, and why did it take until 1941 to fix the date?** Governors and church elders set the days one at a time, until Lincoln proclaimed a national Thursday in 1863 at Sarah Josepha Hale's request. Every president after him named the day by proclamation, so when Franklin Roosevelt moved it in 1939 to help stores, sixteen states refused by the National Archives' count, and Congress ended the confusion with the 1941 law.
- **Which days did Black Americans make their own, from election days to Juneteenth?** Black governors' election days in New England from about 1749, January 1 from 1808, July 5 in New York in 1827, August 1 after 1834, and Juneteenth from 1866, which Congress made a federal holiday in 2021.
- **Why did President Harrison proclaim the first Columbus Day in 1892, and why do some states no longer keep it?** He proclaimed it for the 400th anniversary of Columbus's landing, a year and a half after a New Orleans mob lynched eleven Italian American men, and some writers say the holiday was partly meant to calm Italy. Native groups and others object to honoring a man whose men enslaved Taino people. Since Berkeley in 1992, cities and 17 states have used the day to honor Indigenous peoples, and some of those states keep Columbus Day too.
