## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From about 1910 to 1970, roughly 6 million Black Americans left the South for cities in the North, the Midwest and the West. Most of the early travelers went by train. In the late 1930s more than 250,000 people moved from the dried-out farms of the southern Plains to California.

In 1942 United States Army officers ordered Japanese Americans out of their homes on the West Coast. Soldiers and officials forced them into camps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Migration, the first wave" -->
The movement of Black Americans out of the rural South from about 1910 to 1970 is called the Great Migration. The first wave, from about 1910 to 1940, went mostly to cities in the North and the Midwest. Most people rode the trains north. Later travelers also went by car and by bus.

Between 1910 and 1920 the Black population of Chicago grew by about 148 percent. Detroit's grew by about 611 percent, and Philadelphia's by about 500 percent. In New York City it about doubled, from about 152,000 people to 328,000. In 1900 about 90 percent of Black Americans lived in the South. By 1970 fewer than 55 percent did.

Black Georgians had several reasons to leave. In 1915 and 1916 the boll weevil, a beetle that eats cotton plants, and floods ruined the cotton crop. Between 1880 and 1930, lynch mobs in Georgia killed more than 450 people, most of them Black. A lynch mob is a crowd that kills a person without any trial. Jim Crow laws were the Southern laws that kept Black people apart from white people. Under them, nearly all Black Georgians could not vote. About 74,000 Black Georgians left between 1910 and 1920, and about 260,000 more left in the 1920s.

Some people in the North urged Black Southerners to come. Robert S. Abbott ran a Black newspaper, the *Chicago Defender*, which he had started in 1905. Abbott's paper printed job listings and train schedules. Its editorials, articles that give a newspaper's opinion, asked Black Southerners to come north. Abbott set May 15, 1917, as the date for what he called the "Great Northern Drive." Some Northern employers paid migrants' train fares and then took the money back out of their wages.

White officials in the South worked to keep Black workers from leaving. A labor recruiter is a person hired to find workers. Officials in Macon, Georgia, charged Northern labor recruiters a license fee of $25,000, a payment for permission to work there.

Black people in the North also met violence. On July 27, 1919, Eugene Williams, who was 17, and four friends drifted on a homemade raft in Lake Michigan in Chicago. They crossed an unmarked line into the water off a beach at 29th Street where only white people were allowed. George Stauber, a 24-year-old white man, threw rocks at them until Williams drowned. A white police officer, Daniel Callahan, refused to arrest Stauber and stopped a Black officer from arresting him.

Five days of attacks followed, through August 3. The white attackers included youth gangs and groups that called themselves athletic clubs. Thirty-eight people were killed, 23 Black and 15 white. About 537 people were injured, and two-thirds of them were Black. Hundreds of homes were burned or damaged. Of the 138 people charged with crimes afterward, two-thirds were Black.

### Ida Mae Brandon Gladney

> **Who:** A sharecropper's wife who left Mississippi with her family after white men nearly beat a cousin to death.
> **When and where:** From Chickasaw County, Mississippi, by train, late October 1937. Settled in Chicago.

Ida Mae Brandon Gladney and her family left Mississippi in 1937 after a group of white men beat a cousin of theirs nearly to death. The cousin, Joe Lee, had been falsely accused of stealing turkeys. The men beat him with heavy chains. The turkeys turned up the next day, and no one apologized to him. The men who beat him are not named in the surviving accounts.

Ida Mae and her husband, George Gladney, were sharecroppers in Chickasaw County. A sharecropper farms someone else's land and pays the owner with part of the crop. They grew cotton on the plantation of Edd Pearson, whom they called Mr. Edd. After the beating, George came home and told her, "This is the last crop that we're making."

In late October 1937 the cotton was picked, and George settled with Mr. Edd over what the year's work was worth. None of the family had ever been on a train or out of Mississippi. A brother-in-law drove Ida Mae and the two children, James and Velma, in his truck to the train depot at Okolona. There they met George and rode north through the night.

The family settled in Chicago. One account says they stopped first in Milwaukee, where Ida Mae's sister Irene lived. In Chicago, George hauled ice up four and five flights of stairs. Ida Mae did housework for other people and then found work as a hospital aide. In time the family bought a house in Chicago. Ida Mae died in 2002. The writer Isabel Wilkerson told Ida Mae's life, from interviews with her, in the 2010 book *The Warmth of Other Suns*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-mae-brandon-gladney" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dust Bowl move" -->
In the 1930s, drought and windstorms tore the topsoil off farms across the southern Great Plains. A drought is a long stretch of time with too little rain. The worst-hit land, in parts of Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Colorado and New Mexico, was called the Dust Bowl.

From about 1935 to 1940, more than 250,000 people left the Dust Bowl states for California. Another count covers only Oklahoma but every destination. That count is everyone who left Oklahoma for any place, minus the people who moved in. By that count Oklahoma may have lost as many as 440,000 people. Many of the travelers drove west on U.S. Route 66. In California the newcomers were called "Okies."

In February 1936 the Los Angeles police chief, James E. Davis, sent his officers to the state line to turn back poor migrants. People called it the "bum blockade." From February 3, 136 Los Angeles officers stood at 16 crossing points. Those points were on California's roughly 700-mile border with Arizona, Nevada and Oregon, far outside the city. Davis pointed to a 1933 state law against bringing poor people into California. His officers stopped trains, cars and people hitchhiking. They took people's fingerprints and turned back anyone they judged unable to support themselves.

On February 8 alone, the officers arrested 122 people for vagrancy, the crime of having no home or means of support. Some of the people arrested were put to work quarrying rock without pay. Quarrying means cutting and breaking stone out of the ground.

Railroad executives and some county sheriffs backed the blockade. So did the people who ran the *Los Angeles Times* and the Los Angeles Chamber of Commerce, a group of the city's business owners. California's attorney general, the state's top lawyer, Ulysses S. Webb, said Davis had "no jurisdiction beyond the city's territorial limits." That meant he had no power outside Los Angeles. Arizona's governor, Benjamin Moeur, called the blockade illegal and unconstitutional, which means against the Constitution. Davis called his officers home in early April 1936. He claimed they had turned back about 11,000 people. Historians think the real number was much lower.

### Florence Owens Thompson

> **Who:** A farmworker born in what is now Oklahoma, whose parents said they were of Cherokee descent, who moved from crop to crop in California. She is the woman in Dorothea Lange's 1936 photograph "Migrant Mother."
> **When and where:** Born in Indian Territory in 1903. Moved to California in the 1920s. Photographed at a pea-pickers' camp in Nipomo, California, in March 1936.

Florence Owens Thompson moved to California in the 1920s, years before the Dust Bowl, and worked in its fields as a farm laborer. She was born Florence Leona Christie on September 1, 1903, in Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma. Both of her parents said they were of Cherokee descent.

In 1921 she married Cleo Owens. During the 1920s they moved west with several of his relatives to Oroville, California. Cleo died in 1931. In about 1933 she and her parents moved to Shafter, California. She followed the crops from one harvest to the next.

On March 6, 1936, the timing chain in the family's car snapped. The timing chain is an engine part, and the car could not run without it. The family stopped at a camp of pea pickers near Nipomo. Freezing rain had destroyed the pea crop there. The photographer Dorothea Lange took seven pictures of Thompson in about ten minutes. One of them became known as "Migrant Mother," and it became one of the most reproduced pictures of the 1930s.

Thompson's name was not made public until 1978. That year Emmett Corrigan, a reporter for the *Modesto Bee*, found her. She died on September 16, 1983.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="florence-owens-thompson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The forced removal of Japanese Americans, 1942" -->
In the spring and summer of 1942, United States Army officers forced more than 110,000 Japanese Americans out of their homes on the West Coast. On February 19, 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. An executive order is a rule issued by a president. Under this one, military commanders could keep "any or all persons" out of areas they named. General John L. DeWitt, who commanded the Army on the West Coast, then issued orders aimed at "all persons of Japanese ancestry," meaning everyone whose family came from Japan. United States citizens and people who were not citizens were ordered out alike.

On March 11, DeWitt set up an Army office to carry out the removal. Karl Bendetsen, an Army officer, commanded it. The next day, at 34, he was made a colonel. Bendetsen's staff divided the coast into 108 areas of about 1,000 Japanese Americans each. They issued a separate order for each area. Under each order, the people in that area had to report on a set day, about a week later. They could bring only what they could carry. Under the first order, issued on March 24, the 55 Japanese American families on Bainbridge Island, near Seattle, had six days to leave their homes. Army officers carried out the 108 orders and forced out 110,442 people.

Army engineers built 15 temporary camps, many of them at fairgrounds and racetracks. When people arrived, soldiers searched their bags. At the Tanforan camp in California, half of the people lived in horse stables, three to six people in a stall built for one horse. After about three months, on average, officials moved them again. This time they went to 10 larger camps run by a federal office called the War Relocation Authority. The National Archives counts about 120,000 people removed in all, a larger total than the count for the 108 orders alone.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### George Swanson Starling

> **Who:** A Florida fruit picker who organized the other pickers to ask for better pay, then fled north when he learned that the grove owners were plotting to hang him.
> **When and where:** From Wildwood, Florida, to Harlem in New York City, by train, April 14, 1945.

George Swanson Starling fled Florida on April 14, 1945, because the owners of the citrus groves where he worked were plotting to kill him.

Starling grew up in Eustis, in Lake County, Florida. He finished first in his high school class and went to Florida Agricultural and Mechanical State College in Tallahassee. In the middle of his second year, his father told him he had had enough schooling and must go to work. Starling went to work picking oranges and other citrus fruit. The pickers climbed trees as tall as 40 feet. Some fell and broke arms or legs.

Starling organized the pickers to ask for about 10 cents more a box. In one dispute the packing house offered ten cents a box for picking tangerines, and the pickers asked for twenty. In 1944 tangerines sold at auction for an average of $4.40 a box.

The grove owners were angry. Someone overheard them planning to take Starling and two other pickers out to the cypress swamps at Blackwater Creek and hang them. Starling left within days. A man named Roscoe Colton drove him in his truck to the train station in Wildwood. There Starling boarded the Silver Meteor, a train to New York. He rode in the Jim Crow car, the car set aside for Black passengers, where the railroad also stored luggage. The ride took 23 hours. His wife, Inez, stayed behind until he could send for her.

Starling settled in Harlem, where an aunt and many of his friends lived. He later got a steady job working on the same train that had carried him north. Isabel Wilkerson's book *The Warmth of Other Suns* also describes his life. The grove owners who planned to kill him are not named in the published accounts.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Large numbers of Americans moved from the North to the South and the West between 1950 and 2000. Between 1970 and 1990 the population of the South grew by about 36 percent, and the population of the West by about 51 percent. In the second wave of the Great Migration, many Black Southerners moved to cities on the West Coast. Federal officials paid for Native people to move from reservations to cities. Reservations are lands set aside for Native nations.

By the late 1990s more Black Americans were moving to the South than were leaving it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The second wave of the Great Migration" -->
The second wave of the Great Migration lasted from about 1940 to 1970, and it was larger than the first. More of its travelers went west, to Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle and Portland. They went for factory jobs, first during World War II and then after it. These travelers are counted in the same total of about 6 million people who left the South between 1910 and 1970.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Robert Joseph Pershing Foster

> **Who:** A Black surgeon who could not work in the hospital of his own home town in Louisiana. He drove alone to California and opened his own medical practice in Los Angeles.
> **When and where:** From Monroe, Louisiana, to Los Angeles, California, by car, April 1953.

Robert Joseph Pershing Foster drove from Louisiana to California in 1953 because he could not work as a surgeon at home. He had served as a surgeon in the Army. When he came back to Monroe, Louisiana, he found that he could not work in the hospital in his own home town. The people who ran St. Francis Hospital there would not let Black doctors do even a simple operation to take out a patient's tonsils. Isabel Wilkerson traced his trip in *The Warmth of Other Suns*.

Foster set out alone on Easter Monday, April 6, 1953, in his 1949 Buick Roadmaster, a dark red car with white-walled tires. He planned to send for his family once he was settled. Once he was long past Texas, he thought he was a safe distance from the South. In Arizona, motel clerks turned him away, even under "vacancy" signs, which tell drivers that rooms are free. One clerk told him they had forgotten to turn off the sign. Others said the rooms had just been rented. He could not find a place to stay the night, and he drove on until he reached California. Alone in the dark desert, he asked himself what he was doing. If he turned back, Wilkerson writes, "the I-knew-its would ring in his ear." He meant the people back home who would say they had known he would fail.

In Los Angeles, Foster opened his own medical practice. Many of his patients were migrants from the South, as he was. One of them was the musician Ray Charles.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-joseph-pershing-foster" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The move to the Sun Belt" -->
After World War II, many Americans moved from the older factory states of the North to the South and the West. People later nicknamed the Northern factory states the "Rust Belt" and the South and the West the "Sun Belt." People moved for jobs, for warmer weather and for cheaper land. Air conditioning made hot places comfortable all year, and that helped the Sun Belt grow.

Between 1970 and 1990 the South grew by about 36 percent and the West by about 51 percent. By Census Bureau counts, the whole country grew by about 22 percent in those years, from about 203 million people to about 249 million. In 1962 California passed New York as the state with the most people. It had about 17 million people then, roughly three times as many as before World War II.

In 1956 members of Congress passed the Federal-Aid Highway Act. Under it, work began on the Interstate Highway System, a network of big highways across the country. The interstates made long moves by car easier. They also made it easier to live in a suburb, a town at the edge of a city. With the interstates, people moved to the Sun Belt and to the suburbs faster.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal officials move Native families to cities" -->
From about 1950 into the 1970s, officials of the Bureau of Indian Affairs paid for Native people to move from reservations to cities. The Bureau of Indian Affairs is the federal office in charge of the government's dealings with Native nations. Its officials started the program, called Relocation, between about 1948 and 1952. In 1956 members of Congress backed it with the Indian Relocation Act.

The officials gave people one-way tickets to a city and help for a short time after they arrived. About 30,000 Native people moved to cities in the 1950s. By the time the program ended in the 1970s, more than 100,000 had moved. They went to Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland and San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver and other cities. Many of them lived in poverty there. They also built new Native communities in those cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Migration turns around" -->
In the 1970s a small number of Black Americans began moving back to the South. The number grew through the 1990s. By the late 1990s the South gained more Black migrants than it lost to each of the other three regions of the country, the Northeast, the Midwest and the West. For 35 years before that, more Black Americans had left the South than moved to it. William H. Frey, a researcher at the Brookings Institution, a research group, calls this return the New Great Migration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Hurricane Katrina drove more than 1 million people out of the New Orleans area and the Gulf Coast in 2005. It was the largest sudden move of people in these years. After Hurricane María hit Puerto Rico in 2017, about 130,000 people left the island for the mainland within a year.

The steadier flow toward the South went on through these years. The Census Bureau is the federal office that counts the population. By its estimates, from 2020 to 2024 the South gained about 2.7 million more people from other states than it lost to them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The pull of the South" -->
Census Bureau staff count moves between states as net domestic migration. That is the number of people who move into a state from other states, minus the number who move out to other states. By this measure the South gained about 2.7 million people from 2020 to 2024.

Many of the people who moved between states left coastal states where homes cost a lot. From April 2020 to July 2022, California lost about 343,000 people to other states, and New York lost about 300,000, by Census Bureau counts. A metro area is a city together with its suburbs. The metro areas that lost the most people included New York, Los Angeles, San Francisco and Chicago. After 2020 some people could work from home. The Pew Research Center, a group that studies how Americans live and what they think, reported that this let some of them choose where to live by the cost of a home and the space it offered, rather than by where their job was.

Florida's gains from other states shrank after 2022. By Census Bureau counts, Florida had a net gain of about 311,000 people from other states in 2022 and about 184,000 in 2023. In the year from July 2024 to June 2025, Florida gained about 23,000. The Census Bureau released the 2025 figures in January 2026. In that same year North Carolina gained the most people from other states, about 84,000. Texas and South Carolina each gained about 67,000.

The National Association of Realtors, a group for people who sell houses, ranked the states from the same Census figures. In that ranking, nine of the fifteen states with the biggest gains in 2024 to 2025 were in the South. The Midwest also gained about 16,000 people from other states that year, its first such gain this decade.

The New Great Migration of Black Americans to the South went on. William Frey of the Brookings Institution found that the Black population of metro Atlanta grew about five times over from 1970 to 2020. By 2010 it had passed Chicago's to rank second in the country. From 2000 to 2018, Atlanta was the most common destination of young Black adults aged 16 to 26 who moved. Seven of their ten most common destinations were in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaving New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina, 2005" -->
In late August 2005, Hurricane Katrina struck the Gulf Coast, and the levees around New Orleans broke. A levee is a wall of earth or concrete built to hold back water. The storm and the broken levees forced more than 1 million people from their homes in the New Orleans area and along the Gulf Coast. A month later, up to 600,000 households were still away from home. These counts come from The Data Center, a research group in New Orleans, and from the federal housing department.

Many people never came back. New Orleans had 484,674 people at the 2000 census. By July 2006 an estimated 230,172 people lived there, fewer than half as many, The Data Center reported. In 2024 the city had about 363,000 people, by an estimate reported by the news site The Center Square in 2025. That was about 120,000 fewer than in 2000. In July 2025 the seven parishes of the New Orleans metro area had 970,849 people, 3.6 percent fewer than in April 2020, The Data Center found. In Louisiana a parish is what other states call a county.

Some of the people who tried to walk out of the flooded city were stopped by police. On September 1, 2005, officers of the Gretna police, the Crescent City Connection bridge police and the Jefferson Parish sheriff's office blocked the Crescent City Connection bridge over the Mississippi River. They turned back people on foot at gunpoint. Two paramedics, medical workers who ride ambulances, were in the crowd. They came from San Francisco, and they reported that police "fired their weapons above their heads."

Gretna Police Chief Arthur Lawson made the decision together with officials of the sheriff's office and the bridge police. Gretna's mayor, Ronnie Harris, defended it. He said the town was not a shelter or an evacuation route and had no running water, no electricity and no food. Members of the Gretna City Council voted for a resolution supporting the blockade. It lasted until Labor Day, September 5, 2005. No count survives of how many people the officers turned back.

Evacuees are people who leave a place to escape danger. By Houston officials' own estimates, reported by the *Texas Tribune* in 2010, about 250,000 evacuees came there at the peak, and about 150,000 were still there a year later. By 2010 Houston's mayor, Annise Parker, said city officials no longer counted them apart from other residents: "They are Houstonians."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Kimberly Rivers Roberts

> **Movie:** *Trouble the Water* (2008), a documentary about Kimberly and Scott Roberts, directed by Tia Lessin and Carl Deal and built largely on her own video. It won the Grand Jury Prize for documentary at the 2008 Sundance Film Festival and was nominated for an Academy Award.
> **Who:** A 24-year-old aspiring rap singer from the Lower Ninth Ward of New Orleans who filmed the Katrina flood from inside it, with her husband, Scott Roberts.
> **When and where:** New Orleans, August 2005. Then Alexandria and northern Louisiana, then Memphis, Tennessee, then home to New Orleans.

Kimberly Rivers Roberts and her husband, Scott Roberts, were still in their New Orleans neighborhood when Katrina's flood came, because someone had recently stolen their car. "If I had wheels I'd be gone too," she told a neighbor. On August 28, 2005, she began filming with a camcorder she had bought for $20.

As the water rose, the couple and their neighbors climbed into an attic. They called 911 and heard that police would not come until conditions got better. A neighbor floated people to safety on a punching bag.

Afterward the group tried to take shelter at a Navy base that stood nearly empty. The couple's film shows men at the gate turning them away at gunpoint. A voice says, "Get off our property, or we're going to start shooting." Navy officials deny that this happened. No published account names the men at the gate.

Kimberly's grandmother died in a hospital whose patients were never moved out. Her uncle was later found dead in his home. The accounts do not state the cause of either death.

The couple went to a Red Cross shelter in Alexandria, Louisiana, then to her uncle's trailer in northern Louisiana. Next they moved to Memphis, Tennessee, to stay with one of Kimberly's cousins and try to start over. Officials of FEMA, the federal office for disaster help, had promised them a check. It did not arrive, and they had a hard time settling in a new city.

Kimberly and Scott went back to their home in the Ninth Ward and set out to rebuild it. Scott got a job with a builder repairing houses that the flood had ruined. Kimberly started her own record label, Born Hustler Records.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kimberly-rivers-roberts" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaving Puerto Rico after Hurricane María, 2017" -->
Hurricane María hit Puerto Rico in September 2017, and within a year about 130,000 people had left the island for the mainland. That was almost 4 percent of the people of Puerto Rico. The count is the Census Bureau's estimate for July 2017 to July 2018. Researchers writing in the science journal PNAS in 2020 described other counts, made from air travel records, phone records and social media data. Those estimates run from about 160,000 to 470,000, depending on which data they used.

Puerto Rico is a territory of the United States, land that belongs to the country but is not a state. Puerto Ricans are United States citizens, so a move from the island to the mainland is a move within the country. By Census Bureau figures, about a third of the people who moved from Puerto Rico to the mainland in 2018 went to Florida.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
