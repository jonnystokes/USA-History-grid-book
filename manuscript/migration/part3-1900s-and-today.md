## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 11 minutes**

About 6 million Black Americans left the South between about 1910 and 1970 to get away from lynch mobs, crowds that kill a person without any trial, and from Jim Crow laws, and most of the early travelers went north by train. Plains farm families moved too. In the late 1930s more than 250,000 people left the dried-out farms of the southern Plains for California.

**Forced out.** United States Army officers ordered Japanese Americans out of their homes on the West Coast in 1942. Soldiers and officials forced them into camps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Great Migration, the first wave" -->
The movement of Black Americans out of the rural South from about 1910 to 1970 is called the Great Migration. The first wave, from about 1910 to 1940, went mostly to cities in the North and the Midwest. Most people rode the trains north, and later travelers also went by car and by bus.

**The cities.** Between 1910 and 1920, Chicago's Black population grew to about two and a half times its size, and Detroit's grew to about seven times. New York's and Philadelphia's grew by more than half, and many newcomers also went to Pittsburgh.

The growth went on in the 1920s. New York's Black population more than doubled in those ten years, from about 152,000 to about 328,000. About 90 percent of Black Americans lived in the South in 1900. By 1970 fewer than 55 percent did.

**Why they left.** Georgia is one example. Black Georgians had several reasons to leave. The boll weevil, a beetle that eats cotton plants, and floods ruined the cotton crop in 1915 and 1916. Lynch mobs killed more than 450 people in Georgia between 1880 and 1930, most of them Black.

Under Jim Crow laws, the Southern laws that kept Black people and white people apart, nearly all Black Georgians could not vote. About 74,000 Black Georgians left between 1910 and 1920, and about 260,000 more left in the 1920s.

**The Chicago Defender.** Black Southerners from across the South went north for jobs, and some people in the North urged them to come. Robert S. Abbott started a Black newspaper, the *Chicago Defender*, in 1905. In its pages, job listings and train schedules ran beside editorials, articles that give a newspaper's opinion, and the editorials urged Black Southerners to come north. The *Defender* listed May 15, 1917, as the date for a "Great Northern Drive." Some Northern employers paid migrants' train fares and then took the money back out of their wages.

White officials in the South worked to keep Black workers from leaving. Officials in Macon, Georgia, charged Northern labor recruiters, people hired to find workers, a license fee of $25,000 for permission to work there.

**Chicago, 1919.** Black people in the North also met violence. Eugene Williams, who was 17, and four friends drifted on a homemade raft in Lake Michigan in Chicago on July 27, 1919. They crossed an unmarked line into the water off a beach at 29th Street where only white people were allowed. George Stauber, a 24-year-old white man, threw rocks at them until Williams drowned. A white police officer, Daniel Callahan, refused to arrest Stauber and stopped a Black officer from arresting him.

Five days of attacks followed, through August 3. The white attackers included youth gangs and groups that called themselves athletic clubs. Thirty-eight people were killed, 23 Black and 15 white, and the histories of the riot do not say who killed each of them. About 537 people were injured, two-thirds of them Black. Hundreds of homes were burned or damaged. Of the 138 people charged with crimes afterward, two-thirds were Black.

### Ida Mae Brandon Gladney

> **Who:** A sharecropper's wife who left Mississippi with her family after white men nearly beat a cousin to death.
> **When and where:** From Chickasaw County, Mississippi, by train, late October 1937. Settled in Chicago.

**The turkeys turned up the next day, and no one apologized.**

Ida Mae Brandon Gladney and her husband, George Gladney, were sharecroppers in Chickasaw County, Mississippi, farming another man's land for part of the crop. They grew cotton on the plantation of Edd Pearson, whom they called Mr. Edd.

In 1937 a group of white men beat a cousin of theirs, Joe Lee, nearly to death with heavy chains. The men had falsely accused him of stealing turkeys. The accounts of the beating do not name them. After the beating, George came home and told Ida Mae, "This is the last crop that we're making."

**The train north.** The cotton was picked by late October 1937, and George settled with Mr. Edd over what the year's work was worth. None of the family had ever been on a train or out of Mississippi. A brother-in-law drove Ida Mae and the two children, James and Velma, in his truck to the train station at Okolona. There they met George and rode north through the night.

The family settled in Chicago. One account says they stopped first in Milwaukee, where Ida Mae's sister Irene lived. In Chicago, George hauled ice up four and five flights of stairs, and Ida Mae did housework for other people and then found work as a hospital aide. In time the family bought a house. Ida Mae died in 2002. The writer Isabel Wilkerson told Ida Mae's life, from interviews with her, in the 2010 book *The Warmth of Other Suns*.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="ida-mae-brandon-gladney" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Dust Bowl move" -->
Drought, years with too little rain, and windstorms tore the topsoil off farms across the southern Great Plains in the 1930s. The Dust Bowl was the name for those parts of Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Colorado and New Mexico.

More than 250,000 people left the Dust Bowl states for California from about 1935 to 1940. Another count covers only Oklahoma but every destination: everyone who left Oklahoma for any place, minus the people who moved in. By that count Oklahoma may have lost as many as 440,000 people. Many of the travelers went west on U.S. Route 66, and in California the newcomers were called "Okies."

**The "bum blockade."** The Los Angeles police chief, James E. Davis, sent his officers to the state line in February 1936 to turn back poor migrants. People called it the "bum blockade."

Davis's officers, 136 of them, stood at 16 crossing points from February 3 on California's roughly 700-mile border with Arizona, Nevada and Oregon, far outside the city. Davis pointed to a 1933 state law against bringing poor people into California. His officers stopped trains, cars and people hitchhiking. They took people's fingerprints and turned back anyone they judged unable to support themselves.

The officers arrested 122 people for vagrancy, the crime of having no home or means of support, on February 8 alone. A week into the blockade, Davis announced that he would force the people arrested to work without pay in quarries, digging rock, sand and gravel out of the ground for public projects. Many of the people arrested were locked up in prisons far from the state line.

**For and against.** Railroad executives and some county sheriffs backed the blockade. So did the people who ran the *Los Angeles Times* and the Los Angeles Chamber of Commerce, a group of the city's business owners. California's attorney general, the state's top lawyer, Ulysses S. Webb, said Davis had "no jurisdiction beyond the city's territorial limits," meaning no power outside Los Angeles. Arizona's governor, Benjamin Moeur, called the blockade illegal and against the Constitution.

Davis called his officers home in early April 1936. He claimed they had turned back about 11,000 people. Writing in *Smithsonian* magazine in 2025, Olatunji Osho-Williams reports that the real number was probably much lower.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Florence Owens Thompson

> **Who:** A farmworker born in what is now Oklahoma, whose parents said their families were Cherokee, who moved from crop to crop in California. She is the woman in Dorothea Lange's 1936 photograph "Migrant Mother."
> **When and where:** Born in Indian Territory in 1903. Moved to California in the 1920s. Photographed at a pea-pickers' camp in Nipomo, California, in March 1936.

**Lange took seven pictures of her in about ten minutes.**

Florence Owens Thompson was born Florence Leona Christie in Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma, in 1903. Both of her parents said their families were Cherokee.

She married Cleo Owens in 1921, and during the 1920s, years before the Dust Bowl, they moved west with several of his relatives to Oroville, California. Cleo died in 1931. In about 1933 she and her parents moved to Shafter, California, and she worked in the fields as a farm laborer, following the crops from one harvest to the next.

**Nipomo.** The timing chain in the family's car, an engine part the car could not run without, snapped on March 6, 1936. The family stopped at a camp of pea pickers near Nipomo, where freezing rain had destroyed the pea crop. The photographer Dorothea Lange took seven pictures of Thompson in about ten minutes. One of them became known as "Migrant Mother," and it became one of the most reproduced pictures of the 1930s.

Thompson's name was not made public until 1978, when Emmett Corrigan, a reporter for the *Modesto Bee*, found her. She died on September 16, 1983.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="florence-owens-thompson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The forced removal of Japanese Americans, 1942" -->
United States Army officers forced more than 110,000 Japanese Americans out of their homes on the West Coast in 1942. The United States had been at war with Japan since Japanese forces attacked Pearl Harbor, in Hawaii, on December 7, 1941. President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, a rule issued by a president, on February 19, 1942. Under it, military commanders could keep "any or all persons" out of areas they named.

**The reason given.** Roosevelt gave his reason in the order's opening words. Winning the war, he stated, required "every possible protection" against spying and against damage done on purpose to war supplies and to buildings and services needed for the war. The order does not identify any group of people.

General John L. DeWitt commanded the Army on the West Coast. In March and April 1942 he issued orders aimed at one group, "all persons of Japanese ancestry," meaning everyone whose family came from Japan. United States citizens and people who were not citizens were ordered out alike.

**108 orders.** DeWitt set up an Army office on March 11 to carry out the removal. Karl Bendetsen, an Army officer, commanded it, and the next day, at 34, he was made a colonel. Bendetsen's staff divided the coast into 108 areas of about 1,000 Japanese Americans each and issued a separate order for each area.

Under each order, the people in that area had to report on a set day, about a week later, and they could bring only what they could carry. Under the first order, issued on March 24, the 55 Japanese American families on Bainbridge Island, near Seattle, had six days to leave their homes. Army officers carried out the 108 orders and forced out 110,442 people.

**The camps.** Army engineers built 15 temporary camps, many of them at fairgrounds and racetracks. When people arrived, soldiers searched their bags. At the Tanforan camp in California, half of the people lived in horse stables, three to six people in a stall built for one horse.

After about three months, on average, officials moved them again, to 10 larger camps run by officials of a federal office called the War Relocation Authority. Staff at the National Archives count about 120,000 people removed in all, a larger total than the count for the 108 orders alone.

**The end.** None of the people removed was charged with a crime or tried. Officials lifted the order that kept Japanese Americans off the West Coast on January 2, 1945, and they closed Tule Lake, the last camp, on March 20, 1946.

In 1983 the members of a federal commission that studied the removal found that the order "was not justified by military necessity," meaning there was no military need for it. They found that the causes of the removal were "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership." Under the Civil Liberties Act, which President Ronald Reagan signed in 1988, each surviving person who had been held received a written apology and $20,000.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### George Swanson Starling

> **Who:** A Florida fruit picker who organized the other pickers to ask for better pay, then fled north when he learned that the grove owners were plotting to hang him.
> **When and where:** From Wildwood, Florida, to Harlem in New York City, by train, April 14, 1945.

**For asking ten cents more a box, grove owners plotted to hang him.**

George Swanson Starling grew up in Eustis, in Lake County, Florida. He finished first in his high school class and went to Florida Agricultural and Mechanical State College in Tallahassee. In the middle of his second year, his father told him he had had enough schooling and must go to work.

**The groves.** Starling went to work picking citrus fruit. The pickers climbed trees as tall as 40 feet, and some fell and broke arms or legs. Starling organized the pickers to ask for better pay. In one dispute, the packing house, where the fruit was packed for sale, would pay the pickers ten cents for each box of tangerines. The pickers asked for twenty cents. At auction in 1944, tangerines sold for an average of $4.40 a box.

**The plot.** The grove owners were angry. Someone overheard them planning to take Starling and two other pickers out to the cypress swamps at Blackwater Creek and hang them. Starling left within days, on April 14, 1945. A man named Roscoe Colton drove him in his truck to the train station in Wildwood.

There Starling boarded the Silver Meteor, a train to New York. He rode in the Jim Crow car, the car set aside for Black passengers, where luggage was also stored. The ride took 23 hours. His wife, Inez, stayed behind until he could send for her.

Starling settled in Harlem, where an aunt and many of his friends lived. He later got a steady job working on the same train that had carried him north. Isabel Wilkerson's book *The Warmth of Other Suns* also describes his life. The accounts of Starling's life do not name the grove owners who planned to kill him.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 5 minutes**

Americans moved south and west after 1950, toward jobs, warmer weather and cheaper land. The population of the South grew by about 36 percent between 1970 and 1990, and the population of the West by about 51 percent. In the second wave of the Great Migration, many Black Southerners moved to cities on the West Coast. Federal officials paid for Native people to move from reservations to cities.

By the late 1990s more Black Americans were moving to the South than were leaving it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where the second wave of the Great Migration went" -->
The second wave of the Great Migration lasted from about 1940 to 1970, and it was larger than the first. More of its travelers went west, to Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle and Portland, for factory jobs, first during World War II and then after it. These travelers are counted in the same total of about 6 million people who left the South between 1910 and 1970.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="robert-joseph-pershing-foster" name="Robert Joseph Pershing Foster" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Robert Joseph Pershing Foster

> **Who:** A Black surgeon who could not work in the hospital of his own home town in Louisiana. He drove alone to California and opened his own medical practice in Los Angeles.
> **When and where:** From Monroe, Louisiana, to Los Angeles, California, by car, April 1953.

**Motel clerks in Arizona turned him away under "vacancy" signs.**

Robert Joseph Pershing Foster had served as a surgeon in the Army. Back home in Monroe, Louisiana, he found that the people who ran St. Francis Hospital there would not let Black doctors do even a simple operation to take out a patient's tonsils. Isabel Wilkerson traced his trip in *The Warmth of Other Suns*.

**The drive.** Foster set out alone on Easter Monday, April 6, 1953, in his 1949 Buick Roadmaster, a dark red car with white-walled tires. He planned to send for his family once he was settled. Once he was long past Texas, he thought he was a safe distance from the South.

In Arizona, motel clerks turned him away, a Black man traveling alone, even under "vacancy" signs, which tell drivers that rooms are free. One clerk told him they had forgotten to turn off the sign. Others said the rooms had just been rented. He could not find a place to stay the night, so he drove on until he reached California.

Alone in the dark desert, he asked himself what he was doing. If he turned back, Wilkerson writes, "the I-knew-its would ring in his ear," meaning the people who would say they had known he would fail.

In Los Angeles, Foster opened his own medical practice. Many of his patients were migrants from the South, as he was. One of them was the musician Ray Charles.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="robert-joseph-pershing-foster" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did Americans move to the Sun Belt?" -->
Many Americans moved from the older factory states of the North to the South and the West after World War II, for jobs, for warmer weather and for cheaper land. People later nicknamed the Northern factory states the "Rust Belt" and the South and the West the "Sun Belt." Air conditioning made hot places comfortable all year, which helped the Sun Belt grow.

The South grew by about 36 percent between 1970 and 1990, and the West by about 51 percent. By counts from the Census Bureau, the federal office that counts the population, the whole country grew by about 22 percent in those years, from about 203 million people to about 249 million. California passed New York as the state with the most people in 1962. It had about 17 million people then, roughly three times as many as before World War II.

**The interstates.** Members of Congress passed the Federal-Aid Highway Act in 1956. Under it, work began on the Interstate Highway System, a network of big highways across the country. The interstates made long moves by car easier, and they made it easier to live in a suburb, a town at the edge of a city.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal officials move Native families to cities" -->
Officials of the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the federal office in charge of the government's dealings with Native nations, paid for Native people to move from reservations to cities from about 1950 into the 1970s. They started the program, called Relocation, between about 1948 and 1952, and members of Congress backed it with the Indian Relocation Act in 1956.

**The aim.** Bureau officials opened relocation offices in Denver, Salt Lake City and Los Angeles in 1951 to help Native people from reservations find jobs in big cities. The National Archives describes Relocation as part of termination. Members of Congress declared that policy on August 1, 1953, to end the government's recognition of Native nations and the services that came with it. The federal officials behind termination aimed to make Native people live like most other Americans.

The officials promised people one-way tickets to a city and help for a short time after they arrived. About 30,000 Native people moved to cities in the 1950s, and more than 100,000 had moved by the time the program ended in the 1970s. They went to Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland and San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver and other cities. Many of them lived in poverty there. They also built new Native communities in those cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="When did the Great Migration turn around?" -->
A small number of Black Americans began moving back to the South in the 1970s, and the number grew through the 1990s. By the late 1990s the South gained more Black migrants than it lost to each of the other three regions of the country, the Northeast, the Midwest and the West. For 35 years before that, more Black Americans had left the South than moved to it. William H. Frey, a researcher at the Brookings Institution, a research group, calls this return the New Great Migration.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to Today · about 7 minutes**

Hurricane Katrina and the broken levees around New Orleans forced more than 1 million people from their homes on the Gulf Coast in 2005, the largest sudden move of these years. After Hurricane María hit Puerto Rico in 2017, about 130,000 people left the island for the mainland within a year.

**A steadier flow.** By Census Bureau estimates, the South gained about 2.7 million more people from other states than it lost to them from 2020 to 2024.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How many people moved south after 2020" -->
Census Bureau staff count moves between states as net domestic migration: the number of people who move into a state from other states, minus the number who move out to other states. By this measure the South gained about 2.7 million people from 2020 to 2024.

Many of the people who moved between states left coastal states where homes cost a lot. California lost about 343,000 people to other states from April 2020 to July 2022, and New York lost about 300,000, by Census Bureau counts. The metro areas, cities together with their suburbs, that lost the most people included New York, Los Angeles, San Francisco and Chicago.

**Working from home.** Some people could work from home after 2020. Researchers at the Pew Research Center, a group that studies how Americans live and what they think, reported that working from home let some people choose where to live by the cost of a home and the space it offered, not by where their job was.

**Florida slows down.** Florida's gains from other states shrank after 2022. By Census Bureau counts, Florida had a net gain of about 311,000 people from other states in 2022, about 184,000 in 2023, and about 23,000 in the year from July 2024 to June 2025. Census Bureau staff released the 2025 figures in January 2026. In that same year North Carolina gained the most people from other states, about 84,000, and Texas and South Carolina each gained about 67,000.

Researchers at the National Association of Realtors, a group for people who sell houses, ranked the states from the same Census figures. In that ranking, nine of the fifteen states with the biggest gains in 2024 to 2025 were in the South. The Midwest also gained about 16,000 people from other states that year, its first such gain of the 2020s.

**The New Great Migration.** The movement of Black Americans to the South went on. William Frey of the Brookings Institution found that the Black population of metro Atlanta grew about five times over from 1970 to 2020, and by 2010 it had passed Chicago's to rank second in the country. Atlanta was the most common destination of young Black adults aged 16 to 26 who moved between 2000 and 2018, and seven of their ten most common destinations were in the South.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaving New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina, 2005" -->
Hurricane Katrina struck the Gulf Coast in late August 2005, and the levees around New Orleans, walls of earth or concrete built to hold back water, broke. The storm and the broken levees forced more than 1 million people from their homes in the New Orleans area and along the Gulf Coast. A month later, the people of up to 600,000 homes were still away. These counts come from The Data Center, a research group in New Orleans, and from the federal housing department.

**Many never came back.** New Orleans had 484,674 people at the 2000 census. By July 2006 an estimated 230,172 people lived there, fewer than half as many, The Data Center reported. In 2024 the city had about 363,000 people, about 120,000 fewer than in 2000, by an estimate reported by the news site The Center Square in 2025.

The New Orleans metro area's seven parishes, the Louisiana name for counties, had 970,849 people in July 2025, 3.6 percent fewer than in April 2020, The Data Center found.

**The bridge.** Police stopped some of the people who tried to walk out of the flooded city. On September 1, 2005, officers from three police forces blocked the Crescent City Connection bridge over the Mississippi River: the Gretna police, the bridge police and the Jefferson Parish sheriff's office. They turned back people on foot at gunpoint. Two paramedics from San Francisco, medical workers who ride ambulances, were in the crowd, and they reported that police "fired their weapons above their heads."

Gretna Police Chief Arthur Lawson made the decision together with officials of the sheriff's office and the bridge police. Gretna's mayor, Ronnie Harris, defended it. He said the town was not a shelter or an evacuation route and had no running water, no electricity and no food. Members of the Gretna City Council voted to support the blockade, which lasted until Labor Day, September 5, 2005. No count survives of how many people the officers turned back.

**Houston.** About 250,000 evacuees, people who leave a place to escape danger, came to Houston at the peak, and about 150,000 were still there a year later. Those are Houston officials' own estimates, reported by the *Texas Tribune* in 2010. By 2010 Houston's mayor, Annise Parker, said city officials no longer counted them apart from other residents: "They are Houstonians."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="kimberly-rivers-roberts" name="Kimberly Rivers Roberts" movie="Trouble the Water (2008)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Kimberly Rivers Roberts

> **Who:** A woman of 24 from the Lower Ninth Ward of New Orleans who wanted to be a rap singer. With her husband, Scott Roberts, she filmed the Katrina flood from inside it.
> **When and where:** New Orleans, August 2005. Then Alexandria and northern Louisiana, then Memphis, Tennessee, then home to New Orleans.
> **Movie:** *Trouble the Water* (2008), a documentary about Kimberly and Scott Roberts, directed by Tia Lessin and Carl Deal and built largely on her own video. It won the Grand Jury Prize for documentary at the 2008 Sundance Film Festival and was nominated for an Academy Award.

**She filmed the Katrina flood on a $20 camcorder.**

Kimberly Rivers Roberts and her husband, Scott Roberts, were still in their New Orleans neighborhood when Katrina's flood came, because someone had recently stolen their car. "If I had wheels I'd be gone too," she told a neighbor. She began filming on August 28, 2005, two days before the storm reached the city, with a camcorder she had bought for $20.

As the water rose, the couple and their neighbors climbed into an attic. They called 911 and heard that police would not come until conditions got better. A neighbor floated people to safety on a punching bag.

**The Navy base.** Afterward the group tried to take shelter at a Navy base that stood nearly empty. The couple's film shows men at the gate turning them away at gunpoint, and a voice says, "Get off our property, or we're going to start shooting." Navy officials deny that this happened. The accounts of the film do not name the men at the gate.

Kimberly's grandmother died in a hospital that was not emptied of its patients, and one of her uncles, called Uncle Ned in the film, was later found dead in his home. The accounts do not say who decided to leave the hospital's patients there, and they do not state the cause of either death.

The couple went to a Red Cross shelter in Alexandria, Louisiana, then to the trailer of another uncle, in northern Louisiana. Next they moved to Memphis, Tennessee, to stay with one of Kimberly's cousins and try to start over. They waited for a promised check from FEMA, the federal office for disaster help, but it did not arrive, and they had a hard time settling in a new city.

**Home.** Kimberly and Scott went back to their home in the Ninth Ward and set out to rebuild it. Scott got a job with a builder repairing houses that the flood had ruined. Kimberly started her own record label, Born Hustler Records.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="kimberly-rivers-roberts" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Leaving Puerto Rico after Hurricane María, 2017" -->
About 130,000 people left Puerto Rico for the mainland within a year after Hurricane María hit the island in September 2017. That was almost 4 percent of the people of Puerto Rico. The count is the Census Bureau's estimate for July 2017 to July 2018.

Other counts run higher. Researchers writing in the science journal PNAS in 2020 described estimates made from air travel records, phone records and social media data. Those estimates run from about 160,000 to 470,000, depending on which data the researchers used.

Puerto Rico is a territory of the United States, land that belongs to the country but is not a state, and Puerto Ricans are United States citizens. A move from the island to the mainland is a move within the country. About a third of the people who moved from Puerto Rico to the mainland in 2018 went to Florida, by Census Bureau figures.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- How did horses reach the Native nations of the Plains? In the older account, horses spread north through trade after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, when the Spanish left perhaps more than 1,500 of them behind. A 2023 study of horse bones found that many nations had horses by the early 1600s, decades earlier, as the nations' own histories say.
- How many Cherokee died when soldiers forced them west? Nobody knows exactly. The count most often given is about 4,000, and estimates run from more than 1,000 on the journey itself to about 8,000 or more.
- Why did about 6 million Black Americans leave the South between 1910 and 1970? They left lynch mobs, Jim Crow laws and cotton crops ruined by the boll weevil and floods, and they went toward jobs in Northern and Western cities. In Georgia, one example, lynch mobs killed more than 450 people between 1880 and 1930, and nearly all Black Georgians could not vote.

**What changed:** Before 1900 most of the moves in this chapter went west onto Native land, many of them forced, and after 1900 millions of Black Southerners went north, until the flow began turning back toward the South in the 1970s.

**Five dates to keep**
- April 30, 1598: Oñate claims New Mexico for Spain in La Toma, "the taking."
- May 1838: US soldiers begin rounding up the Cherokee for the Trail of Tears.
- May 20, 1862: Lincoln signs the Homestead Act.
- February 19, 1942: Roosevelt signs Executive Order 9066.
- August 2005: Hurricane Katrina forces more than 1 million people from their homes.

**Not known:** Nobody knows for sure why most of the people of Cahokia had left their city by about 1350.

**Where to go:** The Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, run by the National Park Service, marks more than 2,200 miles of the removal routes across nine states.
