## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 5 of 37 · Migration Across America · 10 eras · about 75 minutes**

**Many of the biggest moves across this land were forced: soldiers marched whole nations west, slave traders marched more than a million people south, and about 6 million Black Southerners went north to get away from lynch mobs and Jim Crow laws, the laws that kept Black and white people apart.**

In about 1805, in Maryland, a slave trader from Georgia locked an iron collar around the neck of a man named Charles Ball. A padlock fastened the collar to a chain about 100 feet long. Fifty-one other people were locked to the same chain, and the trader handcuffed the men in pairs. He had just bought Ball away from his wife and children. The line of people walked south for more than a month, to Columbia, South Carolina.

This chapter follows people moving across the land that became the United States, from the first walkers out of Asia to the families who left New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina in 2005. Some went because they chose to: a church walking to the Connecticut River, wagon families on the Oregon Trail, homesteaders on the Great Plains. Others were driven out: the Acadians, the Tuscarora, the Cherokee, the Navajo, Japanese Americans in 1942. In each move, watch who decided that people would go, and whose land they went to. Before 1900 that land almost always belonged to a Native nation.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- How did horses reach the Native nations of the Plains?
- How many Cherokee died when soldiers forced them west?
- Why did about 6 million Black Americans leave the South between 1910 and 1970?

**Five minutes?** Read "Indian removal and the Trail of Tears" in 1800 to 1850.
**Can skip:** "Seasonal rounds and trade paths", "The cattle drives", "The frontier line disappears from the census, 1890". The chapter still makes sense without them.

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

People were moving across this land for thousands of years before any European saw it. The first people came on foot from Asia during the ice age, and their descendants kept moving. Some built towns and later left them. Some nations traveled south over a long time, and many families moved with the seasons to hunt, fish and gather food.

Hundreds of Native nations lived here. There were no horses and no wagons. Trails and trade paths crossed the continent, and later newcomers followed many of them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia and Chaco Canyon: towns people built and left" -->
Cahokia was the largest town north of Mexico. It stood on the Mississippi River, on the Illinois side, near where the city of St. Louis is today. People lived there from about the year 700 to about 1400. Around 1100, between 10,000 and 20,000 people lived in the city, about as many as lived in London at the time. Some estimates put as many as 50,000 people in the area around it.

Most of Cahokia's people had moved away by about 1350, and by the 1400s the town was nearly empty. Nobody knows for sure why they left. The main explanations are floods, a shortage of wood and food, and drought, a long stretch with little or no rain. No single cause has been settled.

Chaco Canyon, in what is now New Mexico, was a center of the Ancestral Puebloan people, the ancestors of today's Pueblo peoples. People started to leave the canyon around 1140, after a run of bad droughts, and most had gone by the end of the 1100s. They moved out and joined the Pueblo communities of the Southwest.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Navajo and Apache move south" -->
The ancestors of the Navajo and the Apache came from the far north, from what is now western Canada and eastern Alaska. The Navajo call themselves the Diné. Over a long time, groups of them moved south along the edge of the Rocky Mountains and the Great Plains toward the Southwest. Their long move is called the Athabascan migration.

When they arrived in the Southwest is disputed. Many accounts place their arrival between the 1400s and about 1525. Others argue that they came earlier, in the 1200s.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Seasonal rounds and trade paths" -->
Many peoples moved in a pattern called a seasonal round, a yearly circuit from one camp to the next. Over a year, a family moved between its fishing places, its hunting grounds and the spots where wild plants ripened.

Trade goods moved across the land as well. Traders passed turquoise, seashells, copper and obsidian, a black glass made by volcanoes, from nation to nation along trade paths. Some of these goods ended up hundreds of miles from where they were found.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 5 minutes**

Spanish colonists brought two things north in the 1500s that later changed how people moved across this land: horses, and families who came with wagons and herds to stay. The whole land from coast to coast was still Native land. Europeans reached its edges, but no crowds of settlers moved across the interior. The first colony that came over land to stay arrived at the very end of the century, when Juan de Oñate led Spanish colonists north into what is now New Mexico in 1598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why there were no horses here, and how they came back" -->
Horses lived in North America during the ice age and died out here thousands of years ago. Spanish sailors and colonists brought them back.

Christopher Columbus carried horses on his second voyage across the Atlantic, in 1493. Spanish expeditions, groups of men sent out to explore, then brought horses onto the mainland. Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's expedition had them from 1540 to 1542, and Juan de Oñate's colonists brought them to New Mexico in 1598. In the next century, horses changed how people lived on the Great Plains.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oñate's colony on the Camino Real, 1598" -->
Juan de Oñate and his colonists left Santa Bárbara in January 1598. It was a town in northern New Spain, the Spanish colony that included Mexico. Earlier Spanish expeditions had followed the Conchos River north. Oñate's column, the long line of people, wagons and animals, cut straight across the Chihuahuan Desert instead.

The column reached the Rio Grande in late April, at El Paso del Norte, near where El Paso, Texas, and Ciudad Juárez, Mexico, stand today. On April 30, 1598, Oñate claimed New Mexico for Spain in a ceremony called La Toma, which means "the taking." In July he set up the colony's headquarters at San Juan Pueblo, which its people call Ohkay Owingeh.

**Whose land.** The colonists moved onto the homeland of the Pueblo peoples. Families from Chaco Canyon had joined the Pueblo communities after the droughts of the 1100s, about 400 years earlier.

The road the colonists traveled was later called El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, the royal road of the interior. It ran from Mexico City to northern New Mexico, and Oñate's march made it more than 600 miles longer. Its stretch inside the United States is a National Historic Trail today.

**Two colonies by sea.** Before Oñate, two groups of Spanish colonists had come to what is now the United States by ship. Tristán de Luna y Arellano brought about 1,500 people in 11 ships to Pensacola Bay, in what is now Florida, in 1559. They were soldiers, colonists, enslaved people and Aztec allies.

A hurricane sank many of the ships a month later, and Spanish ships took the survivors back to Mexico in 1561. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine, also in Florida, with about 600 settlers in 1565.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="juan-de-onate" name="Juan de Oñate" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Juan de Oñate

> **Who:** The Spanish leader of the first known colonists to travel over land, with families, wagons and herds, into what is now the United States, and the man who ordered the attack on Acoma Pueblo.
> **When and where:** From Santa Bárbara in northern New Spain to San Juan Pueblo (Ohkay Owingeh), New Mexico, January to July 1598. Acoma Pueblo, December 1598 to February 1599.

**He sentenced each Acoma man over 25 to lose a foot.**

Oñate's column had about 400 men, about 130 of them with their families, and 83 wagons and carts. The colonists drove more than 7,000 head of livestock, or farm animals, with horses among them. The colony included soldiers, farmers, workers, servants and Franciscan friars, men who belong to a Catholic religious order. Enslaved people walked in the column too. The surviving records do not say how many.

**Acoma.** Oñate's nephew, Juan de Zaldívar, went to Acoma Pueblo in December 1598 with about thirty soldiers to demand food. In the Acoma people's own account, the soldiers also raped an Acoma woman, forcing her into sex. The Acoma killed Zaldívar and between eleven and fourteen of his men.

Oñate sent Zaldívar's brother, Vicente de Zaldívar, with about seventy soldiers in January 1599. Acoma stands on top of a mesa, a steep hill with a flat top. The soldiers climbed it, killed Acoma men, women and children, and burned much of the town.

Nobody counted the Acoma dead at the time. Estimates run from about 300 to about 1,500. The number given most often is about 800, which Pueblo leaders gave in a 2023 statement from the All Pueblo Council of Governors, the governors who lead the 20 Pueblo nations of New Mexico and Texas.

**The sentences.** The soldiers captured about 500 people. In February 1599 Oñate sentenced the men over 25 to have a foot cut off and to twenty years of forced work as slaves. He sentenced boys and men from 12 to 25, and girls and women over 12, to twenty years as slaves as well. He ordered the children under 12 taken from their families and given to the friars.

The Acoma's own account, passed down since 1599, gives the number of men whose feet were cut off as 24, and so do news reports. Reporters for AP and NPR, and Pueblo leaders, say it was the right foot. Two historians, Marc Simmons and John Kessell, doubt that the feet were ever cut off. Simmons had once written that they were.

A National Park Service page on the Camino Real states that about 70 Acoma girls were sent down the road to convents, homes for nuns, in Mexico. The same page states that probably none of the girls ever came home. Pueblo leaders count 60 children taken to Mexico, and they say none came home.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 5 minutes**

Native nations of the Plains and the Rocky Mountains got horses in this century, and that changed how they moved more than anything else did. People on horseback could travel farther and faster than on foot, and they could follow the buffalo herds.

Along the Atlantic coast, English, Dutch and other colonists settled at places like Jamestown, founded in 1607, and Plymouth, founded in 1620. Almost none of them went far inland. The Appalachian Mountains were hard to cross, and many Native nations lived on the far side of them. The few colonists who did move inland went short distances. Thomas Hooker's group walked from Massachusetts to the Connecticut River in about two weeks.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did horses spread to the Plains nations?" -->
Two accounts explain how horses spread, and the timing is still not settled. In the older account, horses passed north through trade after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. In the newer one, many nations already had horses by the early 1600s.

**The older account.** It starts with the Spanish governor of New Mexico, Juan Francisco Treviño. In 1675 he ordered the arrest of 47 Pueblo medicine men, who were religious leaders, on a charge of sorcery, or witchcraft. Spanish officials sentenced four of them to be hanged. They hanged three, and the fourth killed himself. Treviño's men whipped the others in public and put them in prison.

Pueblo people went to Santa Fe and demanded that the prisoners be let go, and the Spanish freed them. One of the freed men was Popé, a religious leader of the Tewa, one of the Pueblo peoples. In 1680 Popé led the Pueblo peoples of what is now New Mexico in a rising against Spanish rule. They killed about 400 Spaniards and drove the rest out of New Mexico for more than a decade.

The Spanish left behind large numbers of horses, perhaps more than 1,500, the largest number of horses to pass into Native hands at one time. In the older account, these horses then moved north and east through trade. The Ute, Navajo and Apache got them first, and then the Comanche, Kiowa and Shoshone. Over the next few decades horses reached the nations of the northern Plains, among them the Lakota, Crow, Cheyenne, Blackfoot and Nez Perce.

**The newer account.** It comes from a study published in the journal *Science* in 2023. The archaeologist William Taylor, a scientist who studies the past through the things people left behind, led it, working with Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee and other Native partners. The team tested horse bones from Kansas, New Mexico and Wyoming to find their age. Some dated from the 1500s and 1600s.

Marks on the teeth and bones showed that people had put bridles on these horses and fed them corn, so people were keeping and caring for them. The team found that many nations across the Plains and the Rocky Mountains had horses by the early 1600s, decades before the Pueblo Revolt. The finding matches what Native people have long said in their nations' own histories, passed down by word of mouth.

Over the next century, horses changed how the Comanche, Lakota, Cheyenne and the other Plains nations hunted and traveled.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first colonial moves inland" -->
Two English ministers moved out of the colony of Massachusetts Bay in 1636 and started new towns in what are now Connecticut and Rhode Island. Thomas Hooker chose to go. He led about 100 members of his church west to the Connecticut River.

**Roger Williams.** The other minister did not choose. The leaders of Massachusetts ordered Roger Williams out of the colony for what he preached: he taught that the government should not force religious beliefs on people. He fled in the winter rather than be sent back to England, and Wampanoag people helped him survive.

With help from Wampanoag and Narragansett people, Williams then traveled by canoe to a freshwater spring at a place called Moshassuck. He settled there with the permission of the Narragansett and named the place Providence. In 1638 two Narragansett sachems, or leaders, named Miantonomi and Canonicus signed a deed, a paper that says who owns a piece of land, for the settlement.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thomas-hooker" name="Thomas Hooker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thomas Hooker

> **Who:** A minister who led about 100 members of his church from Massachusetts to the Connecticut River, where they settled Hartford.
> **When and where:** From Newtown (now Cambridge), Massachusetts, to Hartford, Connecticut, May and June 1636.

**His church walked for two weeks, living partly on milk from 160 cows.**

Thomas Hooker was the pastor of the church at Newtown, the town that is now Cambridge, Massachusetts. He led about 100 members of his church west to the Connecticut River in May and June of 1636. The walk took about two weeks.

They drove 160 cattle with them and lived partly on the cows' milk. They followed Native trails, a route later called the Old Connecticut Path. John Winthrop, a leader of Massachusetts Bay, wrote about the departure in his journal on May 31, 1636: "Mr. Hooker, pastor of the church of Newtown, and most of his congregation (the people of his church), went to Connecticut." Winthrop also noted that Hooker's wife rode on a horse litter, a stretcher carried by horses.

**Whose land.** The place where they settled was Suckiaug, which means "black earth." It was the main village of the River Indians, and their sachem was Sequassen. In 1636 Sequassen and his people signed the land over by deed to Samuel Stone and William Goodwin, two men in Hooker's group. The land ran along the river between the towns of Wethersfield and Windsor, and six miles west of the river.

Nobody wrote the deed into the official land records at the time, and the settlers and the Native owners argued over its terms for generations. The histories of the deed do not say what Sequassen's people received for the land.

Dutch traders had built a fort there, the House of Hope, before Hooker's group came. The town the settlers built is Hartford. In 1639 the people of Hartford and the towns near it adopted a plan of government called the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 4 minutes**

Settler families began moving inland from the Atlantic coast in the first half of the 1700s, because land near the coast had filled up and grown expensive. They moved into the valleys and hills behind the older settlements, an area colonists called the backcountry. The main road inland was the Great Wagon Road.

Native nations were moving too. After a war with colonists in North Carolina, the Tuscarora walked north to New York. On the southern Plains, the Comanche moved in on horseback and took control, and many Apache there fled south. The country beyond the Appalachian Mountains was still Native land.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who traveled the Great Wagon Road?" -->
Many of the families on the Great Wagon Road were German or Scots-Irish. The road ran about 730 miles into the backcountry. It started in Philadelphia and went southwest through the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia. Then it ran down through the hill country of the Carolinas, called the Piedmont, toward Augusta, Georgia. Heavy traffic began in the 1720s.

Both groups had come to Pennsylvania and found land there too costly. The Scots-Irish were Protestants, Christians whose churches had split from the Catholic Church. Their families had moved from Scotland to the north of Ireland in the 1600s and then on to America. The Germans were called the "Pennsylvania Dutch," from *Deutsch*, the German word for German. Moravians, members of a Protestant church from central Europe, came along the road as well.

**An older path.** The road followed a Native path that colonists called the "Indian Road." When settlers began arriving in the Shenandoah Valley in about 1729, most of the people living near the present-day town of Winchester, Virginia, were Shawnee. Iroquois, Delaware and Conoy people also used the valley. The Iroquois were the Haudenosaunee, a league of six nations, also called the Six Nations. The valley's north-south trail was a war path between the Seneca and Delaware nations to the north and the Catawba nation to the south.

**The Treaty of Lancaster.** Officials sent by Virginia's governor, William Gooch, met Six Nations leaders at Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in 1744. In the treaty they signed there, a written agreement between nations, Gooch's officials bought the Six Nations' claim to the valley, meaning their right to the land. Colonists also got the right to use the Indian Road. Accounts of how much the officials paid do not agree. Even more settlers traveled the road after 1744.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did the Tuscarora walk north?" -->
The Tuscarora nation moved about 500 miles north, from North Carolina toward Pennsylvania and New York, after colonists defeated them in war. The move took from 1713 to 1722, and they traveled in stages.

**Neoheroka.** The war ended at Neoheroka, a Tuscarora fort on Contentnea Creek in what is now Greene County, North Carolina. Colonel James Moore of South Carolina led the attack. He commanded militia, colonists called up to fight as soldiers, from both Carolinas, and Native warriors who fought on the colonists' side.

Moore's men laid siege to the fort, which means they surrounded it and kept attacking it, from March 1, 1713. They dug under an outer wall and set the fort on fire. It fell on March 23.

History pages from North Carolina's state government list 950 Native people killed or captured at Neoheroka. They list about 1,000 Tuscarora sold into slavery during the whole war. The colonists and allies who won the war sold these captives, and those state pages do not name who bought them. The colonists' side also forced more than 3,000 other Native people from their homes. About 200 colonists and about 1,000 Native people died in the war.

About 1,500 Tuscarora survivors made the walk north. The Oneida nation sponsored them in the Haudenosaunee league, which means the Oneida backed their joining. In 1722 the Tuscarora became its sixth nation.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the Comanche took the southern Plains" -->
The Comanche had moved onto the southern Plains by the early 1700s, and they became the strongest power there. The land they held became known as Comanchería, Comanche country.

Apache groups were already living on the southern Plains. The Handbook of Texas, a history reference, states that by 1700 the Comanche, Wichita and Tejas Indians were "better armed through trade with the French." Those nations took control of the southern Plains, and the Apache began moving southwest. Many Apache, most of all the Lipan and the Mescalero, "fled southward into Central Texas as well as into northern Mexico."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
<!-- hb-time:end id="1700-1750" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 8 minutes**

Crowds of American settlers crossed the Appalachian Mountains for the first time in these fifty years, onto land that belonged to Native nations. As many as 300,000 went through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky and beyond between 1775 and about 1810. After the Revolutionary War ended in 1783, more settlers moved into the Ohio Valley. Members of Congress passed two laws, in 1785 and 1787, that set out how western land would be measured, sold and governed.

**The Acadians.** Farther north, British officers began forcing the French-speaking Acadians out of Nova Scotia, north of the British colonies, in 1755. They forced out about 10,000 to 11,500 people, and about 2,600 to 3,000 of them later made their way to Louisiana.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Acadians forced from Nova Scotia, 1755" -->
The Acadians were French-speaking Catholic farm families in Nova Scotia and the land around it, in what is now eastern Canada. Between 14,000 and 18,000 Acadians lived there in 1755. Nova Scotia was a British colony, and Charles Lawrence, its lieutenant governor, was one of the top British officials there.

**The decision.** Lawrence and his council of advisers in Halifax decided on July 28, 1755, to remove the Acadians from the colony. A navy admiral and the colony's top judge sat on the council that day. Some council members had recently come from New England and wanted the Acadians' farmland.

Lawrence had first asked Acadian delegates, men chosen to speak for their villages, to swear loyalty to Britain with no conditions. When they refused, Lawrence and his council had them jailed.

**Grand-Pré.** Lieutenant Colonel John Winslow commanded New England troops. On September 5, 1755, he ordered every Acadian male aged 10 and older at Grand-Pré to gather in the church there at 3 p.m., and he held them there. The order read to them said that their land, houses, cattle and other livestock now belonged to the Crown, meaning the British king. They could keep their money and household goods, and they would be removed from the colony.

Winslow wrote that the order was "very disagreeable to my nature," but that his job was "to obey such orders as I receive." Two other officers led arrests and removals in other districts.

**The plan.** Charles Morris, a New Englander, drew up the plan. Soldiers would surround the churches on a Sunday morning and capture as many men as they could. Then they would break the earth walls that kept the sea off the Acadians' fields, and burn their houses and crops.

New England and British soldiers did burn many Acadian houses, barns and churches that year. Around the Minas Basin, where Grand-Pré stands, they set fire to about 700 houses, barns and other buildings. Winslow kept his own list of what the soldiers under him burned there by November 1755: 255 houses, 276 barns, 155 smaller farm buildings, 11 mills and one Catholic church, 698 buildings in all.

**The ships.** When the men refused to go, the soldiers threatened their families with bayonets, blades fixed to the ends of their guns. By the fall of 1755, soldiers had put about 1,100 Acadians on ships bound for South Carolina, Georgia and Pennsylvania, the historian James H. Marsh writes. Winslow's own table counts the people shipped from the Minas district alone, to all places: 2,242 in all, 1,510 of them by his men and 732 by a second company.

British officers and soldiers kept removing Acadians until 1764. One count gives about 10,000 Acadians forced out between 1755 and 1763, and another gives about 11,500 between 1755 and 1764. British ships carried them to the thirteen colonies, to England and to France. Many Acadians escaped into the forests, and British soldiers hunted them for the next five years. In French the forced removal is called *Le Grand Dérangement*, the great upheaval.

**Known:** In one group of about 3,100 Acadians removed in 1758, about 1,649 died by drowning or disease.
**Disputed:** One count puts all the Acadian dead at close to 5,000, from disease, shipwreck or violence, and other counts put the dead at as many as half of those forced out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did the Acadians become Cajuns?" -->
About 2,600 to 3,000 Acadians made their way to Louisiana between 1764 and 1785, and their descendants are the Cajuns. The word Cajun comes from the word Acadian.

Louisiana was then a Spanish colony. The largest group came in 1785, when Spanish officials paid for seven ships that carried 1,596 Acadians from Nantes, in France, to Louisiana. The first of them reached New Orleans on July 29, 1785. The Acadians settled along the bayous, slow-moving streams through low, wet land, and on the prairies west of New Orleans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-broussard" name="Joseph Broussard (Beausoleil)" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Broussard (Beausoleil)

> **Who:** An Acadian who fought the British removal of his people, then led one of the first Acadian groups to Louisiana.
> **When and where:** Acadia (Nova Scotia and New Brunswick), 1755 to 1762. New Orleans and the Attakapas district of Louisiana, 1765. Born about 1702, died 1765.

**Freed from a British prison, he led about 200 Acadians to Louisiana.**

Joseph Broussard, whom people called Beausoleil, organized armed resistance when British officers began forcing the Acadians out in 1755. He and his men raided British troops. British soldiers captured him and held him at Halifax until 1763. That year Britain and France signed the Treaty of Paris, a peace agreement that ended their war.

**South.** Broussard and about 600 Acadians hired a ship in late 1764 to carry them to Saint-Domingue, the island colony that is now Haiti. He and about 193 to 200 of them sailed on to Louisiana in February 1765 and reached New Orleans late that month. He was one of the first Acadians to settle there.

Colonial officials named him a militia captain on April 8, 1765. They also made him the commander of the "Acadians of the Attakapas," the district around the present-day town of St. Martinville.

A sickness spread through the new settlement that year. Most accounts say it was yellow fever, and Broussard died of it in October 1765. Officials at Parks Canada, the Canadian government office that runs historic sites, have named him a National Historic Person, a title they give to people who were important in Canada's history.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-broussard" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Wilderness Road and the Cumberland Gap" -->
The Cumberland Gap is a low notch through the mountains near the place where Kentucky, Virginia and Tennessee meet. In 1775 Daniel Boone and about 30 axmen cut a road through it called the Wilderness Road. As many as 300,000 settlers traveled that road into Kentucky and beyond between 1775 and about 1810. Kentucky filled with settlers and became a state in 1792.

**The deed.** Boone cut the road for Richard Henderson's Transylvania Company, a business its partners formed to buy up what they called "wild lands" and open them to settlers. At Sycamore Shoals on the Watauga River, from March 14 to 17, 1775, Henderson got a deed from Cherokee leaders, among them Attakullakulla, Oconostota and Old Tassel.

The deed covered about 20 million acres between the Cumberland and Ohio rivers, most of Kentucky and part of Tennessee. In return the Cherokee received trade goods, which one estimate values at about 10,000 British pounds.

**Who objected.** Dragging Canoe, Attakullakulla's son, spoke against selling land that the Cherokee nation held in common, and he rejected the sale. Other nations had claims to the land too, among them the Shawnee, who had once lived on part of it. The Tennessee Encyclopedia describes the Cherokee claim as the strongest of these.

By buying the land, Henderson broke a British rule of 1763, the Royal Proclamation, which banned private buyers from buying Native land. Officials in Virginia and North Carolina and members of the Continental Congress, the meeting of leaders from the colonies, all refused to accept Henderson's colony as lawful.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Into the Ohio Valley, and a grid laid over the land" -->
Settlers moved in large numbers into the Ohio Valley and the Northwest Territory after the Revolutionary War ended in 1783. The Northwest Territory was the land north of the Ohio River that later became the states of Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan and Wisconsin. It was the land of the Shawnee, Miami, Delaware and other nations, and in the 1790s those nations and the settlers fought each other.

**The grid.** Members of Congress adopted the Land Ordinance, a law, on May 20, 1785. Under it, surveyors, workers who measure land, marked the western land into squares called townships, each six miles on a side, and divided each township into 36 sections of 640 acres. In the law, the members of Congress set aside one section in every township to pay for public schools. Because of that grid, much of the American countryside is laid out in squares today.

Two years later, on July 13, 1787, members of Congress passed the Northwest Ordinance. In it they wrote the rules for governing the territory, set out how parts of it could become new states equal to the original thirteen, and banned slavery north of the Ohio River. Five states later came from the territory.
