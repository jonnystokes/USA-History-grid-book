## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1803 the United States about doubled in size, and by 1850 its land reached the Pacific Ocean. Hundreds of thousands of people moved west. Families drove wagons over the Oregon, California and Mormon trails, and from 1848 to 1855 about 300,000 people rushed to California for gold.

Other journeys were forced. United States soldiers drove the Cherokee, Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw and Seminole nations from their homelands in the Southeast. Slave traders and enslavers forced more than a million enslaved Black people to move from the Upper South to the Deep South, in chains or in ships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
In 1803 the United States government bought about 827,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi River from France. The price was 15 million dollars. After the purchase, the country was about twice as big as before. All or part of 15 later states came from this land.

Native nations already lived on this land. After the purchase, American settlers began moving onto it, into the interior of the West.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The domestic slave trade: a forced march south" -->
Between about 1790 and 1860, slave traders and enslavers forced more than one million enslaved people to move. They took them from the Upper South to the Deep South. The Upper South meant Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas, and later Kentucky and Tennessee. The Deep South meant the cotton lands from Georgia west to Texas. From 1820 to 1860, about 200,000 people were moved this way every ten years. This trade inside the country was later called the "Second Middle Passage." The first Middle Passage was the voyage of captured Africans across the Atlantic Ocean.

Traders moved people south in two ways. Some went on foot in coffles. A coffle is a line of enslaved people chained together and marched overland. Others went by ship down the coast to New Orleans, which had the largest slave market in the country.

The largest trading firm was Franklin and Armfield, founded in February 1828 in Alexandria, Virginia. John Armfield bought people in Alexandria. Isaac Franklin sold them in Natchez, Mississippi, and after 1829 in New Orleans. Rice Ballard joined them in the early 1830s and ran their office in Richmond. Franklin, Armfield and Ballard bought, moved and sold more than 8,500 people. In their busiest years, the mid-1830s, the partners moved 1,000 to 1,500 people a year.

Near the end of each summer, the partners sent coffles "in chains over the mountains." Each coffle held from several dozen to several hundred people. They had to reach the Deep South for the fall selling season. The firm also owned three ships fitted to carry enslaved people, named the *Tribune*, the *Uncas* and the *Isaac Franklin*. The voyage from Virginia to Louisiana took about three weeks. At the busiest time, one of these ships left every two weeks. The last shipload left in November 1836.

Historians disagree about how many of these people were sold by traders and how many were moved by enslavers taking their own workers to new land. The historians Fogel and Engerman put the traders' share at about 16 percent. Jonathan Pritchett puts it at about half. Walter Johnson says about two-thirds went by sale and one-third moved with their enslavers. Michael Tadman puts the traders' share at 60 to 70 percent of moves from one state to another after 1808.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-ball-migration" name="Charles Ball" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Ball

> **Who:** An enslaved man who was chained into a coffle and marched from Maryland to South Carolina. He later escaped and told his life story.
> **When and where:** Sold in Maryland about 1805. Marched to Columbia, South Carolina. His story was printed in Philadelphia in 1837.

In about 1805 Charles Ball was sold away from his wife and children in Maryland. The man who bought him was a slave trader from Georgia. The trader chained Ball into a coffle of 52 people and marched them south.

Each person in the coffle wore a strong iron collar around the neck. A padlock on each collar was locked onto a link of one chain about 100 feet long. The men were also handcuffed in pairs. The coffle walked for more than a month, from Maryland to Columbia, South Carolina. Ball was then sold again, into Georgia.

Years later Ball escaped. He walked north back to Maryland, hiding by day and moving at night. He was caught and enslaved again, and he escaped a second time. He settled near Philadelphia.

Ball told his story to Isaac Fisher, a white abolitionist. An abolitionist was a person who worked to end slavery. Fisher wrote it down, and it came out in 1837 as *Slavery in the United States: A Narrative of the Life and Adventures of Charles Ball*. Because Fisher did the writing, the book gives Ball's story in Fisher's words. Many of the dates in Ball's life are known only roughly. He was born in about 1780 in Calvert County, Maryland.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-ball-migration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indian removal and the Trail of Tears" -->
In 1830 President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act. Jackson then directed the removal of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations from their homelands in the Southeast. Federal troops forced them to Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma.

Federal troops removed the Choctaw first, from 1831 to 1833. The first groups left in snow and river ice. Andrew K. Frank, writing for the Oklahoma Historical Society, counts more than 14,000 Choctaw removed and says hundreds died. Other counts give about 15,000 removed and about 2,500 dead.

In August and September of 1836, after a war with the Creek, United States troops marched the remaining Creek people west in five large groups. Soldiers put chains on Creek men taken prisoner in the war and marched them to Montgomery, Alabama. Their families followed in wagons. More than 14,500 Creek were removed. Several hundred died on the way. About 3,200 more died after they arrived, from disease, hunger and exposure. Exposure means harm to the body from cold and bad weather.

In October 1837 the steamboat *Monmouth*, carrying about 600 Creek people, was in a collision. About half of the Creek on board died.

More than 500 Chickasaw died of smallpox during their removal.

The Cherokee removal of 1838 and 1839 is called the Trail of Tears. In May 1838, United States Army troops under General Winfield Scott began rounding up Cherokee people who had not moved west. President Martin Van Buren had sent Scott with about 7,000 soldiers, and state militia, including Georgia's, joined them. Militia are part-time soldiers called up by a state. Soldiers took families from their homes and farms, often with no time to pack.

The soldiers held about 16,000 Cherokee in stockades in Tennessee and Alabama. A stockade is a pen with walls of tall logs. Soldiers sent three groups west by river in June 1838. After that, Scott agreed to let John Ross organize the rest. Ross was the Cherokee's Principal Chief, their top leader. Ross organized fourteen groups, which left between August and December 1838. The walk overland was about 800 miles. People died on the way from cold, hunger and disease.

The number of Cherokee dead is not settled. The National Park Service counts more than 1,000 deaths on the journey itself, and "perhaps several thousand" more who died later because they had been forced from their homes. The count most often given is about 4,000. History.com gives a figure above 5,000. In 1984 Russell Thornton, a scholar who studies population, estimated that about 8,000 or more Cherokee died because of the removal, twice the usual figure. Andrew K. Frank, writing for the Oklahoma Historical Society, says as many as one in four Cherokee died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** A Cherokee woman who, at about three years old, was forced west on the Trail of Tears. She told what she remembered when she was 97 or 98.
> **When and where:** Taken from Georgia in 1838. Interviewed in 1932 in what is now Oklahoma.

Rebecca Neugin was about three years old in 1838 when soldiers rounded up her family in Georgia. Her father was Tickaneesky, and her mother was Sallie Ketcher. The soldiers held the family in a camp in Tennessee.

In September 1838 the family started west with the group led by Mose Daniel and George Still. Rebecca and the smaller children rode in a wagon. Her mother, her father and her older brother Dick walked the whole way.

In 1932 the historian Grant Foreman interviewed her. She was 97 or 98 years old and one of the last living people who had made the journey. Her memory of it is one of the few first-hand accounts by a survivor. She died on July 15, 1932.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The overland trails" -->
Most of the western trails started from towns on the Missouri River, mainly Independence and St. Joseph, in Missouri.

The Santa Fe Trail opened first. In the fall of 1821, William Becknell led the first trading trip from Missouri to Santa Fe, which then belonged to Mexico. The Santa Fe Trail was mainly a road for traders hauling freight.

The Oregon Trail ran about 2,000 to 2,170 miles, from Independence, Missouri, to the Willamette Valley in Oregon. The trip took four to six months. On May 22, 1843, about 1,000 people set out with about 120 wagons and thousands of oxen and cattle. They reached Oregon that fall. This trip was called the "Great Migration" of 1843. After it, other families knew that wagon trains could make the whole journey. Hundreds of thousands followed over the next twenty years. They settled in a valley where at least twenty Native nations lived, among them the Kalapuya, Molala and Chinook peoples.

The California Trail split off from the Oregon Trail and turned southwest toward California. Traffic on it grew fast after gold was found in 1848.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Life and death on the wagon trails" -->
About one in ten people who set out on the overland trails died on the way. That was between 20,000 and 30,000 people. Most of them died of disease.

The main killers were cholera and typhoid. Cholera is a disease that causes severe diarrhea and vomiting. Typhoid is a disease that causes a high fever. Both spread through dirty water at crowded camps. Other emigrants died in accidents, or drowned while crossing rivers. An emigrant is a person who leaves one home to settle in another. Later stories often showed Native people attacking wagon trains. Most of the emigrants who died on the trails died of disease, not in attacks.

Families traveled in groups for safety. They traveled in covered wagons pulled by oxen.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Latter-day Saints move west" -->
Members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, also called Mormons, moved west after armed men attacked them in Missouri and in Illinois. On October 27, 1838, Missouri's governor, Lilburn Boggs, ordered them driven out of Missouri or, if necessary, in the order's own word, "exterminated." To exterminate people means to kill them all. Three days later, on October 30, about 200 armed Missourians led by Colonel Thomas Jennings attacked the Mormon settlement at Haun's Mill. They killed seventeen or eighteen men and boys. One of the boys, Sardius Smith, was ten years old. No one was ever tried for the killings.

That year there was violence on both sides in Missouri. In mid-October 1838, armed Mormon groups raided the towns of Gallatin and Millport and burned property there. Over the next year, about 8,000 church members left Missouri for Illinois, and they settled at Nauvoo.

On June 27, 1844, about 100 men from Illinois militia units broke into the jail at Carthage, Illinois. They killed the church's founder, Joseph Smith, and his brother Hyrum. Five men were tried for the killings, and all five were found not guilty. Vigilantes then attacked Mormon farms around Nauvoo. A vigilante is a person who uses force to punish others without any legal right to do so.

Brigham Young, a leader of the church, planned to move about 15,000 people west. On February 4, 1846, the first wagons crossed the Mississippi River into Iowa. By March 1 about 500 wagons were on the move. By 1869 more than 70,000 Latter-day Saints had traveled the Mormon Trail. Some of the poorer groups pulled handcarts, small two-wheeled carts that people pull by hand, instead of driving wagons.

The Salt Lake Valley lay between the lands of the Ute, to the south, and the Shoshone, to the north. A few Native people crossed the valley itself, but none had camps there. The Ute, Goshute, Shoshone, Paiute and Navajo peoples lived in what is now Utah, and their homelands had been there for thousands of years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="brigham-young" name="Brigham Young" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Brigham Young

> **Who:** The Latter-day Saint leader who led the first company of Mormon emigrants to the Salt Lake Valley.
> **When and where:** From Winter Quarters, in what is now Nebraska, to the Salt Lake Valley, in what is now Utah. Arrived July 1847.

Brigham Young led the first company of Latter-day Saints to the Salt Lake Valley in 1847. Before that, after the killing of Joseph Smith, Young had planned the move of about 15,000 people out of Nauvoo, Illinois, which began in February 1846.

The Saints called the 1847 group the vanguard company, which means the group that goes ahead of the rest. About 148 people traveled in it.

Counts of who was in the company differ. One count lists 143 men, 3 women and 2 children. Three of the men were enslaved Black men.

The company started from Winter Quarters, a camp in what is now Nebraska, and traveled about 1,300 miles. Its members reached the valley in July 1847. Young himself arrived on July 24.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="brigham-young" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New land from Mexico, and the Gold Rush" -->
In 1845 a journalist named John O'Sullivan wrote the phrase "Manifest Destiny" in the *Democratic Review*. It named an idea that many Americans held. They believed the United States was meant to spread across the continent to the Pacific Ocean. People used the idea to argue for moving west and for going to war with Mexico.

The Mexican-American War lasted from 1846 to 1848. It ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848. Mexican officials signed the treaty and gave up California, Nevada and Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming. This land is called the Mexican Cession. Texas had already been annexed in 1845, which means it was made part of the United States. In 1846 officials of the United States and Britain agreed on the border of Oregon.

In early 1848, gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California. The rush came in 1849. About 80,000 people, called forty-niners, arrived that year. From 1848 to 1855 about 300,000 people came to California. About half came by sea, and about half came over land on the California Trail.

California's population, not counting Native people, grew from about 14,000 in 1848 to about 250,000 in 1852. San Francisco grew from about 200 people in 1846 to about 36,000 in 1852.

The forty-niners arrived on the land of California's Native nations. About 150,000 Native people lived in California in 1848. By 1870 the count had fallen to about 30,000, a loss of about 120,000 people. The historian Benjamin Madley documents the causes: killings, kidnapping, forced labor, disease, hunger, and being driven off the land. Between December 1849 and May 1850, vigilantes and United States Army soldiers killed 1,000 or more Native people. On the last day of 1854, 116 volunteer militiamen attacked the Tolowa people and killed hundreds of them. One militiaman died. Madley documents that state and federal officials took part in the killings, and that taxpayers paid for militia campaigns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the second half of the 1800s, settlers moved onto the Great Plains and the far West, where Native nations lived. Under a law of 1862 they could claim free farmland, and settlers filed about 4 million claims under it. After 1869 a railroad could carry them across the country in about a week. Tens of thousands of Black Southerners also moved west, to Kansas, to get away from violence and cheating in the South.

As the settlers came, United States soldiers forced Native nations onto reservations. A reservation is an area of land set aside for a Native nation, often far from its homeland. In 1864 soldiers marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to one of them. By 1890 the government's count of the population no longer showed a clear line between settled land and thinly settled land in the West.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-stewart-knight" name="Amelia Stewart Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Stewart Knight

> **Who:** A wife and mother who kept a diary, day by day, of her family's wagon journey on the Oregon Trail.
> **When and where:** From Monroe County, Iowa, to the Willamette Valley near Milwaukie, Oregon Territory, April to September 1853.

Amelia Stewart Knight crossed the Oregon Trail in 1853 while she was pregnant, and she kept a diary of the trip. She traveled with her husband and their seven children. They left Monroe County, Iowa, in April.

Her diary records rain and mud, dust, sick children and hard river crossings. It also records the daily work of handling the wagons and the cattle.

The family reached Oregon on September 17, 1853. The next day, September 18, she gave birth to her eighth child, a son named Adam. Her diary does not mention that she was pregnant until the day the baby was born.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amelia-stewart-knight" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The handcart companies, 1856" -->
In 1856 more than 1,000 Latter-day Saint emigrants set out late in the season to walk to Salt Lake City pulling handcarts. A handcart is a small two-wheeled cart that people pull by hand. Many of them were trapped by snow in what is now Wyoming, and at least 170 of them died.

The emigrants traveled in two companies. The Willie company, about 500 people, left Florence, in Nebraska Territory, on August 17. The Martin company left on August 27.

An early blizzard struck on October 19, 1856. The Martin company had just waded across the North Platte River, near where Casper, Wyoming, is today. More than 300 rescuers came from Salt Lake with wagons. They brought the survivors in through November.

Counts of the dead differ. Modern counts give about 67 to 77 dead in the Willie company and about 103 to 150 in the Martin company. Older accounts say about 200 to 250 people died in the stranded companies and the wagon trains traveling with them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="levi-savage" name="Levi Savage" movie="17 Miracles (2011)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Levi Savage

> **Who:** A leader in the Willie handcart company who warned the emigrants not to leave so late, then stayed with them through the blizzards. His journal records what happened.
> **When and where:** From Florence, Nebraska Territory, to Salt Lake City, August to November 1856. Born 1820 in Ohio.
> **Movie:** *17 Miracles* (2011), a film directed by T. C. Christensen about the Willie handcart company. It draws on Savage's journal.

Levi Savage warned the Willie handcart company that leaving so late would bring snow and deaths. He was 36 and one of the company's sub-captains, the leaders under the captain.

At a camp meeting at Florence on August 13, 1856, Savage spoke to the roughly 500 emigrants. They still had more than 1,000 miles to go. He urged them to wait until spring. His advice was turned down, and he was scolded for speaking so freely.

Savage went with the company anyway. When the blizzards came, he worked through them to keep people alive. His journal survives, and most modern accounts of the Willie company rely on it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="levi-savage" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Weeping Time, 1859" -->
The domestic slave trade went on through the 1850s. The largest single sale took place on March 2 and 3, 1859, at the Ten Broeck Race Course near Savannah, Georgia. Pierce Mease Butler sold between 429 and 436 men, women and children to pay his gambling debts. The sale brought him $303,850. It rained hard on both days of the sale.

A broker is a person who arranges sales for others. The broker who ran this sale was Joseph Bryan, the largest slave dealer in Savannah. For weeks he advertised it in newspapers across the South, from Richmond to New Orleans. People were sold in family groups as far as the terms of the sale allowed. Even so, some husbands and wives were not bought together. The surviving accounts do not say where the buyers took the people they bought.

Mortimer Thomson, a reporter for the *New York Tribune*, came to the sale dressed as a buyer and wrote a long account of it. Black Georgians remembered the sale as "the weeping time."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Free land and the railroad" -->
President Abraham Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862. Under it, an adult who headed a household could claim up to 160 acres of public land for free. Much of that land had been taken from the Native nations who lived on it. After living on the land and farming it for five years, the settler owned it. Women could claim land. After the Civil War, freed people could claim land too, and so could immigrants who planned to become citizens.

Over the life of the law, settlers filed about 4 million claims. Settlers and other private owners got about 270 million acres this way. That was about 10 percent of all the land in the United States. Many settlers moved onto the Great Plains to claim land under the law.

The first railroad across the country was finished at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869. By wagon, a trip from coast to coast took four months or more. By train it took about a week. Under the Pacific Railroad Act, which Lincoln signed in 1862, the railroad companies got large grants of federal land. They sold that land to settlers and carried settlers and immigrants west by the trainload.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Long Walk of the Navajo, 1864" -->
From 1863 to 1866, United States soldiers forced more than 10,000 Navajo people from their homeland in what is now Arizona and New Mexico. General James Henry Carleton ordered Colonel Christopher "Kit" Carson to make the Navajo surrender. From August 1863, Carson's troops, guided by Ute scouts and warriors, burned Navajo fields, took their animals and destroyed their food. The troops took more than 5,000 sheep, goats and mules and destroyed more than 75,000 pounds of wheat and the Navajo peach orchards.

By March 1864, 6,000 Navajo had gathered at Fort Canby and Fort Wingate, starving. Soldiers then marched about 8,500 people more than 300 miles to the Bosque Redondo reservation at Fort Sumner, New Mexico. The walk took from 18 to 40 days. The soldiers gave them food planned to last 8 days. The Navajo call this march the Long Walk.

About 200 people died on the march, in winter. Navajo accounts passed down in families say soldiers shot people who could not keep up, among them old people, sick people and pregnant women. Soldiers held more than 8,000 Navajo and Mescalero Apache people at Bosque Redondo. Thousands more died there. On June 1, 1868, Navajo leaders signed a treaty with the United States, and under it the Navajo could go home. Through the 1870s and 1880s, soldiers forced many other nations onto reservations.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" name="Laura Ingalls Wilder" movie="Little House on the Prairie (NBC series, 1974-1983)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Laura Ingalls Wilder

> **Who:** The author of the Little House books. As a child she moved with her family by covered wagon again and again.
> **When and where:** Born February 7, 1867, near Pepin, Wisconsin. Family moves from 1869 to 1879. Her own last move, to Mansfield, Missouri, in 1894.
> **Movie:** *Little House on the Prairie* (NBC series, 1974 to 1983). It ran for nine seasons and 205 episodes, with Michael Landon as Charles Ingalls. It is loosely based on the books.

Laura Ingalls Wilder's family moved six times between 1869 and 1879. She was born near Pepin, Wisconsin, on February 7, 1867.

In 1869 the family moved to Montgomery County, Kansas, near the town of Independence. Their cabin stood on the Osage Diminished Reserve, land that still belonged to the Osage Nation. The family had no right to be there. They left in 1871 and went back to Pepin.

The family moved to Walnut Grove, Minnesota, in 1874, and swarms of grasshoppers ate their wheat crops there in 1875 and 1876. Next came Burr Oak, Iowa, where they helped run a hotel from 1876, then Walnut Grove again in 1877. In 1879 they settled at De Smet, in Dakota Territory. There Laura's father, Charles Ingalls, took a job at a railroad camp and then claimed a homestead.

Laura married Almanzo Wilder in 1885. In 1894, a year of drought, Laura, Almanzo and their daughter Rose drove a covered hack, a light wagon with a cover, about 650 miles from De Smet to Mansfield, Missouri. She kept a diary of the trip. It was published in 1962 as *On the Way Home*. In Mansfield she later wrote the Little House books.

The books came out from 1932 to 1943. In them, Wilder changed the real story. In *Little House on the Prairie*, Laura is several years older than she really was in Kansas, where she was 2 to 4 years old. The books leave out the Burr Oak years completely. They also do not say that the family was living on Osage land.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The cattle drives" -->
After the Civil War, cowboys drove herds of longhorn cattle north from Texas to Kansas. They took the cattle to railheads, the towns at the end of a railroad line, where the cattle were loaded onto trains. Abilene was one of these towns. The railroad reached Abilene in 1867, and after that the Chisholm Trail from Texas was used heavily.

About 5 million cattle or more were driven north from Texas between 1866 and 1884. Some estimates put the total for the whole era at about 6 million. In 1871 alone, about 700,000 cattle reached the railheads in Kansas.

The long drives ended in the mid-1880s for several reasons. Barbed wire fences went up across the open range. Railroads reached deeper into Texas, so the cattle no longer had to walk to Kansas. In 1885 and 1886 Kansas lawmakers passed a quarantine law to keep Texas cattle out of the state. A quarantine keeps animals or people apart to stop the spread of disease. Then the cold of the winter of 1886 and 1887 killed much of the cattle herd.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Exodusters, 1879" -->
In 1879 thousands of Black Southerners left their homes and moved to Kansas. They were called Exodusters, after the Exodus, the story in the Bible of the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt. Many came from Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas and Tennessee. As many as 40,000 moved to Kansas, Oklahoma and Colorado between about 1879 and 1881. About 6,000 reached Kansas in the spring of 1879 alone.

They left because white Southerners took away their votes, cheated them and terrorized them. Former Confederates took back control of the Southern states after the presidential election of 1876, and they took away Black Southerners' right to vote. Many Black Southerners were sharecroppers. A sharecropper farms someone else's land and pays with a share of the crop. Singleton and others said that white landowners cheated the Black sharecroppers who farmed their land. The historian Damani Davis calls what Black Southerners faced "relentless terror." In Louisiana, the Black organizer Henry Adams collected the names of nearly 700 Black people who had been attacked. For each one he listed where it happened and what was done. Most of them had been whipped, beaten, shot or killed by white men, often by armed groups. Some entries name the man. One entry says that a white man, Dr. Moore, killed a Black man named Calvin in 1871, after Calvin was accused of cursing a white woman.

Many Exodusters traveled up the Mississippi River by steamboat. Crowds of them waited on the docks at St. Louis. In 1880 members of a United States Senate committee looked into why they had left. The members who disagreed with the majority wrote their own report. It said the migrants had fled "the intolerable hardships, injustice, and suffering inflicted upon them by a class of Democrats in the South." The migrants saw Kansas as a safe place. John Brown, who fought to end slavery, had lived there. Before the Civil War, many Kansans had wanted it to be a free state, a state without slavery.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-pap-singleton" name="Benjamin 'Pap' Singleton" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin "Pap" Singleton

> **Who:** A man born into slavery who organized Black Southerners to move to Kansas. He called himself the "Father of the Exodus."
> **When and where:** Born enslaved in Nashville, Tennessee, about 1809. Led the move to Kansas in the late 1870s. Died 1900.

Benjamin Singleton helped thousands of Black Southerners move to Kansas in the 1870s. He called himself the "Father of the Exodus."

He was born into slavery in Nashville, Tennessee, in about 1809. He ran away several times. In the end he escaped to the North and to Canada. In about 1871, after the Civil War, he came back to Tennessee. He worked there as a carpenter, a cabinetmaker and an undertaker, a person who prepares the dead for burial.

Singleton was angry that white landowners cheated the Black sharecroppers who worked their land. He believed Black Southerners would never get a fair chance at land in the South. He printed handbills, short printed notices, to draw Black settlers to Kansas. He formed a group called the Edgefield Real Estate and Homestead Association to move people there.

Singleton testified that his work helped more than 7,400 people move. Between 1877 and 1879, his group steered more than 20,000 people toward Kansas. He died in 1900.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-pap-singleton" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Oklahoma Land Run, 1889" -->
At noon on April 22, 1889, about 50,000 people raced into central Oklahoma to claim land. The land they entered was called the Unassigned Lands. It covered about 1.9 million acres in the middle of Indian Territory. Nearly 2 million acres were claimed in a single day.

Indian Territory was the land in present-day Oklahoma where federal officials had moved many Native nations. The Unassigned Lands were the last part of it that had not been given to any of those nations. The land had been taken from Native nations. After the Civil War, federal officials pressed the Creek and Seminole nations to give up land. Dennis N. Cooley, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, was the federal official in charge of dealings with Native nations. In 1865 he told the nations' leaders that they had lost their rights under the old treaties by joining the Confederacy. In treaties of 1866 the Creek gave up the western half of their land for $975,168. The Seminole gave up all of theirs for fifteen cents an acre. The Five Tribes were the Cherokee, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw and Seminole nations. With these treaties, they lost the western half of present-day Oklahoma.

From 1879 to 1888, groups of settlers called boomers, led by David L. Payne and William Couch, entered the Unassigned Lands against the law. Cavalry soldiers, soldiers who fought on horseback, arrested them and took them out again and again. In 1889 Representative William Springer of Illinois changed a bill in Congress so that President Benjamin Harrison could open the land. Harrison announced the opening on March 23, 1889.

Each settler could claim 160 acres under the Homestead Act. People who sneaked in before the start to grab land were called "Sooners." By the end of that day, Guthrie and Oklahoma City were towns of about 10,000 to 15,000 people each.

In 1890 the United States census reported that the country no longer had a clear "frontier line." The census is the official count of the population, taken every ten years. The frontier line had been the edge of thinly settled land in the West, where two to six people lived on each square mile.

In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner wrote an essay about that census finding. It was called "The Significance of the Frontier in American History." Many people later treated 1890 as the end of the years when settlers could still find open land in the West, land that had belonged to Native nations.
