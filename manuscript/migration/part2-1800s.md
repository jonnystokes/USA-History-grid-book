## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 14 minutes**

Two kinds of journey crossed the country in these fifty years: families who chose to go west in wagons, and whole nations and enslaved people whom soldiers and slave traders forced to move. The United States about doubled in size in 1803, and by 1850 its land reached the Pacific Ocean. Families drove wagons over the Oregon, California and Mormon trails, and about 300,000 people rushed to California for gold between 1848 and 1855.

**Forced journeys.** United States soldiers drove the Cherokee, Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw and Seminole nations from their homelands in the Southeast. Slave traders and enslavers forced more than a million enslaved Black people to move from the Upper South to the Deep South, in chains or in ships.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
American officials bought about 827,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi River from France in 1803. The price was 15 million dollars, and afterward the country was about twice as big as before. All or part of 15 later states came from this land.

Thousands of Native people, in dozens of nations, already lived on this land. None of them had a part in the sale. After the purchase, American settlers began moving onto it, into the interior of the West.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The domestic slave trade: a forced march south" -->
Slave traders and enslavers forced more than one million enslaved people to move from the Upper South to the Deep South between about 1790 and 1860. The Upper South meant Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas, and later Kentucky and Tennessee. The Deep South meant the cotton lands from Georgia west to Texas.

Traders and enslavers moved about 200,000 people this way every ten years from 1820 to 1860. This trade inside the country was later called the "Second Middle Passage," after the first Middle Passage, the voyage of captured Africans across the Atlantic Ocean.

Traders moved people south in two ways. They marched some on foot in coffles, lines of enslaved people chained together and marched over land. They shipped others down the coast to New Orleans, which had the largest slave market in the country.

**Franklin and Armfield.** The largest slave-trading company in the country was Franklin and Armfield, founded in Alexandria, Virginia, in February 1828. John Armfield bought people in Alexandria. Isaac Franklin sold them in Natchez, Mississippi, and after 1829 in New Orleans. Rice Ballard joined them in the early 1830s and ran their office in Richmond. The partners bought, moved and sold more than 8,500 people, 1,000 to 1,500 a year in their busiest years, the mid-1830s.

Near the end of each summer, the partners sent coffles "in chains over the mountains." Each coffle held from several dozen to several hundred people, and the partners timed it to reach the Deep South for the fall selling season. They also owned three ships fitted to carry enslaved people, the *Tribune*, the *Uncas* and the *Isaac Franklin*. The voyage from Virginia to Louisiana took about three weeks, and at the busiest time one of these ships left every two weeks. The last shipload left in November 1836.

**Sold, or taken along.** Some of these people were sold to traders. Others were forced to move when the enslavers who held them moved to new land and took them along. Historians' estimates of the traders' share run from about 16 percent to about two-thirds. Walter Johnson puts it near the top, at about two-thirds sold and one-third taken along, and Michael Tadman gives 60 to 70 percent for the years after 1808.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="charles-ball-migration" name="Charles Ball" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Charles Ball

> **Who:** An enslaved man who was chained into a coffle and marched from Maryland to South Carolina. He later escaped and told his life story.
> **When and where:** Sold in Maryland about 1805. Marched to Columbia, South Carolina. His story was printed in Philadelphia in 1837.

**He escaped slavery twice, hiding by day and walking at night.**

The chapter opening describes how a Georgia slave trader chained Charles Ball into a coffle of 52 people in Maryland in about 1805 and marched them for more than a month to Columbia, South Carolina. At Columbia the trader sold Ball again, into Georgia.

**Escape.** Years later Ball escaped and walked north back to Maryland, hiding by day and moving at night. He was captured again and held as a slave. After his second escape he settled near Philadelphia.

Ball told his story to Isaac Fisher, a white abolitionist, someone who worked to end slavery. Fisher wrote it down, and it came out in 1837 as *Slavery in the United States: A Narrative of the Life and Adventures of Charles Ball*. Because Fisher did the writing, the book records Ball's story in Fisher's words. Many of the dates in Ball's life are known only roughly, even his birth, in about 1780 in Calvert County, Maryland.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="charles-ball-migration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Indian removal and the Trail of Tears" -->
President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act in 1830. He then directed the removal of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations from their homelands in the Southeast. Federal troops forced them to Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma.

**The Choctaw.** Federal troops removed the Choctaw from 1831 to 1833, forcing the first groups out in snow and river ice. Andrew K. Frank, writing for the Oklahoma Historical Society, counts more than 14,000 Choctaw removed and says hundreds died. Other counts give about 15,000 removed and about 2,500 dead.

**The Creek.** After a war with the Creek, United States troops marched the remaining Creek people west in five large groups in August and September of 1836. Soldiers put chains on Creek men taken prisoner in the war and marched them to Montgomery, Alabama, and their families followed in wagons. More than 14,500 Creek were removed. Several hundred died on the way. About 3,200 more died after they arrived, from disease, hunger and exposure, the harm cold and bad weather do to the body.

The steamboat *Monmouth*, carrying about 600 Creek people, was in a collision in October 1837. About half of the Creek on board died.

**The Chickasaw.** Under the Treaty of Pontotoc, signed in 1832, the Chickasaw had to leave their lands in Mississippi. They moved west from 1837, and more than 500 Chickasaw died of smallpox during the removal.

**The Seminole.** Many Seminole in Florida fought removal in a war that lasted from 1835 to 1842. For every four Seminole sent west, US Army soldiers killed one, the National Park Service states. A few hundred Seminole stayed in the Everglades, the swamps of southern Florida, and never surrendered.

**The Cherokee.** The Cherokee removal of 1838 and 1839 is called the Trail of Tears. United States Army troops under General Winfield Scott began rounding up the Cherokee who had not moved west in May 1838. President Martin Van Buren had sent Scott with about 7,000 soldiers, and state militia, part-time soldiers called up by a state, joined them, Georgia's among them. Soldiers took families from their homes and farms, often with no time to pack.

The soldiers held about 16,000 Cherokee in stockades, pens with walls of tall logs, in Tennessee and Alabama. They sent three groups west by river in June 1838. After that, Scott agreed to let John Ross, the Cherokee's Principal Chief, organize the rest. Ross organized fourteen groups, which left between August and December 1838. The walk over land was about 800 miles, and people died on the way from cold, hunger and disease.

**How many died.** The number of Cherokee dead is not settled. The count most often given is about 4,000. The National Park Service's history of the trail states that more than 1,000 people died on the journey itself, and "perhaps several thousand" more died later because they had been forced from their homes.

Higher estimates run from above 5,000, in an article on the History.com website, to about 8,000 or more, twice the usual figure. Russell Thornton, a scholar who studies population, gave that highest estimate in 1984. Andrew K. Frank, writing for the Oklahoma Historical Society, says as many as one in four Cherokee died.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" name="Rebecca Neugin" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Rebecca Neugin

> **Who:** A Cherokee woman who, at about three years old, was forced west on the Trail of Tears. She told what she remembered when she was 97 or 98.
> **When and where:** Soldiers took her from Georgia in 1838. Interviewed in 1932 in what is now Oklahoma.

**She rode west in a wagon at three and remembered it at 97.**

Soldiers rounded up Rebecca Neugin's family in Georgia in 1838. Her father was Tickaneesky, and her mother was Sallie Ketcher. The soldiers held the family in a camp in Tennessee.

The family started west in September 1838 with the group led by Mose Daniel and George Still. Rebecca and the smaller children rode in a wagon. Her mother, her father and her older brother Dick walked the whole way.

The historian Grant Foreman interviewed her in 1932, when she was 97 or 98 years old and one of the last living people who had made the journey. Her memory of it is one of the few first-hand accounts by a survivor. She died on July 15, 1932.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="rebecca-neugin-migration" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The overland trails" -->
Most of the western trails started from towns on the Missouri River, mainly Independence and St. Joseph, in Missouri.

The Santa Fe Trail opened in 1821, when William Becknell led the first trading trip from Missouri to Santa Fe, which then belonged to Mexico. It was mainly a road for traders hauling freight.

The Oregon Trail ran about 2,000 to 2,170 miles, from Independence, Missouri, to the Willamette Valley in Oregon, and the trip took four to six months. About 1,000 people set out on it on May 22, 1843, with about 120 wagons and thousands of oxen and cattle, and they reached Oregon that fall. This trip was called the "Great Migration" of 1843. It is a different event from the Great Migration of Black Southerners in the 1900s, which this chapter describes later.

After it, other families knew that wagon trains could make the whole journey, and hundreds of thousands followed over the next twenty years. From the 1840s on, tens of thousands of them came to the Willamette Valley, where at least twenty Native nations lived, among them the Kalapuya, Molala and Chinook peoples.

The California Trail split off from the Oregon Trail and turned southwest toward California. Traffic on it grew fast after gold was found in 1848.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How did people die on the wagon trails?" -->
About one in ten people who set out on the wagon trails died on the way, between 20,000 and 30,000 people. Most of the dead had cholera, a disease that causes severe diarrhea and vomiting, or typhoid, a disease that causes a high fever. Both spread through dirty water at crowded camps.

Other emigrants, people leaving one home to settle in another, died in accidents or drowned while crossing rivers. Later stories often showed Native people attacking wagon trains, but most of the emigrants who died on the trails died of disease. Families traveled in groups for safety, in covered wagons pulled by oxen.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why the Latter-day Saints moved west" -->
Members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, also called Mormons, moved west after armed men attacked them in Missouri and in Illinois.

**Missouri.** Missouri's governor, Lilburn Boggs, ordered on October 27, 1838, that the Latter-day Saints be driven out of the state. If necessary, in the order's own word, they were to be "exterminated," killed off entirely. Three days later about 200 armed Missourians led by Colonel Thomas Jennings attacked the Mormon settlement at Haun's Mill. They killed seventeen or eighteen men and boys. One of the boys, Sardius Smith, was ten years old. No record shows that anyone was ever tried for the killings.

Both sides in Missouri used force that year. The Missouri State Archives records that mobs of older Missouri settlers and armed Mormon groups roamed the countryside. Armed Mormon groups raided the towns of Gallatin and Millport in mid-October 1838 and burned property there. Over the next year, about 8,000 church members left Missouri for Illinois, many of them ragged and stripped of their property, and settled at Nauvoo.

**Illinois.** About 100 men from Illinois militia units broke into the jail at Carthage, Illinois, on June 27, 1844. They killed the church's founder, Joseph Smith, and his brother Hyrum. Five men were tried for the killings, and all five were found not guilty. Vigilantes, people who use force to punish others without any legal right to, then attacked Mormon farms around Nauvoo.

Brigham Young, a leader of the church, planned to move about 15,000 people west. The first wagons crossed the Mississippi River into Iowa on February 4, 1846, and by March 1 about 500 wagons were on the move. More than 70,000 Latter-day Saints had traveled the Mormon Trail by 1869. Some of the poorer groups pulled handcarts, small two-wheeled carts pulled by hand, instead of driving wagons.

**Whose land.** The Salt Lake Valley lay between the lands of the Ute, to the south, and the Shoshone, to the north. A few Native people crossed the valley itself, but none had camps there. The Ute, Goshute, Shoshone, Paiute and Navajo peoples lived in what is now Utah, and their homelands had been there for thousands of years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="brigham-young" name="Brigham Young" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Brigham Young

> **Who:** The Latter-day Saint leader who led the first company of Mormon emigrants to the Salt Lake Valley.
> **When and where:** From Winter Quarters, in what is now Nebraska, to the Salt Lake Valley, in what is now Utah. Arrived July 1847.

**Three of the men in his first company were enslaved.**

The Saints called Young's first company the vanguard company, the group that goes ahead of the rest. About 148 people traveled in it in 1847, about 1,300 miles from Winter Quarters, a camp in what is now Nebraska, to the Salt Lake Valley.

Counts of who was in the company differ. One count lists 143 men, 3 women and 2 children. Three of the men were enslaved Black men: Hark Lay, Green Flake and Oscar Crosby.

**The three men.** William Lay and his wife, Sytha, held Hark Lay in slavery and took him along. James Madison Flake held Green Flake in slavery, and William Crosby held Oscar Crosby, according to the Century of Black Mormons project at the University of Utah. The two enslavers sent Green Flake and Oscar Crosby ahead to build shelters and plant crops for their white families, who came later.

The three enslaved men hunted for the travelers, guarded the animals, and went into the Salt Lake Valley on July 22, 1847, ahead of most of the company. The rest reached the valley that July, and Young himself arrived on July 24.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="brigham-young" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New land from Mexico, and the Gold Rush" -->
A journalist named John O'Sullivan wrote the phrase "Manifest Destiny" in the *Democratic Review* in 1845. It named an idea that many Americans held: that the United States was meant to spread across the continent to the Pacific Ocean. People used the idea to argue for moving west and for going to war with Mexico.

**The Mexican Cession.** The Mexican-American War lasted from 1846 to 1848 and ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. In it, Mexican officials, after losing the war, gave up California, Nevada and Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming. The land they gave up is called the Mexican Cession. Texas had already been annexed, made part of the United States, in 1845. In 1846 officials of the United States and Britain agreed on the border of Oregon.

**Gold.** Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in California in early 1848, and the rush came in 1849. About 80,000 people, called forty-niners, arrived that year. From 1848 to 1855 about 300,000 people came to California, about half by sea and about half over land on the California Trail.

California's population, not counting Native people, grew from about 14,000 in 1848 to about 250,000 in 1852. San Francisco grew from about 200 people in 1846 to about 36,000 in 1852.

**Whose land.** The forty-niners arrived on the land of California's Native nations. About 150,000 Native people lived in California in 1848, and by 1870 only about 30,000 were left, about 120,000 fewer. The historian Benjamin Madley documents the causes: killings, kidnapping, forced labor, disease, hunger, and newcomers driving Native people off their land.

Vigilantes and United States Army soldiers killed 1,000 or more Native people between December 1849 and May 1850. On the last day of 1854, 116 volunteer militiamen attacked the Tolowa people and killed hundreds of them. One militiaman died. Madley documents that state and federal officials took part in the killings, and that taxpayers paid for militia campaigns.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 16 minutes**

Settlers filled the Great Plains and the far West after 1850, and United States soldiers forced the Native nations who lived there onto reservations, areas of land that federal officials set aside for them, often far from their homelands. In 1864 soldiers marched about 8,500 Navajo people more than 300 miles to one of them.

**Free land and fast travel.** Under a law of 1862, settlers could claim free farmland, and they filed about 4 million claims under it. After 1869 a railroad could carry them across the country in about a week. Tens of thousands of Black Southerners also moved west, to Kansas, to get away from violence and cheating in the South. By 1890 the officials who counted the country's population no longer found a clear line between settled land and thinly settled land in the West.

**Still on the trail.** Wagon trains kept rolling west on the Oregon Trail after 1850. One family that set out in 1853 was Amelia Stewart Knight's.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="amelia-stewart-knight" name="Amelia Stewart Knight" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Amelia Stewart Knight

> **Who:** A wife and mother who kept a diary, day by day, of her family's wagon journey on the Oregon Trail.
> **When and where:** From Monroe County, Iowa, to the Willamette Valley near Milwaukie, Oregon Territory, April to September 1853.

**Her diary says nothing of her pregnancy until the baby came.**

Amelia Stewart Knight, her husband and their seven children left Monroe County, Iowa, for Oregon in April 1853. She was pregnant, and she kept a diary of the trip.

Her diary records rain and mud, dust, sick children and hard river crossings. It also records the daily work of handling the wagons and the cattle.

The family reached Oregon on September 17, 1853. The next day she gave birth to her eighth child, a son named Adam.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="amelia-stewart-knight" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why were the handcart companies trapped in the snow?" -->
More than 1,000 Latter-day Saint emigrants set out late in the season in 1856 to walk to Salt Lake City pulling handcarts, small carts they pulled by hand. Church leaders urged them to go forward that late in the year, even after one of their own leaders, Levi Savage, warned that snow would catch them. Snow trapped many of them in what is now Wyoming. Counts of the dead run from about 170 to about 250.

The emigrants traveled in two companies. The Willie company, about 500 people, left Florence on August 17, and the Martin company left on August 27. Florence was in Nebraska Territory, land the United States governed that was not yet a state.

An early blizzard struck on October 19, 1856, just after the Martin company had waded across the North Platte River, near where Casper, Wyoming, is today. More than 300 rescuers came from Salt Lake with wagons, and they brought the survivors in through November.

Counts of the dead differ. Modern counts give about 67 to 77 dead in the Willie company and about 103 to 150 in the Martin company. Older accounts say about 200 to 250 people died in the stranded companies and the wagon trains traveling with them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="levi-savage" name="Levi Savage" movie="17 Miracles (2011)" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Levi Savage

> **Who:** A leader in the Willie handcart company who warned the emigrants not to leave so late, then stayed with them through the blizzards. His journal records what happened.
> **When and where:** From Florence, Nebraska Territory, to Salt Lake City, August to November 1856. Born 1820 in Ohio.
> **Movie:** *17 Miracles* (2011), a film directed by T. C. Christensen about the Willie handcart company. It draws on Savage's journal.

**He warned that leaving so late would bring snow and deaths.**

Levi Savage was 36 and one of the Willie company's sub-captains, the leaders under the captain. At a camp meeting at Florence on August 13, 1856, he spoke to the roughly 500 emigrants. They still had more than 1,000 miles to go, and he urged them to wait until spring.

In his journal, Savage wrote that most of the emigrants still meant to go on if the church's leaders said go. At later meetings, church leaders urged them to go forward whatever happened. Out on the trail on September 12, the church leader Franklin D. Richards scolded Savage "very severely in open meeting for his lack of faith in God."

Savage went with the company anyway, and when the blizzards came he worked through them to keep people alive. His journal survives, and most modern accounts of the Willie company rely on it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="levi-savage" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Weeping Time, 1859" -->
The domestic slave trade went on through the 1850s. Its largest single sale took place at the Ten Broeck Race Course near Savannah, Georgia, on March 2 and 3, 1859. Pierce Mease Butler sold between 429 and 436 enslaved men, women and children there to pay his gambling debts. The sale brought him $303,850. It rained hard on both days.

Joseph Bryan, a broker who arranged sales for others and the largest slave dealer in Savannah, ran the sale. For weeks he advertised it in newspapers across the South, from Richmond to New Orleans. Mortimer Thomson, a reporter for the *New York Tribune*, came to the sale dressed as a buyer and wrote a long account of it. The surviving accounts do not say where the buyers took the people they bought.

**Sold in families.** Butler required that people be sold in "families" as far as possible, and Bryan's advertisement promised that they would be "sold in families." Thomson told the story of a 23-year-old man named Jeffrey, who planned to marry a woman named Dorcas. Because they were not married, they could not be sold as a family. Jeffrey begged his buyer, "Please buy Dorcas," but she was being sold with her family of four and could not be bought alone, and another buyer took her. Parents were also separated from children at the sale. Black Georgians remembered the sale as "the weeping time."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Free land and the railroad" -->
President Abraham Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862. Under it, an adult who headed a household could claim up to 160 acres of public land for free, and after living on the land and farming it for five years, the settler owned it. Federal officials had taken much of that land from the Native nations who lived on it. Women could claim land. After the Civil War, freed people could claim land too, and so could immigrants who planned to become citizens.

Settlers filed about 4 million claims over the years the law was in force. Settlers and other private owners got about 270 million acres this way, about 10 percent of all the land in the United States. Many settlers moved onto the Great Plains to claim land under the law.

**The railroad.** The first railroad across the country was finished at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869. By wagon, a trip from coast to coast took four months or more, and by train it took about a week. Under the Pacific Railroad Act, which Lincoln signed in 1862, each railroad company got 6,400 acres of federal land for every mile of track it built. The railroads' owners sold that land to settlers, and their trains carried settlers and immigrants west by the trainload.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Long Walk of the Navajo, 1864" -->
United States soldiers forced more than 10,000 Navajo people from their homeland in what is now Arizona and New Mexico from 1863 to 1866. General James Henry Carleton ordered Colonel Christopher "Kit" Carson to make the Navajo surrender.

**Burning the food.** Carson's troops, guided by Ute scouts and warriors, began burning Navajo fields in August 1863. They took more than 5,000 sheep, goats and mules, and they destroyed more than 75,000 pounds of wheat and the Navajo peach orchards.

**The march.** Starving, 6,000 Navajo had gathered at Fort Canby and Fort Wingate by March 1864. Soldiers then marched about 8,500 people more than 300 miles to the Bosque Redondo reservation at Fort Sumner, New Mexico. The walk took from 18 to 40 days, and the soldiers gave them food planned to last 8 days. This march is known as the Long Walk.

About 200 people died on the march, in winter. Navajo accounts passed down in families say soldiers shot people who could not keep up, among them old people, sick people and pregnant women.

Soldiers held more than 8,000 Navajo and Mescalero Apache people at Bosque Redondo, and thousands more died there. On June 1, 1868, Navajo leaders signed a treaty with the United States, and under it the Navajo could go home.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Reservations on the Plains" -->
Through the 1870s and 1880s, soldiers forced many nations of the Great Plains onto reservations. In the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868, United States officials had set aside the Great Sioux Reservation, all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River, the Black Hills included, "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota.

After soldiers on an Army expedition reported gold in the Black Hills in 1874, federal officials ignored the treaty. In December 1875 they ordered the Lakota bands in their winter camps to move onto the reservation by January 31, 1876, and then they sent the Army against the bands that stayed out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" name="Laura Ingalls Wilder" movie="Little House on the Prairie (NBC series, 1974-1983)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Laura Ingalls Wilder

> **Who:** The author of the Little House books. As a child she moved with her family by covered wagon again and again.
> **When and where:** Born February 7, 1867, near Pepin, Wisconsin. Family moves from 1869 to 1879. Her own last move, to Mansfield, Missouri, in 1894.
> **Movie:** *Little House on the Prairie* (NBC series, 1974 to 1983). It ran for nine seasons and 205 episodes, with Michael Landon as Charles Ingalls. It is loosely based on the books.

**Her family's Kansas cabin stood on land that belonged to the Osage.**

Laura Ingalls Wilder's family moved six times between 1869 and 1879. In 1869 they went to Montgomery County, Kansas, near the town of Independence. Their cabin stood on the Osage Diminished Reserve, land that still belonged to the Osage Nation, and the family had no right to be there. They left in 1871 and went back to Pepin, Wisconsin.

The family moved to Walnut Grove, Minnesota, in 1874, and swarms of grasshoppers ate their wheat crops there in 1875 and 1876. Then they moved to Burr Oak, Iowa, where they helped run a hotel from 1876, and back to Walnut Grove in 1877. In 1879 they settled at De Smet, in Dakota Territory. There Laura's father, Charles Ingalls, took a job at a railroad camp and then claimed a homestead.

**Her own move.** Laura married Almanzo Wilder in 1885. In 1894, a year of drought, Laura, Almanzo and their daughter Rose drove a covered hack, a light wagon with a cover, about 650 miles from De Smet to Mansfield, Missouri. She kept a diary of the trip, published in 1962 as *On the Way Home*. In Mansfield she later wrote the Little House books.

**What the books changed.** The books came out from 1932 to 1943, and in them Wilder changed the real story. In *Little House on the Prairie*, Laura is several years older than she really was in Kansas, where she was 2 to 4 years old. The books leave out the Burr Oak years completely, and they do not say that the family was living on Osage land.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="laura-ingalls-wilder" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The cattle drives" -->
Cowboys drove herds of longhorn cattle north from Texas to Kansas after the Civil War. They took the cattle to railheads, the towns at the end of a railroad line, where the cattle were loaded onto trains. Abilene was one of these towns. The railroad reached Abilene in 1867, and after that cowboys used the Chisholm Trail from Texas heavily.

About 5 million cattle or more were driven north from Texas between 1866 and 1884. Some estimates put the total for the whole era at about 6 million. About 700,000 cattle reached the railheads in Kansas in 1871 alone.

The long drives ended in the mid-1880s for several reasons. Barbed wire fences went up across the open range. Railroads reached deeper into Texas, so the cattle no longer had to walk to Kansas. Kansas lawmakers passed a quarantine law in 1885 and 1886, keeping Texas cattle out of the state to stop the spread of disease. Then the cold of the winter of 1886 and 1887 killed much of the cattle herd.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why did the Exodusters go to Kansas?" -->
Thousands of Black Southerners left their homes in 1879 and moved to Kansas, to get away from violence and cheating in the South. People called them Exodusters, after the Exodus, the story in the Bible of the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt. Many came from Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas and Tennessee. As many as 40,000 moved to Kansas, Oklahoma and Colorado between about 1879 and 1881, and about 6,000 reached Kansas in the spring of 1879 alone.

**What they left.** Former Confederates took away their votes, white landowners cheated them, and armed white men attacked them. Many were sharecroppers, who farmed someone else's land and paid the owner with a share of the crop.

The Confederates had been the Southerners who fought against the United States in the Civil War to keep slavery. After the presidential election of 1876, former Confederates took back control of the Southern states and took away Black Southerners' right to vote. The historian Damani Davis calls what Black Southerners faced "relentless terror."

**Henry Adams's list.** In Louisiana, the Black organizer Henry Adams collected the names of nearly 700 Black people who had been attacked. For each one he listed where it happened and what was done. Most of them had been whipped, beaten, shot or killed by white men, often by armed groups. Some entries name the man. One entry says that a white man, Dr. Moore, killed a Black man named Calvin in 1871, after Calvin was accused of cursing a white woman.

Many Exodusters traveled up the Mississippi River by steamboat, and crowds of them waited on the docks at St. Louis. In 1880 a United States Senate committee of three Democrats and two Republicans looked into why they had left. The three Democrats, the majority, held that the move had been planned to settle Black voters in Northern states for political gain. A 1912 history of Kansas states that the evidence hardly supported that idea.

The two Republicans wrote their own report. It said the migrants had fled because they could no longer bear the hardship, unfair treatment and suffering that "a class of Democrats in the South" had brought on them.

The migrants saw Kansas as a safe place. John Brown, who fought to end slavery, had lived there, and before the Civil War many Kansans had wanted it to be a free state, a state without slavery.

### Benjamin "Pap" Singleton

> **Who:** A man born into slavery who organized thousands of Black Southerners to move to Kansas.
> **When and where:** Born enslaved in Nashville, Tennessee, about 1809. Led the move to Kansas in the late 1870s. Died 1900.

**He called himself the "Father of the Exodus."**

Benjamin Singleton ran away from slavery several times, and in the end he escaped to the North and to Canada. He came back to Tennessee in about 1871, after the Civil War. He worked there as a carpenter, a cabinetmaker and an undertaker, a person who prepares the dead for burial.

Singleton was angry that white landowners cheated the Black sharecroppers who worked their land, and he believed Black Southerners would never get a fair chance at land in the South. He printed handbills, short printed notices, to draw Black settlers to Kansas. He formed a group called the Edgefield Real Estate and Homestead Association to move people there.

Singleton said under oath, at a hearing, that his work helped more than 7,400 people move. Between 1877 and 1879, his group steered more than 20,000 people toward Kansas. He died in 1900.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Oklahoma Land Run, 1889" -->
About 50,000 people raced into central Oklahoma at noon on April 22, 1889, to claim land. The land they entered was called the Unassigned Lands. It covered about 1.9 million acres in the middle of Indian Territory, and nearly 2 million acres were claimed in a single day.

Indian Territory was the land in present-day Oklahoma where federal officials had moved many Native nations. The Unassigned Lands were the last part of it that federal officials had not given to any of those nations. Federal officials had taken land in Indian Territory from the nations there.

**How the land was taken.** After the Civil War, federal officials pressed the Creek and Seminole nations to give up land. Dennis N. Cooley, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, was the federal official in charge of dealings with Native nations. In 1865 he told the nations' leaders that they had lost their rights under the old treaties by joining the Confederacy.

The Creek had to sign over the western half of their land for $975,168 in treaties of 1866, and the Seminole had to sign over all of theirs for fifteen cents an acre. With these treaties the Five Tribes, the Cherokee, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw and Seminole nations, lost the western half of present-day Oklahoma.

**The boomers.** Groups of settlers called boomers, led by David L. Payne and William Couch, entered the Unassigned Lands against the law from 1879 to 1888. Cavalry soldiers, who fought on horseback, arrested them and took them out again and again. Representative William Springer of Illinois changed a bill in Congress in 1889 so that President Benjamin Harrison could open the land, and Harrison announced the opening on March 23, 1889.

Each settler could claim 160 acres under the Homestead Act. People who sneaked in before the start to grab land were called "Sooners." By the end of that day, Guthrie and Oklahoma City were towns of about 10,000 to 15,000 people each.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The frontier line disappears from the census, 1890" -->
The officials who ran the United States census, the official count of the population taken every ten years, reported in 1890 that the country no longer had a clear "frontier line." The frontier line had been the edge of thinly settled land in the West, where two to six people lived on each square mile.

The historian Frederick Jackson Turner wrote an essay about that census finding in 1893, called "The Significance of the Frontier in American History." Many people later treated 1890 as the end of the years when settlers could still find open land in the West, land that had belonged to Native nations.
