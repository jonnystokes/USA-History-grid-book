## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 8 of 10 · 1900 to 1950 · about 18 minutes**

New York's officials wrote the first rules for a whole American city on what could be built where, and builders in New York went on to put up the tallest buildings in the world. Millions of people moved into American cities in these decades. In 1907 alone, more than a million immigrants came through Ellis Island in New York, and millions of Black Americans moved from the South to cities such as Chicago and Detroit.

White property owners and judges kept Black families out of most neighborhoods, and federal officials refused to back home loans in the neighborhoods where Black families lived. To build a secret city for the atomic bomb, Army officials took farmland in Tennessee from the families who lived on it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinking water for Chicago and San Francisco" -->
People in Chicago drank water from Lake Michigan, drawn in through intake pipes about two miles offshore. The city's sewers emptied into the Chicago River, and the river flowed into the lake. In 1885 a great storm carried the river's filth far out into the lake, and people feared it would reach the intakes. The Sanitary District of Chicago, the public body in charge of the work, was set up to solve the problem.

The Sanitary District's engineers made the river flow the other way. Workers dug a canal 28 miles long, the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal. Through it, the river now flowed away from the lake, toward the Des Plaines River and on down to the Mississippi, carrying the city's sewage with it.

Missouri lay downstream on the Mississippi. Sanitary District officials feared that Missouri's officials would ask a court for an injunction, an order telling someone to stop. Partly to finish before a judge could issue that order, crews blasted out the last barrier at dawn on New Year's morning, and the reversal was complete on January 2, 1900.

Missouri's state officials sued the Sanitary District that year. A court later dismissed the case because Missouri could not prove that the river's pollution came from Chicago. The sewers of St. Louis also emptied that city's waste into the Mississippi.

Stephenson Strobel, a health researcher at McMaster University in Canada, estimated in a 2025 study that the reversal cut the number of deaths in Chicago by about 4 percent in 1900. That is about 985 people who would otherwise have died that year.

An earthquake broke the main pipes from San Francisco's reservoirs on April 18, 1906. It also broke the water mains, the large pipes that carry water under the streets, in more than 300 places. When firefighters hooked their hoses to the hydrants, no water came out. The fires burned for about three days and destroyed more than four square miles of the city.

**Hetch Hetchy.** After the fire, city officials planned to dam the Hetch Hetchy Valley in Yosemite National Park and pipe its water to the city. Damming the valley meant flooding it, and conservationists, people who work to protect wild land, fought to stop the dam.

President Woodrow Wilson signed the Raker Act on December 19, 1913. Under it, San Francisco's city officials could build the dam, and no other American city has been allowed to build in a national park for its own use. The members of Congress who passed the Act wrote into it that nobody could make a private profit from that water and power.

Workers finished the O'Shaughnessy Dam in 1923, and by 1934 the full system was carrying water to San Francisco. City officials later sold the dam's electricity to a private company, Pacific Gas and Electric, and people fought in court for decades over whether that broke the Act.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal work crews of the 1930s" -->
Federal crews laid new water and sewer pipes in cities across the country in the 1930s. They worked for the Works Progress Administration (WPA), a federal program that hired people to build public projects such as roads, pipes and schools, from 1935 to 1943. WPA crews laid about 24,000 miles of storm and sewer lines, which carry away rainwater and waste, and about 19,700 miles of water mains.

WPA crews built about 500 water-treatment plants, more than 4,000 new schools, hundreds of hospitals, and airports, and they built or improved about 651,000 miles of road. Counts of the WPA's work differ from one record to another, so each of these figures is close but not exact.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Zoning: rules for what may be built where" -->
Under zoning, officials divide a city into districts and set rules for what may be built in each one. The rules state how tall a building may be, how close it may stand to the street, and what it may be used for. No American city had zoning rules that covered the whole city before 1916. That year, on July 25, New York's officials voted to adopt the first citywide zoning resolution in the country.

Popular accounts state that the Equitable Building, finished in New York in 1915, caused the resolution. It rose about 40 stories straight up from the sidewalk, with no setback, no place where the upper floors step back from the street. It cast a shadow of about seven acres on the streets around it and cut off the sun from the buildings next to it.

Jason Barr, who writes about the history of skyscrapers, states that officials had been preparing the resolution for years. In his account they would have passed it without the Equitable Building, which became the best-known example of what the rules were meant to stop.

The resolution's height rule depended on the width of the street. A building's front wall could rise straight up to a height set by multiplying the street's width by a fixed number. Above that height, the building had to step back from the edge of its lot each time it went higher. A tower could go up with no height limit if it covered no more than a quarter of the lot.

**The wedding cake.** Architects who followed that formula designed New York's skyscrapers of the next decades as stacked boxes, each one smaller than the one below, and people called the shape a wedding cake. The officials who wrote the resolution meant the setbacks to keep air and sunlight reaching the sidewalks.

Officials also used city laws to decide who could live where. In Louisville, Kentucky, a city law barred Black buyers from buying homes on blocks where most people were white.

William Warley led the Louisville branch of the NAACP, a group that fought for Black Americans' rights. He agreed to buy a lot in a mostly white neighborhood so that the law could be tested in court. In 1917, in *Buchanan v. Warley*, the justices of the Supreme Court all agreed that the Louisville law could not stand. After that ruling, local officials turned to zoning rules that did not name race.

Officials in the village of Euclid, Ohio, had banned two-family houses and apartment buildings from parts of the village, so families who could not afford a whole house of their own could not live there. In 1926, in the case *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that a local government had the power to zone. After the ruling, officials in cities across the country wrote zoning rules.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-burnham" name="Daniel Burnham" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Burnham

> **Who:** The Chicago architect who directed the building of the 1893 world's fair and then wrote the first plan for the whole of a big American city.
> **When and where:** Chicago, from the 1893 world's fair to the *Plan of Chicago* in 1909.

**Burnham spent about thirty months on Chicago's plan and charged nothing.**

Daniel Burnham ran the building of the White City at the 1893 world's fair in Chicago, where the buildings were designed together as one planned city. After the fair, Burnham applied the same idea to a real city that already existed.

Burnham and Edward Bennett published the *Plan of Chicago* in 1909. The members of the Commercial Club of Chicago, a club of the city's business leaders, asked for the plan and raised about $80,000 to pay for it. Burnham worked on it for about thirty months and charged nothing. The club spent more than $40,000 more to print 1,650 copies of the finished book.

It was the first comprehensive plan for a large American city, setting out what the whole city should become, with its streets, parks and waterfront planned together. In it, Burnham and Bennett called for the lakefront to stay open to the public, for rings of parks around the city and for wider streets. Much of Chicago's lakefront is still open to the public because of that plan.

**Known:** Many people know Burnham for the line "make no little plans," but people began to credit it to him only after he died in 1912.
**Not known:** No record from his lifetime has him saying or writing it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="daniel-burnham" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Which tower would be tallest?" -->
The builders of the Chrysler Building beat the builders of 40 Wall Street in 1929, in a race for the tallest building in the world. Then, in 1931, the builders of the Empire State Building went higher than both.

**The hidden spire.** The Chrysler Building's architect, William Van Alen, was racing the builders of 40 Wall Street. His workers put together a spire, a tall pointed top, 125 feet long. They built it in secret inside the building's fire tower, a shaft that ran up through the building, where nobody outside could see it. In October 1929 they raised the spire up through the roof and fixed it in place.

The spire brought the Chrysler Building to 1,046 feet, the official figure from the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, though some popular accounts print 1,048. The tower at 40 Wall Street stood 927 feet tall. The Chrysler Building opened on May 27, 1930, and it was the tallest building in the world for eleven months.

The Empire State Building opened on May 1, 1931. It had 102 stories and stood 1,250 feet to the roof, taller than both. Its builders put it up in 410 days, for about $41 million. President Hoover turned on its lights by pressing a button in Washington.

The building opened during the Depression, the years of the 1930s when businesses failed and people lost their jobs. Through the Depression the building stood about half empty.

Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake and Akwesasne worked on the steel of both the Empire State and Chrysler buildings. From the 1920s to the 1960s, their families lived in a Brooklyn neighborhood that people called Little Caughnawaga, after an older spelling of Kahnawake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where Great Migration families could live" -->
The Great Migration was the move of about 6 million Black Americans out of the South between 1910 and 1970. In its first ten years, from 1910 to 1920, the Black population of Chicago rose 148 percent. Detroit's rose 611 percent, to about seven times its 1910 size. Philadelphia's rose about 59 percent, and New York's about 66 percent. In the 1920s New York's Black population more than doubled.

Black families who arrived could not live wherever they chose. White property owners kept them out of neighborhoods with restrictive covenants, sections written into the deed of a house, the legal paper that records who owns it. A covenant forbade the owner to sell or rent the house to a Black buyer, and sometimes to Jewish or Asian buyers as well. Each later owner of the house had to obey it too.

Through the 1920s, white owners in Chicago and across the country signed covenants into their deeds, and judges enforced the covenants by ordering owners to obey them. Because of the covenants and the judges, Black families could rent or buy homes in only a few districts. In Chicago that was a strip of the South Side called the Black Belt, which its own residents named Bronzeville. In New York it was Harlem.

**Kitchenettes.** The number of people who needed rooms in those districts kept rising. Landlords in the crowded districts split their flats, or apartments, into smaller units: one apartment became several one-room units, each rented separately. Each unit had a hotplate, a small electric burner for cooking, and the families shared one bathroom. Bronzeville had more than 80,000 of these kitchenettes. For each square foot of floor, families in them paid more rent than white families paid in white neighborhoods.

**The 1948 ruling.** In the case *Shelley v. Kraemer*, in 1948, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that courts could no longer enforce the covenants. The ruling did not stop owners, builders and lenders from refusing Black buyers on their own, and many kept refusing for years.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The maps with red lines" -->
Federal officials set up an agency called the Home Owners' Loan Corporation in 1933, during the Depression, the hard times of the 1930s. Its staff worked with mortgages, loans to buy a house. They bought more than a million home loans from lenders and replaced each one with a new 15-year loan at 5 percent interest, easier terms than banks then gave. By 1936 they had swapped in new loans for about one in ten home loans outside farms.

The same staff made maps of 239 cities between 1935 and 1940, called "Residential Security" maps. They went through each city neighborhood by neighborhood and gave each one a grade for how safe they judged it was to lend money on the houses there.

**Four colors.** A was the best grade, shaded green on the map, followed by B in blue and C in yellow. D was the worst grade. The maps described D neighborhoods as "hazardous," and the staff shaded them red. From those red areas comes the word redlining: drawing a line around a neighborhood on a map and then refusing to lend money for houses inside it.

The appraisers, the staff members who judged each neighborhood, wrote notes that describe Black families moving in as "infiltration," a word for an enemy sneaking in. If Black families lived in a neighborhood, that all but guaranteed it a D.

**What the scholars dispute.** Scholars disagree about how much these particular maps changed who got loans. In a 2017 study, researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, a branch of the country's central bank, found lasting harm in the neighborhoods graded red.

Price Fishback, a historian of money and business, and three other scholars found in a 2021 study that the maps changed little about where the government lent. They also found that officials at the Federal Housing Administration, another federal agency, had already built their own way of redlining before these maps were drawn.

**What both sides agree on.** Federal Housing Administration officials insured home loans, promising to repay the lender if a buyer could not. They refused to insure loans in the neighborhoods where Black homeowners lived, and they backed loans mostly on new houses, which were sold almost only to white buyers.

Researchers at the National Community Reinvestment Coalition compared the old maps with present-day data in a study published in 2018. They found that about 74 percent of the neighborhoods graded red are low-to-moderate income today, meaning the families there earn well below the typical family in their area. About 64 percent are majority-minority, meaning most of the people living there are not white.

Researchers at the University of Richmond scanned the maps for their Mapping Inequality project and put them online, so anyone can look up what the appraisers wrote about a street.

**By 1940:** White owners and judges had kept Black families in a few crowded districts for twenty years, and federal staff had shaded nearly every neighborhood where Black families lived red on their lending maps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oak Ridge: a secret city on taken farmland" -->
Brigadier General Leslie Groves, an Army officer, had just taken charge of the Manhattan Project, the Army's secret program to build an atomic bomb. On September 19, 1942, he approved taking about 59,000 acres along the Clinch River in eastern Tennessee, a site Army officials called "Site X." Officers of the US Army Corps of Engineers took the land in October 1942.

**Who lived there.** About 1,000 families lived on that land. Some farmed it, and others lived in its small crossroads communities: Wheat, Elza, Scarboro (also spelled Scarborough) and Robertsville, with New Bethel and New Hope also named. Many of the families had farmed the same ground for generations. Accounts of how many people lived there differ: the Tennessee Encyclopedia states roughly 4,000 people, and other accounts state more than 3,000.

To take the land, the Corps officers filed a paper called a declaration of taking. Under it, the government could take the land right away, whether or not the owners wanted to sell, and settle the price later. Army officials paid an average of $46.86 an acre.

Army officials told the residents they had to leave, sometimes only by notices nailed to their front fence posts. The Tennessee Encyclopedia's account of the removals does not name the men who put up the notices. Families who had lived there for generations got a few weeks to go, and within a year all the families were gone.

Most families accepted the price the government set, and a few sued in court over what their land had been valued at. About an eighth of Roane County's land and about a seventh of Anderson County's land went into the site.

Workers built a city for the Army on that ground from 1942. The site's official name was the Clinton Engineer Works, and the city built there is Oak Ridge. The plans were for about 13,000 people, but by 1945 about 75,000 lived there, which for a short time made it the fifth-largest city in Tennessee.

**Alphabet houses.** Workers built about 3,000 houses from cemesto, ready-made panels of cement and asbestos, a mineral fiber. The houses came in a few standard types named by letters, such as the A house and the B house, so people called them alphabet houses. The flat-tops, flat-roofed houses, came in pieces made ahead of time by workers for the Tennessee Valley Authority, a federal agency, and crews put them together on the site.

The city had schools, buses, a 300-bed hospital and a symphony orchestra. Fences and gates surrounded it, and people had to show a badge to get in or out.

Oak Ridge did not appear on any published map. Los Alamos in New Mexico and Hanford and Richland in Washington were built for the same program. The plants at Oak Ridge separated the uranium, a heavy metal, for the atomic bomb that American forces dropped on Hiroshima. Most of the more than 70,000 people who lived and worked there did not know what they were making until the bombs fell. They had promised not to talk about their jobs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Colleen Black

> **Who:** A teenager from Nashville whose family moved to a city left off every published map, and who spent the rest of her life there.
> **When and where:** Oak Ridge, Tennessee, from 1944 until her death in March 2015.

**At nineteen, she hunted leaks in pipes without knowing what they carried.**

Colleen Black was born Colleen Rowan, one of more than ten children in a Nashville family. In 1944 the family moved to Oak Ridge for war work. They lived in a trailer camp. The ground was mud, with boardwalks laid across it, and people needed a pass to get through the gates.

At nineteen, Colleen worked as a leak detector at the K-25 plant. Her employer was Ford, Bacon & Davis, a company hired to do work at the plant. Her job was to find holes in the welded joints of pipes, the places where two pieces of metal had been melted together. She climbed over the pipes with a probe that sensed helium, looking for pinhole leaks.

Helium atoms are small enough to escape through a flaw too fine to see, so where the probe found helium, the weld was leaking. The plant had hundreds of miles of pipe. Even the gas's name was kept quiet: the workers were not supposed to say the word "helium." Years later she said, "I didn't know where those pipes were going, I didn't know what was going through them ... I just knew that I had to find the leak and mark it."

She learned what the plant had been separating only after American forces dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima, in Japan, on August 6, 1945. The bomb killed between 90,000 and 166,000 people in the city within two to four months. That count comes from the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, a research group that the United States and Japan run together and that studies the bomb's survivors. An American survey in 1946 recorded a lower count, 70,000 to 80,000. The exact number is not known.

She married a man called Blackie Black and stayed in Oak Ridge for the rest of her life. In 2005 she gave an oral history, a recorded interview about her life, to the Atomic Heritage Foundation's Voices of the Manhattan Project. The K-25 Virtual Museum includes her account, and she is featured in Denise Kiernan's 2013 book *The Girls of Atomic City*.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 9 of 10 · 1950 to 2000 · about 11 minutes**

St. Louis lost about 60 percent of its people between 1950 and 2000, and Detroit lost nearly half. Families moved out of the centers of older American cities to new suburbs at their edges. Cities in the hot South and West grew fast. Phoenix, Arizona, went from about 107,000 people in 1950 to more than 1.3 million in 2000.

At the edges of metropolitan areas, cities together with their suburbs, builders put up the same kind of neighborhood again and again: the subdivision, a tract of land divided into lots for single-family houses. White families bought those houses with mortgages that federal officials insured, promising to repay the lender if a buyer could not. Federal officials refused to insure the same kind of mortgage in the neighborhoods where Black families lived.

In the old centers, officials cleared whole neighborhoods and cut freeways through others. Under the Housing Act of 1949, city officials labeled neighborhoods blighted, run-down enough to tear down, and took the property by law. They had the buildings torn down, sold the cleared land to developers, and forced at least 300,000 families out of their homes.

Federal officials paid for new freeways with money from a highway law of 1956. Highway planners ran many of those freeways through poor neighborhoods, most of them home to Black families and other people of color.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Urban renewal: clearing neighborhoods for developers" -->
Members of Congress voted for the Housing Act of 1949. Under Title I, one section of that law, federal officials offered money to city governments for a program the law identifies as "urban redevelopment." People later called it urban renewal.

Once city officials had given an area the legal label blighted, they took the property in it by eminent domain, the government's power to take private property at a price that officials set. They had crews tear the buildings down. Then they sold the cleared land to private developers for much less than it had cost to take and clear, and federal money covered the gap. In 1954, in *Berman v. Parker*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that this was allowed.

Officials ran the program from 1949 to 1974, in more than 400 cities and on more than 1,200 projects, and crews tore down about 2,000 neighborhoods. Officials forced at least 300,000 families out of their homes, the lowest count the records support. Counting people instead of families, researchers estimate about 1.2 million.

**Who was forced out.** Black Americans were about 13 percent of the country's population in 1960, and they were at least 55 percent of the people forced out. These figures come from Brent Cebul's 2020 article in the Boston Review and from the Digital Scholarship Lab's "Renewing Inequality" project.

The writer James Baldwin said in a filmed interview in San Francisco in 1963 that urban renewal "means Negro removal," using what was then the usual word for Black Americans.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Why freeways ran through Black neighborhoods" -->
President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act on June 29, 1956. It provided about $25 to $26 billion for a system of 41,000 miles of freeway.

Highway planners laid many of the routes where land was cheap and where the people who lived there were least able to organize against them. Those were mostly poor neighborhoods of Black families and other people of color. Builders defended taking land in Black neighborhoods by saying it was the cheapest. It was cheap partly because of redlining: for years, lenders and federal officials had refused to lend money for homes in those neighborhoods.

Rondo, a neighborhood in St. Paul, Minnesota, is one well-documented case. About 80 percent of St. Paul's Black residents lived there. Between 1956 and 1968, crews built Interstate 94 through the neighborhood.

Counts of what Rondo lost differ. Guides from the Minnesota Historical Society state about 600 families. ReConnect Rondo and reporting in Sahan Journal state about 700 homes and 300 businesses, and they record that Rondo lost 61 percent of its residents. One in eight of St. Paul's Black residents lost a home to the building of Interstate 94.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marvin-roger-anderson" name="Marvin Roger Anderson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Marvin Roger Anderson

> **Who:** A young man whose family lost its home and its apartment buildings to a freeway, and who has spent his later years working to build a land bridge over it.
> **When and where:** Rondo, St. Paul, Minnesota. His family learned of the freeway in 1958.

**Crews loaded his father's townhomes onto flatbed trucks and moved them away.**

Marvin Roger Anderson grew up in Rondo. His father built Rangh Court there, twelve two-story townhomes, in 1947 or 1948 (accounts give both years). To build them, his father got "the largest Federal Housing Authority mortgage backed loan ever given to nonwhite applicants in the state."

The family learned in 1958, the year Anderson finished Central High School, that the new Interstate 94 would run through their land. State officials condemned the buildings for the freeway, taking them by law, and called the homes "inferiorly constructed." Crews loaded the buildings onto flatbed trucks and moved them away. Four of them still stand, on other ground, on the University of Minnesota's St. Paul campus.

Anderson became the State Law Librarian of Minnesota, the librarian in charge of the state's law library. In 1983 he and Floyd Smaller founded Rondo Days to keep alive "the history, culture and memories of the Rondo that existed before the highway."

He now leads ReConnect Rondo. Its members propose to build a lid over the freeway, a land bridge of about 21 acres, with homes, businesses and community space on top. Early design studies for the land bridge list between 350 and 1,400 homes.

The *Voices of Rondo* oral-history collection records his account, and so do press interviews.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marvin-roger-anderson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sprawl, and the people who left the old centers" -->
Levittown, New York, opened in 1947, and by 1951 its builders had put up more than 17,000 houses there. The builders, Levitt and Sons, mass-produced the houses, building the same house over and over. They split the work into 27 steps and trained each crew to do one step. The houses stayed in place, and the crews moved from house to house. At the busiest time, about 30 houses went up in a day.

The first houses rented for $60 a month, and a renter could buy one for $7,500 after a year. From 1949, the builders sold ranch houses for $7,990. The lease for the first houses stated that they could not be "used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race," meaning white people. In 1960 every family in Levittown was still white.

Builders at the edges of other cities copied the pattern, and the spreading of cities outward in subdivisions like these came to be called sprawl.

The older cities lost people in the same fifty years. Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 950,000 in 2000, a loss of nearly half. St. Louis fell from about 857,000 to about 348,000, a loss of roughly 60 percent, and it kept shrinking after that, to about 300,000 by 2020.

Real estate agents and speculators, people who buy property to sell it again at a profit, pushed white families to sell and move out, a practice called blockbusting. They frightened white owners into selling cheaply by telling them Black families were moving in. Then they sold the same houses to Black families for much more, often "on contract."

**On contract.** Under a contract sale, if a buyer missed a single payment, the seller could put the family out, and the buyer lost everything already paid. In Chicago's North Lawndale neighborhood, about 3,000 buildings were sold on contract. North Lawndale went from 87 percent white in 1950 to 91 percent Black in 1960.

At the same time, federal officials insured mortgages on the new houses at the edge, under rules they set for which loans they would insure. Under those rules, they refused to insure mortgages in the neighborhoods where Black families lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jane-jacobs-robert-moses" name="Jane Jacobs and Robert Moses" movie="Citizen Jane: Battle for the City (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jane Jacobs and Robert Moses

> **Who:** Robert Moses, who built roads, bridges and parks across New York for decades without ever winning an election, and Jane Jacobs, a writer who helped defeat three of the projects planned for the city.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1950s to 1968.
> **Movie:** *Citizen Jane: Battle for the City* (2016), a documentary directed by Matt Tyrnauer, 92 minutes.

**Police arrested Jane Jacobs at a public hearing in April 1968.**

Robert Moses held as many as twelve appointed posts at the same time, including parks commissioner and head of the Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority. Officials gave him every one of those posts, and he never won an election to any of them.

From those posts he directed the building of 13 bridges, 2 tunnels, 658 playgrounds, 17 state parks, and hundreds of miles of parkways and expressways, wide roads for fast traffic. To build the Cross-Bronx Expressway, Moses's crews cut a path through crowded blocks of the Bronx and forced thousands of families out of their homes.

Jane Jacobs lived in the West Village in New York and wrote about cities. She had never been trained in a school as a city planner. In 1961 she published *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. In it she wrote that a busy street with both homes and shops stays safe because of "eyes on the street," the people who use it and watch it.

**Three fights.** Jacobs helped defeat three projects. In 1958 she and other opponents stopped a road planned through Washington Square Park. In 1961 and 1962 they beat city officials' attempt to label their own West Village a slum and clear it under urban renewal.

The third was the Lower Manhattan Expressway, a road ten lanes wide, raised above the street, planned across what is now SoHo and Little Italy. Jacobs led the Joint Committee to Stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway. Police arrested her at a public hearing in April 1968. The charges included inciting to riot, urging a crowd to violence, and they were later reduced.

Jacobs moved to Toronto in 1968. In 1969 Mayor John Lindsay declared the expressway "dead for all time," and city officials took it off the city's map that year.

Robert Caro's book about Moses, *The Power Broker*, came out in 1974 and won a Pulitzer Prize in 1975. It is the standard account of Moses's career, and in it Caro criticizes much of what Moses did. In 2007 the historians Hilary Ballon and Kenneth T. Jackson edited a book, *Robert Moses and the Modern City*. In it they give parts of his record, especially his parks and swimming pools, more credit than Caro gives them. The argument is not settled.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jane-jacobs-robert-moses" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-stokes" name="Carl Stokes" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Stokes

> **Who:** The first Black mayor of a major American city, elected in the years when officials were clearing neighborhoods and building freeways through cities.
> **When and where:** Cleveland, Ohio. Elected on November 7, 1967, and mayor from January 1, 1968, to 1971.

**In 1969 oil and trash on Cleveland's river caught fire.**

Carl Stokes was the grandson of a man who had been enslaved. His mother was a widow, and she raised him in the Outhwaite public housing in Cleveland, apartments owned by a government and rented to families with low incomes.

Cleveland's voters elected Stokes mayor on November 7, 1967. He won about 50.5 percent of the vote and defeated Seth Taft. The 1960 census had counted 876,050 people in Cleveland, the eighth-largest city in the country.

Because Cleveland was one of the ten largest cities, Stokes counts as the first Black mayor of a major American city. On the same day, voters in Gary, Indiana, a much smaller city, elected Richard Hatcher their mayor. Stokes was re-elected in 1969 and served until 1971.

He had campaigned on housing, on bringing money into neighborhoods that had gone without it, and on the city's pollution. On June 22, 1969, an oil slick and piled-up trash on the Cuyahoga River caught fire under a railroad bridge, set off by sparks from a passing train. It burned for about twenty minutes and damaged two railroad bridges. It was at least the thirteenth fire on that river since 1868, and for a century people had used the working river as a sewer.

The next day Stokes held a press conference on the riverbank and demanded that state and federal officials act. In October 1972 the members of Congress passed the Clean Water Act over President Richard Nixon's veto, his refusal to sign it. Members of Congress can pass a law over a veto when two-thirds of each house vote for it. Stokes was one of the local leaders who had pushed for the Clean Water Act.

Black mayors followed Stokes in other big cities. By 1972 voters had elected Kenneth Gibson in Newark, James McGee in Dayton and Ted Berry in Cincinnati. In 1973 they elected Tom Bradley in Los Angeles, Maynard Jackson in Atlanta and Coleman Young in Detroit.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="carl-stokes" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Growth in the Sun Belt" -->
While Detroit and St. Louis lost people, cities in the Sun Belt, the warm states of the South and the West, gained them. Phoenix, Arizona, had about 106,818 people in 1950 and 1,321,045 in 2000, and its metropolitan area went from about 323,000 people to about 3,014,000 in the same fifty years.

Phoenix grew in area too. It covered about 17 square miles in 1950, more than 475 square miles by the 2000s, and about 518 square miles now. City officials added that land by annexation, moving the city's legal boundary out to take in neighboring land.

Houston, Texas, went from 596,163 people in 1950 to 1,953,631 in 2000, and by 1990 it was the fourth-largest city in the country. Air conditioners cooled the air inside homes and offices, so people in Phoenix and Houston could live and work indoors through the hot summers. Land at the edge of these cities was cheap, and city officials kept annexing it. In the 1950s alone, Phoenix more than quadrupled in population.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 10 of 10 · 2000 to today · about 7 minutes**

Downtowns in most big American cities gained people in the 2000s, and in the 2020s many of their office towers began to empty. In the neighborhoods that buyers and lenders have returned to, rents and prices have risen, and some of the people who lived there can no longer afford to stay. Cities run hotter than the land around them, and in Miami Beach the sea now floods some streets at high tide.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Downtown living and empty offices" -->
Researchers at the Brookings Institution studied census counts for big American cities. Between 2000 and 2010, the downtowns grew faster than the areas around them. Of 53 downtowns they studied, 42 gained people. Young adults had already begun moving in. By 2000, people aged 25 to 34 made up nearly a quarter of downtown residents, up from 13 percent in 1970.

After the coronavirus pandemic of 2020, many office workers kept working from home for some or all of the week. Companies shrank the office space they rented, and as their leases, the contracts for renting the space, ran out, they gave up floors.

**A fifth of the space.** By 2024 about 20 percent of American office space stood empty, with figures for that year running from about 19.6 to 20.1 percent. That was the highest share since Moody's Analytics, a research company, began keeping track in 1979, and it came to more than 900 million square feet of empty floor. In downtown Manhattan the share was about 23 percent. Axios and ABC News reported these Moody's figures in January 2024.

Builders began turning empty office towers into apartments. In 2024 they had plans under way to make more than 55,300 apartments out of former office buildings, a record and more than four times the number in 2021. The Washington, D.C., area led, with 5,820 apartments planned. Researchers at the firm RentCafe counted those 2024 plans, as Time reported in January 2024.

Builders had finished, or were working on, 11.8 million square feet of former office space as apartments in 2025, the highest figure on record, according to industry reports from CBRE and Brevitas.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gentrification, and who can no longer afford to stay" -->
In gentrification, buyers and lenders return to a neighborhood they had avoided for years. New owners fix up the houses, landlords and sellers raise rents and prices, and the people who lived there through the years without investment can no longer afford to stay.

Buyers and lenders have returned to close-in neighborhoods, the ones near downtown, that appraisers had marked red in the 1930s. The work of the National Community Reinvestment Coalition and the Mapping Inequality project documents that pattern.

Washington, D.C., is one documented case. Census figures record the city as 71 percent Black in 1970, when people called it Chocolate City. By 2011 the Black share of the population had fallen below half, to 49.2 percent, for the first time in about fifty years.

**U Street.** New money went mostly into Shaw, U Street and Columbia Heights, close-in neighborhoods that appraisers had redlined. Around U Street, builders put up more than 2,000 expensive condominiums (apartments that each owner buys) and apartments between 1997 and 2007. The neighborhood went from about 60 percent Black in 2000 to about 20 percent Black in 2010.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="virginia-ali" name="Virginia Ali" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Virginia Ali

> **Who:** A co-founder of Ben's Chili Bowl, a diner on U Street in Washington, D.C., who has helped run it since 1958 while the neighborhood around it changed.
> **When and where:** 1213 U Street NW, Washington, D.C., from August 22, 1958.

**Ben Ali wrote "Soul Brother" in soap on the diner's front window.**

Virginia Rollins came from Chance, Virginia. Her husband, Mahaboob Ali, who went by Ben, had come from Trinidad. On August 22, 1958, the two of them opened a diner at 1213 U Street NW with a bank loan of $5,000.

People then called U Street "Black Broadway." Hundreds of Black-owned businesses, churches and theaters lined it and the streets nearby. At the Lincoln Theatre and the Howard Theatre, Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong and Billie Holiday performed.

Martin Luther King Jr. was shot and killed in Memphis, Tennessee, on April 4, 1968. James Earl Ray later pleaded guilty to his murder. For the next four days, riots broke out across Washington, and the corner of 14th and U was at the center of them. Thirteen people died, and about 1,200 homes and businesses were damaged.

**Open past curfew.** Stokely Carmichael led the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, a civil rights group. He asked the Alis to keep the Bowl open past the curfew, an order to stay off the streets at night. According to the shop's own history, they did, and they fed police officers and activists. Ben wrote "Soul Brother" in soap on the front window. Like many other Black business owners, he wrote it to mark the diner as Black-owned and keep rioters from wrecking it, and the diner came through the riots untouched.

Drug dealing at 14th and U rose sharply in the mid-1970s. From 1986 to 1991, crews digging the Metro, Washington's subway, tore up U Street. From the 1990s, buyers and lenders returned, and the new condominiums went up around the Bowl.

Ben's is still open, and Virginia Ali, in her nineties, is still one of the people who run it. When the diner turned 65 in 2023, she told a reporter: "We really mean something to this community."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="virginia-ali" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building for heat and water" -->
Daytime air in a city is 1 to 6 degrees Fahrenheit warmer than in the areas around it, according to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). At night the air can be up to 22 degrees warmer, and a roof or road in the sun can be 50 to 90 degrees hotter than the air above it.

**Why cities run hot.** The EPA's guides state the reasons. Roads, sidewalks and buildings soak up much of the sun's heat and hold it. A city has fewer trees and plants than the country around it, so it gets less shade and less cooling from water drying off leaves. Tall buildings and narrow streets block cooling breezes. The EPA's list of ways to cool a city includes planting trees, growing plants on roofs, and building roofs and pavements that reflect sunlight.

In Miami Beach, Florida, streets flood on clear days with no storm, when the tide is at its highest. At those tides the ocean pushes the water of Biscayne Bay back up the storm drains, and bay water pours out of the drains into the lowest streets. Seawater also rises up through the soft, holey limestone under the city. In the fall of 2014, crews began raising streets, including West Avenue and Sunset Harbour, and putting in pumps to push the water back out.

Crews had put in twelve pump stations along the west side of South Beach by 2016 and 2017. The cost was first reported as about $400 million and later as about $600 million, as the project grew. During the King Tides, the name used in Miami Beach for the highest tides of the year, the raised and pumped blocks stayed mostly dry after the work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tallest buildings now stand in other countries" -->
The tallest building in the world has stood in Asia since 1998. Chicago's Sears Tower, finished in 1974, was the last building in the United States to be the tallest in the world. It lost that title because of a rule about how to measure height.

In April 1996, members of the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat ruled that a spire designed as part of a building counts toward its height. Under that rule, the Petronas Towers in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, measured 1,483 feet to the tops of their spires, and in 1998 they became the tallest buildings in the world. The Sears Tower's roof and its highest floor that people used were both higher than those of the Petronas Towers.

The Burj Khalifa in Dubai, finished in 2010, stands 2,717 feet (828 meters). That is more than 900 feet taller than the tallest building in the United States.

Workers set the last piece of the spire on One World Trade Center, in New York, on May 10, 2013. That November, members of the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat ruled that its spire counts toward its height. The building opened in 2014 at 1,776 feet, a height its builders chose for 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence.

One World Trade Center is the tallest building in the Western Hemisphere, the half of the world that holds North and South America. Mohawk ironworkers worked on its steel, as Mohawk ironworkers had on the towers of the 1930s and on the bridges before them.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter closing" -->
**The three questions, answered**
- Why do so many American cities have straight streets that cross in a grid? Town founders chose the grid on purpose, from Spain's town rules of 1573 to Penn's Philadelphia in 1682. The three men who laid a grid over Manhattan in 1811 wrote that square-cornered houses were "the most cheap to build and the most convenient to live in."
- How did people get clean water into crowded cities? City leaders paid for big waterworks after epidemics and fires: steam pumps in Philadelphia in 1801, the Croton Aqueduct in New York in 1842, and a canal that turned Chicago's river around in 1900.
- Who decided which families could live in which neighborhoods? White owners wrote rules called covenants into their deeds to bar Black buyers, and judges enforced them until 1948. Federal officials refused to back home loans where Black families lived, and after 1949 city officials cleared about 2,000 neighborhoods.

**What changed:** Builders went from carrying baskets of earth at Cahokia to raising steel towers, and owners, judges and officials wrote rules about who could live where.

**Whose land:** Every colonial town and boomtown in this chapter went up on a Native nation's land, from Seloy's Timucua town at St. Augustine to the Lenape homeland at Philadelphia and the Potawatomi lands at Chicago.

**Five dates to keep**
- About 1100: Cahokia reaches its peak.
- 1573: Spain's king orders a plaza and a grid for every new Spanish town.
- June 22, 1842: Croton water reaches New York City.
- July 25, 1916: New York adopts the first citywide zoning rules.
- 1948: The Supreme Court rules that courts can no longer enforce covenants.

**Not known:** No builder's, planner's or ruler's name survives from Cahokia.
