## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1916, New York's officials adopted the first rules for a whole American city on what could be built where. They divided the city into districts. They also set a formula for how far a tall building had to step back from the street as it rose. Between 1929 and 1931, builders in New York put up the tallest buildings in the world.

Between 1935 and 1940, staff at a federal agency, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, graded the neighborhoods of 239 cities from A to D. Each grade stated how safe the staff judged it was to lend money there. If Black families lived in a neighborhood, that all but guaranteed it a D, colored red on the map. Scholars still argue over how much lenders used these maps. They agree that federal officials refused to back home loans in the neighborhoods where Black homeowners lived.

Millions of people moved into these cities in these decades. In 1907 alone, more than a million immigrants came through Ellis Island in New York. About 6 million Black Americans left the South between 1910 and 1970. White property owners wrote clauses into their deeds, the legal papers that record who owns a house, that forbade selling or renting to Black buyers. Judges enforced those clauses, so Black families could find homes in only a few districts. Landlords there charged them more rent for each square foot than white families paid.

From 1942, workers for the Manhattan Project built whole cities, and no published map showed them. The Manhattan Project was the Army's secret program to build an atomic bomb. One of its cities, Oak Ridge in Tennessee, went up on land where about 1,000 families had lived and farmed. In October 1942 Army officials took that land and made the families leave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Drinking water for Chicago and San Francisco" -->
People in Chicago drank water from Lake Michigan, drawn in through intake pipes about two miles offshore. The city's sewers emptied into the Chicago River, and the river flowed into the lake. In 1885 a great storm carried the river's filth far out into the lake, and people feared it would reach the intakes. The Sanitary District of Chicago, the public body in charge of the work, was set up to solve the problem.

The Sanitary District's engineers made the river flow the other way. Workers dug a canal 28 miles long, the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal. Through it, the river now flowed away from the lake, toward the Des Plaines River and on down to the Mississippi, carrying the city's sewage with it.

Missouri lay downstream on the Mississippi. Sanitary District officials feared that Missouri's officials would ask a court for an injunction, which is an order telling someone to stop. Partly to finish before a judge could issue that order, crews blasted out the last barrier at dawn on New Year's morning. The reversal was complete on January 2, 1900. Missouri sued that year, and the case was later thrown out because Missouri's lawyers could not prove that the river's pollution came from Chicago. St. Louis was dumping its own waste into the Mississippi too.

The authors of one economic history estimate that the reversal cut the number of deaths in Chicago by about 4 percent in 1900. That is about 985 people who would otherwise have died that year.

San Francisco's officials wanted a bigger and safer water supply after 1906. On April 18 of that year an earthquake broke the main pipes from the city's reservoirs and broke the water mains in more than 300 places. When firefighters hooked their hoses to the hydrants, no water came out. The fires burned for about three days and destroyed more than four square miles of the city. City officials then planned to dam the Hetch Hetchy Valley in Yosemite National Park and pipe its water to the city. Damming the valley meant flooding it. Conservationists, people who work to protect wild land, fought to stop the dam.

On December 19, 1913, President Woodrow Wilson signed the Raker Act. Under the Act, San Francisco's city government could build the dam. No other American city has been allowed to build in a national park for its own use. The members of Congress who passed the Act wrote into it that nobody could make a private profit from that water and power. Workers finished the O'Shaughnessy Dam in 1923. By 1934 the full system was carrying water to San Francisco. City officials later sold the dam's electricity to a private company, Pacific Gas and Electric, and people fought in court for decades over whether that broke the Act.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Zoning: rules for what may be built where" -->
Zoning means dividing a city into districts and setting rules for what may be built in each one. The rules state how tall a building may be, how close it may stand to the street, and what it may be used for. Before 1916, no American city had zoning rules that covered the whole city.

On July 25, 1916, New York's officials adopted the first citywide zoning resolution in the country. A resolution is a formal rule that officials vote to adopt.

Popular accounts say the Equitable Building, finished in New York in 1915, caused the resolution. It rose about 40 stories straight up from the sidewalk, with no setback. A setback is a place where the upper floors of a building step back from the street. The Equitable Building cast a shadow of about seven acres on the streets around it, and it cut off the sun from the buildings next to it. Jason Barr, who writes about the history of skyscrapers, states that officials had been preparing the resolution for years. In his account they would have passed it without the Equitable Building, which became the best-known example of what the rules were meant to stop.

The resolution's height rule depended on the width of the street. A building's front wall could rise straight up to a height set by multiplying the street's width by a fixed number. Above that height, the building had to step back from the edge of its lot each time it went higher. A tower could go up with no height limit if it covered no more than a quarter of the lot. Architects who followed that formula designed New York's skyscrapers of the next decades as stacked boxes, each one smaller than the one below. People called the shape a wedding cake.

The setbacks were meant to keep air and sunlight reaching the sidewalks. Officials in other cities also wrote zoning rules to keep people out. In 1917, in *Buchanan v. Warley*, the justices of the Supreme Court struck down a Louisville, Kentucky, law that barred Black buyers from buying homes on white-majority blocks. William Warley, the Black buyer in the case, was a lawyer for the NAACP, a group that fought for Black Americans' rights. After that ruling, local officials turned to rules that never named race.

In 1926, in the case *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that a local government had the power to zone. Euclid, Ohio, had banned two-family houses and apartment buildings from parts of the village. That kept out families who could not afford a whole house of their own. After the ruling, zoning spread to cities across the country.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="daniel-burnham" name="Daniel Burnham" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Daniel Burnham

> **Who:** The Chicago architect who directed the building of the 1893 world's fair and then wrote the first comprehensive plan for a big American city.
> **When and where:** Chicago, from the 1893 world's fair to the *Plan of Chicago* in 1909.

Daniel Burnham ran the building of the White City at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. The White City was a matched set of white plaster buildings, designed together as one planned city. After the fair, Burnham applied the same idea to a real city that already existed.

Burnham and Edward Bennett published the *Plan of Chicago* in 1909. The members of the Commercial Club of Chicago, a club of the city's business leaders, commissioned the work and raised about $80,000 to pay for it. Burnham worked on it for about thirty months and charged nothing. The club spent more than $40,000 more to print 1,650 copies of the finished book. It was the first comprehensive plan for a large American city. A comprehensive plan sets out what a whole city should become, with its streets, parks and waterfront planned together.

In the plan, Burnham and Bennett called for the lakefront to stay open to the public. They also called for rings of parks around the city and for wider streets. Much of Chicago's lakefront is open to the public today because of that plan.

Many people know Burnham for the line "make no little plans." People began to credit it to him only after he died in 1912. No record from his lifetime has him saying or writing it.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="daniel-burnham" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The contest for the tallest building" -->
Between 1929 and 1931, the builders of two New York towers competed to put up the tallest building in the world. Then the builders of a third tower went higher than both.

The Chrysler Building's architect, William Van Alen, was racing the builders of 40 Wall Street. His workers put together a spire, a tall pointed top, 125 feet long. They built it in secret inside the building's fire tower, a shaft that ran up through the building, where nobody outside could see it. In October 1929 they raised the spire up through the roof and fixed it in place. The spire brought the Chrysler Building to 1,046 feet. The tower at 40 Wall Street stood 927 feet tall. Some popular accounts print 1,048 feet for the Chrysler Building. The official figure, from the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, is 1,046 feet. The Chrysler Building opened on May 27, 1930, and it was the tallest building in the world for eleven months.

The Empire State Building opened on May 1, 1931. It had 102 stories and stood 1,250 feet to the roof, taller than both. Its builders put it up in 410 days, for about $41 million. President Hoover turned on its lights by pressing a button in Washington.

The building opened during the Depression, the years of the 1930s when businesses failed and people lost their jobs. Through the Depression the building stood about half empty.

Ironworkers put together the steel frames of bridges and tall buildings. Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake and Akwesasne, two Mohawk communities, worked on the steel of the Empire State and Chrysler buildings. Mohawk ironworkers had done the same work on the bridges and buildings of the generation before. From the 1920s to the 1960s, Mohawk ironworkers' families lived in a Brooklyn neighborhood that people called Little Caughnawaga, after an older spelling of Kahnawake.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Where Great Migration families could live" -->
The Great Migration was the move of about 6 million Black Americans out of the South between 1910 and 1970. In its first ten years, from 1910 to 1920, the Black population of Chicago rose 148 percent. Detroit's rose 611 percent, to about seven times its 1910 size. Philadelphia's rose about 500 percent, and New York's roughly doubled.

Black families who arrived could not live wherever they chose. White property owners wrote rules to keep them out of neighborhoods. These rules were called restrictive covenants. A restrictive covenant is a clause, or section, written into the deed of a house. The deed is the legal paper that records who owns the house. The covenant forbade the owner to sell or rent the house to a Black buyer, and sometimes to Jewish or Asian buyers as well. Each later owner of the house had to obey the clause too. Through the 1920s, white owners in Chicago and across the country signed covenants into their deeds.

Judges enforced the covenants, which means they ordered people to obey them. Because of the covenants and the judges who enforced them, Black families could rent or buy homes in only a few districts. In Chicago that was a strip of the South Side called the Black Belt. Its own residents named it Bronzeville. In New York it was Harlem. The number of people who needed rooms in those districts kept rising. In 1948, in the case *Shelley v. Kraemer*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that courts could no longer enforce the covenants.

Landlords in the crowded districts split their flats, or apartments, into smaller units. A landlord divided one apartment into several one-room units and rented each one separately. Each unit had a hotplate, a small electric burner for cooking, and the families shared one bathroom down the hall. These units were called kitchenettes. Bronzeville had more than 80,000 of them. For each square foot of floor, families in the kitchenettes paid more rent than white families paid in white neighborhoods.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The maps with red lines" -->
In 1933, during the Depression, federal officials set up an agency called the Home Owners' Loan Corporation. Its staff refinanced mortgages. A mortgage is a loan to buy a house. The agency's staff bought more than a million home loans from lenders. They replaced each one with a new 15-year loan at 5 percent interest, easier terms than banks then gave. By 1936 they had refinanced about one in ten home loans outside farms. Between 1935 and 1940, the agency's staff also made maps of 239 cities, called "Residential Security" maps.

The staff went through each city neighborhood by neighborhood. They gave each neighborhood a grade for how safe they judged it was to lend money on the houses there. A was the best grade, shaded green on the map, followed by B in blue and C in yellow. D was the worst grade. The maps described D neighborhoods as "hazardous," and the staff shaded them red. The word redlining comes from these red areas. To redline a neighborhood is to draw a line around it on a map and then refuse to lend money for houses inside that line.

The appraisers were the staff members who judged each neighborhood. Their written notes describe Black families moving in as "infiltration," a word for an enemy sneaking in. If Black families lived in a neighborhood, that all but guaranteed it a D.

Scholars disagree about how much these particular maps changed who got loans. In a 2017 study, economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago found lasting harm in the neighborhoods graded red. In a 2021 study, the economic historian Price Fishback and three other scholars found that the maps had little effect on where the government lent. They also found that the Federal Housing Administration, another federal agency, had worked out its own way of redlining before these maps were drawn. Both sides agree on what that agency did. Federal Housing Administration officials refused to insure home loans in the neighborhoods where Black homeowners lived. They backed loans mostly on new houses, which were sold almost only to white buyers.

In a study published in 2018, researchers at the National Community Reinvestment Coalition compared the old maps with present-day data. They found that about 74 percent of the neighborhoods graded red are low-to-moderate income today, which means the families there earn well below the typical family in their area. About 64 percent are majority-minority, which means most of the people living there are not white. Researchers at the University of Richmond scanned the maps for their Mapping Inequality project and put them online. Anyone can look up what the appraisers wrote about a street.

From 1935 to 1943, crews worked for the Works Progress Administration (WPA), a federal program that hired people to build public works. They laid about 24,000 miles of storm and sewer lines, which carry away rainwater and waste, and about 19,700 miles of water mains, the large pipes that bring clean water to a city. WPA crews also built about 500 water-treatment plants, more than 4,000 new schools, hundreds of hospitals, and airports. On the roads, they built or improved about 651,000 miles. Different sources give different totals, so each figure is about right, not exact.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Oak Ridge: a secret city on taken farmland" -->
On September 19, 1942, Brigadier General Leslie Groves, the Army officer who had just been put in charge of the Manhattan Project, approved taking about 59,000 acres along the Clinch River in eastern Tennessee. Army officials called the site "Site X." In October 1942, officers of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers took the land. About 1,000 families lived on that land. Some farmed it, and others lived in its small crossroads communities: Wheat, Elza, Scarboro (also spelled Scarborough) and Robertsville, with New Bethel and New Hope also named. Many of the families had farmed the same ground for generations. Accounts of how many people lived there differ. The Tennessee Encyclopedia states roughly 4,000 people, and other accounts state more than 3,000.

To take the land, the Corps of Engineers officers filed a paper called a declaration of taking, under eminent domain. Eminent domain is the power of a government to take private property whether or not the owner wants to sell, at a price the government sets. A declaration of taking lets the government take the land right away and settle the price later. Army officials paid an average of $46.86 an acre.

Army officials told the residents they had to leave, sometimes only by notices nailed to their front fence posts. The Tennessee Encyclopedia's account of the removals does not name the men who put up the notices. Families who had lived there for generations got a few weeks to go, and within a year all the families were gone. Most families accepted the price the government set. A few sued in court over what their land had been valued at. About an eighth of Roane County's land and about a seventh of Anderson County's land went into the site.

From 1942, workers built a city for the Army on that ground. The site's official name was the Clinton Engineer Works, and the city built there is Oak Ridge. The plans were for about 13,000 people. By 1945 about 75,000 lived there, which for a short time made it the fifth-largest city in Tennessee.

Workers built about 3,000 houses from cemesto, ready-made panels of cement and asbestos, a mineral fiber. The houses came in a few standard types named by letters, such as the A house and the B house, so people called them alphabet houses. Workers also put up flat-tops, flat-roofed houses that the Tennessee Valley Authority made ahead of time in pieces and that crews put together on the site. The city had schools, buses, a 300-bed hospital and a symphony orchestra. Fences and gates surrounded it, and people had to show a badge to get in or out.

Oak Ridge did not appear on any published map. Neither did Los Alamos in New Mexico or Hanford and Richland in Washington, which workers built the same way for the same program. The plants at Oak Ridge separated the uranium for the atomic bomb that American forces dropped on Hiroshima. Most of the more than 70,000 people who lived and worked there did not know what they were making until the bombs fell. They had promised not to talk about their jobs, even to each other.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Colleen Black

> **Who:** A teenager from Nashville whose family moved to a city left off every published map, and who spent the rest of her life there.
> **When and where:** Oak Ridge, Tennessee, from 1944 until her death in March 2015.

Colleen Black was born Colleen Rowan, one of more than ten children in a Nashville family. In 1944 the family moved to Oak Ridge for war work. They lived in a trailer camp. The ground was mud, with boardwalks laid across it, and people needed a pass to get through the gates.

At nineteen, Colleen worked as a leak detector at the K-25 plant. Her employer was Ford, Bacon & Davis, a contractor, which is a company hired to do a job. Her job was to find holes in the welded joints of pipes, the places where two pieces of metal had been melted together. She climbed over the pipes with a probe that sensed helium, looking for pinhole leaks. Helium atoms are small enough to escape through a flaw too fine to see. Where the probe found helium, the weld was leaking. The plant had hundreds of miles of pipe, and the workers were not even supposed to say the word "helium." Years later she said, "I didn't know where those pipes were going, I didn't know what was going through them ... I just knew that I had to find the leak and mark it."

She learned what the plant had been separating only after American forces dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima, in Japan, on August 6, 1945. The bomb killed between 90,000 and 166,000 people in the city within two to four months, according to the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, a research body run by Japan and the United States. An American survey in 1946 gave a lower count, 70,000 to 80,000. The exact number is not known.

She married a man called Blackie Black and stayed in Oak Ridge for the rest of her life. In 2005 she gave an oral history, a recorded interview about her life, to the Atomic Heritage Foundation's Voices of the Manhattan Project. The K-25 Virtual Museum includes her account. She is featured in Denise Kiernan's 2013 book *The Girls of Atomic City*. She also appeared on television programs, including NBC Nightly News and the History Channel.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
St. Louis lost about 60 percent of its people between 1950 and 2000, and Detroit lost nearly half. Families moved out of the centers of older American cities to new suburbs at their edges. Cities in the hot South and West that had been small in 1950 grew into some of the largest in the country.

At the edges of metropolitan areas, which are cities together with their suburbs, builders put up the same kind of neighborhood again and again. It was a subdivision, a tract of land divided into lots for single-family houses. White families bought those houses with mortgages that federal officials insured. Federal officials refused to insure the same kind of mortgage in the neighborhoods where Black families lived.

In the old centers, officials cleared whole neighborhoods and cut freeways through others. Under the Housing Act of 1949, city officials labeled neighborhoods blighted, which meant run-down enough to tear down, and took the property by law. They had the buildings torn down, sold the cleared land to developers, and moved at least 300,000 families out of their homes. Federal officials also paid for new freeways with money from a highway law of 1956. Highway planners ran many of those freeways through poor neighborhoods, most of them home to Black families and other people of color.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Urban renewal: clearing neighborhoods for developers" -->
In 1949, members of Congress voted for the Housing Act of 1949. Under Title I, one section of that law, federal officials offered money to city governments for a program the law identifies as "urban redevelopment." People later called it urban renewal.

Blighted was a legal label for an area that officials judged run-down enough to tear down. Once city officials had put that label on an area, they took the property in it by eminent domain, the government power to take private property at a price the government sets, and had crews tear the buildings down. Then they sold the cleared land to private developers for much less than it had cost to take and clear, and federal money covered the gap. In 1954, in *Berman v. Parker*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that this was allowed.

Officials ran the program from 1949 to 1974, in more than 400 cities and on more than 1,200 projects. Crews tore down about 2,000 neighborhoods. Officials moved at least 300,000 families out of their homes, and that is the lowest count the records support. Counting people instead of families, researchers estimate about 1.2 million. Black Americans were about 13 percent of the country's population in 1960, and they were at least 55 percent of the people forced out. These figures come from Brent Cebul's 2020 article in the Boston Review and from the Digital Scholarship Lab's "Renewing Inequality" project.

In a filmed interview in San Francisco in 1963, the writer James Baldwin said that urban renewal "means Negro removal." Negro was the usual word for Black Americans at the time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Highways through neighborhoods" -->
On June 29, 1956, President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act. The Act provided about $25 to $26 billion for a system of 41,000 miles of freeway.

Highway planners laid many of the routes where land was cheap and where the people who lived there were least able to organize against them. Those were mostly poor neighborhoods of Black families and other people of color. Builders defended taking land in Black neighborhoods by saying it was the cheapest. It was cheap partly because redlining had kept loans and money out of those neighborhoods for years.

Rondo, a neighborhood in St. Paul, Minnesota, is one well-documented case. About 80 percent of St. Paul's Black residents lived there. Between 1956 and 1968, crews building Interstate 94 cut a trench through the neighborhood. The Minnesota Historical Society's guides to Rondo do not name the officials who chose the route.

Sources give different counts of what Rondo lost. Guides from the Minnesota Historical Society state about 600 families. ReConnect Rondo and reporting in Sahan Journal state about 700 homes and 300 businesses, and they record that Rondo lost 61 percent of its residents. One in eight of St. Paul's Black residents lost a home to the building of Interstate 94.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="marvin-roger-anderson" name="Marvin Roger Anderson" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Marvin Roger Anderson

> **Who:** A young man whose family lost its home and its apartment buildings to a freeway, and who has spent his later years working to build a land bridge over it.
> **When and where:** Rondo, St. Paul, Minnesota. His family learned of the freeway in 1958.

Marvin Roger Anderson was born in 1940 and grew up in Rondo. His father built Rangh Court there, twelve two-story townhomes, in 1947 or 1948. Accounts give both years. In his own words, his father got "the largest Federal Housing Authority mortgage backed loan ever given to nonwhite applicants in the state" to build them. In 1958, the year Anderson finished Central High School, the family learned that the new Interstate 94 would run through their land. State officials condemned the buildings for the freeway, which means they took them by law, and called the homes "inferiorly constructed." Crews loaded the buildings onto flatbed trucks and moved them away. Four of them still stand, on other ground, on the University of Minnesota's St. Paul campus.

Anderson became the State Law Librarian of Minnesota, the librarian in charge of the state's law library. In 1983 he and Floyd Smaller founded Rondo Days, a yearly festival, to keep alive "the history, culture and memories of the Rondo that existed before the highway."

He now leads ReConnect Rondo. Its members propose to build a lid over the freeway trench. The lid would be a land bridge of about 21 acres, with homes, businesses and community space on top. Early design studies for the land bridge list between 350 and 1,400 homes.

The *Voices of Rondo* oral-history collection records his account, and so do press interviews.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="marvin-roger-anderson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Sprawl, and the people who left the old centers" -->
Levittown, on Long Island in New York, opened in 1947. By 1951 its builders had put up more than 17,000 houses there. They mass-produced the houses, building the same house over and over. The builders, Levitt and Sons, split the work into 27 steps and trained each crew to do one step. The houses stayed in place, and the crews moved from house to house. At the busiest time, about 30 houses went up in a day. The houses sold at first for $7,990. The lease for the first houses said they could not be "used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race," meaning white people. In 1960 every family in Levittown was still white.

Builders at the edges of other cities copied the pattern. Spreading a city outward in subdivisions of houses like these is called sprawl.

In the same fifty years, the older cities lost people. Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 951,270 in 2000. St. Louis had 856,796 in 1950 and 348,189 in 2000, a fall of roughly 60 percent. St. Louis kept losing people after that, down to about 300,000 by 2020.

Real estate agents and speculators, people who buy property to sell it again at a profit, sped up the change. They frightened white owners into selling cheaply by telling them Black families were moving in. Then they sold the same houses to Black families for much more, often "on contract." Under a contract sale, a buyer who missed a single payment could be thrown out and lose everything already paid. This practice was called blockbusting. In Chicago's North Lawndale neighborhood, about 3,000 buildings were sold on contract. North Lawndale went from 87 percent white in 1950 to 91 percent Black in 1960.

At the same time, federal officials insured mortgages on the new houses at the edge. To insure a mortgage, the government promised to repay the lender if the buyer could not. Officials set rules for which loans they would insure. Under those rules, they refused to insure mortgages in the neighborhoods where Black families lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jane-jacobs-robert-moses" name="Jane Jacobs and Robert Moses" movie="Citizen Jane: Battle for the City (2016)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jane Jacobs and Robert Moses

> **Who:** Robert Moses, who built roads, bridges and parks across New York for decades without ever winning an election, and Jane Jacobs, a writer who helped defeat three of the projects planned for the city.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1950s to 1968.
> **Movie:** *Citizen Jane: Battle for the City* (2016), a documentary directed by Matt Tyrnauer, 92 minutes.

Robert Moses held as many as twelve appointed posts at the same time, including parks commissioner and head of the Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority. Officials gave him every one of those posts, and he never won an election to any of them. From those posts he directed the building of 13 bridges, 2 tunnels, 658 playgrounds, 17 state parks, and hundreds of miles of parkways and expressways, which are wide roads for fast traffic. To build the Cross-Bronx Expressway, Moses's crews cut a path through crowded blocks of the Bronx and forced thousands of families out of their homes.

Jane Jacobs lived in Greenwich Village in New York and wrote about cities. She had no training in planning. In 1961 she published *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. In it she argued that officials were wrong to condemn crowded old neighborhoods as failures. She wrote that a busy street with both homes and shops stays safe because of "eyes on the street," the people who use it and watch it.

Jacobs and her neighbors defeated three projects. In 1958 they stopped a road planned through Washington Square Park. In 1961 and 1962 they beat city officials' attempt to label their own West Village a slum and clear it under urban renewal. The third was the Lower Manhattan Expressway, a road ten lanes wide, raised above the street, planned across what is now SoHo and Little Italy. Jacobs led the Joint Committee to Stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway. Police arrested her at a public hearing in April 1968. The charges included inciting to riot, which means urging a crowd to violence, and they were later reduced. Jacobs moved to Toronto in 1968. In 1969 Mayor John Lindsay declared the expressway "dead for all time," and it was taken off the city's map that year.

Robert Caro's book about Moses, *The Power Broker*, came out in 1974 and won a Pulitzer Prize in 1975. It is the standard account of Moses's career, and it finds fault with much of what he did. Some more recent historians argue that parts of his record, especially his parks and swimming pools, deserve more credit than Caro gives them. Historians have not settled that argument.

<!-- hb-story:start slug="carl-stokes" name="Carl Stokes" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Carl Stokes

> **Who:** The first Black mayor of a major American city, elected in the years when officials were clearing neighborhoods and building freeways through cities.
> **When and where:** Cleveland, Ohio. Elected on November 7, 1967, and mayor from January 1, 1968, to 1971.

Carl Stokes was the grandson of a man who had been enslaved. His mother was a widow, and she raised him in the Outhwaite public housing in Cleveland. Public housing is apartments owned by a government and rented to families with low incomes.

On November 7, 1967, Cleveland's voters elected Stokes mayor. He won about 50.5 percent of the vote and defeated Seth Taft. The 1960 census had counted 876,050 people in Cleveland, the eighth-largest city in the country. Because Cleveland was one of the ten largest cities, Stokes counts as the first Black mayor of a major American city. On the same day, voters in Gary, Indiana, a much smaller city, elected Richard Hatcher their mayor. Stokes was re-elected in 1969 and served until 1971.

He had campaigned on housing, on bringing money into neighborhoods that had gone without it, and on the city's pollution. On June 22, 1969, an oil slick and piled-up trash on the Cuyahoga River caught fire under a railroad bridge, set off by sparks from a passing train. It burned for about twenty minutes and damaged two railroad bridges. It was at least the thirteenth fire on that river since 1868. For a century, people had used the working river as a sewer. The next day Stokes held a press conference on the riverbank and demanded that state and federal officials act. In October 1972 the members of Congress passed the Clean Water Act over President Richard Nixon's veto, and Stokes was one of the local leaders who had pushed for it.

Stokes became mayor of a city that was losing jobs and people. Black mayors followed him in other big cities. By 1972 voters had elected Kenneth Gibson in Newark, James McGee in Dayton and Ted Berry in Cincinnati. In 1973 they elected Tom Bradley in Los Angeles, Maynard Jackson in Atlanta and Coleman Young in Detroit.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Growth in the Sun Belt" -->
While Detroit and St. Louis lost people, cities in the Sun Belt, the warm states of the South and the West, gained them. Phoenix, Arizona, had about 106,818 people in 1950 and 1,321,045 in 2000. Its metropolitan area went from about 323,000 people to about 3,014,000 in the same fifty years. The city also grew in area. It covered about 17 square miles in 1950, more than 475 square miles by the 2000s, and about 518 square miles now. City officials added that land by annexation. Annexation means that city officials move the city's legal boundary out to take in neighboring land. Houston, Texas, went from 596,163 people in 1950 to 1,953,631 in 2000, and by 1990 it was the fourth-largest city in the country.

Air conditioners cooled the air inside homes and offices, so people in Phoenix and Houston could live and work indoors through the hot summers. Land at the edge of these cities was cheap, and city officials kept annexing it. In the 1950s alone, Phoenix more than quadrupled in population.

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In the 2000s, people moved back into the downtowns of most big American cities. During the coronavirus pandemic, from 2020, working from home became normal. Companies gave up office space, and by 2024 about a fifth of American office space stood empty.

Rents and prices have risen in the neighborhoods that buyers and lenders have returned to. Some of the people who lived there can no longer afford to stay. Owners and city officials are deciding what to do with more than 900 million square feet of empty office space. Cities also run several degrees hotter than the land around them, and in Miami Beach the sea now floods some streets at high tide. City officials there have raised streets and put in pumps.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Downtown living and empty offices" -->
Between 2000 and 2010, the downtowns of big American cities grew faster than the areas around them, according to census counts studied by researchers at the Brookings Institution. Of 53 downtowns they studied, 42 gained people. Young adults had already begun moving in. By 2000, people aged 25 to 34 made up nearly a quarter of downtown residents, up from 13 percent in 1970.

During the coronavirus pandemic of 2020, employers closed their offices and their staff worked from home. Afterward many kept working from home for some or all of the week. Companies shrank the office space they rented, and as their leases, the contracts for renting the space, ran out, they gave up floors. By 2024 about 20 percent of American office space stood empty. Figures for that year run from about 19.6 to 20.1 percent. That was the highest share in thirty years, and it came to more than 900 million square feet of empty floor. In downtown Manhattan the share was about 23 percent. The source of these figures is Moody's Analytics, a research company, as reported by Axios and ABC News in January 2024.

Building owners and city officials began turning empty office towers into apartments. In 2024, builders had plans under way to make more than 55,300 apartments out of former office buildings. That was a record, and more than four times the number in 2021. The Washington, D.C., area led, with 5,820 apartments planned. In 2025, builders had finished, or were working on, 11.8 million square feet of former office space as apartments, the highest figure on record. The source of the 2024 figures is the research firm RentCafe, as reported in Time in January 2024. The sources of the 2025 figure are industry reports from CBRE and Brevitas. None of these figures are government statistics.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Gentrification, and who can no longer afford to stay" -->
Gentrification is the change that follows when buyers and lenders return to a neighborhood they had avoided for years. New owners fix up the houses, and landlords and sellers raise rents and prices. The people who lived there through the years without investment can then no longer afford to stay.

Buyers and lenders have returned to close-in neighborhoods, the ones near downtown, that appraisers had marked red in the 1930s. The work of the National Community Reinvestment Coalition and the Mapping Inequality project documents that pattern.

Washington, D.C., is one documented case. In 1970 the city was 71 percent Black, and people called it Chocolate City. By 2011 the Black share of the population had fallen below half, to 49.2 percent, for the first time in about fifty years. Those are census figures. New money went mostly into Shaw, U Street and Columbia Heights, close-in neighborhoods that appraisers had redlined. Around U Street, builders put up more than 2,000 expensive condominiums and apartments between 1997 and 2007. A condominium is an apartment that each owner buys. The neighborhood went from about 60 percent Black in 2000 to about 20 percent Black in 2010.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="virginia-ali" name="Virginia Ali" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Virginia Ali

> **Who:** A co-founder of Ben's Chili Bowl, a diner on U Street in Washington, D.C., who has helped run it since 1958 while the neighborhood around it changed.
> **When and where:** 1213 U Street NW, Washington, D.C., from August 22, 1958.

Virginia Rollins came from Chance, Virginia. Her husband, Mahaboob Ali, who went by Ben, had come from Trinidad. On August 22, 1958, the two of them opened a diner at 1213 U Street NW with a bank loan of $5,000.

People then called U Street "Black Broadway." Hundreds of Black-owned businesses, churches and theaters lined it and the streets nearby. At the Lincoln Theatre and the Howard Theatre, Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong and Billie Holiday performed.

On April 4, 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was shot and killed in Memphis, Tennessee. James Earl Ray later pleaded guilty to his murder. For the next four days, riots tore through Washington, and the corner of 14th and U was at the center of them. Thirteen people died, and about 1,200 homes and businesses were damaged. Stokely Carmichael, a leader of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, a civil rights group, asked the Alis to keep the Bowl open past the curfew, an order to stay off the streets at night. According to the shop's own history, they did, and they fed police officers and activists. Ben wrote "Soul Brother" in soap on the front window.

In the mid-1970s, drug dealing at 14th and U rose sharply. From 1986 to 1991, crews digging the Metro, Washington's subway, tore up U Street. From the 1990s, buyers and lenders returned, and the new condominiums went up around the Bowl.

Ben's is still open, and Virginia Ali, in her nineties, is still one of the people who run it. She speaks on the record about how the neighborhood around the diner has changed.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="virginia-ali" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Building for heat and water" -->
According to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), daytime air in a city is 1 to 6 degrees Fahrenheit warmer than in the areas around it. At night the air can be up to 22 degrees warmer. A roof or road in the sun can be 50 to 90 degrees hotter than the air above it. EPA scientists explain why. Roads, sidewalks and buildings soak up much of the sun's heat and hold it. A city has fewer trees and plants than the country around it, so it gets less shade and less cooling from water drying off leaves. Tall buildings and narrow streets also block cooling breezes. The EPA's list of ways to cool a city includes planting trees, growing plants on roofs, and building roofs and pavements that reflect sunlight.

In Miami Beach, Florida, streets flood on clear days with no storm, when the tide is at its highest. At those tides the ocean pushes the water of Biscayne Bay back up the storm drains, and bay water pours out of the drains into the lowest streets. Seawater also rises up through the soft, holey limestone under the city. In the fall of 2014, crews began raising streets, including West Avenue and Sunset Harbour, and putting in pumps to push the water back out.

Reports from 2016 and 2017 record twelve pump stations along the west side of South Beach. Early reports stated a cost of about $400 million. Later reports stated about $600 million, after the work was expanded. The highest tides of the year are called King Tides in Miami Beach. During the King Tides after the work, the raised and pumped blocks stayed mostly dry.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tallest buildings now stand in other countries" -->
Since 1998, the tallest building in the world has stood in Asia.

Chicago's Sears Tower, finished in 1974, was the last building in the United States to be the tallest in the world. It lost that title because of a rule about how to measure height. In April 1996, members of the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat ruled that a spire designed as part of a building counts toward its height. Under that rule, the Petronas Towers in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, measured 1,483 feet to the tops of their spires. In 1998 they became the tallest buildings in the world. The Sears Tower's roof and its highest floor that people used were both higher than those of the Petronas Towers.

The Burj Khalifa in Dubai, finished in 2010, stands 2,717 feet (828 meters). That is more than 900 feet taller than the tallest building in the United States.

One World Trade Center opened in New York in 2014 at 1,776 feet, a height its builders chose for 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence. Workers set the last piece of its spire on May 10, 2013. That November, members of the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat ruled that its spire counts toward its height. It is the tallest building in the Western Hemisphere, the half of the world that holds North and South America. Mohawk ironworkers worked on its steel, as Mohawk ironworkers had on the towers of the 1930s and on the bridges before them.
