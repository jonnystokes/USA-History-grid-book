## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Rochester, New York, grew from about 1,500 people in 1820 to about 12,630 in 1834, after the Erie Canal reached it. In the first half of the 1800s, people built cities on sites that had been a crossroads, a mud flat, or a small coastal settlement only ten years before. They built them where canals and rivers ran, because boats could stop there. Mill owners put up mills where the boats stopped, and workers came to work in them.

In New York, people lived packed close together, with no piped water. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 of them. In December 1835 a fire burned about seventeen blocks while the firefighters ran short of water. Because of those two disasters, New York's leaders had workers bring clean water into the city from the Croton River, forty-one miles away. The water arrived in 1842.

In 1811 three men appointed by New York's state lawmakers finished a plan of straight streets for all of Manhattan north of the built-up town. Much of that ground was still farms, woods and swamp, and nobody had leveled its hills. For about sixty years afterward, workers cut down hills and filled in low ground to make the island fit the plan.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boomtowns" -->
A boomtown is a place that grows from almost nothing into a city in about ten years. Rochester, Chicago and San Francisco all grew that way between 1800 and 1850.

The City of Rochester's published history describes Rochester as America's first boomtown, and so does the Rochester Voices project of the Monroe County Library. The Erie Canal ran through Rochester from 1825, and workers finished the whole canal across New York that October. About 1,500 people lived in Rochester in 1820. Census takers counted 9,269 there in 1830. By 1834, the year Rochester became a city, about 12,630 people lived there. That was more than eight times as many people as in 1820. Wheat came in by canal boat, and flour went out the same way. By 1838 the mills at Rochester ground more flour than the mills of any other American city, and people called it the Flour City.

Chicago became a town in 1833, when about 350 people lived there. A charter is an official paper from state lawmakers that lists the powers of a town or city government. Chicago got its city charter on March 4, 1837, when 4,170 people lived there. People settled at that spot because a short river met Lake Michigan there, so boats could stop. The ground was low, flat and wet, and rainwater and waste did not drain off it. Starting in 1858, crews raised Chicago's streets and buildings so that sewers could drain it.

About 200 people lived in San Francisco in 1846. By 1852, during the California Gold Rush, about 36,000 people lived there. Nobody had planned a city for that many people. They lived in tents and in hulks, which are the hulls of old ships that no longer sailed. Great fires burned the town again and again, and people rebuilt it each time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1811 grid" -->
On April 3, 1807, New York's state lawmakers appointed three commissioners, men given the job of laying out the streets of Manhattan. They were Gouverneur Morris, John Rutherfurd and Simeon De Witt. They filed their plan in March 1811.

The plan used only one kind of street layout, a grid. It had twelve numbered avenues running the length of the island and 155 numbered cross streets running across it, which cut the land into rectangles. The commissioners drew the lines straight over hills, streams and outcrops without bending them. An outcrop is a place where bare rock sticks up out of the ground.

John Randel Jr. was the chief surveyor, a person who measures land and marks where lines fall on it. He was hired for the job at twenty. The surveyors walked the whole island and drove a marker into the ground at every corner of every future crossing. Most of that land was still farms, woods and swamp.

Workers then had to make the island match the drawing. They leveled the hills, filled in the low ground and dug the streets through, removing whatever stood in the way. The work took about sixty years to reach 155th Street.

The commissioners set aside very little land for parks, and Central Park was not in their plan. State lawmakers added it in 1853, in a separate law. People already lived on part of that ground, in a place called Seneca Village. By the fall of 1857 New York officials had made them all leave, and the next era describes how.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cholera, fire, and the Croton" -->
Cholera is a disease people catch by swallowing water that has human waste in it. It causes watery diarrhea so heavy that the body loses its water very fast. A person who cannot replace that water fast enough can die of it within a day. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 New Yorkers out of a population of about 250,000. That is about one person in every seventy.

New York had no piped water then. People drew water from wells and street pumps, bought it from carts, or caught rain in barrels. The wells were in the same ground that the privies drained into. A privy is an outdoor toilet built over a pit in the ground.

In December 1835 a fire started in lower Manhattan. It burned about seventeen blocks and destroyed between 600 and 700 buildings, and the damage came to about $20 million. Two people died in it. The firefighters could not get enough water onto the fire. There was little water to be had, and the hydrants and wells were frozen.

After the cholera and the fire, New York's leaders decided to bring in clean water from a river in the countryside, enough for drinking and for fighting fires. Workers began building the Croton Aqueduct in 1837, with John B. Jervis as chief engineer. An aqueduct is a channel built to carry water a long way. The workers dammed the Croton River north of the city. Then they dug and lined a stone tunnel forty-one miles long to carry the water down to Manhattan. Philadelphia's waterworks used steam pumps, but no pumps moved the Croton water. The tunnel sloped downhill the whole way, so gravity carried the water along it. The records identify Jervis but not the men who dug the tunnel.

Water entered the city on June 22, 1842. On July 4 the distributing reservoir at 42nd Street and Fifth Avenue began filling. It was a large stone basin that held the water and fed it out to the city's pipes. On October 14, 1842, New Yorkers held a celebration across the whole city. A parade seven miles long marched through the streets, and the new fountain in City Hall Park threw water fifty feet into the air. People drank what they called Croton cocktails.

The Croton Aqueduct was the first large city water supply in the United States that ran without a break.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-templeton-strong" name="George Templeton Strong" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Templeton Strong

> **Who:** A young New York lawyer whose private diary recorded what piped water meant inside one ordinary household.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1842.

George Templeton Strong was born in 1820 and was twenty-two when the Croton water arrived. He kept a diary from 1835 until his death in 1875, and it runs to about 2,250 pages. Historians use it as a first-hand record of New York in the 1800s. Two editors, Nevins and Thomas, published it in 1952.

In 1842, while the water was still on its way, Strong wrote that "Croton Water is slowly flowing towards the city, which at last will stand a chance of being cleaned." The sentence goes on: "if water can clean it."

When Croton water reached his house, Strong could turn a tap and take a bath at home. Almost nobody living in an American city had been able to do that before. His diary records how much he enjoyed it. Before Croton water came, New Yorkers got their water from a well, a street pump, a peddler's cart (a peddler is a seller who goes from street to street) or a rain barrel, and carried it home.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, builders made American cities taller, dug sewers under them, and spread them outward along streetcar lines.

Once elevators had a safety catch, people were willing to ride them to upper floors. Builders also began to hold up tall buildings with frames of iron and steel, so the outside walls no longer had to carry the weight.

In cities where people had thrown waste into the street or the nearest river, crews dug sewers and water tunnels. Chicago's ground was so flat and wet that a sewer could not run downhill through it. Crews there raised the streets over new sewer pipes and lifted the downtown buildings up to the new street level.

Electric streetcars began running in 1888. A ten-minute ride on one covered the distance of a thirty-minute walk, and people built houses along the tracks.

In New York in the same years, landlords housed poor families in tenements, apartment buildings put up to hold as many people as a lot would take. In the census of 1890, Chicago had 1,099,850 people. It was the second-largest city in the country, after New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Raising Chicago's streets and buildings" -->
Chicago's ground was almost level and sat barely above Lake Michigan. Waste had nowhere to drain, so it stayed where it was, and the streets were mud. A sewer is an underground pipe that carries waste water away. The sewers of the 1850s had no pumps, so a sewer worked only if it ran downhill, and under Chicago there was no downhill for it to follow.

In December 1855 the members of the Board of Sewerage Commissioners, the officials in charge of Chicago's sewers, hired Ellis S. Chesbrough. He had been the water engineer in Boston. He published his plan in 1856, and it was the first plan for a complete sewer system covering a whole American city. Workers did not dig the sewers down into the flat ground. They laid the pipes on top of the existing streets and then buried them under several feet of new fill, which is earth dumped on top. That raised the level of the whole street. Every building along it now sat below its own street, and its ground floor became a cellar.

Crews then lifted the buildings up to the new street level. Their tool was the jackscrew, a screw inside a case that a man turned by hand with a lever. Each turn raised a heavy weight a fraction of an inch. Crews set hundreds of jackscrews under a building's foundation, and the men turned them all together, a little at a time, so the load rose evenly and the walls did not crack.

In January 1858 crews raised the first masonry building, one built of brick or stone, this way. It was a four-story brick block at Randolph and Dearborn streets that weighed about 750 tons. It went up 6 feet 2 inches on 200 jackscrews. Newspaper reports of the time state that it rose "without the slightest injury" to the building. That same year crews raised more than fifty buildings like it.

In 1860 a group of engineers that included George Pullman raised half a city block. It was the row of brick and stone buildings on Lake Street between Clark and LaSalle streets, 320 feet long. With its sidewalks, the row weighed an estimated 35,000 tons. About 600 men worked about 6,000 jackscrews, and the block went up 4 feet 8 inches in five days. The shopkeepers kept their shops open the whole time. Customers went on walking in and buying things while the floor under them rose.

Every figure for these raisings comes from Chicago newspaper reports of 1858 and 1860, so each one is approximate.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Up: the elevator and the steel frame" -->
Before buildings could rise higher than people were willing to climb, builders needed two things: an elevator people trusted, and a frame to hold the building up.

Elevators could already lift loads. The danger was that the rope could break and drop the platform. In 1854 Elisha Otis showed his safety catch at the Crystal Palace exposition in New York, a large public show of new machines and goods. He stood on a raised platform in front of a crowd and had the rope cut with an axe. His safety catch gripped, and the platform stopped.

On March 23, 1857, workers put the first commercial passenger elevator in the country into the E. V. Haughwout Building at 488 Broadway in New York. A commercial elevator is one sold for everyday use in a building. A steam engine drove it. It cost $300 and rose about two-thirds of a foot per second. Before elevators, the rooms at the top of a building rented for the least, because people had to climb the stairs to reach them. Once elevators ran, landlords charged the most for the top floors.

In a wall-bearing building, the outside walls hold up everything. The taller the building, the thicker the walls at the bottom have to be. Past a certain height the ground floor would be mostly brick and stone, with no room left for windows or doors. In a framed building, a skeleton of iron or steel columns and beams carries the weight. The walls hang on that frame as a covering.

The Home Insurance Building in Chicago is the building most often called the first skyscraper, which means a very tall building held up by a metal frame. William Le Baron Jenney designed it, and it was finished in 1885. It stood ten stories and 138 feet tall, and builders added two more floors in 1890. Its frame of iron and steel carried loads that walls had carried in older buildings. Workers tore it down in 1931 to make room for the Field Building.

Historians argue over that title. The Home Insurance Building was not built all of steel, and it was not the first building with a metal frame. Jenney's own account of the building, written in 1885, describes mostly its foundations, not a new way of holding up a building. Some historians trace the labels "first skyscraper" and "father of the skyscraper" partly to a later publicity campaign. People close to Jenney ran it, along with Chicago boosters, people who promoted their city to bring in money and business. This work by historians goes by the name of the "Jenney Myth." Jason Barr makes the argument in his book *Building the Skyline* and in his Skynomics writing. Other writers name other buildings as the first. New York's Equitable Life Building of 1870 was the first office building with an elevator. The Produce Exchange of 1884 had an inner skeleton designed by George B. Post. The Tacoma Building went up in 1889.

Historians do agree on what builders did with the frame and the elevator. Starting in the 1880s, builders in Chicago and New York used them to roughly triple the height of downtown buildings within one generation.

In 1893 the World's Columbian Exposition, a world's fair, opened in Chicago. Daniel Burnham directed the building work, and Frederick Law Olmsted drew the site plan, which is the layout of the grounds. Workers put up a matched set of white buildings in the neoclassical style, which copies the columns and shapes of ancient Greek and Roman buildings. Electric lights lit them, and people called the fair the White City. The buildings were made of plaster over frames, not of stone, and were built to last one season. Millions of visitors walked through them. Visitors took away the idea that a whole city could be planned as one design. Up to then, American cities had been put together one building at a time. Planners in the next era built on that idea in what they called the City Beautiful movement.

Ironworkers from two Mohawk communities, Kahnawake and Akwesasne, helped build bridges and tall buildings across the Northeast from 1886 on. That year, at a bridge job on Mohawk land at Kahnawake, near Montreal, Mohawk men started working high steel. Working high steel means joining the steel frames of bridges and buildings high above the ground. From Kahnawake and Akwesasne, the men traveled to bridge and skyscraper jobs wherever the steel work was, and they called that "booming out." An exhibition at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, *Booming Out: Mohawk Ironworkers Build New York*, documents their work. It follows Mohawk ironworkers into the next century, to the Empire State Building, the Chrysler Building, the George Washington Bridge and the World Trade Center.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement" -->
A tenement is an apartment building put up cheaply to hold as many poor families as its lot will take. New York landlords housed most of the city's newly arrived poor people in tenements. For decades, no New York law required a tenement landlord to provide anything for the people who lived there.

New York's state lawmakers first set rules for tenements in the Tenement House Act of 1879, which people later called the Old Law. Under it, every room a person lived in had to have a window that opened to outside air. Builders met that rule with the dumbbell tenement.

Builders pinched each building in at its middle, so that a narrow shaft of open air ran up its side from the ground to the roof. Rooms in the middle of the building got their required window onto that shaft. Seen from above, the building was wide at both ends and thin in the middle. A dumbbell, a weight for exercise with a heavy ball at each end of a short bar, has the same shape, and the building took its name from it.

The shaft was too narrow and too deep to bring daylight or moving air down below the top floors, so most of the rooms it was built for stayed dark and stale. Tenants threw garbage down it, and the garbage piled up at the bottom. The builders had not meant it to, but the shaft also worked as a flue, a channel that draws hot air and smoke upward, as a chimney does. A fire that started at the bottom of the shaft was pulled straight up it and spread to every floor on the way.

New York's state lawmakers first required windows facing outward in every room in the Tenement House Act of 1901. That law also banned new dumbbell tenements and required courtyards, indoor toilets and protection against fire.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jacob-riis" name="Jacob Riis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jacob Riis

> **Who:** A Danish immigrant and police reporter who was among the first Americans to use flash photography to show how the poor of a city lived.
> **When and where:** New York's tenement districts. *How the Other Half Lives* came out in 1890.

Jacob Riis used flash photographs as evidence in an argument about how poor families in New York lived. He was born on May 3, 1849, in Ribe, Denmark, and moved to the United States in 1870. He worked as a police reporter for the *New York Tribune* and then the *Evening Sun*. As a police reporter he worked nights in the neighborhoods where people called the police.

The rooms Riis wanted to show had no daylight, and a camera of that time needed bright light to take a picture. They were tenement apartments, cellars where men paid a few cents to sleep, and back rooms with no fresh air. Riis used magnesium flash. Magnesium powder burns for an instant with a light bright enough to record a picture on a photographic plate, a glass sheet coated with chemicals that change where light hits them. He carried the powder into those rooms and set it off.

In 1889 Riis published an eighteen-page article in *Scribner's* magazine. In 1890 he published the book *How the Other Half Lives*, with seventeen photographs and a number of engravings, pictures printed from lines cut into metal or wood. Readers who had never been inside a tenement could now see inside one. Historians credit the book with pushing New York's lawmakers to pass the state's first major tenement laws. Theodore Roosevelt, as New York City's police commissioner, became Riis's ally. Riis died in 1914.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-riis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bridget-joseph-moore" name="Bridget and Joseph Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bridget and Joseph Moore

> **Who:** An Irish immigrant waiter and his wife, one tenement family the records document, whose rooms still exist.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, on New York's Lower East Side, 1869.

Bridget Moore married at seventeen, in 1865. Joseph Moore worked as a waiter, and some of the Tenement Museum's accounts record him as a bartender. In 1869 the couple moved with their daughters into 97 Orchard Street, a tenement built in 1863. They were one of the few Irish families in a building where most people were German. They lived there about a year.

On April 21, 1869, their daughter Agnes Mary died at five months old. The cause given in the records was marasmus. Marasmus is severe malnutrition, a form of starvation. The body gets too little food of every kind, so it uses up its own fat and then its own muscle, and the child wastes away. That is how Agnes Mary died.

Researchers at the Tenement Museum rebuilt the family's life from church records, census records and death records. The museum's account states that two conditions in the city were tied to the deaths of babies at that time. One was swill milk. Dairy owners kept cows beside distilleries, where liquor was made, and fed the cows the spent mash left over from making it. They sold the milk cheaply in the city, and it made babies sick. The other condition was the building itself. The water at 97 Orchard Street came from a pump in the back yard, beside the privies.

Of the eight children Bridget and Joseph Moore had, four lived to be adults.

Their rooms are still there, and museum staff have rebuilt the apartment as it was. Between 1863 and 1935, when the owner sealed the floors where people lived, an estimated 7,000 people from more than twenty countries lived at 97 Orchard Street. The Moores are one of the families among them whose names appear in the records.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bridget-joseph-moore" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Central Park and Seneca Village" -->
The commissioners who drew the 1811 plan had left almost no land for public parks. In 1853 New York's state lawmakers set aside more than 700 acres in the middle of Manhattan for a park. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux won the design contest in 1858 with a plan they called Greensward, and building began that year.

People already lived on part of that land, in Seneca Village. In 1825 John and Elizabeth Whitehead had divided their farmland there into about 200 lots. On September 27 of that year, Andrew Williams, a twenty-five-year-old Black shoeshiner, bought the first three lots for $125. Epiphany Davis bought twelve lots for $578, and the AME Zion Church bought lots as well. By 1855 about 225 people lived in Seneca Village. Roughly two-thirds of them were African American, and Irish and German families lived there too. They had built three churches and a school.

Under New York law at the time, a Black man could vote only if he owned property worth a set amount. Several Black families in Seneca Village owned enough land to meet that rule, so the men in those households could vote.

New York officials took the whole park site by eminent domain. Eminent domain is the power of a government to take private property for public use. Officials pay the owner a price they set, whether the owner wants to sell or not. The sources for this chapter do not identify the officials who took the land. They took it from its owners, including Black families whose men could vote only because they owned that land. By the fall of 1857 the officials had forced everyone in Seneca Village to leave. Crews tore down the buildings, and workers began building the park in 1858.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-law-olmsted" name="Frederick Law Olmsted" movie="Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Law Olmsted

> **Who:** The designer, with Calvert Vaux, of Central Park, and the man who established landscape architecture as a profession in the United States.
> **When and where:** New York from 1858, and park work across the country through the 1890s.
> **Movie:** *Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America* (PBS, 2014), a documentary that premiered June 20, 2014.

Olmsted had been a farmer and a journalist. He had never designed a park when he and Calvert Vaux entered the Central Park contest in 1858 with the Greensward plan. They won, and then they ran the work of building the park. Landscape architecture, the work Olmsted made into a profession, means designing outdoor land: its paths, lawns, trees, water and roads.

Olmsted argued that the people who ran a crowded industrial city should give everyone living in it, including people who could not afford to leave the city, a piece of made countryside to walk into. Made countryside here means land that workers shaped to look like open country. He took the same argument to other cities. He worked on Prospect Park in Brooklyn and on the Emerald Necklace in Boston, a chain of connected parks. He also worked on parks and college campuses in dozens of other places. In 1893 he laid out the grounds of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago.

After Olmsted and Vaux's work, people in American cities expected their city governments to provide public parks, as they still do today.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-law-olmsted" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Out: streetcar lines and the first suburbs" -->
Before streetcars, a city could spread no farther than people were willing to walk to work. Frank J. Sprague changed that in Richmond, Virginia. On February 2, 1888, cars began running on a regular schedule on his Richmond Union Passenger Railway, an electric streetcar line. It started with ten cars on about twelve miles of track, and by May twenty-three cars ran on it. It was the first large electric street railway anywhere that worked reliably.

A ten-minute ride on a streetcar covered ground that took thirty minutes to walk. Within two years, about 110 electric railways using Sprague's equipment were built or under contract. Builders put up houses along the new lines, out past the old edge of town. For the first time, builders could spread a city outward along its tracks instead of only filling in land inside its edge. Those new neighborhoods were the first streetcar suburbs. A suburb is a neighborhood of homes at the edge of a city, where people live and from which they travel in to work.

In 1895 New York's Mayor Strong, who had been elected as a reformer to change how the city was run, appointed George E. Waring Jr. as street-cleaning commissioner. Waring held the job until 1898. He put about 2,000 street sweepers in white uniforms, and people called them the White Wings. Each day they cleaned about 450 miles of street. Waring set up the country's first planned systems for getting rid of waste and for recycling it. He also organized Juvenile Street Cleaning Leagues, clubs for children who helped keep their streets clean. Jacob Riis wrote that Waring's broom "saved more lives in the crowded tenements than a squad of doctors."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A city in one day" -->
At noon on April 22, 1889, the land run into Oklahoma began. A land run was a race: at a set signal, settlers rushed in to claim pieces of land. The land had belonged to Native nations, and it had been taken from them. The research behind this chapter does not identify those nations or the people who took the land.

That morning, Guthrie was a railroad station. By nightfall about 10,000 people were camped there. By the next morning the settlers had set up a provisional government, meaning a temporary one, along with a land office, a newspaper and three banks. All of them worked out of tents. Oklahoma City filled up on the same scale on the same day.

At Guthrie, surveying, laying out lots, setting up a government and starting a newspaper and a bank took from noon to the next morning. Colonial towns had taken years to do the same work. Philadelphia had needed a plan drawn on paper and a shipload of settlers sent out to it.
