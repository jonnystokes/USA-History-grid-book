## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Rochester, New York, grew from about 1,500 people in 1820 to about 12,630 in 1834, after the Erie Canal reached it. In the first half of the 1800s, people built cities on sites that had been a crossroads, a mud flat, or a small coastal settlement only ten years before. They built them where canals and rivers ran, because boats could stop there. Mill owners put up mills where the boats stopped, and workers came to work in them.

In New York, people lived packed close together, with no piped water. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 of them. In December 1835 a fire burned about seventeen blocks while the firefighters ran short of water. Because of those two disasters, New York's leaders had workers bring clean water into the city from the Croton River, forty-one miles away. The water arrived in 1842.

In 1811 three men appointed by New York's state lawmakers finished a plan of straight streets for all of Manhattan north of the built-up town. Much of that ground was still farms, woods and swamp, and nobody had leveled its hills. For about sixty years afterward, workers cut down hills and filled in low ground to make the island fit the plan.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boomtowns" -->
A boomtown is a place that grows from almost nothing into a city in about ten years. Rochester, Chicago and San Francisco all grew that way between 1800 and 1850.

The City of Rochester's published history describes Rochester as America's first boomtown, and so does the Rochester Voices project of the Monroe County Library. The Erie Canal ran through Rochester from 1825, and workers finished the whole canal across New York that October. About 1,500 people lived in Rochester in 1820. Census takers counted 9,269 there in 1830. By 1834, the year Rochester became a city, about 12,630 people lived there. That was more than eight times as many people as in 1820. Wheat came in by canal boat, and flour went out the same way. By 1838 the mills at Rochester ground more flour than the mills of any other American city, and people called it the Flour City.

Chicago became a town in 1833, when about 350 people lived there. A charter is an official paper from state lawmakers that lists the powers of a town or city government. Chicago got its city charter on March 4, 1837, when 4,170 people lived there. People settled at that spot because a short river met Lake Michigan there, so boats could stop. The ground was low, flat and wet, and rainwater and waste did not drain off it. Starting in 1858, crews raised Chicago's streets and buildings so that sewers could drain it.

About 200 people lived in San Francisco in 1846. By 1852, during the California Gold Rush, about 36,000 people lived there. Nobody had planned a city for that many people. They lived in tents and in hulks, which are the hulls of old ships that no longer sailed. Great fires burned the town again and again, and people rebuilt it each time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1811 grid" -->
On April 3, 1807, New York's state lawmakers appointed three commissioners, men given the job of laying out the streets of Manhattan. They were Gouverneur Morris, John Rutherfurd and Simeon De Witt. They filed their plan in March 1811.

The plan used only one kind of street layout, a grid. It had twelve numbered avenues running the length of the island and 155 numbered cross streets running across it, which cut the land into rectangles. The commissioners drew the lines straight over hills, streams and outcrops without bending them. An outcrop is a place where bare rock sticks up out of the ground.

John Randel Jr. was the chief surveyor, a person who measures land and marks where lines fall on it. He was hired for the job at twenty. The surveyors walked the whole island and drove a marker into the ground at every corner of every future crossing. Most of that land was still farms, woods and swamp.

Workers then had to make the island match the drawing. They leveled the hills, filled in the low ground and dug the streets through, removing whatever stood in the way. The work took about sixty years to reach 155th Street.

The commissioners set aside very little land for parks, and Central Park was not in their plan. State lawmakers added it in 1853, in a separate law. People already lived on part of that ground, in a place called Seneca Village. By the fall of 1857 New York officials had made them all leave, and the next era describes how.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cholera, fire, and the Croton" -->
Cholera is a disease people catch by swallowing water that has human waste in it. It causes watery diarrhea so heavy that the body loses its water very fast. A person who cannot replace that water fast enough can die of it within a day. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 New Yorkers out of a population of about 250,000. That is about one person in every seventy.

New York had no piped water then. People drew water from wells and street pumps, bought it from carts, or caught rain in barrels. The wells were in the same ground that the privies drained into. A privy is an outdoor toilet built over a pit in the ground.

In December 1835 a fire started in lower Manhattan. It burned about seventeen blocks and destroyed between 600 and 700 buildings, and the damage came to about $20 million. Two people died in it. The firefighters could not get enough water onto the fire. There was little water to be had, and the hydrants and wells were frozen.

After the cholera and the fire, New York's leaders decided to bring in clean water from a river in the countryside, enough for drinking and for fighting fires. Workers began building the Croton Aqueduct in 1837, with John B. Jervis as chief engineer. An aqueduct is a channel built to carry water a long way. The workers dammed the Croton River north of the city. Then they dug and lined a stone tunnel forty-one miles long to carry the water down to Manhattan. Philadelphia's waterworks used steam pumps, but no pumps moved the Croton water. The tunnel sloped downhill the whole way, so gravity carried the water along it. The records name Jervis but not the men who dug the tunnel.

Water entered the city on June 22, 1842. On July 4 the distributing reservoir at 42nd Street and Fifth Avenue began filling. It was a large stone basin that held the water and fed it out to the city's pipes. On October 14, 1842, New Yorkers held a celebration across the whole city. A parade seven miles long marched through the streets, and the new fountain in City Hall Park threw water fifty feet into the air. People drank what they called Croton cocktails.

The Croton Aqueduct was the first large city water supply in the United States that ran without a break.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-templeton-strong" name="George Templeton Strong" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Templeton Strong

> **Who:** A young New York lawyer whose private diary recorded what piped water meant inside one ordinary household.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1842.

George Templeton Strong was born in 1820 and was twenty-two when the Croton water arrived. He kept a diary from 1835 until his death in 1875, and it runs to about 2,250 pages. Historians use it as a first-hand record of New York in the 1800s. Two editors, Nevins and Thomas, published it in 1952.

In 1842, while the water was still on its way, Strong wrote that "Croton Water is slowly flowing towards the city, which at last will stand a chance of being cleaned." The sentence goes on: "if water can clean it."

When Croton water reached his house, Strong could turn a tap and take a bath at home. Almost nobody living in an American city had been able to do that before. His diary records how much he enjoyed it. Before Croton water came, New Yorkers got their water from a well, a street pump, a peddler's cart or a rain barrel, and carried it home. A peddler is a seller who goes from street to street.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900 American cities grew in three directions at once, and each direction was somebody's engineering problem.

They grew up. A safety catch on an elevator meant people would ride one, and a frame of iron and steel meant the outside walls no longer had to carry the building's weight.

They grew down. In cities where people had thrown waste into the street or the nearest river, crews began digging sewers and water tunnels, and in Chicago the ground was so flat and so wet that the only way to get a sewer to run downhill was to lift the entire downtown into the air.

And they grew out. Electric streetcars from 1888 put a ten-minute ride where a thirty-minute walk had been, and people built houses along the tracks.

In the same years, in New York, landlords housed poor families in buildings called tenements, built to hold as many people as a lot would take. In 1890 Chicago passed a million people — 1,099,850 — and was the second-largest city in the country after New York.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Chicago lifts itself out of the mud" -->
A sewer is a pipe that carries waste water away underground, and it has no pump. It works only if it runs downhill. Chicago's ground was almost level and barely above the lake, so there was no downhill to give it. Waste stayed where it was, and the streets were mud.

In December 1855 the Board of Sewerage Commissioners hired Ellis S. Chesbrough, who had been the water engineer in Boston. His plan, published in 1856, was the first comprehensive sewer system designed for an American city, and he solved the problem backwards. Instead of digging the sewers down into ground that had no fall, workers laid the pipes on top of the existing streets, then buried them under several feet of new fill. That raised the whole street level. Every building along it was now sunk below its own street, its ground floor turned into a cellar.

So people lifted the buildings.

The tool was the jackscrew: a screw in a housing, turned by hand with a lever, that raises a heavy weight a fraction of an inch at a time. Hundreds of them were set under a building's foundation, and men turned them together, a little at each turn, so the load rose evenly and the walls did not crack.

In January 1858 crews raised the first masonry building this way — a four-story brick block at Randolph and Dearborn, weighing about 750 tons. It went up 6 feet 2 inches on 200 jackscrews, and the contemporary press reported it done "without the slightest injury" to the building. More than fifty comparable raisings followed in that year alone.

In 1860 a group of contractors that included George Pullman took on half a city block: the row of brick and stone buildings on Lake Street between Clark and LaSalle, 320 feet long, estimated at about 35,000 tons with its sidewalks. About 600 men worked about 6,000 jackscrews. The block went up 4 feet 8 inches in five days. The shops stayed open the whole time and customers went on walking in and buying things while the floor under them rose.

These figures come from what the Chicago press reported at the time, in 1858 and 1860, which is why each of them is "about."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Up: the elevator and the steel frame" -->
Two things had to change before a building could go higher than people were willing to climb.

The first was the elevator, and the problem with it was not lifting — it was the rope breaking. In 1854, at the Crystal Palace exposition in New York, Elisha Otis stood on a hoisted platform in front of a crowd and had the rope cut with an axe. His safety catch gripped and the platform stopped. On March 23, 1857 the first commercial passenger elevator in the country went into the E. V. Haughwout Building at 488 Broadway in New York. It was driven by steam, cost $300, and rose about two-thirds of a foot per second. Until then the cheapest rooms in a building were at the top, because of the stairs. Afterward landlords charged the most for the top floors.

The second was the frame. In a wall-bearing building, the outside walls hold up everything, so the taller you go the thicker the walls at the bottom must be, until the ground floor is mostly masonry and there is no room left for windows or doors. A frame changes that: a skeleton of iron or steel columns and beams carries the weight, and the walls hang on it as a covering.

The building usually named as the first skyscraper is the Home Insurance Building in Chicago, designed by William Le Baron Jenney and finished in 1885 — ten stories, 138 feet, with two more floors added in 1890. Its frame of iron and steel carried loads the walls had carried before. Workers demolished it in 1931 to make room for the Field Building.

The title is disputed. It was not built all of steel, and it was not the first building with a metal frame. Jenney's own account in 1885 dwelt on the foundations rather than on any structural revolution. Historians who study this — the work is collected under the name of the "Jenney Myth," argued in Jason Barr's *Building the Skyline* and on his Skynomics writing — trace the "first skyscraper" and "father of the skyscraper" labels partly to a later publicity effort by Jenney's circle and by Chicago boosters. Other buildings are named as the first by other people: New York's Equitable Life Building of 1870, which was the first office building with an elevator; the Produce Exchange of 1884, with George B. Post's interior skeleton; the Tacoma Building of 1889.

What is not disputed is the result. In Chicago and New York in the 1880s, frame construction plus the elevator let builders roughly triple the height of a downtown within a single generation.

In 1893 Chicago held the World's Columbian Exposition. Daniel Burnham directed the works and Frederick Law Olmsted did the site plan, and together they put up a matched set of white neoclassical buildings lit by electricity, which people called the White City. The buildings were not stone. They were plaster over frames, built to last one season. Millions of visitors walked through them, and what they took away was the idea that a city could be planned as one thing rather than assembled building by building.

In 1886, at a bridge job on Mohawk land at Kahnawake near Montreal, Mohawk men started working high steel. From Kahnawake and from Akwesasne they went out to bridge and skyscraper work across the Northeast — "booming out," they called traveling to where the steel was. The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian documents that work in its exhibition *Booming Out: Mohawk Ironworkers Build New York*, and it runs forward through the towers of the next century. Cities in the Northeast were raised in part by the ironworkers of those two Mohawk communities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement" -->
A tenement is an apartment building put up cheaply to hold as many poor families as the lot will take. New York landlords housed most of the city's arriving poor in them, and for decades nothing in the law required a landlord to provide anything.

The first attempt was the Tenement House Act of 1879, which people later called the Old Law. Under it, every room a person lived in had to have a window opening to outside air. That sounds like a fix. What builders made of it was the dumbbell tenement.

Builders complied by pinching the building in at its middle, so that a narrow shaft of open air ran up the side of it from the ground to the roof. Rooms in the middle of the building got their required window onto that shaft. Seen from above the building was wide at both ends and thin at the waist, which is where the name came from.

The shaft did not work. It was too narrow and too deep to bring daylight or moving air down to the floors below the top, so most of the rooms it was built for stayed dark and stale. Tenants threw garbage down it, and it collected there. And it did one thing nobody had designed it to do: an open shaft running the full height of a building draws air upward the way a chimney does, so a fire starting at the bottom of it was pulled straight up and fed to every floor on the way.

New York's legislators did not require real light, real air, indoor toilets, and fire protection until the Tenement House Act of 1901, and that belongs to the next era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Jacob Riis

> **Who:** A Danish immigrant and police reporter who was among the first Americans to use flash photography to show how the poor of a city lived.
> **When and where:** New York's tenement districts; *How the Other Half Lives* published in 1890.

Jacob Riis was born on May 3, 1849 in Ribe, Denmark, and emigrated to the United States in 1870. He worked as a police reporter, for the *New York Tribune* and then the *Evening Sun*, which meant working nights in the districts where the police were called.

The rooms he wanted to show could not be photographed. Tenement interiors, cellars where men paid a few cents to sleep, and airless back rooms had no daylight in them, and a camera then needed light. What Riis used was magnesium flash: magnesium powder burns for an instant with a light bright enough to expose a photographic plate. He carried it into those rooms and set it off.

He published an eighteen-page article in *Scribner's* in 1889 and then, in 1890, the book *How the Other Half Lives*, with seventeen photographs in it along with engravings. Readers who had never set foot in a tenement could now see inside one. The book pushed the state into New York's first significant tenement legislation. Theodore Roosevelt, then the city's police commissioner, became his ally.

Riis died in 1914. What he had demonstrated was that a photograph could be used as evidence in an argument about how a city housed people.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-riis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bridget-joseph-moore" name="Bridget and Joseph Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bridget and Joseph Moore

> **Who:** An Irish immigrant waiter and his wife — one documented tenement family, whose rooms still exist.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, on New York's Lower East Side, 1869.

Bridget Moore was married at seventeen, in 1865. Joseph Moore worked as a waiter; some of the museum's tellings record him as a bartender. In 1869 the two of them moved with their daughters into 97 Orchard Street, a tenement built in 1863. They were one of the few Irish families in a building that was mostly German. They lived there about a year.

On April 21, 1869 their daughter Agnes Mary died. She was five months old, and the cause recorded was marasmus. Marasmus is starvation: the body takes in too little food of any kind, so it consumes its own fat and then its own muscle, and the child wastes away. That is what happened to her.

The Tenement Museum, which reconstructed the family's life from church records, census records, and death records, sets that death beside two things about how the city worked. One was swill milk — milk from cows kept beside distilleries and fed on the spent mash left over from making liquor, sold cheap in the city, and known to sicken infants. The other was the building itself: the water at 97 Orchard came from a pump in the back yard, standing beside the privies.

Of the eight children Bridget and Joseph Moore had, four reached adulthood.

Their rooms are still there. Museum staff rebuilt the apartment as it was. Between 1863 and 1935, when the owner sealed the residential floors, an estimated 7,000 people from more than twenty countries lived at 97 Orchard Street. The Moores are the ones the records let us name.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bridget-joseph-moore" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Central Park, and what stood there first" -->
The commissioners who drew the 1811 plan had set aside almost no public open ground. In 1853 New York's legislators set aside more than 700 acres in the middle of Manhattan for a park. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux won the design competition in 1858 with a plan they called Greensward, and building began that year.

The ground was not empty.

Seneca Village stood on part of it. John and Elizabeth Whitehead had divided their farmland into about 200 lots in 1825. On September 27 of that year Andrew Williams, a twenty-five-year-old Black shoeshiner, bought the first three lots for $125. Epiphany Davis bought twelve lots for $578. The AME Zion Church bought lots as well. What grew there over the next thirty years was a settled community: by 1855 about 225 people lived in Seneca Village, roughly two-thirds of them African American, with Irish and German families among them, and they had built three churches and a school.

Owning the land mattered for a reason beyond shelter. Under New York law at the time, a Black man could vote only if he owned property worth a set amount. Several Black families in Seneca Village owned enough land to meet that requirement. Ground there was not only a place to live; for the men in those households it was the thing that made them voters.

The men governing New York took the whole park site by eminent domain — the power of a government to take private property for public use, paying the owner a price the government fixes, whether the owner wants to sell or not. They cleared Seneca Village with the rest of it. Its residents were out by the fall of 1857. Crews demolished the buildings, and construction of the park began in 1858.

Central Park exists, and it stands on ground taken from people who owned it — including from Black families for whom owning that ground had also meant the vote. Both of those are the record.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-law-olmsted" name="Frederick Law Olmsted" movie="Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Law Olmsted

> **Who:** The designer, with Calvert Vaux, of Central Park, and the man who established landscape architecture as a profession in the United States.
> **When and where:** New York from 1858; park work across the country through the 1890s.
> **Movie:** *Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America* (PBS, 2014), a documentary that premiered June 20, 2014.

Olmsted had been a farmer and a journalist. He had never designed a park when he and Calvert Vaux entered the Central Park competition in 1858 with the Greensward plan, won it, and then ran the construction.

The argument behind the plan was that a crowded industrial city owes every person living in it — not only the ones who can afford to leave it — a piece of made countryside they can walk into. Olmsted carried that argument to other cities. He worked on Prospect Park in Brooklyn, on the chain of connected parks in Boston known as the Emerald Necklace, and on parks and campuses in dozens of places. In 1893 he laid out the grounds of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago.

After Olmsted and Vaux, public green space was something an American city was expected to provide, and it has been ever since.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-law-olmsted" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Out: streetcars stretch the city" -->
A city could be no wider than the distance people were willing to walk to work. Frank J. Sprague moved that limit, in Richmond, Virginia. His Richmond Union Passenger Railway began regular service on February 2, 1888 with ten cars on about twelve miles of track, and by May it was running twenty-three. It was the first large electric street railway anywhere that worked reliably.

A ten-minute ride covered ground that had taken thirty minutes to walk. Within two years about 110 electric railways were built or under contract using Sprague's equipment. Builders then put up houses along the new lines, out past the old edge of town, and those became the first streetcar suburbs. For the first time a city could spread outward along its tracks instead of only filling in what was already inside it.

The streets themselves got attention in the same decade. In 1895 New York's reform mayor, Strong, appointed George E. Waring Jr. street-cleaning commissioner, and Waring held the post until 1898. He put about 2,000 sweepers in white uniforms — people called them the White Wings — and had them clean about 450 miles of street every day. He set up the country's first systematic waste-disposal and recycling programs and organized Juvenile Street Cleaning Leagues for children. Jacob Riis wrote that Waring's broom "saved more lives in the crowded tenements than a squad of doctors."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A city in one day" -->
At noon on April 22, 1889, the land run into Oklahoma began. The ground thrown open that day was Native land, taken from the nations who held it.

Guthrie was a railroad station that morning. By nightfall about 10,000 people were camped there. By the next morning the place had a provisional government, a land office, a newspaper, and three banks, all of them operating out of tents. Oklahoma City filled up on the same scale on the same day.

Surveying, laying out lots, settling where the government would sit, getting a newspaper and a bank running: colonial towns had taken years over that work, and Philadelphia had needed a plan drawn on paper and a shipload of settlers sent out to it. At Guthrie people did it between noon and the following sunrise.
