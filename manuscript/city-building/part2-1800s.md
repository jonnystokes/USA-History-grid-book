## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 6 of 10 · 1800 to 1850 · about 7 minutes**

Towns beside water that boats could reach grew into cities in a few years, on land that had belonged to Native nations. In New York, three commissioners laid a grid of straight streets over Manhattan's hills. After a deadly disease and a great fire, the city's leaders had clean water brought in from a river to the north.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boomtowns" -->
Rochester, Chicago and San Francisco were all boomtowns between 1800 and 1850, towns whose number of people grew very fast, in a few years.

The City of Rochester's own history and the Monroe County Library's Rochester Voices project both describe Rochester as America's first boomtown. The land there was Seneca land. In 1788 Seneca leaders signed a treaty, a written agreement between nations or governments, that handed about 2,600,000 acres east of the Genesee River to two land dealers, Oliver Phelps and Nathaniel Gorham.

The Seneca leaders also granted land west of the river for a mill, on the condition that a mill be built for the Seneca to use. Ebenezer Allen built a sawmill and a mill for grinding grain on that tract in 1789.

The Erie Canal ran through Rochester from 1825, and workers finished the whole canal across New York that October. The falls of the Genesee River turned the mills' wheels. Grain from the Genesee Valley came to the mills, and canal boats carried the flour away. Before the canal, shipping a ton of flour from Rochester cost $100. By canal it cost $7. By 1838 the mills at Rochester ground more flour than the mills of any other American city, and Rochester got the nickname the Flour City.

About 1,500 people lived in Rochester in 1820. Census takers, who count the country's people every ten years, counted 9,269 there in 1830. By 1834, the year Rochester became a city, about 12,630 people lived there. That was more than eight times as many people as in 1820.

Chicago grew where the Chicago River runs into Lake Michigan, beside Fort Dearborn, an army fort built at the river's mouth in 1803. It became a town in 1833, when about 350 people lived there.

That September, in the Treaty of Chicago, Potawatomi leaders signed over about 5,000,000 acres: their last lands in Illinois and Wisconsin, and their last reservations, or lands kept for them, in Michigan. The Potawatomi leaders agreed to move west of the Mississippi River. Chicago became a city on March 4, 1837, when 4,170 people lived there.

For most of the year Chicago's streets were mud, until crews began in 1855 to raise them over sewers, underground pipes that carry waste water away.

San Francisco grew on the homeland of the Yelamu, an Ohlone people. About 200 people lived in the town in 1846, and by 1852 about 36,000 lived there. They had come in the California Gold Rush, a race to California to dig for gold.

People put the town together in a hurry. They lived in tents and in hulks, the hulls of old ships that no longer sailed. Six great fires burned the town between December 1849 and June 1851, and the fire of May 3 and 4, 1851, destroyed as much as three-quarters of it. People rebuilt after each one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A grid laid over Manhattan's hills" -->
New York's state lawmakers appointed three commissioners on April 3, 1807, and gave them the job of laying out the streets of Manhattan. They were Gouverneur Morris, John Rutherfurd and Simeon De Witt, and they filed their plan in March 1811.

The commissioners laid the streets out as a grid. In the remarks they filed with the plan, they gave their reason: "straight-sided and right-angled houses are the most cheap to build and the most convenient to live in." Their plan covered Manhattan from Houston Street north to 155th Street. It had twelve avenues and 155 numbered cross streets, which cut the land into rectangles.

The commissioners drew the lines straight over the island's hills without bending them. Manhattan was Lenape land, and by 1811 much of this part of it was rocky hills, swamps and marshes, with farms, houses and barns scattered across it.

The chief surveyor, John Randel Jr., was hired for the job at twenty. In 1808 he mapped the island's hills, rocks, swamps, streams and ponds, and its houses, barns, fences and fields. Then his crews set 1,549 marble markers and 98 iron bolts into the ground to fix the grid in place.

Workers then had to make the island match the drawing. To build each street, they dug away hills or filled in hollows, leveled the strip of ground and paved it. The new streets ran through the spots where about 721 of the 1,825 buildings north of Houston Street stood in 1811, so those buildings had to be moved out of the way. The work took about sixty years to reach 155th Street.

The commissioners set aside very little land for parks, and Central Park came only later, on ground where the people of Seneca Village already lived.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How New York got clean water" -->
Cholera causes watery diarrhea so heavy that the body loses its water very fast. A person with a bad case who does not get water back into the body fast enough can die of it within hours. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 out of about 250,000 New Yorkers, about one person in every seventy.

New York had no clean piped water then. People drew water from wells, ponds and springs inside the city. Workers for a private firm, the Manhattan Company, sank wells and laid wooden pipes to send the water out. Carters drove barrels of country spring water around town and sold it for a penny a gallon, and that water was often dirty too. Others caught rain in tanks called cisterns.

The water from the downtown wells was dirty by the 1830s. More and more privies, outdoor toilets built over pits in the ground, stood near the wells, and liquid from old graves soaked into the ground as well. When people sick with cholera used the privies, the waste overflowed into nearby wells and cellars.

A fire started in a five-story warehouse on Merchant Street, near Wall Street in lower Manhattan, on the evening of December 16, 1835. A strong wind blew it toward the East River. It burned about seventeen blocks and destroyed between 600 and 700 buildings, and the damage came to about $20 million. Two people died in it.

**No water to fight it.** The firefighters could not get enough water onto the fire. There was little water to be had, and what there was froze in the wells, in the hoses and in the hydrants, the street pipes that firefighters draw water from.

The Croton Aqueduct, a channel built to carry water a long way, became the first large city water supply in the United States that ran without a break. New York's city leaders had decided by the late 1820s that they needed a cleaner and bigger supply of water. After the cholera and the fire, they had the aqueduct built. Workers began building it in 1837, with John B. Jervis as chief engineer.

The workers dammed the Croton River in Westchester County, north of the city. Then they built a covered tunnel of brick on a stone base, forty-one miles long, to carry the water down to Manhattan. Inside, the tunnel was eight and a half feet high and seven and a half feet wide. It dropped about 13 inches in every mile, so gravity carried the water along it.

Water entered the city on June 22, 1842. On July 4 the distributing reservoir, a large basin that held the water and fed it out to the city's pipes, began filling at 42nd Street and Fifth Avenue.

New Yorkers held a celebration across the whole city on October 14, 1842. A parade seven miles long marched through the streets, and the new fountain in City Hall Park threw water fifty feet into the air. People called a glass of the new water a "Croton cocktail," though it had no alcohol in it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-templeton-strong" name="George Templeton Strong" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Templeton Strong

> **Who:** A young New York lawyer whose diary, kept for forty years, records the coming of Croton water.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1842.

**In 1842 Strong doubted that even Croton water could clean New York.**

George Templeton Strong was twenty-two when the Croton water arrived. He kept a diary from 1835 until his death in 1875, about 2,250 pages in all. It is a first-hand record of New York in the 1800s, and two editors, Nevins and Thomas, published it in 1952.

While the water was still on its way in 1842, Strong wrote that "Croton Water is slowly flowing towards the city, which at last will stand a chance of being cleaned." The sentence goes on: "if water can clean it." He thought the city was so dirty that even water might not get it clean.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-templeton-strong" -->

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 7 of 10 · 1850 to 1900 · about 20 minutes**

Builders made American cities taller in these fifty years, dug sewers under them, and spread them outward along streetcar lines. In Chicago, crews raised the streets over new sewers and lifted the buildings to match. Census takers counted 1,099,850 people in Chicago in 1890, which made it the second-largest city in the country.

In New York in the same years, poor families crowded into tenements, apartment buildings put up cheaply to hold as many families as a plot of land would take.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How crews lifted Chicago out of the mud" -->
Rain and waste had nowhere to drain off Chicago's low, almost level clay ground, so pools of dirty water stood in the streets, and for most of the year the streets were mud. Diseases spread from that standing water, among them typhoid fever, a fever caught from food or water dirtied by human waste, and dysentery, a gut infection that causes bloody diarrhea. In 1854 cholera killed six of every hundred people in the city.

The members of the Board of Sewerage Commissioners, the officials in charge of Chicago's sewers, hired Ellis S. Chesbrough in December 1855. He had been the water engineer in Boston. His plan of 1856 was the first plan for a complete sewer system covering a whole American city.

**Streets on top of the sewers.** Workers laid the sewer pipes along the existing streets and then buried them under several feet of new fill, earth dumped on top. That raised the level of the street by an average of four to five feet, and by as much as eight feet in places. A building left at the old level now sat below its own street, and its ground floor became a basement.

Next the buildings had to come up to the new street level. The tool for lifting them was the jackscrew, a screw inside a case that a man turned by hand with a lever. Crews set many jackscrews under a building, and the men turned them together.

The first masonry building, one built of brick or stone, went up this way in January 1858. It was a four-story brick block at Randolph and Dearborn streets that weighed about 750 tons. It rose 6 feet 2 inches on 200 jackscrews. Reporters of the time wrote that it rose "without the slightest injury" to the building. More than fifty buildings like it were raised that same year. Every figure for these raisings comes from those newspaper reports.

A group of engineers that included George Pullman raised half a city block in 1860. It was the row of brick and stone buildings on Lake Street between Clark and LaSalle streets, 320 feet long. With its sidewalks, the row weighed about 35,000 tons. About 600 men worked about 6,000 jackscrews, and the block went up 4 feet 8 inches in five days. The shops in it stayed open the whole time, and people came and went, shopped and worked in them while the buildings rose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Up: the elevator and the steel frame" -->
Builders could not make tall buildings without two inventions: an elevator people trusted, and a frame that could hold the building up.

The danger in an elevator was that the rope could break and drop the platform. Elisha Graves Otis made a brake from a steel wagon spring that locked into a ratchet, a bar with teeth, and held the platform if the rope broke.

**The test.** Otis showed his brake in 1854 at the Crystal Palace exposition in New York, a large public show of new machines and goods. He rode a platform up an open-sided shaft, and halfway up he had the rope cut with an axe. The brake caught, and the platform held.

The first passenger elevator sold for everyday use in a building went into the E. V. Haughwout Building, at 488 Broadway in New York, on March 23, 1857. A steam engine drove it. It cost $300 and rose about two-thirds of a foot per second.

**Rent on the top floor.** Before elevators, the higher a room was, the lower its rent, because people had to climb the stairs to reach it. Once elevators ran, floors above the fourth or fifth could rent for as much as the floors below, or more.

In a wall-bearing building, the outside walls hold up everything. The taller the building, the thicker the walls at the bottom have to be. The Monadnock Building in Chicago, finished in 1891, is 16 stories tall, the tallest building ever held up by brick walls. Its walls are six feet thick at the bottom. In a framed building, a skeleton of iron or steel columns and beams carries the weight. The walls hang on that frame as a covering.

The Home Insurance Building in Chicago is the building most often called the first skyscraper, a very tall building held up by a metal frame. William Le Baron Jenney designed it, and it was finished in 1885. It stood ten stories and 138 feet tall, and builders added two more floors in 1890. Its frame of iron and steel carried loads that walls had carried in older buildings. Workers tore it down in 1931 to make room for the Field Building.

That title is in dispute. The Home Insurance Building was not built all of steel, and it was not the first building with a metal frame. Jason Barr, who writes about the history of skyscrapers, traces the label "first skyscraper" partly to a later publicity campaign. People close to Jenney ran it, along with Chicago boosters, people who promoted their city to bring in money and business.

Other buildings have a claim to be first. One is New York's Equitable Life Building of 1870, the first office building with an elevator. A fire destroyed it in 1912 and killed six people, and the Equitable Building of 1915, about 40 stories tall, went up on the same ground. Whichever building came first, builders used the frame and the elevator to roughly triple the height of buildings within one generation.

**By 1893:** Chicago's downtown stood on streets raised over its sewers, and its office buildings rose ten stories and more on frames of iron and steel.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Mohawk ironworkers" -->
Mohawk ironworkers built steel frames like these. From 1886 on, men from two Mohawk communities, Kahnawake and Akwesasne, helped build bridges and tall buildings across the Northeast. That year, at a bridge job at Kahnawake, a Mohawk town near Montreal, Mohawk men started working high steel, joining the steel frames of bridges and buildings high above the ground.

From Kahnawake and Akwesasne, the men traveled to bridge and skyscraper jobs wherever the steel work was, and they called that "booming out." An exhibition at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, *Booming Out: Mohawk Ironworkers Build New York*, documents their work. It includes their work in the next century on the Empire State Building, the Chrysler Building, the George Washington Bridge and the World Trade Center.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement" -->
New York's state lawmakers passed a tenement law on May 14, 1867, the first broad housing law in the country. Under it, cellar apartments were banned unless the ceiling stood at least a foot above the street, and every building needed fire escapes and one toilet for every twenty people. The law also set the first rules for letting fresh air into rooms.

Officials did little to make builders obey it. To meet its rules about fresh air, builders cut windows in the walls between inside rooms, so the windows let in no outside air. The lawmakers tried again with the Tenement House Act of 1879, later known as the Old Law. Under it, every room a person lived in had to have a window that opened to outside air. Builders met that rule with the dumbbell tenement.

Builders pinched each building in at its middle, which left room for a narrow air shaft, and rooms in the middle of the building got their required window onto that shaft. Seen from above, the building was wide at both ends and thin in the middle, the shape of a dumbbell, an exercise weight with a ball at each end of a short bar.

The shaft was too narrow and too deep to bring daylight or moving air down below the top floors, so most of the rooms it was built for stayed dark and stale. The shafts filled up with garbage. The shaft also worked as a flue, a channel that draws hot air and smoke upward, as a chimney does. A fire in the shaft could climb it to the floors above.

New York's state lawmakers passed a new Tenement House Act in 1901, later known as the New Law. In it they banned the dumbbell design and required windows facing outward in every room, along with courtyards, indoor toilets and protection against fire. The rules covered old buildings as well, so owners had to change those too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="jacob-riis" name="Jacob Riis" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Jacob Riis

> **Who:** A Danish immigrant and police reporter who took up flash photography early and used it to show how the poor of a city lived.
> **When and where:** New York's tenement districts. *How the Other Half Lives* came out in 1890.

**Riis took his photographs at night, by the flash of burning magnesium.**

Jacob Riis used flash photographs as evidence in an argument about how poor families in New York lived. He came to the United States from Denmark in 1870 and worked as a police reporter for the *New York Tribune* and then the *Evening Sun*. As a reporter he covered the city's poorest streets, among them Mulberry Street. The police there also ran shelters for homeless people, called police lodging houses, and Riis saw inside them and wrote about them.

Riis began using magnesium flash around 1887. Magnesium powder burns for an instant with a light bright enough to record a picture on a photographic plate, a glass sheet coated with chemicals that change where light hits them. With the flash he photographed tenements, saloons and slums at night.

Riis published an eighteen-page article in *Scribner's* magazine in 1889. The next year he published the book *How the Other Half Lives*, with seventeen photographs and a number of engravings, pictures printed from lines cut into metal or wood.

The *Dictionary of American History* states that Riis's work, along with the work of other reformers, pushed New York's lawmakers toward the tenement law of 1901, which banned the dumbbell design. Theodore Roosevelt, as New York City's police commissioner, became Riis's ally.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-riis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bridget-joseph-moore" name="Bridget and Joseph Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bridget and Joseph Moore

> **Who:** An Irish immigrant waiter and his wife, one tenement family the records document, whose apartment the Tenement Museum has recreated.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, on New York's Lower East Side, 1869.

**The Moores' water came from a backyard pump, beside the privies.**

Bridget Moore married at seventeen, in 1865. Joseph Moore worked as a waiter, and some of the Tenement Museum's accounts record him as a bartender. In 1869 the couple moved with their daughters into 97 Orchard Street, a tenement built in 1863. They were one of the few Irish families in a building where most people were German, and they lived there about a year.

Their daughter Agnes Mary died on April 21, 1869, at five months old. The cause given in the records was marasmus, severe malnutrition, a form of starvation. The body gets too little food of every kind, so it uses up its own fat and then its own muscle, and the child wastes away.

The Tenement Museum's account describes two things linked to the deaths of babies at that time. One was swill milk, which came from cows kept in stables beside distilleries, where liquor was made. The cows ate the leftover grain mush from making the liquor. Their milk was thin and dirty, and in the 1850s New Yorkers blamed it for the deaths of many babies. The museum says Agnes Mary's marasmus came from tainted milk.

The other was the building itself. The water at 97 Orchard Street came from a pump in the back yard, beside the privies.

Of the eight children Bridget and Joseph Moore had, four lived to be adults.

The building still stands, and museum staff have recreated the Moores' apartment as it was in 1869. Between 1863 and 1935, when the floors where people lived were sealed shut, an estimated 7,000 people from more than twenty countries lived at 97 Orchard Street. The Moores are one of the families among them whose names appear in the records.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bridget-joseph-moore" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The White Wings" -->
The reporter Jacob Riis wrote that one man's broom "saved more lives in the crowded tenements than a squad of doctors." The man was George E. Waring Jr. New York's reform mayor, William L. Strong, appointed him street-cleaning commissioner in 1895, and Waring held the job until 1898. He put about 2,000 street sweepers in white uniforms, and people called them the White Wings. Each day they cleaned about 450 miles of street.

Waring set up the first planned systems for getting rid of the city's waste and for recycling it. He organized Juvenile Street Cleaning Leagues too, clubs for children who helped keep their streets clean.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Central Park and Seneca Village" -->
The commissioners who drew the 1811 plan had left almost no land for public parks. In 1853 New York's state lawmakers set aside more than 700 acres in the middle of Manhattan for a park. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux won the design contest in 1858 with a plan they called Greensward, and building began that year.

People already lived on part of that land, in Seneca Village. John and Elizabeth Whitehead had divided their farmland there into about 200 lots, or pieces of land for sale, in 1825. On September 27 of that year, Andrew Williams, a twenty-five-year-old Black shoeshiner, bought the first three lots for $125. Epiphany Davis bought twelve lots for $578, and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church bought lots as well.

About 225 people lived in Seneca Village by 1855. Roughly two-thirds of them were African American, and Irish and German families lived there too. They had built three churches and a school.

**Land and the vote.** Under New York law at the time, a Black man could vote only if he owned at least $250 worth of property. In 1845 about 100 Black men in the whole city met that rule, and 10 of them lived in Seneca Village.

New York City officials took the whole park site by eminent domain, the power of a government to take private property for public use, paying the owner whether the owner wants to sell or not. Officials set a price for each piece of land, and the owners in Seneca Village were paid. The histories of the village by the Central Park Conservancy, the group that looks after the park, do not name the commissioners who set the prices.

Many of the owners said the price of their land was too low, and they objected in court. Researchers at the Conservancy have studied the records and found that, with few exceptions, the owners got about what their land would have sold for.

The New York State Supreme Court, the state's main trial court, approved the prices in February 1856, and the land became the city's property. The officials had taken the land from its owners, including Black men whose right to vote depended on owning it.

Many people left in 1856, and others stayed on as renters of the city. In the summer of 1856, Mayor Fernando Wood sent the people of Seneca Village a final notice to leave. In October 1857, once the money to build the park was in place, city officials evicted the last of them, forcing them out of their homes under the law. In all, city officials forced about 1,600 people off the whole park site by the end of 1857.

Accounts differ on how the last people were made to leave. The historians Roy Rosenzweig and Elizabeth Blackmar studied the records from the time for their 1992 history of the park, and they found that the people left "quietly and without violence." The Conservancy's researchers say their work since the early 1990s has found no sign of a fight over the taking.

A New York Public Library research guide describes it differently. It states that the people of Seneca Village fought the police sent to put them out, and that in August 1857 the last Black landowners were "forcibly and violently scattered throughout the city." Rosenzweig and Blackmar think the stories of police clubbing people out mix up the taking of the park land with police raids on pig farms near the park's western edge two years later.

Crews tore down the buildings, and workers began building the park in 1858.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-law-olmsted" name="Frederick Law Olmsted" movie="Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Law Olmsted

> **Who:** The designer, with Calvert Vaux, of Central Park, whom the Olmsted Network, a group that studies his work, calls the founder of landscape architecture as a job in the United States.
> **When and where:** New York from 1858, and parks in Brooklyn, Boston, Washington and Chicago afterward.
> **Movie:** *Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America* (PBS, 2014), a documentary that premiered June 20, 2014.

**Editors once sent Olmsted through the slave states as a reporter.**

Olmsted had been a sailor on a trading voyage to China. From 1848 to 1855 he ran a farm on Staten Island, and in 1852 he published a book called *Walks and Talks of an American Farmer in England*. That year editors at the *New York Daily Times* sent him as a reporter through the Southern states, where slavery was legal.

His travels through the South and Texas lasted until 1857, and his reports were later collected in three books. In them he argued that slavery had held the South back. By his count, the same amount of work took four times as long in Virginia as in the North, and the profits of slavery went to no more than 8,000 owners of large plantations.

He and Calvert Vaux won the Central Park contest in 1858 with the Greensward plan. They designed the land itself, its paths, lawns, trees, water and roads, which is the work called landscape architecture. Olmsted meant the park for everyone in the city, rich and poor. He and Vaux gave it meadows and woods shaped to look like open country, so that city people could get some relief from the city.

Olmsted's best-known designs after Central Park include Prospect Park in Brooklyn, the Emerald Necklace in Boston, a chain of connected parks, and the grounds of the US Capitol in Washington. In 1893 he laid out the grounds of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, in Jackson Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-law-olmsted" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The White City" -->
A world's fair, the World's Columbian Exposition, opened in Chicago in 1893. Daniel Burnham directed the building work, and Olmsted drew the site plan, the layout of the grounds. Workers put up a matched set of white buildings in the style of ancient Greek and Roman buildings, with rows of columns. Electric lights lit them, and the fair got the name the White City.

The buildings were temporary, built to stand only while the fair ran. Under their fronts they were sheds on steel frames. The fronts were made of staff, a mix of plaster, cement and plant fiber that workers poured into molds, and then painted white. More than 27 million people visited the fair in its six months. Planners in the City Beautiful movement of the 1890s and 1900s took the White City as their model. They worked to bring grand public buildings, wide avenues and parks into American cities.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Out: streetcar lines and streetcar suburbs" -->
American cities of the 1800s were walking cities. Most people lived close enough to walk to their work and their shops. Horses pulled streetcars along rails in many cities, at 5 or 6 miles an hour.

**Electric cars.** Frank J. Sprague's Richmond Union Passenger Railway, an electric streetcar line in Richmond, Virginia, began running cars on a regular schedule on February 2, 1888. It started with ten cars on about twelve miles of track, and by May twenty-three cars ran on it. It was the first large electric street railway that worked well.

Electric streetcars ran at 10 to 15 miles an hour, so a ten-minute ride covered ground that took thirty minutes to walk. Within two years, about 110 electric railways using Sprague's equipment were built or under contract.

Builders put up houses along the new lines, out past the old edge of town, and many white city families moved out to them. These new neighborhoods of homes at the edge of a city, where people lived and from which they traveled in to work, were called streetcar suburbs.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A city in one day" -->
The land run into Oklahoma, a race in which settlers rushed in at a set signal to claim pieces of land, began at noon on April 22, 1889. That morning, Guthrie was a railroad station. By nightfall about 10,000 people were camped there.

The land was part of Indian Territory, where United States officials had forced many Native nations to move from their homelands. Dennis N. Cooley was the federal Commissioner of Indian Affairs in 1865, the official in charge of the government's dealings with Native nations. He told the leaders of five of those nations that they had lost their rights to their land. His reason was that they had sided with the Confederacy, the Southern states that fought the United States in the Civil War.

In treaties signed in 1866, the Creek signed over the western half of their land for $975,168, and the Seminole signed over all of theirs for fifteen cents an acre. The treaties stated that the land was for other Native nations and for people freed from slavery.

**The Unassigned Lands.** The land opened in 1889, about 1.9 million acres, lay in the middle of the land the Creek and Seminole had signed over. It was called the Unassigned Lands, because federal officials had not assigned it to any nation. In early 1889, members of Congress voted to pay the Creek about $2.28 million and the Seminole about $1.91 million to drop the rule about who the land was for. Then settlers could claim it.

Congressman William Springer wrote the change to a law that let President Benjamin Harrison open the land. Harrison announced the opening on March 23, 1889.

By the morning after the run, the settlers in Guthrie had set up a temporary government, a land office where settlers filed their claims, a newspaper and three banks, all of them working out of tents. Oklahoma City filled up on the same scale on the same day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

**If you remember one thing:** In these fifty years American cities grew taller and spread farther out, and in Chicago crews raised the streets over new sewers and lifted whole buildings to match.
In 1850: Chicago's streets were mud for most of the year, buildings were held up by their own walls, and most city people walked to work.
In 1900: Chicago's streets ran on top of its sewers, frames of iron and steel held up buildings ten stories and taller, and electric streetcars carried families out to new suburbs.
Who lived where: poor families in New York crowded into tenements, city officials forced the people of Seneca Village off their land to make Central Park, and in 1889 settlers raced onto Oklahoma land that the Creek and Seminole nations had signed over in 1866.
**Next:** In the 1900s, officials wrote rules about what could be built in each part of a city, and white owners and judges kept Black families out of most neighborhoods.
