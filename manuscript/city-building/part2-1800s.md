## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Rochester, New York, grew from about 1,500 people in 1820 to about 12,630 in 1834, after the Erie Canal reached it. Chicago went from about 350 people in 1833 to 4,170 in 1837. San Francisco went from about 200 people in 1846 to about 36,000 in 1852. Each of these towns grew up beside water that boats could reach, on land that had belonged to Native nations.

In New York, people drew their water from wells, springs and rain barrels, and there was no clean piped water. In 1832 cholera, a disease people catch from water with human waste in it, killed about 3,500 New Yorkers. In December 1835 a fire burned about seventeen blocks while the firefighters ran short of water. After those two disasters, New York's city leaders had workers bring water into the city through a channel forty-one miles long from the Croton River. The water arrived in 1842.

In 1811 three men finished a plan of straight streets for Manhattan, from Houston Street north. New York's state lawmakers had appointed them. Much of that ground was rocky hills or swamp, with farms, houses and barns on it. For about sixty years afterward, workers dug away hills and filled in low ground to make the island fit the plan.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Boomtowns" -->
A boomtown is a town whose number of people grows very fast, in a few years. Rochester, Chicago and San Francisco all grew that way between 1800 and 1850.

Writers of the City of Rochester's own history call Rochester America's first boomtown, and so do the writers of the Monroe County Library's Rochester Voices project. The land there was Seneca land. In 1788 Seneca leaders gave up about 2,600,000 acres east of the Genesee River to two land dealers, Oliver Phelps and Nathaniel Gorham. They also granted land west of the river for a mill, on the condition that a mill be built for the Seneca to use. In 1789 Ebenezer Allen built a sawmill and a mill for grinding grain on that tract, beside the river's falls.

The Erie Canal ran through Rochester from 1825, and workers finished the whole canal across New York that October. The falls of the Genesee River turned the mills' wheels. Grain from the Genesee Valley came to the mills, and canal boats carried the flour away. Before the canal, shipping a ton of flour from Rochester cost $100. The canal cut that to $7. By 1838 the mills at Rochester ground more flour than the mills of any other American city, and Rochester got the nickname the Flour City.

About 1,500 people lived in Rochester in 1820. The census, the count of the country's people taken every ten years, found 9,269 there in 1830. By 1834, the year Rochester became a city, about 12,630 people lived there. That was more than eight times as many people as in 1820.

Chicago grew where the Chicago River runs into Lake Michigan, beside Fort Dearborn, an army fort built at the river's mouth in 1803. It became a town in 1833, when about 350 people lived there. That September, in the Treaty of Chicago, Potawatomi leaders gave up their last lands in Illinois and Wisconsin and their last reservations in Michigan, about 5,000,000 acres. They agreed to move west of the Mississippi River. Illinois lawmakers made Chicago a city on March 4, 1837, when 4,170 people lived there.

Chicago's ground was low and flat, and its clay soil soaked up little water. Nothing drained off it, and for most of the year the streets were mud and standing water. Starting in 1855, crews raised Chicago's streets so that sewers, underground pipes that carry waste water away, could drain the city. From 1858 crews raised its buildings to match.

San Francisco grew on the homeland of the Yelamu, an Ohlone people. About 200 people lived in the town in 1846. By 1852, during the California Gold Rush, when people rushed to California to dig for gold, about 36,000 people lived there. The town grew with no plan. People lived in tents and in hulks, which are the hulls of old ships that no longer sailed. Between December 1849 and June 1851, six great fires burned the town. The fire of May 3 and 4, 1851, destroyed as much as three-quarters of it. People rebuilt after each one.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The 1811 grid" -->
On April 3, 1807, New York's state lawmakers appointed three commissioners, men given the job of laying out the streets of Manhattan. They were Gouverneur Morris, John Rutherfurd and Simeon De Witt. They filed their plan in March 1811.

The commissioners laid the streets out as a grid. Their plan covered Manhattan from Houston Street north to 155th Street. It had twelve avenues running the length of the island and 155 numbered cross streets running across it, which cut the land into rectangles. The commissioners drew the lines straight over the island's hills without bending them. Manhattan was Lenape land, and by 1811 much of this part of it was rocky hills, swamps and marshes, with farms, houses and barns scattered across it.

John Randel Jr. was the chief surveyor, a person who measures land and marks where lines fall on it. He was hired for the job at twenty. In 1808 he mapped the island's hills, rocks, swamps, streams and ponds, and its houses, barns, fences and fields. Then his crews set 1,549 marble markers and 98 iron bolts into the ground to fix where the streets would cross.

Workers then had to make the island match the drawing. To build each street, they dug away hills or filled in hollows, leveled the strip of ground and paved it. About 721 of the 1,825 buildings that stood north of Houston Street in 1811 had to be moved out of the way. The work took about sixty years to reach 155th Street.

The commissioners set aside very little land for parks, and Central Park was not in their plan. State lawmakers added it in 1853, in a separate law. People already lived on part of that ground, in a place called Seneca Village. By the fall of 1857 New York officials had made them all leave.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cholera, fire, and the Croton" -->
Cholera is a disease people catch by swallowing water that has human waste in it. It causes watery diarrhea so heavy that the body loses its water very fast. A person with a bad case who does not get water back into the body fast enough can die of it within hours. In 1832 cholera killed about 3,500 New Yorkers out of a population of about 250,000. That is about one person in every seventy.

New York had no clean piped water then. People drew water from wells, ponds and springs inside the city. A private firm, the Manhattan Company, sank wells and sent water out through wooden pipes. Carters drove barrels of country spring water around town and sold it for a penny a gallon, and that water was often dirty too. Others caught rain in tanks called cisterns. By the 1830s the downtown wells brought up tainted water, because more and more privies stood near them, and old graveyards seeped into the ground as well. A privy is an outdoor toilet built over a pit in the ground. When people sick with cholera used the privies, the waste overflowed into nearby wells and cellars.

On the evening of December 16, 1835, a fire started in a five-story warehouse on Merchant Street, near Wall Street, in lower Manhattan. A strong wind blew it toward the East River. It burned about seventeen blocks and destroyed between 600 and 700 buildings, and the damage came to about $20 million. Two people died in it. The firefighters could not get enough water onto the fire. There was little water to be had, and the water froze in the wells, the hoses and the hydrants, the street pipes that firefighters draw water from.

The Croton Aqueduct became the first large city water supply in the United States that ran without a break. New York's city leaders had decided by the late 1820s that they needed a cleaner and bigger supply of water. After the cholera and the fire, they had it built. Workers began building the Croton Aqueduct in 1837, with John B. Jervis as chief engineer. An aqueduct is a channel built to carry water a long way. The records identify Jervis but not the men who dug the aqueduct.

The workers dammed the Croton River in Westchester County, north of the city. Then they built a covered tunnel of brick on a stone base, forty-one miles long, to carry the water down to Manhattan. Inside it stood eight and a half feet high and seven and a half feet wide. It dropped about 13 inches in every mile, so gravity carried the water along it. Water entered the city on June 22, 1842. On July 4 the distributing reservoir at 42nd Street and Fifth Avenue began filling. It was a large basin that held the water and fed it out to the city's pipes.

On October 14, 1842, New Yorkers held a celebration across the whole city. A parade seven miles long marched through the streets, and the new fountain in City Hall Park threw water fifty feet into the air. People drank what they called Croton cocktails.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-templeton-strong" name="George Templeton Strong" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Templeton Strong

> **Who:** A young New York lawyer whose private diary recorded what piped water meant inside one ordinary household.
> **When and where:** New York City, 1842.

George Templeton Strong was born in 1820 and was twenty-two when the Croton water arrived. He kept a diary from 1835 until his death in 1875, and it runs to about 2,250 pages. Historians use it as a first-hand record of New York in the 1800s. Two editors, Nevins and Thomas, published it in 1952.

In 1842, while the water was still on its way, Strong wrote that "Croton Water is slowly flowing towards the city, which at last will stand a chance of being cleaned." The sentence goes on: "if water can clean it." Strong was saying that New York was filthy.

Once Croton water reached his house, Strong could take a bath at home, and his diary records how much he enjoyed it.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Between 1850 and 1900, builders made American cities taller, dug sewers under them, and spread them outward along streetcar lines.

Once elevators had a safety catch, people were willing to ride them to upper floors. Builders also began to hold up tall buildings with frames of iron and steel, so the outside walls no longer had to carry the weight.

Chicago's ground was so flat and wet that a sewer could not run downhill through it. Crews there laid new sewer pipes along the streets, raised the streets over them, and lifted the downtown buildings up to the new street level. In the census of 1890, Chicago had 1,099,850 people. It was the second-largest city in the country, after New York.

The first large electric streetcar line began regular service in Richmond, Virginia, on February 2, 1888. A ten-minute ride on one covered the distance of a thirty-minute walk, and builders put up houses along the tracks.

In New York in the same years, poor families crowded into tenements, apartment buildings put up to hold as many people as a lot would take.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Raising Chicago's streets and buildings" -->
Chicago's ground was almost level and sat only a little higher than the shore of Lake Michigan. Rain and waste had nowhere to drain, so pools of dirty water stood in the streets, and for most of the year the streets were mud. Diseases spread from that standing water. Typhoid fever and dysentery struck again and again, and in 1854 cholera killed six of every hundred people in the city.

In December 1855 the members of the Board of Sewerage Commissioners, the officials in charge of Chicago's sewers, hired Ellis S. Chesbrough. He had been the water engineer in Boston. His plan of 1856 was the first plan for a complete sewer system covering a whole American city. Workers laid the sewer pipes along the existing streets and then buried them under several feet of new fill, which is earth dumped on top. That raised the level of the street, by four or five feet in most places and by as much as eight in some. A building left at the old level now sat below its own street, and its ground floor became a basement.

Crews then lifted the buildings up to the new street level. Their tool was the jackscrew, a screw inside a case that a man turned by hand with a lever. Crews set many jackscrews under a building, and the men turned them together.

In January 1858 crews raised the first masonry building, one built of brick or stone, this way. It was a four-story brick block at Randolph and Dearborn streets that weighed about 750 tons. It went up 6 feet 2 inches on 200 jackscrews. Newspapers of the time reported that it rose "without the slightest injury" to the building. That same year crews raised more than fifty buildings like it. Every figure for these raisings comes from those newspaper reports.

In 1860 a group of engineers that included George Pullman raised half a city block. It was the row of brick and stone buildings on Lake Street between Clark and LaSalle streets, 320 feet long. With its sidewalks, the row weighed about 35,000 tons. About 600 men worked about 6,000 jackscrews, and the block went up 4 feet 8 inches in five days. The shops in it stayed open the whole time, and people came and went, shopped and worked in them while the buildings rose.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Up: the elevator and the steel frame" -->
Tall buildings needed two inventions: an elevator people trusted, and a frame that could hold the building up.

The danger in an elevator was that the rope could break and drop the platform. Elisha Graves Otis made a brake from a steel wagon spring that caught on a toothed rail if the rope went slack. In 1854 he showed it at the Crystal Palace exposition in New York, a large public show of new machines and goods. He rode a platform up an open-sided shaft, and halfway up he had the rope cut with an axe. The brake caught, and the platform held.

On March 23, 1857, workers put the first commercial passenger elevator in the country into the E. V. Haughwout Building at 488 Broadway in New York. A commercial elevator is one sold for everyday use in a building. A steam engine drove it. It cost $300 and rose about two-thirds of a foot per second. Before elevators, the higher a room was, the lower its rent, because people had to climb the stairs to reach it. Once elevators ran, floors above the fourth or fifth could rent for as much as the floors below, or more.

In a wall-bearing building, the outside walls hold up everything. The taller the building, the thicker the walls at the bottom have to be. The Monadnock Building in Chicago, finished in 1891, shows the limit. It is 16 stories tall, the tallest building ever held up by brick walls, and its walls are six feet thick at the bottom. In a framed building, a skeleton of iron or steel columns and beams carries the weight. The walls hang on that frame as a covering.

The Home Insurance Building in Chicago is the building most often called the first skyscraper, which means a very tall building held up by a metal frame. William Le Baron Jenney designed it, and it was finished in 1885. It stood ten stories and 138 feet tall, and builders added two more floors in 1890. Its frame of iron and steel carried loads that walls had carried in older buildings. Workers tore it down in 1931 to make room for the Field Building.

Historians argue over that title. The Home Insurance Building was not built all of steel, and it was not the first building with a metal frame. Jenney's own account of the building, written in 1885, describes mostly its foundations, not a new way of holding up a building. Some historians trace the labels "first skyscraper" and "father of the skyscraper" partly to a later publicity campaign. People close to Jenney ran it, along with Chicago boosters, people who promoted their city to bring in money and business. These historians call the story the "Jenney Myth." Jason Barr makes the argument in his book *Building the Skyline* and in his Skynomics writing.

Writers in the same argument name other buildings as the first. New York's Equitable Life Building of 1870 was the first office building with an elevator. The Produce Exchange of 1884 had an inner skeleton designed by George B. Post. Chicago's Tacoma Building of 1889 is named as well. Historians do agree on what builders did with the frame and the elevator. Builders used them to roughly triple the height of buildings within one generation.

In 1893 the World's Columbian Exposition, a world's fair, opened in Chicago. Daniel Burnham directed the building work, and Frederick Law Olmsted drew the site plan, which is the layout of the grounds. Workers put up a matched set of white buildings in the neoclassical style, which copies the columns and shapes of ancient Greek and Roman buildings. Electric lights lit them, and the fair got the name the White City.

The buildings were meant to stand only for the fair. Under their fronts they were steel-framed sheds. The fronts were made of staff, a mix of plaster, cement and plant fiber that workers poured into molds, and then painted white. More than 27 million people visited the fair in its six months. Planners in the City Beautiful movement of the 1890s and 1900s took the White City as their model. They worked to bring grand public buildings, wide avenues and parks into American cities.

Ironworkers from two Mohawk communities, Kahnawake and Akwesasne, helped build bridges and tall buildings across the Northeast from 1886 on. That year, at a bridge job at Kahnawake, a Mohawk town near Montreal, Mohawk men started working high steel. Working high steel means joining the steel frames of bridges and buildings high above the ground. From Kahnawake and Akwesasne, the men traveled to bridge and skyscraper jobs wherever the steel work was, and they called that "booming out." An exhibition at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, *Booming Out: Mohawk Ironworkers Build New York*, documents their work. It records their work in the next century on the Empire State Building, the Chrysler Building, the George Washington Bridge and the World Trade Center.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The tenement" -->
A tenement is an apartment building put up cheaply to hold as many poor families as its lot will take. On May 14, 1867, New York's state lawmakers passed their first tenement law, the first broad housing law in the country. It banned cellar apartments unless the ceiling stood at least a foot above the street. It required one toilet for every twenty people, and fire escapes. Officials did little to make builders obey it. To meet its rule about windows, builders often cut windows in the walls between inside rooms, so the windows let in no outside air.

The Tenement House Act of 1879, later known as the Old Law, was the lawmakers' answer to that failure. Under it, every room a person lived in had to have a window that opened to outside air. Builders met that rule with the dumbbell tenement.

Builders pinched each building in at its middle, so that a narrow shaft of open air ran up its side from the ground to the roof. Rooms in the middle of the building got their required window onto that shaft. Seen from above, the building was wide at both ends and thin in the middle. A dumbbell, a weight for exercise with a heavy ball at each end of a short bar, has the same shape, and the building took its name from it.

The shaft was too narrow and too deep to bring daylight or moving air down below the top floors, so most of the rooms it was built for stayed dark and stale. Tenants threw garbage down it, and the garbage piled up at the bottom. The shaft also worked as a flue, a channel that draws hot air and smoke upward, as a chimney does. A fire in the shaft could climb it to the floors above.

The Tenement House Act of 1901, later known as the New Law, required windows facing outward in every room. It banned the dumbbell design. It required courtyards, indoor toilets and protection against fire, and it made owners change their old buildings too.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Jacob Riis

> **Who:** A Danish immigrant and police reporter who was among the first Americans to use flash photography to show how the poor of a city lived.
> **When and where:** New York's tenement districts. *How the Other Half Lives* came out in 1890.

Jacob Riis used flash photographs as evidence in an argument about how poor families in New York lived. He was born on May 3, 1849, in Ribe, Denmark, and moved to the United States in 1870. He worked as a police reporter for the *New York Tribune* and then the *Evening Sun*. His beat covered the city's poorest streets, among them Mulberry Street. The police there also ran shelters for homeless people, called police lodging houses, and Riis saw inside them and wrote about them.

The rooms Riis wanted to show had no daylight, and a camera of that time needed bright light to take a picture. Around 1887 he began using magnesium flash. Magnesium powder burns for an instant with a light bright enough to record a picture on a photographic plate, a glass sheet coated with chemicals that change where light hits them. With the flash he photographed tenements, saloons and slums at night.

In 1889 Riis published an eighteen-page article in *Scribner's* magazine. In 1890 he published the book *How the Other Half Lives*, with seventeen photographs and a number of engravings, pictures printed from lines cut into metal or wood. Historians credit Riis's work, along with the work of other reformers, with pushing New York's lawmakers toward the strong tenement law of 1901. Theodore Roosevelt, as New York City's police commissioner, became Riis's ally. Riis died in 1914.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="jacob-riis" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="bridget-joseph-moore" name="Bridget and Joseph Moore" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Bridget and Joseph Moore

> **Who:** An Irish immigrant waiter and his wife, one tenement family the records document, whose apartment the Tenement Museum has recreated.
> **When and where:** 97 Orchard Street, on New York's Lower East Side, 1869.

Bridget Moore married at seventeen, in 1865. Joseph Moore worked as a waiter, and some of the Tenement Museum's accounts record him as a bartender. In 1869 the couple moved with their daughters into 97 Orchard Street, a tenement built in 1863. They were one of the few Irish families in a building where most people were German. They lived there about a year.

On April 21, 1869, their daughter Agnes Mary died at five months old. The cause given in the records was marasmus. Marasmus is severe malnutrition, a form of starvation. The body gets too little food of every kind, so it uses up its own fat and then its own muscle, and the child wastes away.

Researchers at the Tenement Museum rebuilt the family's life from the records. The museum's account states that two conditions in the city were tied to the deaths of babies at that time. One was swill milk. Dairy owners kept cows in stables beside distilleries, where liquor was made, and fed the cows the leftover grain mush from making it. Their milk was thin and dirty, and people blamed it for the deaths of many babies. The museum says Agnes Mary's marasmus came from tainted milk. The other condition was the building itself. The water at 97 Orchard Street came from a pump in the back yard, beside the privies.

Of the eight children Bridget and Joseph Moore had, four lived to be adults.

The building still stands, and museum staff have recreated the Moores' apartment as it was in 1869. Between 1863 and 1935, when the floors where people lived were sealed shut, an estimated 7,000 people from more than twenty countries lived at 97 Orchard Street. The Moores are one of the families among them whose names appear in the records.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="bridget-joseph-moore" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Central Park and Seneca Village" -->
The commissioners who drew the 1811 plan had left almost no land for public parks. In 1853 New York's state lawmakers set aside more than 700 acres in the middle of Manhattan for a park. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux won the design contest in 1858 with a plan they called Greensward, and building began that year.

People already lived on part of that land, in Seneca Village. In 1825 John and Elizabeth Whitehead had divided their farmland there into about 200 lots. On September 27 of that year, Andrew Williams, a twenty-five-year-old Black shoeshiner, bought the first three lots for $125. Epiphany Davis bought twelve lots for $578, and the AME Zion Church bought lots as well. By 1855 about 225 people lived in Seneca Village. Roughly two-thirds of them were African American, and Irish and German families lived there too. They had built three churches and a school.

Under New York law at the time, a Black man could vote only if he owned at least $250 worth of property. In 1845 about 100 Black men in the whole city met that rule, and 10 of them lived in Seneca Village.

New York City officials took the whole park site by eminent domain. Eminent domain is the power of a government to take private property for public use, paying the owner whether the owner wants to sell or not. The owners in Seneca Village were paid, and many of them said the officials had set the price of their land too low. Published histories of the park do not name the officials who carried out the taking. The officials took the land from its owners, including Black men whose right to vote depended on owning it. About 1,600 people were forced off the whole park site. By the end of 1857 the officials had forced everyone in Seneca Village to leave. Crews tore down the buildings, and workers began building the park in 1858.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="frederick-law-olmsted" name="Frederick Law Olmsted" movie="Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America (2014)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Frederick Law Olmsted

> **Who:** The designer, with Calvert Vaux, of Central Park, called the founder of landscape architecture as a profession in the United States.
> **When and where:** New York from 1858, and parks in Brooklyn, Boston, Washington and Chicago afterward.
> **Movie:** *Frederick Law Olmsted: Designing America* (PBS, 2014), a documentary that premiered June 20, 2014.

Before he designed parks, Olmsted had been a sailor on a trading voyage to China. From 1848 to 1855 he ran a farm on Staten Island, and in 1852 he published a book called *Walks and Talks of an American Farmer in England*. That year the *New York Daily Times* sent him through the slave states of the South as a reporter. He and Calvert Vaux won the Central Park contest in 1858 with the Greensward plan, and then they ran the work of building the park. Landscape architecture means designing outdoor land: its paths, lawns, trees, water and roads.

Olmsted meant Central Park for everyone in the city, rich and poor. He and Vaux gave it meadows and woods shaped to look like open country, so that city people could get away from the city without leaving it. Olmsted's best-known designs after Central Park include Prospect Park in Brooklyn, the Emerald Necklace in Boston, a chain of connected parks, and the grounds of the U.S. Capitol in Washington. In 1893 he laid out the grounds of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, in Jackson Park.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="frederick-law-olmsted" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Out: streetcar lines and streetcar suburbs" -->
American cities of the 1800s were walking cities. Most people lived close enough to walk to their work and their shops. Horses pulled streetcars along rails in many cities, at 5 or 6 miles an hour. On February 2, 1888, cars began running on a regular schedule on Frank J. Sprague's Richmond Union Passenger Railway in Richmond, Virginia, an electric streetcar line. It started with ten cars on about twelve miles of track, and by May twenty-three cars ran on it. It was the first large electric street railway that worked well.

Electric streetcars ran at 10 to 15 miles an hour. A ten-minute ride covered ground that took thirty minutes to walk. Within two years, about 110 electric railways using Sprague's equipment were built or under contract. Builders put up houses along the new lines, out past the old edge of town, and many white city families moved out to them. These neighborhoods were called streetcar suburbs. A suburb is a neighborhood of homes at the edge of a city, where people live and from which they travel in to work.

In 1895 New York's reform mayor, William L. Strong, appointed George E. Waring Jr. as street-cleaning commissioner. Waring held the job until 1898. He put about 2,000 street sweepers in white uniforms, and people called them the White Wings. Each day they cleaned about 450 miles of street. Waring set up the first planned systems for getting rid of the city's waste and for recycling it. He also organized Juvenile Street Cleaning Leagues, clubs for children who helped keep their streets clean. Jacob Riis wrote that Waring's broom "saved more lives in the crowded tenements than a squad of doctors."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A city in one day" -->
At noon on April 22, 1889, the land run into Oklahoma began. A land run was a race: at a set signal, settlers rushed in to claim pieces of land. The land was part of Indian Territory, where United States officials had moved many Native nations from their homelands. In 1865 Dennis N. Cooley, the federal Commissioner of Indian Affairs, told the leaders of five of those nations that they had lost their rights to their land by siding with the Confederacy in the Civil War. In treaties signed in 1866, the Creek gave up the western half of their land for $975,168, and the Seminole gave up all of theirs for fifteen cents an acre. The land opened in 1889, about 1.9 million acres, was called the Unassigned Lands, because federal officials had not assigned it to any nation. The Oklahoma Historical Society's histories of the run do not say which of those lost lands the 1889 acres came from.

Congressman William Springer of Illinois wrote the change to a law that let President Benjamin Harrison open the land. Harrison announced the opening on March 23, 1889.

That April morning, Guthrie was a railroad station. By nightfall about 10,000 people were camped there. By the next morning the settlers had set up a provisional government, meaning a temporary one, along with a land office, where settlers filed their claims, a newspaper and three banks. All of them worked out of tents. Oklahoma City filled up on the same scale on the same day.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->
