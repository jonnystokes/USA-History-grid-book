## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
People built planned cities on this land between about 900 and about 1350, hundreds of years before any European came here. In a planned city, people decide ahead of time where each open square, wall and mound will go, and then they build it that way. The largest of these cities stood beside the Mississippi River. In the Southwest, people cut and stacked stone to build towns. Their descendants still live in towns along the Rio Grande.

The people who built these places kept no written records. No builder's, planner's or ruler's name has come down to us. Archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study what people left behind long ago, know them only from the mounds, walls, rooms and roads they left.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia, a city laid out on purpose" -->
In what is now Illinois, across the Mississippi River from where St. Louis, Missouri, stands today, people built up the city of Cahokia quickly from about 1050. It was the largest town north of Mexico before Europeans came. At its peak, around 1100, roughly 10,000 to 20,000 people lived in the center. London had about as many people at the same date. Some estimates put as many as 50,000 people in the wider settlement around the center. Scholars have not settled those larger figures.

The people of Cahokia planned where the big pieces of their city would go. At its center they raised Monks Mound, the largest prehistoric structure made of earth in the Americas. Prehistoric means built before anyone living here kept written records. The mound holds about 814,000 cubic yards of earth. Workers carried all of it in baskets and packed it down by hand, and they raised the mound in stages.

It has four flat terraces, or wide steps, and its top stands about 100 feet high. Its base covers roughly 14 acres. UNESCO is the United Nations body that keeps the World Heritage list of historic sites. Its listing for Cahokia records fourteen separate building stages in the mound.

The Grand Plaza, about 50 acres of open ground, lay at the foot of the mound. Its builders leveled the ground and filled in the low spots by hand so that it was flat. Crowds gathered there for ceremonies and played chunkey, a game played with a rolling stone disk.

West of Monks Mound the builders set up a circle of tall red cedar posts. They built five of these circles, one after another, and each new circle took the place of the last. The posts line up with the points on the horizon where the sun rises at the solstices and the equinoxes. The solstices are the longest day and the shortest day of the year. The equinoxes are the two days between them when day and night are equal in length. So the posts worked as a calendar, and the builders set them into the plan of the city.

A palisade, a wall of upright logs about two miles long, ran around the central part of the city. People began it around 1100 and rebuilt it three times over about two hundred years. Each rebuilding took between 15,000 and 20,000 logs of oak and hickory, each roughly a foot thick and twenty feet tall.

By about 1350 the people of Cahokia had scattered and left the city.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone towns of the Southwest" -->
In what is now northwestern New Mexico, Chaco Canyon was the center of the Ancestral Puebloan world. Ancestral Puebloan people are the ancestors of the people who live in the pueblos of the Southwest today. Pueblo is the Spanish word for a town, and Spanish colonists used it for the stone and adobe towns they found there. At Chaco they built great houses, which are large buildings of shaped stone. They went on building and using the great houses for roughly 280 years, from about 900 to about 1180. One of them, Pueblo Bonito, had more than 650 rooms.

The people of Chaco also built roads. The roads were engineered, which means people surveyed each route ahead of time and then built it. The roads run straight for miles across the Four Corners country, where Colorado, Utah, Arizona and New Mexico meet. They average about 30 feet wide, and they connected farther-off villages to the canyon.

In southwestern Colorado, at Mesa Verde, people built rooms of sandstone blocks and mud into the hollows under overhanging cliffs. Cliff Palace is the largest of those cliff dwellings. It has 150 rooms and 23 kivas, and people built it between 1190 and 1280. A kiva is a round room, usually sunk into the ground, used for ceremonies and for meetings. More than 100 people lived at Cliff Palace.

People began leaving Chaco in the 1140s. That date marks the start of the leaving, and the years 900 to 1180 mark the whole time the great houses were built and used. Fewer people built or lived in the canyon in each of the next decades, and by about 1180 to 1200 the last of them had gone. People had left Mesa Verde by the 1280s. The people who left both places gathered again in the pueblo towns along the Rio Grande, and their descendants still live in those pueblos today.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1565 the Spaniard Pedro Menendez de Aviles founded St. Augustine on the Florida coast, in a Timucua town. People have lived in St. Augustine ever since. Eight years later the king of Spain issued written orders that stated how Spanish officials must lay out every new town. No surviving record from these years tells the life of any single town-builder in detail.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine and the Laws of the Indies" -->
In September 1565 Pedro Menendez de Aviles founded St. Augustine on the coast of Florida. He placed it in Seloy, a town of the Timucua people, named for its chief. Menendez landed there on September 8 with about 800 colonists, among them 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. The colonists moved into Seloy's town, and they used the chief's council house as their first fort. Timucua council houses were round, roofed with thatch, and big enough to hold up to a thousand people.

The Timucua soon turned against the newcomers and attacked them again and again to drive them away. On April 1, 1566, half of the fort burned, and all its supplies were lost. In May 1566, about nine months after landing, Menendez moved the settlement across the bay to Anastasia Island. The Spaniards came back to the mainland, to the site of today's St. Augustine, by about 1572. People have lived in St. Augustine without a break ever since 1565. Of all the cities Europeans founded in the continental United States, it is the oldest one that people have lived in the whole time.

In 1573 King Philip II of Spain issued a set of ordinances called the Laws of the Indies. The Indies was the Spanish name for Spain's lands in the Americas. An ordinance is a written order with the force of law. These ordinances stated exactly how the men who founded Spanish towns had to lay them out.

Each town began with a central plaza, which is a public square of open ground with the town built around it. The plaza's sides had to face north, south, east and west, and it had to be one and a half times as long as it was wide. The streets ran out from it in a grid. A grid is a pattern of straight streets that cross each other at right angles, like the lines on a sheet of graph paper. The founders had to set aside certain lots, or pieces of land, for the church and for the government buildings. Writers on the history of town planning count these among the first standard town-planning rules written anywhere in the world. A standard rule is one that sets the same pattern for every town, wherever Spanish officials founded one.

St. Augustine's Plaza de la Constitucion was laid out under those 1573 ordinances, after the town already existed. Government buildings and the church faced onto it, and traders began holding a market there in 1598. It is the oldest public square in the country. Across what is now the Southwest, Spanish town-builders went on laying out towns in the same plaza-and-grid pattern.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Each of the colonial towns in this era went up in the homeland of a Native nation. English colonists began Jamestown in 1607 as a fort. Boston, founded in 1630, had no plan at all, and its streets formed around the houses and the hills. Spanish officials laid out Santa Fe around a plaza. In 1682 William Penn had his surveyor, the man who measured the land and marked out the streets, draw the whole of Philadelphia on paper before a single street existed on the ground. Penn's plan set the pattern for many American towns that came after it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort towns, walls, and watchmen" -->
English colonists landed on Jamestown Island, on the James River in Virginia, in May 1607. The island lay inside the land of the Paspahegh people, who hunted there and had a town about four miles away. The Paspahegh were part of a large group of nations led by the chief Powhatan. On May 26 the Paspahegh attacked the newcomers. By June 15 the colonists had finished James Fort, a three-sided fort. Its wall was a stockade, a fence of upright oak and poplar logs about 14 feet high. In the 1620s the surveyor William Claiborne laid out a settlement east of the fort called New Towne. Archaeologists have dug up the foundations of rows of houses there.

Governor Pedro de Peralta founded Santa Fe about 1610 and planned it around a central plaza, in the Spanish pattern. He and his men laid it out at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, on the ruins of an older Pueblo town. The Tewa people call that place Ogha Po'oge, which means "white shell water place." The Palace of the Governors stands on Santa Fe's plaza. People have used it as a public building without a break for longer than any other building in the United States.

English colonists founded Boston in 1630 without any plan, on a peninsula that the Massachusett people called Shawmut. A peninsula is land with water on almost every side. The colonists put their houses up near the waterfront, and the streets formed around the hills, brooks and marshes that were already on the ground. The biggest hill was the Trimountain, a steep hill with three tops, which is now called Beacon Hill. According to a well-known story, Boston's crooked streets follow old cow paths. The story is a legend. Writers who have checked it, among them a writer for Boston Magazine in 2018, trace it to a line the writer Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in 1860.

Boston's first houses had chimneys made of wood and roofs made of thatch, which is dried straw or reeds. Several of them burned in the colony's first months. In March 1631 Thomas Dudley, one of the colony's leaders, wrote in a letter that the leaders had ordered "that noe man shall build his chimney with wood nor cover his house with thatch." The fires, he wrote, always began "in the wooden chimneys." That same year the people of Boston set up a night watch. The watchmen walked the streets at night to guard the town against criminals, wild animals and fire. The Boston Police Department's own history lists that watch as the start of the city's police.

Wall Street in New York City runs along the line of a wall that is long gone. The wall ran across the northern edge of New Amsterdam, the Dutch town on the southern tip of Manhattan. Manhattan was part of the homeland of the Lenape people. The wall stretched from the East River to the North River, which is now called the Hudson. A gang of Black men, owned by the Dutch West India Company or held by it under half-freedom, dug the trench for it in 1653. The Company was a Dutch trading company, and its officials ran the colony. According to a Columbia University history project, the men dug as long as daylight allowed. Their trench was three feet deep and ran all the way across the island. They stood logs 12 feet long and 18 inches around up in it and packed dirt and stones around each one. The finished wall was made of planks and stood about five or six feet high, from river to river.

Petrus Stuyvesant and the men who ran the town, called burgomasters and schepens, ordered the wall on March 13, 1653. Stuyvesant was the director general, the man who governed the Dutch colony of New Netherland for the Company. He and the town's officials had heard that the English in New England were preparing to attack. Under their order, the soldiers, the other servants of the Company and the half-free Black men, "no one excepted," had to build up the fort. The townsmen, also "without exception," had to dig the ditch from river to river. The officials put the cost at 3,166 guilders, the Dutch money of the time. Townsmen paid that money in, and the minutes of the officials' meetings, their written record, name the men who paid. Officials reported the wall finished on July 28, 1653.

The Columbia project, called MAAP, names three of the men who dug the trench: Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo and Anthony Portuguese. All three had what the Dutch called half-freedom. They were among eleven enslaved men who had worked for the Company for eighteen or nineteen years. The eleven asked the colony's council to free them, saying the Company had promised them their freedom. On February 25, 1644, the Company's director, Willem Kieft, and his council granted half-freedom to the eleven men and their wives, on conditions. Under half-freedom the eleven could hold land, and the Company gave them plots north of the town. But their children, "at present born or yet to be born," stayed the property of the Dutch West India Company. Each of the eleven owed the Company a payment every year that Dutch colonists did not owe: 30 skepels of grain or beans, a skepel being a Dutch measure, and one hog. Each also had to come back and work for the Company whenever its officials called.

On August 12, 1658, a rattle watch began walking the streets of New Amsterdam. Its members were paid night patrolmen. They carried wooden rattles, noisemakers that clack loudly when spun, to raise the alarm. The rattle watch was among the first paid public police forces in America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Penn's plan" -->
In 1682 William Penn made Thomas Holme his surveyor-general, the chief surveyor of his colony of Pennsylvania. The land along the Delaware River was the homeland of the Lenape people. When Penn's colonists arrived, about 2,500 Lenape and about 3,000 Europeans lived on the two sides of the river. The Europeans were Dutch, Swedish and Finnish settlers, and English settlers in West New Jersey. From 1682 to 1684 Penn bought land in southeastern Pennsylvania from the Lenape, paying at least £1,200, which is 1,200 English pounds, in goods. Penn himself landed on October 28, 1682. Holme drew the grid of Philadelphia in December 1682 and had it measured onto the ground. His map, "A Portraiture of the City of Philadelphia," was printed in 1683. Penn used the map to guide the work. He also used it to advertise the town and draw settlers to it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-penn" name="William Penn" movie="Penn of Pennsylvania (1941)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Penn

> **Who:** The founder of Pennsylvania, a Quaker, who had Philadelphia planned on paper before it existed on the ground. Quakers, also called the Religious Society of Friends, are a Christian group that began in England in the 1600s.
> **When and where:** The grid laid out by his surveyor-general Thomas Holme in December 1682, and Holme's map published in 1683.
> **Movie:** *Penn of Pennsylvania* (1941), released in the United States as *The Courageous Mr. Penn* (1942). The film tells Penn's life in general, not the making of the city plan.

Penn wrote down the kind of town he wanted: "a greene Country Towne which will never be burnt, and always be wholesome." He wrote those words in his instructions for laying out the town. Wholesome meant healthy to live in.

Holme drew a straight grid on the land between the Delaware and the Schuylkill rivers. Two wide main streets crossed it, Broad Street and High Street. High Street is now called Market Street. Holme drew the streets wide, partly because of the fire that burned London in 1666. Where the two main streets crossed, Holme placed a large central square, with one smaller public square in each of the four quarters of the town.

All five squares are still there. The four quarter squares are called Logan, Franklin, Washington and Rittenhouse squares, and Philadelphia's City Hall stands on the center square.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
By about 1750, four colonial ports each held between 8,000 and 13,000 people. In towns that size, fire was the first danger that people organized against.

Around 1750 about 13,000 people lived in Philadelphia, about 12,000 in Boston, about 11,000 in New York, and about 8,000 in Charleston. Some sources date the Charleston count to 1760 rather than 1750. These four were the biggest towns in the colonies. London at the same date had over 700,000 people.

Boston was the busiest port until about 1750. After that, New York and Philadelphia passed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fire in wooden towns" -->
On October 2 and 3, 1711, a fire burned through Boston. It destroyed some of the town's main buildings and left many families without homes. A few months later, twelve men were appointed to Boston's first Board of Fire Wards, a group of town officers for fires. In Charleston, the townspeople elected "firemasters." At a fire, a firemaster's orders had the force of law.

People in Boston also formed mutual fire societies. Mutual means that the members help one another. The members of each society agreed to protect one another's houses, and they had no duty to fight a fire at the house of anyone outside the society.

In 1736 men in Philadelphia formed a company of volunteers who agreed to fight a fire at any house in the town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-fire-company-city-building" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A printer who organized groups of Philadelphians to improve their town. He helped start the first volunteer fire company in the colonies that protected a whole town rather than only its own members.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, December 7, 1736.

Franklin printed a newspaper, the *Pennsylvania Gazette*. On February 4, 1735, he printed an unsigned letter in it, "On Protection of Towns from Fire," which he had written himself. The letter warned people about carrying live coals from room to room. Its advice began with a line people still repeat: "an Ounce of Prevention is worth a Pound of Cure." The next year he and other men in the city set up a fire company themselves.

On December 7, 1736, about twenty-five men, Franklin among them, signed the Articles of the Union Fire Company, the written rules of the group. Each member had to bring six leather buckets and two strong linen bags to every fire. Members carried water in the buckets. They used the bags to pack up people's belongings and carry them out of a burning house. Every member had to come out at every fire alarm, to any house in the town, whether its owner was a member or not.

Other Philadelphians soon set up more fire companies, among them the Heart-in-Hand, the Britannia and the Fellowship, and Franklin encouraged them. Volunteer companies fought the fires in American towns until April 1, 1853. On that day the first fire department in the United States whose firefighters were all paid began work in Cincinnati, Ohio, with Miles Greenwood as its first chief.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-franklin-fire-company-city-building" -->

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1790 and 1791 the members of Congress and President George Washington chose ground on the Potomac River for the capital city of the new country. Pierre L'Enfant drew the whole city on paper in 1791, before anyone built it. Three Native towns had once stood on that ground. By the spring of 1791 the chosen square held two towns that shipped tobacco, and enslaved people worked the farmland around them.

In 1793 yellow fever killed about one person in every ten in Philadelphia. After the fever came back in 1798, workers in Philadelphia built the first public waterworks in the country driven by steam.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ground the capital was built on" -->
On July 16, 1790, President George Washington signed the Residence Act, a law the members of Congress had passed. In it they placed the capital somewhere on the Potomac River, inside a square no bigger than ten miles on each side. They left it to Washington to say exactly where, and to appoint three commissioners and a surveyor for the work. He rode out and looked at the country himself and chose the ground at and beside Georgetown. He told Congress his choice on January 24, 1791.

Three Native towns had stood inside that square of land. The largest was Nacotchtank, on the southeast side of the Anacostia River, and the river's name comes from the town's name. A second town, Nameroughquena, stood on the west bank of the Potomac, across from today's Theodore Roosevelt Island. The third stood on a bluff in the northwest part of today's city, and the record does not give its name.

In 1608 Captain John Smith reached that stretch of the Potomac. A National Park Service history of the Native peoples of Washington states what followed. Within forty years of that first contact, only about a quarter as many Native people lived in the region as before 1608. People had died of diseases that Europeans brought, and they had died in wars. Others joined nations to the north, south and west. The Histories of the National Mall project at George Mason University puts it this way: the Native communities on that land "had been driven away by war and disease."

Two towns stood inside the square by the spring of 1791, and both are still there. In 1749 Virginia's colonial lawmakers voted to create the town of Alexandria. The county surveyor, John West, laid out its first sixty acres around Hugh West's tobacco warehouse. In 1751 Maryland's colonial lawmakers approved the town of Georgetown. They bought sixty acres for it from George Gordon and George Beall for £280, which is 280 English pounds. The site already had a tobacco inspection house, a building where tobacco was checked before it was shipped. Georgetown grew into a tobacco port. Washington chose ground at and next to Georgetown. On March 3, 1791, the members of Congress changed the law so that the district could reach south and take in Alexandria too. Jones Point, where the survey of the district's boundary began, is at Alexandria.

The rest of the land was farms, and the main crop was tobacco. Five men owned the ground where the National Mall is now: Daniel Carroll of Duddington, David Burnes, Notley Young, Benjamin Oden and Samuel Davidson. The Histories of the National Mall project reports that the men who owned this land enslaved people, and the enslaved men and women raised tobacco, corn, wheat and cattle on it.

Notley Young's tobacco plantation covered 800 acres. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture stands on part of that plantation. The museum gives the number of people enslaved there as 245, from the census of 1790. The Histories of the National Mall project gives 260. Federal officials bought Young's land in 1791.

In all, nineteen men owned the land for the new city. Washington met them at a dinner in Georgetown, and they signed an agreement on March 30, 1791. The owners put about 6,000 acres together. They got nothing for the land that became streets. For land taken for public buildings and squares, they were paid £25 an acre. The rest was split into 20,272 building lots, and half of the lots went back to the owners. These men were later called the Original Proprietors, which means the owners of the land when the city was laid out.

Before anyone could build the city, a crew had to survey the boundary, which means measuring the ground and marking exactly where the district's corners and edges lay. Someone also had to draw the plan of the city that would go inside it. Andrew Ellicott ran the boundary survey. Benjamin Banneker, a free Black farmer from Maryland, did the astronomy for it, working under Ellicott. Pierre L'Enfant, an engineer born in France, drew the city plan in 1791, and Washington dismissed him the next year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-lenfant" name="Pierre L'Enfant" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pierre L'Enfant

> **Who:** The French-born engineer who designed the plan of Washington, D.C., and was dismissed before it was built.
> **When and where:** The federal district, 1791 to February 1792.

In 1791 Pierre Charles L'Enfant drew the plan of the capital. He laid down a street grid and then drew diagonal avenues across it that meet at circles and squares.

Under the Residence Act, Washington appointed three commissioners to oversee the new city, and L'Enfant worked under them. The commissioners asked for his original drawing so that an engraver could cut it into a printing plate and print copies. L'Enfant refused to hand it over, and he clashed with the commissioners. In February 1792 George Washington dismissed him for insubordination, which means refusing to obey the people in charge of you.

Andrew Ellicott, with help from his brother Benjamin, rebuilt the plan and changed parts of it. He straightened Massachusetts Avenue and took out some of the squares. The engravers Thackara and Vallance made an engraving of Ellicott's version in March 1792. Copies of their engraving were the first plan of the city that many people saw. The city was built from that engraving, which was L'Enfant's plan as Ellicott had changed it.

L'Enfant died poor in 1825. He was buried on a farm in Prince George's County, Maryland, that belonged to his friend William Dudley Digges. In 1908 the members of Congress set aside $1,000 for a new grave. On April 28, 1909, his coffin lay at the U.S. Capitol for three hours, so that people could pay their respects. He was the first person from another country to be given that honor. That afternoon he was buried again at Arlington National Cemetery, on a hillside above the city he had planned.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pierre-lenfant" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-banneker" name="Benjamin Banneker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Banneker

> **Who:** A free Black tobacco farmer and self-taught astronomer who made the observations that fixed the starting corner of the federal district.
> **When and where:** The survey's base camp at Jones Point, Virginia, February to April 1791.

George Ellicott recommended Banneker, and Major Andrew Ellicott hired him as scientific assistant on the survey of the ten-mile square. Banneker was nearly sixty. He farmed tobacco, and he had taught himself astronomy, the science of the stars and planets.

Banneker worked at night. To find a position on the earth by astronomy, an observer watches particular stars cross a particular point in the sky and records the exact moment. The observer then calculates the position from those times. Banneker made those observations and did those calculations. He also kept the regulator clock running true. A regulator clock is a very exact clock, and every one of those timings was measured against it. Banneker used his observations and calculations to fix the district's south corner at Jones Point. He was paid $2 a day, an ordinary day's pay for an assistant. Andrew Ellicott was paid $5.

Banneker left the survey in the spring of 1791, before the crew had run the full forty miles of the boundary. He was in poor health. His first almanac was the one for the year 1792. An almanac is a book printed once a year with a calendar and tables of the sun, moon and stars.

According to a popular story, Banneker redrew the whole plan of Washington from memory after L'Enfant was dismissed. The story is a legend. Banneker had left the project about ten months before L'Enfant was dismissed, and Andrew Ellicott rebuilt the plan. The historian Silvio Bedini and writers for the National Park Service and the Library of Congress have worked through the records. They credit Banneker with the astronomy for the boundary survey. His observations fixed the survey's south corner at Jones Point.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="benjamin-banneker" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Water after the fevers" -->
Yellow fever is a disease caused by a virus, a germ far too small to see. People catch it from the bite of a mosquito that carries the virus. Most people who fall sick with it have fever, chills, headache and vomiting, and they get better within a week.

In about one case in seven, the fever eases and then comes back worse. In that stage the virus destroys cells in the liver. As the liver fails, the skin and the whites of the eyes turn yellow, and that color gives the disease its name. The person bleeds from the mouth, the nose and the eyes, and into the stomach. In the stomach the blood mixes with stomach acid, and the person vomits it up black. People at the time called it the black vomit. The kidneys fail as well. Of the people who reach that stage, between 30 and 60 percent die.

Yellow fever killed about 5,000 people in Philadelphia between August 1 and November 9, 1793. About 50,000 people lived in the city, so the fever killed roughly one of every ten of them. About 20,000 other people fled the city, among them the officials of the new federal government.

In 1797 a group of Philadelphians asked the city's councils to take charge of the water supply, and the council members formed a Watering Committee. The fever came back in 1798. The city's leaders then hired Benjamin Henry Latrobe, an English-born architect and engineer, to design a waterworks, and in 1799 the members of the Watering Committee accepted his design. Workers began building the Centre Square Water Works that year. Nicholas J. Roosevelt built its engines and pumps under a contract worth $33,000. The steam engine went up from February 1800.

On January 21, 1801, the steam pumps started supplying the city. The engines pumped water from the Schuylkill River through a tunnel under Chestnut Street to the pump house, a white marble building on Centre Square. From there the water ran through the streets in pipes made of hollowed-out logs. Philadelphia was the first American city with a public water supply driven by steam.

The engines broke down often and cost a great deal to keep running. Centre Square ran for only fourteen years. In 1815 the Fairmount Water Works took its place.
