## Before 1500

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" label="Chapter opening" -->
**Chapter 6 of 37 · City Building · 10 eras · about 90 minutes**

**American cities were built by people who decided where the streets, pipes and buildings would go, and who could live where.**

About 600 men lifted a row of brick and stone buildings on Lake Street in Chicago in 1860. The row was 320 feet long, and with its sidewalks it weighed about 35,000 tons. The men turned about 6,000 lifting screws, and in five days the whole row rose 4 feet 8 inches, up to a new street that crews had built on top of the city's sewers. The shops inside stayed open the whole time, and people kept shopping while the buildings rose.

This chapter follows American cities in order, from the earth mounds of Cahokia to the empty office towers of today. As you read, watch the problems people were trying to fix: fire in wooden towns, sickness from dirty water, streets of mud, families packed into dark rooms. And watch who got to live where. Every colonial town and boomtown here went up on a Native nation's land. In the 1900s, white owners and judges kept Black families out of most neighborhoods, and federal officials refused to back home loans where Black families lived. Later, officials cleared whole neighborhoods for developers and ran freeways through others.

**Three questions this chapter answers**
- Why do so many American cities have straight streets that cross in a grid?
- How did people get clean water into crowded cities?
- Who decided which families could live in which neighborhoods?

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 1 of 10 · Before 1500 · about 4 minutes**

Hundreds of years before any European came, people on this land built planned cities, deciding ahead of time where each open square, wall and mound would go. They built them between about 900 and about 1350. The largest stood beside the Mississippi River. In the Southwest, people cut and stacked stone to build towns, and their descendants still live in towns along the Rio Grande.

The people who built these places kept no written records, and no builder's, planner's or ruler's name survives. Archaeologists, the scientists who dig up and study what people left behind long ago, know them only from the mounds, walls, rooms and roads they left.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cahokia, a city laid out on purpose" -->
People built up the city of Cahokia quickly from about 1050, in what is now Illinois, across the Mississippi River from St. Louis, Missouri. It was the largest town north of Mexico before Europeans came. At its peak, around 1100, roughly 10,000 to 20,000 people lived in the center, about as many as lived in London at the same date. Some estimates put as many as 50,000 people in the wider settlement around the center, but those larger estimates do not agree with one another.

The people of Cahokia planned where the big pieces of their city would go. At its center they raised Monks Mound. It is the largest prehistoric structure made of earth in the Americas, built before anyone here kept written records. The mound holds about 814,000 cubic yards of earth. Workers carried every basketload of it by hand, and they raised the mound in stages.

**The mound.** It has four terraces, or wide steps, and its top stands about 100 feet high. Its base covers roughly 14 acres. The World Heritage listing for Cahokia, kept by UNESCO, the United Nations body for historic sites, records fourteen separate building stages in the mound.

The Grand Plaza, about 50 acres of open ground, lay at the foot of the mound. Its builders leveled it by hand and filled in the low spots, to make a flat field for ceremonies and for the game of chunkey. In chunkey, one player rolled a stone disk across the ground, and the players threw spears to see whose spear landed closest to the spot where the stone stopped.

West of Monks Mound the builders set up a circle of red cedar posts. They built five of these circles, one after another, and each new circle took the place of the last.

**A calendar of posts.** The posts line up with the points on the horizon where the sun rises on four days of the year. Two are the solstices, the longest day and the shortest day. The other two are the equinoxes, the days between them when day and night are the same length. So the posts worked as a calendar, and the builders set them into the plan of the city.

A palisade, a wall of upright logs about two miles long, ran around the central part of the city. People began it around 1100 and rebuilt it three times over about two hundred years. Each rebuilding took between 15,000 and 20,000 logs of oak and hickory, each roughly a foot thick and twenty feet tall.

The people of Cahokia had scattered and left the city by about 1350.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Stone towns of the Southwest" -->
Chaco Canyon, in what is now northwestern New Mexico, was the center of the Ancestral Puebloan world. The Ancestral Puebloans were the ancestors of the people who live in the pueblos of the Southwest today. Spanish colonists later used pueblo, their word for a town, for the towns of stone and adobe, or sun-dried mud brick, that they found there.

At Chaco the people built great houses, large buildings of shaped stone. They went on building and using the great houses for roughly 280 years, from about 900 to about 1180. One of them, Pueblo Bonito, had more than 650 rooms.

The people of Chaco planned their roads too. The roads run straight for miles across the Four Corners country, where Colorado, Utah, Arizona and New Mexico meet. They average about 30 feet wide, and they connected farther-off villages to the canyon.

At Mesa Verde, in southwestern Colorado, people built rooms of sandstone blocks and mud into the hollows under overhanging cliffs. They built Cliff Palace, the largest of these cliff dwellings, between 1190 and 1280. It has 150 rooms and 23 kivas, round rooms used for ceremonies and meetings and usually sunk into the ground. More than 100 people lived there.

People began leaving Chaco in the 1140s, and by about 1180 to 1200 they had stopped building and living in the canyon. People had left Mesa Verde by the 1280s. The people who left both places gathered again in the pueblo towns along the Rio Grande, and their descendants still live in those pueblos.

## The 1500s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 2 of 10 · The 1500s · about 3 minutes**

The oldest lasting European town on the US mainland began inside a Timucua town. Eight years after Spanish colonists founded it, the king of Spain set written rules for how every new Spanish town must be laid out.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="St. Augustine and the Laws of the Indies" -->
Pedro Menéndez de Avilés landed on the coast of Florida on September 8, 1565, with about 800 colonists, among them 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans. He placed his town, St. Augustine, inside Seloy's town, a town of the Timucua people whose chief was named Seloy. Chief Seloy offered Menéndez his council house, and the colonists used it as their first fort. Timucua council houses were round and roofed with thatch, dried straw or reeds, and they were big enough to hold up to a thousand people.

Within months the Timucua had tired of the Spaniards living in their town, and they attacked them again and again to drive them away. In 1566, according to the Florida Museum of Natural History, the Timucua shot flaming arrows at the fort. The fort was the Timucua's own council house, and the museum's account states that this is why the attack did so much harm. On April 1, 1566, half of the fort burned, and the Spaniards lost all their supplies.

**Three places in seven years.** Menéndez moved the settlement across the bay to Anastasia Island in May 1566, about nine months after landing. There the Spaniards felt safer from attack, and they lived on the island for about six years. By about 1572 they had moved back to the mainland, to the site of today's St. Augustine.

Through both moves, people went on living in the town. Of all the cities Europeans founded on the mainland of the United States, St. Augustine is the oldest one that people have lived in the whole time.

King Philip II of Spain issued the Laws of the Indies in 1573. They were written orders for Spain's lands in the Americas, which the Spanish called the Indies, and they stated exactly how the men who founded Spanish towns had to lay them out.

Each town began with a central plaza, a public square of open ground with the town built around it. The plaza's sides had to face north, south, east and west, and it had to be one and a half times as long as it was wide. The streets ran out from it in a grid, straight streets crossing at right angles. The founders had to set aside certain lots, or pieces of land, for the church and for the government buildings.

St. Augustine's Plaza de la Constitucion was laid out under those orders, after the town already existed. It is the oldest public square in the country. Government buildings and the church faced onto it, and traders began holding a market there in 1598.

The 1573 orders were among the first written rules anywhere in the world that specified the same town plan for every new town, wherever Spanish officials founded one. Across what is now the Southwest, Spanish town-builders went on laying out towns in the same plaza-and-grid pattern.

## The 1600s

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 3 of 10 · The 1600s · about 7 minutes**

Each colonial town of the 1600s went up in the homeland of a Native nation, and some were planned while others simply grew. English colonists began Jamestown in 1607 as a fort. Spanish officials laid out Santa Fe around a plaza. Boston, founded in 1630, had no plan at all, and its streets formed around the houses and the hills.

William Penn planned the most. In 1682 he had his surveyor, the man who measured the land and marked out the streets, draw the whole of Philadelphia on paper before a single street existed on the ground. The founders of many early American towns later followed Penn's example.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Fort towns, walls, and watchmen" -->
Three English ships sailed into the Chesapeake Bay on April 26, 1607, with 144 English men and boys. They settled on Jamestown Island, on the James River in Virginia. The island lay inside the land of the Paspahegh people, who hunted there and had a town about four miles away. The Paspahegh were one of 28 to 32 nations, each with its own chief, that paid tribute, a payment of goods, to one ruler, Powhatan.

The Paspahegh attacked the newcomers on May 26. By June 15 the colonists had finished James Fort, a three-sided fort. Its wall was a stockade, a fence of upright oak and poplar logs about 14 feet high. In the 1620s the surveyor William Claiborne laid out a settlement east of the fort called New Towne, and archaeologists have dug up the foundations of rows of houses there.

Governor Pedro de Peralta founded Santa Fe about 1610 and planned it around a central plaza, in the Spanish pattern. He and his men laid it out at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, on the ruins of an older Pueblo town. The Tewa people call that place O'Ga P'Ogeh, which means "white shell water place." The Palace of the Governors stands on Santa Fe's plaza. People have used it as a public building without a break for longer than any other building in the United States.

English colonists founded Boston in 1630 without any plan, on a peninsula that the Massachusett people called Shawmut, a piece of land with water on almost every side. The colonists put their houses up near the waterfront, and the streets formed around the hills, brooks and marshes that were already on the ground. The biggest hill was the Trimountain, a steep hill with three tops, which is now called Beacon Hill.

**The cow-path story.** According to a well-known story, Boston's crooked streets follow old cow paths. The story is a legend. A writer for Boston Magazine checked it in 2018 and traced it to a line that the American writer Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in 1860.

Boston's first houses had chimneys made of wood and roofs made of thatch, and several of them burned in the colony's first months. Thomas Dudley, one of the colony's leaders, wrote a letter to the Countess of Lincoln in March 1631. In it he wrote that he and the other leaders had ordered "that noe man shall build his chimney with wood nor cover his house with thatch." The fires, he wrote, always began "in the wooden chimneys."

The people of Boston set up a night watch that same year. The watchmen walked the streets at night to guard the town against criminals, wild animals and fire. The Boston Police Department's own history lists that watch as the start of the city's police.

Wall Street in New York City runs along the line of a wall that is long gone. The wall ran across the northern edge of New Amsterdam, the Dutch town on the southern tip of Manhattan, in the homeland of the Lenape people. It stretched from the East River to the North River, which is now called the Hudson.

**Who dug it.** A gang of Black men dug a trench across the island for the wall in 1653. The Dutch West India Company, the trading company whose officials ran the colony, owned them. Some of the men had gained what the Dutch called half-freedom: a half-free man could hold land of his own, but he still owed the Company work and a yearly payment, and his children stayed the Company's property.

According to a Columbia University history project, the men dug as long as daylight allowed, all the way from the East River to the Hudson. The town's records show what went up along that line: a wall of planks 9 feet high, nailed to posts, from river to river. The officials had first planned a wall of sharpened logs 12 feet long, but nobody would build it for the price they offered, so they changed the plan to planks.

**Who ordered it.** Petrus Stuyvesant and the town's officials ordered the wall on March 13, 1653. Stuyvesant was the director general, the man who governed the Dutch colony of New Netherland for the Company. He and the officials had heard that the English in New England were preparing to attack.

In April, Stuyvesant's council gave out the rest of the work. The townsmen, "without exception," had to dig a ditch in front of the wall from river to river. The ditch was 4 to 5 feet deep and 11 to 12 feet wide, a much bigger hole than the trench, and it was there to slow down attackers. The soldiers, the other servants of the Company and the half-free Black men, "no one excepted," had to finish building up the town's fort.

So the order sent the half-free Black men to the fort, not to the ditch. The city's archivists write that enslaved and free workers built the wall itself together. The officials put the cost at 3,166 guilders, the Dutch money of the time. Townsmen paid that money in, and the minutes of the officials' meetings, their written record, name the men who paid. Officials reported the wall finished on July 28, 1653.

The Columbia project names three of the men who dug the trench: Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo and Anthony Portuguese. All three were half-free. They were among eleven enslaved men who had worked for the Company for eighteen or nineteen years. The eleven asked the colony's council to free them, saying the Company had promised them their freedom.

The Company's director at the time, Willem Kieft, and his council granted half-freedom to the eleven men and their wives on February 25, 1644, nine years before the wall, on conditions. Kieft and his council gave the men plots of land north of the town. But their children, "at present born or yet to be born," stayed the property of the Dutch West India Company.

Each of the eleven owed the Company a yearly payment that Dutch colonists did not owe: 30 skepels (a Dutch measure for dry goods) of grain or beans, plus one hog. And whenever the Company's officials called, each had to come back and work for the Company.

A rattle watch began walking the streets of New Amsterdam on August 12, 1658. Its members were paid night patrolmen who carried wooden rattles, noisemakers that clack loudly when spun. The rattle watch was among the first paid public police forces in America.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Philadelphia, drawn before it was built" -->
William Penn made Thomas Holme the surveyor-general, or chief surveyor, of his colony of Pennsylvania in 1682. The land along the Delaware River was the homeland of the Lenape people. When Penn's colonists arrived, about 2,500 Lenape and about 3,000 Europeans lived on the two sides of the river. The Europeans were Dutch, Swedish and Finnish settlers, and English settlers in West New Jersey.

Penn bought land in southeastern Pennsylvania from the Lenape from 1682 to 1684, paying goods worth at least 1,200 English pounds. He landed on October 28, 1682, and Holme laid out the grid of Philadelphia that December. Holme's map, "A Portraiture of the City of Philadelphia," was printed in 1683. Penn used the map to guide the work, and to advertise the town and draw settlers to it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="william-penn" name="William Penn" movie="Penn of Pennsylvania (1941)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### William Penn

> **Who:** The founder of Pennsylvania, a Quaker (a member of the Religious Society of Friends, a Christian group that began in England in the 1600s), who had Philadelphia planned on paper before it existed on the ground.
> **When and where:** The grid laid out by his surveyor-general Thomas Holme in December 1682, and Holme's map published in 1683.
> **Movie:** *Penn of Pennsylvania* (1941), released in the United States as *The Courageous Mr. Penn* (1942). The film is about Penn's life in general, not the making of the city plan.

**Holme drew the streets wide partly because London had burned in 1666.**

In his instructions for laying out the town, Penn asked for "a greene Country Towne which will never be burnt, and always be wholesome," meaning healthy to live in.

Holme drew a straight grid on the land between the Delaware and the Schuylkill rivers. Two wide main streets crossed it, Broad Street and High Street, which is now called Market Street. Holme drew the streets wide, partly because of the fire that burned London in 1666. Where the two main streets crossed, he placed a large central square, with one smaller public square in each of the four quarters of the town.

All five squares are still there. The four quarter squares are called Logan, Franklin, Washington and Rittenhouse squares, and Philadelphia's City Hall stands on the center square.

## 1700 to 1750

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 4 of 10 · 1700 to 1750 · about 2 minutes**

As the four biggest colonial ports grew to between 8,000 and 13,000 people each, the people in them organized to fight fire. Around 1750 about 13,000 people lived in Philadelphia, about 12,000 in Boston, about 11,000 in New York, and about 8,000 in Charleston, though some of the Charleston counts come from 1760 rather than 1750. London at the same date had over 700,000 people.

Boston was the busiest port until about 1750, when New York and Philadelphia passed it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who fought fires in a wooden town?" -->
Town officers and volunteers fought the fires. In Boston, a fire burned through the town on October 2 and 3, 1711. It destroyed some of the town's main buildings and left many families without homes.

A few months later, twelve men became Boston's first Board of Fire Wards, a group of town officers for fires. The Boston Public Library's guide to the city's great fires does not state who chose them or list their duties. Charleston had "firemasters," officers chosen by a vote. At a fire, everyone had to obey a firemaster's orders as if they were the law.

People in Boston also formed mutual fire societies, groups whose members helped one another. The members of each society agreed to protect one another's houses, and they had no duty to fight a fire at the house of anyone outside the society.

Men in Philadelphia went further in 1736. They formed a company of volunteers who agreed to fight a fire at any house in the town.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-franklin-fire-company-city-building" name="Benjamin Franklin" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Franklin

> **Who:** A Philadelphia printer who helped start the first volunteer fire company in the colonies that protected a whole town, not only its own members.
> **When and where:** Philadelphia, December 7, 1736.

**Every member had to bring six leather buckets to every fire.**

Franklin printed a newspaper, the *Pennsylvania Gazette*. On February 4, 1735, he printed an unsigned letter in it, "On Protection of Towns from Fire," which he had written himself. In the letter he warned people about carrying live coals from room to room. His advice began with the line "an Ounce of Prevention is worth a Pound of Cure." The next year he and other men in the city set up a fire company themselves.

About twenty-five men, Franklin among them, signed the Articles of the Union Fire Company, the written rules of the group, on December 7, 1736. Each member had to bring six leather buckets and two strong linen bags to every fire. Members carried water in the buckets, and they used the bags to pack up people's belongings and carry them out of a burning house.

Every member had to come out at every fire alarm, to any house in the town, whether its owner was a member or not. Other Philadelphians soon set up more fire companies, among them the Heart-in-Hand, the Britannia and the Fellowship, and Franklin encouraged them.

Volunteer companies fought the fires in American towns for more than a hundred years. Then, on April 1, 1853, firefighters in Cincinnati, Ohio, began work as the first fire department in the United States whose firefighters were all paid. Miles Greenwood was their first chief.

## 1750 to 1800

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
**Era 5 of 10 · 1750 to 1800 · about 9 minutes**

The leaders of the new country had their capital city drawn on paper before anyone built it, on ground where three Native towns had once stood. The members of Congress put the capital on the Potomac River by a law of 1790, and in 1791 President George Washington chose the exact ground. By the spring of 1791 the chosen square held two towns that shipped tobacco, and enslaved people worked the farmland around them. Pierre L'Enfant drew the whole city on paper that year.

In Philadelphia, yellow fever killed about one person in every ten in 1793. After the fever came back in 1798, workers there built the first public waterworks in the country driven by steam.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The ground the capital was built on" -->
President George Washington signed the Residence Act, a law the members of Congress had passed, on July 16, 1790. In it they placed the capital somewhere on the Potomac River, inside a square no bigger than ten miles on each side. They left it to Washington to say exactly where, and to appoint a surveyor and three commissioners, officials put in charge of building the capital.

Washington rode out and looked at the country himself. He chose the ground at and beside Georgetown, and he told Congress his choice on January 24, 1791.

Three Native towns had stood inside that square of land. The largest was Nacotchtank, on the southeast side of the Anacostia River, and the river's name comes from the town's name. A second town, Nameroughquena, stood on the west bank of the Potomac, across from today's Theodore Roosevelt Island. The third stood on a bluff, a high, steep bank, in the northwest part of today's city, and the record does not give its name.

Captain John Smith reached that stretch of the Potomac in 1608. Within forty years of that first contact, only about a quarter as many Native people lived in the region as before 1608, according to a National Park Service history of the Native peoples of Washington. Many had died of diseases that Europeans brought, and others had been killed in wars, though the Park Service history does not say who fought them. Others joined nations to the north, south and west.

Two towns stood inside the square by the spring of 1791, and both are still there. Virginia's colonial lawmakers voted to create the town of Alexandria in 1749. The county surveyor, John West, laid out its first sixty acres around Hugh West's tobacco warehouse.

Maryland's colonial lawmakers approved the town of Georgetown in 1751. They bought sixty acres for it from George Gordon and George Beall for 280 English pounds. The site already had a tobacco inspection house, a building where tobacco was checked before it was shipped, and Georgetown grew into a tobacco port.

The members of Congress changed the law on March 3, 1791, so that the district, the land set aside for the capital, could reach south and take in Alexandria too. Jones Point, where the survey of the district's boundary began, is at Alexandria.

The rest of the land was farms, and the main crop was tobacco. Five men owned the ground where the National Mall is now. The Histories of the National Mall project at George Mason University reports that these men enslaved people, and the enslaved men and women raised tobacco, corn, wheat and cattle on the land.

The largest of the five farms was Notley Young's tobacco plantation, a big farm worked by enslaved people. It covered 800 acres, and the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture stands on part of it. The museum gives the number of people enslaved there as 245, from the 1790 census, the government's count of the people. The Histories of the National Mall project gives 260. Federal officials bought Young's land in 1791.

In all, nineteen men owned the land for the new city, about 6,000 acres. Washington met them at a dinner in Georgetown, and they signed an agreement on March 30, 1791.

**The deal.** The owners got nothing for the land that became streets. For land taken for public buildings and squares, they were paid by the acre. The rest of the land was split into building lots, and half of the lots went back to the owners. These men were later called the Original Proprietors, which means the owners of the land when the city was laid out.

Before anyone could build the city, a crew had to survey the boundary of the district, measuring the ground and marking exactly where its corners and edges lay. Someone also had to draw the plan of the city that would go inside it.

Andrew Ellicott ran the boundary survey. Benjamin Banneker, a free Black farmer from Baltimore County, Maryland, worked under him and did the survey's astronomy, the science of the stars and planets. Pierre L'Enfant, an engineer born in France, drew the city plan in 1791, and Washington dismissed him the next year.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="pierre-lenfant" name="Pierre L'Enfant" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Pierre L'Enfant

> **Who:** The French-born engineer who designed the plan of Washington, D.C., and was dismissed before it was built.
> **When and where:** The federal district, 1791 to February 1792.

**L'Enfant would not hand over his drawing, so Washington fired him.**

Pierre Charles L'Enfant drew the plan of the capital in 1791. He laid down a street grid and then drew diagonal avenues across it that meet at circles and squares.

The three commissioners Washington had appointed under the Residence Act asked for L'Enfant's original drawing, so that an engraver could cut it into a printing plate and print copies. L'Enfant refused to hand it over, and he clashed with the commissioners. In February 1792 George Washington dismissed him for insubordination, refusing to obey the people in charge of him.

Andrew Ellicott, with help from his brother Benjamin Ellicott, rebuilt the plan and changed parts of it. He straightened Massachusetts Avenue and took out some of the squares. The engravers Thackara and Vallance made an engraving of Ellicott's version in March 1792. Copies of their engraving were the first plan of the city that many people saw, and the city was built from it: L'Enfant's plan as Ellicott had changed it.

L'Enfant died poor in 1825. He was buried on a farm in Prince George's County, Maryland, that belonged to his friend William Dudley Digges.

**A second burial.** In 1908 the members of Congress set aside $1,000 for a new grave. On April 28, 1909, his coffin lay at the US Capitol for three hours, so that people could pay their respects. He was the first person from another country to be given that honor. That afternoon he was buried again at Arlington National Cemetery, on a hillside above the city he had planned.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="pierre-lenfant" -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="benjamin-banneker" name="Benjamin Banneker" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Benjamin Banneker

> **Who:** A free Black tobacco farmer and self-taught astronomer who made the observations that fixed the starting corner of the federal district.
> **When and where:** The survey's base camp at Jones Point, Virginia, February to April 1791.

**At nearly sixty, Banneker worked nights, timing the stars.**

George Ellicott, Andrew Ellicott's cousin and Banneker's neighbor and friend, recommended him, and Major Andrew Ellicott hired him as scientific assistant on the survey of the ten-mile square. Banneker was nearly sixty. He farmed tobacco, and he had taught himself astronomy.

Banneker worked at night. To find a position on the earth by astronomy, an observer watches particular stars cross a particular point in the sky and records the exact moment, then calculates the position from those times. Banneker made those observations and did those calculations, and he kept the survey's regulator clock, a very exact clock, running true.

He used his observations and calculations to fix the district's south corner at Jones Point. He was paid $2 a day, an ordinary day's pay for an assistant. Andrew Ellicott was paid $5.

Banneker left the survey in the spring of 1791, before the crew had run the full forty miles of the boundary. He was in poor health. His first almanac, a book printed once a year with a calendar and tables of the sun, moon and stars, was the one for the year 1792.

According to a popular story, Banneker redrew the whole plan of Washington from memory after L'Enfant was dismissed. The story is a legend. Banneker had left the project about ten months before L'Enfant was dismissed, and Andrew Ellicott rebuilt the plan. The historian Silvio Bedini and writers for the National Park Service and the Library of Congress have worked through the records. They credit Banneker with the astronomy for the boundary survey.

Yellow fever killed about 5,000 people in Philadelphia between August 1 and November 9, 1793. About 50,000 people lived in the city, so the fever killed roughly one of every ten of them. About 20,000 other people fled the city, among them the officials of the new federal government.

Yellow fever comes from a virus that mosquitoes carry from person to person. Most people who catch it get better within a week. In about one case in seven, the illness comes back worse and the virus destroys the liver, so the skin and the whites of the eyes turn yellow. The person bleeds from the mouth, nose and eyes and vomits blood turned black by stomach acid, which people then called the black vomit. Between 30 and 60 percent of these people die.

**What people blamed.** Nobody then knew that mosquitoes spread the fever. Many Philadelphians thought it came from dirty water or from rotting matter in the streets, so they wanted clean water for the city. In 1797 a group of them asked the city's councils to take charge of the water supply, and the council members formed a Watering Committee. The fever came back in 1798. The city's leaders then hired Benjamin Henry Latrobe, an English-born architect and engineer, to design a waterworks.

**The waterworks.** The members of the Watering Committee accepted Latrobe's design in 1799, and workers began building the Centre Square Water Works that year. Nicholas J. Roosevelt built its engines and pumps under a contract worth $33,000. The steam engine went up from February 1800.

The steam pumps started supplying the city on January 21, 1801. The engines pumped water from the Schuylkill River through a tunnel along Chestnut Street to the pump house, a white marble building on Centre Square. From there the water ran through the streets in pipes made of hollowed-out logs. Philadelphia was the first American city with a public water supply driven by steam.

**What the water could not do.** Clean water could not stop a disease that mosquitoes spread. The fever came back seven times in the twelve years after 1793. Each time, only the winter frost, which killed the mosquitoes, brought it to an end.

The engines broke down often and cost a great deal to keep running. Centre Square ran for only fourteen years. In 1815 the Fairmount Water Works took its place.
