## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Americans added amendments to the Constitution between 1913 and 1933 that let Congress tax incomes, let voters choose their senators, banned alcohol and then brought it back, and barred states from denying women the vote. President Franklin Roosevelt fought the Supreme Court's justices over his New Deal laws and was elected four times. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld a Virginia law under which doctors sterilized about 8,300 people, and they upheld the removal of Japanese Americans from their homes during World War II. City political machines still traded jobs and favors for votes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-washington-plunkitt" name="George Washington Plunkitt" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Washington Plunkitt

> **Who:** A New York machine politician who explained to a reporter, from a shoeshine stand outside the courthouse, how he made his money and kept his votes.
> **When and where:** The Fifteenth Assembly District, New York City. His talks were printed in 1905. He died in 1924.

George Washington Plunkitt was a district leader for Tammany Hall in New York's Fifteenth Assembly District. He also served as a state senator and as a member of the state assembly. He grew up poor and died rich.

A newspaperman named William L. Riordon wrote down Plunkitt's talks and published them in 1905 as *Plunkitt of Tammany Hall*. The title page says Plunkitt gave the talks "from His Rostrum," a speaker's stand, which was the shoeshine stand at the New York County Court House. The book is not a word-for-word record. Riordon gathered and shaped what Plunkitt said.

Plunkitt split graft into two kinds. Graft is money a person gets by using a public office. Dishonest graft, he said, meant squeezing money out of gamblers and illegal businesses, and he said he never did it. Honest graft meant learning where the city planned to build something, buying the land first and then selling it to the city at a profit. "I ain't no looter," he said. "The looter hogs it. I never hogged." His summary of himself is cut into his gravestone: "I seen my opportunities and I took 'em."

He also described the everyday work of a machine. When a building burned at night, Plunkitt and his captains came to the fire and found the families shelter and clothes on the spot. He knew employers across the city and got people jobs. His men found families in trouble before the charity groups did. He said he did it because those people remembered him on election day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-washington-plunkitt" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The food laws of 1906" -->
On June 30, 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt signed two laws on the same day. The Pure Food and Drug Act banned food, drink and drugs that were mislabeled or made impure from being sold across state lines. Harvey Wiley's Bureau of Chemistry enforced it. In 1930 that office was renamed the Food and Drug Administration. The Meat Inspection Act required federal inspectors in meatpacking plants, set rules for cleanliness and banned false labels. The laws followed Upton Sinclair's book *The Jungle*, published in February 1906, and years of work by Wiley's team.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two amendments in 1913" -->
The Sixteenth Amendment was ratified on February 3, 1913. It gave Congress the power to tax incomes "without apportionment among the several States." That meant the money did not have to be divided among the states by population. In 1894 members of Congress had put a 2 percent tax on incomes over $4,000 into a tariff bill, and the Supreme Court's justices had struck it down, 5 to 4. According to the National Archives, some of the conservatives who proposed the Sixteenth Amendment expected it never to be ratified.

The Seventeenth Amendment was ratified on April 8, 1913, when Connecticut became the state that completed the three-quarters needed. Before it, the members of each state's legislature chose that state's senators. After it, the voters chose them. Augustus Bacon of Georgia was the first senator elected by the voters under the new amendment, on July 15, 1913.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The grandfather clause struck down, 1915" -->
In 1910 an amendment to Oklahoma's constitution added a reading test for voters. Anyone who had been able to vote "on January 1st, 1866, or at any time prior thereto," and any "lineal descendant," meaning child or grandchild, of such a person, did not have to take the test. Almost no Black man could vote before 1866. So the test fell on Black men, and white men were excused from it.

Federal prosecutors charged two Oklahoma election officers, Frank Guinn and J. J. Beal, with keeping Black citizens from voting under this rule. On June 21, 1915, in *Guinn v. United States*, the justices of the Supreme Court held the 1866 clause void under the Fifteenth Amendment. Chief Justice Edward White wrote the opinion.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition and repeal" -->
Members of Congress passed the Eighteenth Amendment on December 18, 1917, and state legislatures ratified it on January 16, 1919. It banned "the manufacture, sale, or transportation of intoxicating liquors," meaning alcoholic drinks. The ban began one year after ratification, in January 1920.

Members of Congress passed the Twenty-first Amendment on February 20, 1933, and it was ratified on December 5, 1933. Its first section reads: "The eighteenth article of amendment to the Constitution of the United States is hereby repealed." It is the only amendment that repeals another. It is also the only one that was sent to special conventions in each state, not to the state legislatures, to be ratified.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Nineteenth Amendment, 1920" -->
The House passed the Nineteenth Amendment on May 21, 1919, and the Senate on June 4, 1919. On August 18, 1920, Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state to ratify it, which was enough. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified it on August 26, 1920. Its words are: "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." It ended the rule of *Minor v. Happersett*, under which each state could keep the vote for men only.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quotas, and citizenship for Native people, 1921 and 1924" -->
In 1921 members of Congress passed the Emergency Quota Act, the first law to put a number on how many immigrants could come. A quota is a fixed share. Each year, each nationality could send 3 percent of the number of people born in that country who were living in the United States in 1910.

The Immigration Act of 1924 cut the number further, to 2 percent of each nationality counted in the 1890 census. It cut the number of immigrants allowed from Southern and Eastern Europe. The act also barred nearly all immigrants from Asia. The total was capped at about 165,000 a year.

On June 2, 1924, President Calvin Coolidge signed the Indian Citizenship Act, which made Native Americans citizens. Some states still kept Native citizens from voting. Two court rulings in 1948 ended those bans in Arizona, in *Harrison v. Laveen* on July 15, and in New Mexico, in *Trujillo v. Garley* in August. Utah was the last state to let Native people vote, in 1957.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The anti-lynching bill, 1922" -->
Lynching is murder by a mob, carried out without any trial. Members of Congress introduced bills to make lynching a federal crime, starting with George Henry White's bill on January 20, 1900. Representative Dyer introduced his bill on April 8, 1918. The House passed it on January 26, 1922. Senators opposed to it blocked it in the Senate, and it never became law. No federal anti-lynching law passed in this era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Teapot Dome" -->
Albert B. Fall was Secretary of the Interior under President Warren G. Harding. Fall persuaded Harding to move the Navy's oil reserves, land kept for the Navy's future oil, to the Interior Department. Secretary of the Navy Edwin Denby carried out the transfer. Fall then leased the Teapot Dome reserve in Wyoming to Harry F. Sinclair's Mammoth Oil company. He leased the Elk Hills reserve in California to Edward Doheny's Pan-American Petroleum. Doheny gave Fall a $100,000 "loan" with no interest. Sinclair gave him about $300,000 in government bonds and cash.

In October 1923 the Senate's Public Lands Committee opened an investigation. Senator John B. Kendrick of Wyoming had called for it, and Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana led it for about two years. Walsh found Doheny's $100,000. In 1927 the Supreme Court ruled that the leases had been gained by corruption. On October 25, 1929, a jury found Fall guilty of taking a bribe from Doheny. He was sentenced to one year in prison and a $100,000 fine. He was the first person convicted of a crime he committed while serving in a president's cabinet. Doheny was found not guilty of paying the same bribe. Sinclair went to jail for several months, for contempt of Congress and for having the jury in his case followed.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law: who decides, and who pays" -->
Oregon's Compulsory Education Act of 1922 required children aged 8 to 16 to attend the public school in their district. A convent school run by the Society of the Sisters of the Holy Names of Jesus and Mary sued, and so did Hill Military Academy. On June 1, 1925, in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, the justices of the Supreme Court struck the law down. Justice James McReynolds wrote that it interfered with the liberty of parents to direct their children's education. He wrote: "The child is not the mere creature of the state." Under that ruling, a state may require a child to be educated, but it may not require every child to attend a government school. Private schools, church schools and, later, homeschooling all rest on it.

On March 21, 1925, Tennessee's governor, Austin Peay, signed the Butler Act. It made it unlawful to teach in the state's public schools "any theory that denies the story of Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals." Civic leaders in Dayton, Tennessee, set up a test case. They asked John T. Scopes, a football coach and science teacher, whether biology could be taught without evolution. He said it could not, because the state's own textbook covered it. His trial opened in Dayton on July 10, 1925, before Judge John T. Raulston. On July 21 the jury found him guilty, and he was fined $100, the smallest fine the law allowed.

In January 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court threw out his conviction. The state constitution required a jury, not a judge, to set any fine over $50, and Raulston had set the fine himself. So the judges never ruled on the Butler Act itself. It stayed in force until 1967.

In these years, thousands of small school districts paid for their schools mainly with local money. In the school year 1919-20, local sources paid 83.2 percent of the money for public schools, by the count of the National Center for Education Statistics. By 1949-50 the local share had fallen to 57.3 percent, and the states paid 39.8 percent. The federal government paid 2.9 percent. Small districts were also merged into larger ones. There were 119,001 school districts in 1937-38, and 83,718 in 1949-50.

In June 1947 Governor Earl Warren of California signed the Anderson Bill. It made California the first state to repeal its own law on segregated schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buck v. Bell, 1927" -->
In 1924 Virginia's lawmakers passed a law that allowed state officials to sterilize people they judged "feeble-minded" and likely to have "socially inadequate offspring." Sterilization is an operation that makes a person unable ever to have children. Aubrey Strode wrote the law and then defended it in court.

Carrie Buck was born on July 2, 1906, in Charlottesville. She was raised by foster parents. She became pregnant after, by her account, the foster parents' nephew raped her. Her foster parents said the pregnancy showed she was "feebleminded," and on January 23, 1924, they asked a court to have her committed to a state institution.

Her case reached the Supreme Court. On May 2, 1927, the justices upheld Virginia's law, 8 to 1. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote the opinion. It includes the sentence "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." Justice Pierce Butler dissented, without writing an opinion.

On October 19, 1927, Dr. John H. Bell performed the operation on Carrie Buck. It is called a salpingectomy. He cut her fallopian tubes, the tubes that carry eggs from the ovaries to the womb, so that she could never become pregnant again. Between 1927 and 1972, doctors in Virginia sterilized about 8,300 people under the law. Virginia's lawmakers repealed it in 1974. On May 2, 2002, Governor Mark Warner apologized for the state's program. The Supreme Court has never overruled *Buck v. Bell*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="franklin-d-roosevelt" name="Franklin D. Roosevelt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Franklin D. Roosevelt

> **Who:** The president who lost key cases in the Supreme Court, tried to add justices to it, and was elected four times.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1933 to 1945. His plan for the courts went to Congress on February 5, 1937.

Franklin D. Roosevelt tried to change the size of the Supreme Court after its justices struck down one of his laws. On May 27, 1935, in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, the justices struck down the part of the National Industrial Recovery Act that let the president approve "codes of fair competition," rules for whole industries. They held that Congress had handed the president powers it had no right to hand over. They also held that the poultry business in the case was not trade between the states, which Congress could regulate.

On February 5, 1937, Roosevelt sent Congress a plan to reorganize the federal courts. It added one justice to the Supreme Court for each justice over the age of 70, up to six more. The Court could have grown from nine justices to fifteen, and Roosevelt would have chosen the new ones.

On March 29, 1937, in *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, the justices upheld Washington State's minimum wage for women and minors. Soon after, they upheld other New Deal laws too. Legal historians still disagree about whether Roosevelt's plan caused that change. The dates fit, and the cause is not settled.

On June 14, 1937, the Senate Judiciary Committee reported against the plan. Its members called it "needless, futile, and utterly dangerous." On July 22, 1937, the senators rejected it, 70 to 20. According to the Federal Judicial Center, Roosevelt lost a great deal of political support for proposing it.

Roosevelt was elected four times, in 1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944. He was the first president to win more than two terms.

On June 25, 1941, he signed Executive Order 8802. An executive order is a written order from the president to the officials under him. It said: "There shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries and in Government, because of race, creed, color, or national origin." It set up the Committee on Fair Employment Practice. After the war, members of Congress refused to make that committee permanent.

On February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It let the Secretary of War and military commanders mark off military areas and shut "any or all persons" out of them. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, commander of the Army's Western Defense Command, used it to force people of Japanese descent from their homes on the West Coast. Over six months, under his orders, about 122,000 men, women and children were forced into assembly centers and then into ten camps in remote parts of six western states and Arkansas. Nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens, by the count of the National Archives. None was charged with a crime, and none had any way to appeal.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="franklin-d-roosevelt" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The courts and the camps" -->
A man named Korematsu was convicted of breaking the military's exclusion orders. On December 18, 1944, in *Korematsu v. United States*, the justices of the Supreme Court upheld his conviction, 6 to 3. They held that forcing citizens out of their homes in wartime was justified. On the same day, in *Ex parte Endo*, all the justices agreed that the government could not hold a citizen it admitted was loyal. That ruling opened the way to closing the camps.

In the 1980s federal judges threw out the convictions of Korematsu and others. Judge Marilyn Hall Patel vacated, or cancelled, Korematsu's conviction on November 10, 1983. In the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, members of Congress apologized for the imprisonment and paid $20,000 to each person who had been held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poll taxes and the white primary" -->
Georgia charged a poll tax of $1 a year, and no one could vote without paying it. People under 21 or over 60, blind people, and women who did not register to vote did not have to pay. Nolan Breedlove sued the tax collector, T. Earl Suttles. On December 6, 1937, in *Breedlove v. Suttles*, all the justices of the Supreme Court upheld the tax. Justice Pierce Butler wrote that "the equal protection clause does not require absolute equality."

In Texas, the Democratic Party was the only party that won elections, so its primary was the election that counted. A primary is the election in which a party chooses its candidates. On May 24, 1932, the leaders of the Texas Democratic Party resolved that only "white citizens" could be members. Lonnie Smith, a Black voter in precinct 48 of Harris County, was refused a ballot in the primary. He sued an election judge named Allwright and the other judges of his precinct. On April 3, 1944, in *Smith v. Allwright*, the justices held that the party was acting for the state, so the white primary broke the Fifteenth Amendment. Justice Stanley Reed wrote the opinion. Justice Owen Roberts dissented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1948: the armed forces, refugees and the first televised conventions" -->
On July 26, 1948, President Harry Truman signed Executive Order 9981. It said: "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." By about 1953 nearly all the armed services had been integrated, with Black and white troops serving together.

The Displaced Persons Act of 1948 was the first American law for refugees, people forced from their home countries. It first allowed 200,000 Europeans who had lost their homes in the war to come to the United States. Congress raised the number later. Between 1948 and 1952 about 350,000 to 400,000 people came in under it.

In 1948 both major parties held their conventions in Philadelphia, and television networks broadcast them. The National Constitution Center puts the audience at as many as 10 million viewers on the East Coast. On July 12, 1948, after a speech by Hubert Humphrey, the Democratic delegates added a civil rights plank to their platform. A plank is one position in a party's platform. Two Southern delegations walked out on live television. On July 17, Southern delegates met in Birmingham, Alabama, and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party, called the Dixiecrats. They chose Strom Thurmond, the governor of South Carolina, as their candidate. Their platform said, "We stand for the segregation of the races." Thurmond won four states and 39 electoral votes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1954 to 1977, the justices of the Supreme Court decided case after case that changed how schools, elections and police stations worked. In 1964 and 1965, members of Congress passed laws that banned segregation in public places and made the places covered by a voting law ask federal officials before changing any voting rule. Five amendments were added to the Constitution between 1951 and 1971. In 1974 President Richard Nixon resigned before the House could vote to impeach him. In 1998 the members of the House impeached President Bill Clinton, and the senators found him not guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Five amendments, 1951 to 1971" -->
Each of the five amendments added between 1951 and 1971 changed who holds office or who votes.

Under the Twenty-second Amendment, ratified on February 27, 1951, "No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice." Washington's two terms had been a custom, and Franklin Roosevelt had broken it. The amendment made two terms the rule.

The District of Columbia, the capital city, got electors in presidential elections through the Twenty-third Amendment, ratified on March 29, 1961. It got no senators and no voting member of the House.

Poll taxes in national elections ended with the Twenty-fourth Amendment, ratified on January 23, 1964. Its ban covered only elections for president, vice president, senator and representative. A state could still charge a poll tax in its own elections. Then, on March 24, 1966, in *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections*, the justices of the Supreme Court struck down state poll taxes too. Virginia had charged up to $1.50 a year. Justice William O. Douglas wrote that a state breaks the Equal Protection Clause when it makes "the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard." Affluence means wealth.

In 1967 the states ratified the Twenty-fifth Amendment, on February 10. It set out what happens when a president dies, resigns or is removed. It also gave a way to fill an empty vice presidency: the president names someone, and both houses of Congress vote to confirm.

Eighteen-year-olds got the vote under the Twenty-sixth Amendment. It was ratified on July 1, 1971, faster than any other amendment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Brown v. Board of Education and all deliberate speed" -->
On May 17, 1954, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, the justices of the Supreme Court held that segregated public schools broke the Constitution. The ruling said nothing about how or when to end segregation.

A year later, on May 31, 1955, they decided a second case, often called *Brown* II. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for a unanimous Court. The justices sent the cases back to federal district judges, the judges of the lowest federal courts. They told school boards to make "a prompt and reasonable start toward full compliance." They ordered the judges to admit the Black children who had sued to public schools "with all deliberate speed." The order set no date. A school board that moved slowly was not clearly breaking it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thurgood-marshall" name="Thurgood Marshall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thurgood Marshall

> **Who:** The lawyer who won 29 of the 32 cases he argued in the Supreme Court and later became its first Black justice.
> **When and where:** Baltimore, New York and Washington, D.C. Born 1908. Died 1993.

Thurgood Marshall worked in four different offices of the American legal system, and he was the first Black justice of the Supreme Court. He was born in Baltimore, Maryland, on July 2, 1908. He wanted to study law at the University of Maryland, and the law school turned him away because he was Black. He went to Howard University's law school instead and graduated first in his class in 1933. One of his first cases, *Murray v. Pearson*, forced the University of Maryland's law school to admit Black students.

From 1940 to 1961 he ran the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund. He argued 32 cases in the Supreme Court and won 29. Among them were *Smith v. Allwright* in 1944, *Morgan v. Virginia* in 1946 on segregated buses between states, *Sweatt v. Painter* in 1950 on the University of Texas law school, and *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954.

On October 5, 1961, President John F. Kennedy made Marshall a judge of the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. He did it by recess appointment, which means a president fills the seat while the Senate is away. A recess appointment ends unless the senators confirm it. Southern senators opposed Marshall, and the Senate did not confirm him until September 11, 1962, eleven months later.

On August 23, 1965, he left that court to become Solicitor General of the United States, the officer who argues the government's own cases in the Supreme Court. He won 14 of the 19 cases he argued in that job.

President Lyndon Johnson nominated him to the Supreme Court on June 13, 1967, to the seat Tom C. Clark had left. The senators confirmed him on August 30, 1967. He took the oath as a justice on October 2, 1967. He stepped back from full-time service on October 1, 1991, and died on January 24, 1993.

After his death, Chief Justice William Rehnquist said most justices are remembered for their work on the Court. "Justice Marshall, however, is unique because his contributions to constitutional law before becoming a member of the Court were so significant."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thurgood-marshall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prayer and the Bible in public schools, 1962 and 1963" -->
The New York State Board of Regents, the officials who oversaw the state's schools, wrote a prayer for public school children to say each morning: "Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee, and we beg Thy blessings upon us, our parents, our teachers and our Country." No child had to say it. On June 25, 1962, in *Engel v. Vitale*, the justices of the Supreme Court held the prayer unconstitutional anyway, 6 to 1. Justice Hugo Black wrote that the First Amendment's ban on establishing a religion "must at least mean that in this country it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group." It did not matter that the prayer was short or that a child could stay silent. A government body had written it.

On June 17, 1963, in *Abington School District v. Schempp*, the justices decided two cases together. In Pennsylvania, public schools opened the day with Bible readings. In the Baltimore city schools, children said the Lord's Prayer together. The justices struck down both. Justice Tom Clark wrote the opinion, and Justice Potter Stewart was the only one to dissent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One person, one vote" -->
Georgia used a county unit system to count votes in statewide elections. The winner in each county got all of that county's unit votes, so a vote in a small rural county counted many times as much as a vote in a city. On March 18, 1963, in *Gray v. Sanders*, the justices struck the system down, 8 to 1. Justice William O. Douglas wrote: "The conception of political equality from the Declaration of Independence, to Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, to the Fifteenth, Seventeenth, and Nineteenth Amendments can mean only one thing." He ended the sentence with the words "one person, one vote."

On June 15, 1964, in *Reynolds v. Sims*, the justices applied the same rule to state legislatures. Alabama's lawmakers had not redrawn the state's legislative districts since 1901, and they still used the 1900 census. By 1960, 25.1 percent of Alabamians could elect a majority of the state senate. In the worst case, one senate district had about 41 times as many people as another. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the opinion. Under it, districts had to hold about equal numbers of people, and almost every state legislature in the country had to redraw its districts because of it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lyndon-b-johnson" name="Lyndon B. Johnson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lyndon B. Johnson

> **Who:** A Senate leader who counted votes for a living, then a president who used the same skill on civil rights laws.
> **When and where:** Texas and Washington, D.C., 1948 to 1969.

Lyndon Johnson reached the Senate in 1948 by 87 votes, out of nearly a million cast. That was his final margin over Coke Stevenson in the Texas Democratic runoff election. For the rest of his life people called him "Landslide Lyndon."

On the night of the runoff, Johnson seemed to have lost. Six days after the polls closed, 202 more votes were reported from Precinct 13 in Jim Wells County: 200 for Johnson and 2 for Stevenson. The added names on the voter list were in alphabetical order, in the same ink and the same handwriting. Some of the people listed said they had not voted that day. The state party's executive committee certified Johnson as the winner by 29 votes to 28. A federal judge, T. Whitfield Davidson, blocked that result. Then Supreme Court Justice Hugo Black, acting alone, ruled for Johnson on September 28, 1948, and in writing the next day, and Johnson's name went on the ballot. In 1977 the precinct's election judge, Luis Salas, told a reporter that the local political boss, George B. Parr, had ordered about 200 votes added. No court ever ruled that the votes were forged, and Johnson was never charged.

Johnson became his party's whip in the Senate in 1951, its minority leader in 1953 and its majority leader from 1955 to 1961. The whip counts votes and keeps party members in line. At 45, still in his first term, he was the youngest party leader the Senate had ever had. The Senate's historians describe how he worked on other senators. He stood close to a senator's face and pulled "clippings, memos, statistics" out of his pockets. His tone "could be supplication, accusation, cajolery, exuberance, scorn, tears, complaint, the hint of threat." Supplication means begging, and cajolery means coaxing.

In 1957 he pushed through the first civil rights law since Reconstruction. He wanted it passed, but he feared a long filibuster would split his party. A filibuster is a way of blocking a bill by talking on and on so that no vote can happen. To get the bill through, he took out its main enforcement sections. It passed and did very little.

As president, Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964, and the Voting Rights Act on August 6, 1965. The Voting Rights Act kept the enforcement tools that the 1957 law had lost. Federal examiners could register voters themselves, and some states had to ask federal officials before changing any voting rule. On April 11, 1965, he signed the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. He said, "no law I have signed or will ever sign means more to the future of America."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lyndon-b-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the civil rights laws passed" -->
Southern senators led by Richard Russell of Georgia tried to block the Civil Rights Act of 1964 with a filibuster. They held the Senate floor for 60 working days, including seven Saturdays. On June 10, 1964, the senators voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the motion that ends debate. It was the first time the Senate had ever cut off debate on a civil rights bill. Senator Clair Engle of California had a brain tumor and could not speak. When his name was called, he raised his weakened arm and pointed to his eye to vote aye. The Senate passed the bill 73 to 27 on June 19.

The act has ten parts, called titles. Title II bans segregation in places that serve the public. Its words are: "All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, and privileges ... of any place of public accommodation ... without discrimination or segregation." Title VI bans discrimination in any program paid for with federal money. Later, federal officials used Title VI to cut off money to school districts that stayed segregated. Title VII bans discrimination in jobs and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 worked in a different way. Section 4 set a formula. A state or county was covered if it had used a literacy test, a reading test for voters, or a similar device on November 1, 1964, and if fewer than half of its voting-age people had registered or voted in the 1964 presidential election. Under Section 5, every covered place had to get permission from the Attorney General or a federal court in Washington before changing any voting rule at all. By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had been registered, one-third of them by federal examiners, according to the National Archives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal money for schools, 1965" -->
On April 11, 1965, Johnson signed the Elementary and Secondary Education Act outside the former Junction Elementary School near Stonewall, Texas. It was the one-room school where he had started at the age of four. His first teacher, Kate Deadrich Loney, sat beside him. The National Center for Education Statistics gives the date as April 12. The printed law gives April 11.

Title I of the act sends federal money to school districts according to how many children in them come from families with low incomes. The money comes with conditions the districts have to meet. In the 2015 budget year, Title I gave out about $14.3 billion, according to the National Center for Education Statistics in 2019.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Immigration law after 1965" -->
The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended the national quotas set in the 1920s. It admitted immigrants on the basis of family ties and skills. It also set the first cap on immigration from the Americas. The Refugee Act of 1980 set up a permanent system for admitting refugees. The Immigration Reform and Control Act became law on November 6, 1986. It gave legal status to about 2.7 million people who had lived in the country for years without permission, and it set penalties for employers who hired workers without permission to work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Miranda v. Arizona, 1966" -->
On March 13, 1963, police arrested Ernesto Miranda at his home in Phoenix, Arizona. They questioned him in a police interrogation room. At his trial the officers admitted that no one told him he could have a lawyer with him. After about two hours he signed a written confession, and he was convicted of kidnapping and rape.

On June 13, 1966, in *Miranda v. Arizona*, the justices of the Supreme Court set a rule for police. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the opinion. Before questioning a person in custody, police must tell the person that they have the right to remain silent, that anything they say can be used against them in court, that they have the right to a lawyer, and that a lawyer will be appointed if they cannot pay for one. If the person asks for those rights, the questioning must stop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law in the 1970s" -->
Wisconsin required children to attend school until age 16. Three Amish fathers, Jonas Yoder, Wallace Miller and Adin Yutzy, did not send their children to high school after the eighth grade. Their children were Frieda Yoder and Barbara Miller, both 15, and Vernon Yutzy, 14. Each father was fined $5. On May 15, 1972, in *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, the justices of the Supreme Court held 6 to 1 that the state could not make them send their children to high school. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote the opinion.

The Education Amendments of 1972 became law on June 23, 1972. Title IX of that law states: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

Texas paid for its schools partly with local property taxes. A property tax is a tax on the value of land and buildings. In the school year 1967-68, two districts in San Antonio showed the difference. Edgewood, whose students were mostly Mexican American, spent $356 on each pupil. Alamo Heights spent $594. On March 21, 1973, in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, the justices decided 5 to 4 that education is not a fundamental right under the Constitution. Justice Lewis Powell wrote the opinion. Texas only had to show that its system was rational, and keeping schools under local control counted as rational. After 1973, people who sued over unequal school money had to go to state courts, under state constitutions.

On November 29, 1975, President Gerald Ford signed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act. It guaranteed every child with a disability a free, appropriate public education. The Department of Education says that in 1970, American schools educated only one in five children with disabilities. More than 1 million children with disabilities were shut out of school entirely.

In October 1970, at Drew Junior High School in Dade County, Florida, a student named James Ingraham was held over a table in the principal's office and hit more than 20 times with a wooden paddle, because he was slow to follow a teacher's instructions. Ingraham and another student sued three school officials: the principal, Willie J. Wright, an assistant principal, Lemmie Deliford, and an assistant to the principal, Solomon Barnes. The Supreme Court's opinion does not say which of them held him or struck him. School policy allowed one to five blows with a flat wooden paddle less than two feet long. The beating raised a hematoma, a pocket of blood under the skin. He needed a doctor, and he missed school for several days. Another student, Roosevelt Andrews, was once hit on the arms and lost the full use of an arm for a week.

On April 19, 1977, in *Ingraham v. Wright*, the justices decided 5 to 4 that the Eighth Amendment's ban on "cruel and unusual punishments" does not cover school discipline. They wrote that it "was designed to protect those convicted of crimes." They also held that a school does not have to give a child a hearing before hitting him, because the child can sue afterward if the beating was too severe. Because the Constitution did not forbid school paddling, it ended one state at a time, where state lawmakers voted to end it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Congress and the president: war powers and Watergate" -->
Members of Congress passed the War Powers Resolution in 1973. President Nixon vetoed it on October 24, 1973. On November 7, 1973, the House voted 284 to 135 and the Senate voted 75 to 18 to override the veto, which made it law. Under it, when a president sends troops into fighting without a declaration of war, he must report to Congress in writing within 48 hours. Within 60 days he must end the fighting unless Congress declares war, votes to approve it or gives more time.

Early on June 17, 1972, five men broke into the offices of the Democratic National Committee at the Watergate building in Washington, D.C. Police arrested them with cash and equipment for spying on the offices. Investigators later tied them to Nixon's re-election campaign. The White House at first denied any link. This scandal and the cover-up that followed are called Watergate.

On February 7, 1973, the senators voted to create a committee to investigate, led by Sam Ervin of North Carolina. A White House aide, Alexander Butterfield, told the committee that the president secretly recorded conversations in rooms of the White House. In October 1973 Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliott Richardson to fire the special prosecutor, Archibald Cox, who had demanded the tapes. Richardson and his deputy resigned rather than obey.

On July 24, 1974, in *United States v. Nixon*, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 8 to 0 that Nixon had to hand the tapes over to the prosecutor. Justice William Rehnquist took no part. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote that the president's right to keep his talks private "must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."

In late July the members of the House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment, or formal charges: obstruction of justice on July 27, abuse of power on July 29 and contempt of Congress on July 30. On the morning of August 9, 1974, Nixon signed a one-sentence letter of resignation. He addressed it to the Secretary of State, because a law passed in 1792 says that is where a president's resignation is filed. It took effect at 11:35 a.m., when Secretary of State Henry Kissinger initialed it. The full House never voted on the articles.

Gerald Ford took the oath of office at noon. He said: "My fellow Americans, our long national nightmare is over. Our Constitution works." He also said: "I am acutely aware that you have not elected me as your President by your ballots." Vice President Spiro Agnew had resigned in October 1973. Nixon had named Ford to replace him under the Twenty-fifth Amendment, and both houses had confirmed him. As president, Ford named Nelson Rockefeller as his vice president the same way. For about two and a half years, the country had a president and a vice president whom no voter had chosen for either office.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New kinds of schools, 1991 to 1996" -->
In 1991 Minnesota's lawmakers passed the first state law allowing charter schools, public schools run by independent groups under a contract with the state. The law called them "outcome-based schools" and allowed eight of them. State Senator Ember Reichgott Junge was its chief author. The first one, City Academy in St. Paul, opened in 1992.

Homeschooling became legal across the country state by state during the 1980s and early 1990s, through new laws and court rulings. Michigan is usually named as the last state. On May 25, 1993, in *People v. DeJonge*, the Michigan Supreme Court struck down the state's rule that parents who taught their children at home must use certified teachers. The ruling covered only families whose religious beliefs forbade certified teachers. On the same day, in *People v. Bennett*, the same court upheld the rule for a family with no religious objection. Michigan's lawmakers wrote a home-education exemption into the state's school attendance law in 1995. It took effect on July 1, 1996.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An impeachment at the end of the century" -->
The members of the House of Representatives impeached President Bill Clinton on December 19, 1998. The Constitution requires two-thirds of the Senate, 67 votes, to convict. On February 12, 1999, the senators voted 45 guilty and 55 not guilty on the first article. On the second article the vote was 50 to 50. Clinton stayed in office.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, the way elections are run and counted has been one of the main fights in American politics, and judges have decided many of those fights. In 2000 the justices of the Supreme Court ended a recount that decided who became president. On January 6, 2021, while members of Congress counted the electoral votes, a crowd attacked the Capitol. Judges also kept ruling on schools, on public money for religious schools and on how children are assigned to schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bush v. Gore, 2000" -->
The presidential election of 2000, between George W. Bush and Al Gore, came down to Florida. According to the United States Supreme Court's opinion, the Florida Supreme Court had ordered a hand recount of about 9,000 ballots in Miami-Dade County that counting machines had read as blank. It also ordered 215 votes from Palm Beach County and 168 from Miami-Dade added to the certified total, and it called for recounts of blank ballots in counties that had not yet hand-counted them.

On December 12, 2000, the justices of the United States Supreme Court decided *Bush v. Gore* in an unsigned opinion. Seven justices saw a problem under the Constitution: different counties used different rules to decide what a ballot meant, so two identical ballots could be counted differently depending on where they were cast. Five justices voted to stop the recount instead of sending it back to be fixed, because there was no time left. Justices John Paul Stevens, David Souter, Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Stephen Breyer dissented. The opinion said the ruling was "limited to the present circumstances." The recount ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money and maps" -->
On January 21, 2010, the justices decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, 5 to 4. Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, had made a film attacking Hillary Clinton. It wanted to advertise the film during a period before an election when federal law barred corporations from spending money on elections. Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote for the majority that "the Government may not suppress political speech based on the speaker's corporate identity." The ruling overturned a 1990 decision and part of one from 2003.

On June 27, 2019, the justices decided *Rucho v. Common Cause*, also 5 to 4. Gerrymandering is drawing the lines of voting districts so that one side wins more seats. Two maps were before the Court: North Carolina's map for Congress drawn by Republicans in 2016, and Maryland's drawn by Democrats in 2011. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that "partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts." Justice Elena Kagan wrote in dissent: "For the first time ever, this Court refuses to remedy a constitutional violation because it thinks the task beyond judicial capabilities." After 2019, a voter who believed a map was drawn to favor one party could not take that complaint to a federal court. State courts and Congress could still act on it.

A map drawn by race is a separate question, decided under the Voting Rights Act. On June 25, 2013, in *Shelby County v. Holder*, the justices struck down the formula in Section 4(b) of that act, which decided which places had to ask permission before changing voting rules. As of September 2026, members of Congress had passed no new formula.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools and the courts" -->
The No Child Left Behind Act is named for 2001, but it became law on January 8, 2002. It rewrote the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 and kept its method: federal money for schools, with conditions attached. It required yearly testing and set targets that schools had to meet. On December 10, 2015, the Every Student Succeeds Act replaced it. States still had to set standards and test students every year in reading, mathematics and science. Under the new law, states had more say over what to do about the results. In the 2023 budget year, federal spending on all the programs under the 1965 act came to $29.0 billion, according to a Congressional Research Service report of February 2024.

Four Supreme Court rulings between 2002 and 2022 opened public money to religious schools. On June 27, 2002, in *Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, the justices upheld Ohio's scholarships for students in Cleveland, 5 to 4. The scholarships paid up to $2,250 a year for families with low incomes. According to the Court's 2002 opinion, 82 percent of the private schools taking part were religious. The justices held that such a program is allowed if it treats religion neutrally and the money reaches a religious school only because a family chose that school.

The next three rulings went further, from allowing such aid to requiring it. On June 26, 2017, in *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia v. Comer*, the justices held that Missouri officials could not refuse a church preschool a grant for resurfacing its playground, a grant open to others, just because it was a church. On June 30, 2020, in *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, they decided 5 to 4 that Montana officials could not keep scholarship money from religious schools. On June 21, 2022, in *Carson v. Makin*, they decided 6 to 3 that Maine officials could not require schools to be nonreligious before paying tuition for children from towns with no high school.

On June 28, 2007, in *Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1*, the justices struck down two plans that used a child's race to decide which school the child attended, 5 to 4. Seattle had used race to break ties in handing out high school places. Jefferson County, Kentucky, had used it in placing children in elementary schools. Chief Justice Roberts wrote: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race." Only four justices joined that sentence. Justice Kennedy cast the fifth vote and wrote separately. He wrote that a district may still work toward a mix of students by choosing where to build new schools, drawing attendance zones and recruiting students and teachers. After 2007, a district could still try to mix its schools, but it could not assign a single child to a school because of that child's race.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counting the votes, January 6, 2021" -->
Every four years, the members of the House and Senate meet together in the House chamber to count the electoral votes for president. The vice president presides. Tellers open each state's certificate and read out its votes. Until 2022, one member of the House and one senator together could object in writing to a state's votes. The two houses then met separately, debated and voted.

In 2020, people in some states prepared papers that claimed to be certificates of electoral votes and sent them to the Senate and the National Archives. According to a Congressional Research Service report of December 2024, those papers did not carry the certificate that each state's governor or other executive must issue under federal law, so they were not presented at the count.

On January 6, 2021, members of Congress took up two objections. According to the Congressional Research Service report, the senators rejected the objection to Arizona's votes, 6 to 93, and the representatives rejected it, 121 to 303. The senators rejected the objection to Pennsylvania's votes, 7 to 92, and the representatives rejected it, 138 to 282. The members of Congress then finished the count and declared the result.

A crowd attacked the Capitol while the count was under way. The Government Accountability Office, a federal agency that checks the government's work, reported in 2022 that over about seven hours, about 140 police officers were assaulted, about 80 of them from the United States Capitol Police. About 114 Capitol Police officers reported injuries. The damage cost about $1.5 million. The same office found that the Capitol Police's plans for the day "did not reflect the potential for extreme violence aimed at the Capitol," even though the force had information that protesters might be armed and were planning to target Congress.

Members of Congress then changed the rules. The Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act became law on December 29, 2022. Under it, an objection needs one-fifth of the members of each house, about 87 representatives and 20 senators. Only the state's executive, usually the governor, may certify the state's electors. Electors must be chosen under state laws passed before election day. The act also states: "The President of the Senate shall have no power to solely determine, accept, reject, or otherwise adjudicate or resolve disputes over the proper certificate of ascertainment of appointment of electors, the validity of electors, or the votes of electors." The President of the Senate is the vice president.

On January 20, 2025, the day he took office again, President Donald Trump signed Proclamation 10887. With it, he cut the prison sentences of fourteen named members of two groups, the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys, to the time they had already served. Among them were Stewart Rhodes and Kelly Meggs. He gave "a full, complete and unconditional pardon" to everyone else convicted of crimes connected with the events at the Capitol on January 6, 2021, and he ordered everyone still in prison for them released.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Impeached twice, convicted neither time" -->
The Senate can convict a president, and so remove him from office, only if two-thirds of the senators, 67 votes, find him guilty. No president has ever been convicted.

After the members of the House impeached President Donald Trump, the senators voted on February 5, 2020, 48 guilty and 52 not guilty on the first article, and 47 to 53 on the second. The members of the House impeached him a second time, and the Senate held his trial after he had left office. On February 13, 2021, the senators voted 57 guilty and 43 not guilty, ten votes short of conviction. Seven Republican senators voted guilty: Richard Burr, Bill Cassidy, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Mitt Romney, Ben Sasse and Pat Toomey.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

### Wandrea ArShaye "Shaye" Moss

> **Who:** A Fulton County election worker who was publicly and falsely accused of cheating in the 2020 count, and who told a committee of Congress under oath what followed.
> **When and where:** Atlanta, Georgia, 2017 to 2022. Sworn testimony in Washington, D.C., June 21, 2022.

Shaye Moss worked in the Department of Registration and Elections in Fulton County, Georgia, from 2017 until 2022. She handled applications from voters and requests for absentee ballots, which are ballots mailed to voters who vote from home. She also helped count the votes. Her mother, Ruby Freeman, worked as a temporary election worker in the same county for the 2020 election.

On June 21, 2022, Moss testified under oath to the House committee that investigated January 6. She said: "I have always been told by my grandmother how important it is to vote and how people before me, a lot of people, older people in my family did not have that right." Older voters were her favorite part of the job: "they like to call, they like to talk to you, they like to get my card, they like to know that every election I am here." She once drove to a hospital to bring a woman her absentee ballot application.

About five million Georgians voted in 2020. From November 11 to 19, 2020, election workers counted every ballot by hand, in 41,881 batches. In no county did the margin change by more than 0.73 percent. Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger certified the result on November 20, and Governor Brian Kemp certified it too. After a machine recount that the Trump campaign asked for, state officials certified the result again on December 7, 2020.

On December 3, 2020, a lawyer working for the Trump campaign played clips of a security video from the State Farm Arena at a hearing of the Georgia Senate. The lawyer said election workers there had pulled 18,000 hidden ballots from "suitcases of ballots under a table, under a tablecloth." The two women in the clips were Freeman and Moss.

Georgia's state election officials and the Georgia Bureau of Investigation watched the whole security tape, which ran for hours, and interviewed everyone who had been there. They found no evidence for any of the claims. Early on December 4, the state's Voting Implementation Manager posted that the video "Shows normal ballot processing." The containers held ballots that had been processed earlier that night, and no observer had been told to leave. Moss testified that the object her mother handed her in the video was "a ginger mint."

On December 10, 2020, Rudolph Giuliani told a hearing of the Georgia House that the two women were "quite obviously surreptitiously passing around USB ports as if they are vials of heroin or cocaine." Surreptitiously means secretly. He said their homes and workplaces should have been searched. On January 2, 2021, Trump telephoned Raffensperger and named Ruby Freeman again and again. He called her "a vote scammer, a professional vote scammer and hustler." Raffensperger answered on the call that Giuliani "or his people, they sliced and diced that video and took it out of context."

Moss testified about the messages she received: "a lot of threats, wishing death upon me, telling me that, you know, I will be in jail with my mother, and saying things like, 'Be glad it's 2020 and not 1920.'" Many were racist, she said. Strangers went to her grandmother's house and pushed their way in, saying they had come to make a citizen's arrest and needed to find Moss and her mother. FBI agents advised Freeman to leave her home around the week of January 6, 2021, and she stayed away about two months. "I've lost my name," Freeman said, "and I've lost my reputation." Moss said she stopped giving out her business cards and stopped going to the grocery store. Asked how many of the permanent election workers and supervisors in that video were still doing the job, Moss answered: "There is no permanent election worker or supervisor in that video that is still there." She had left too.

In December 2021 Freeman and Moss sued Giuliani for defamation in federal court in Washington, D.C. Defamation is harming a person's good name with false statements. On August 30, 2023, Chief Judge Beryl Howell ruled against Giuliani on all their claims, as a penalty for refusing to hand over evidence. On December 15, 2023, a jury awarded the two women about $148 million, a figure reported by PBS. The amount was later reduced to about $146 million, according to NBC News. On April 15, 2024, Judge Howell refused Giuliani's request to throw out or change the verdict. In January 2025 a federal judge in New York, Lewis Liman, found Giuliani in contempt of court for failing to give information about his property. A court filing showed that the judgment had been satisfied, meaning its terms had been met, on February 21, 2025.

In November 2025 President Trump pardoned Giuliani and others who had worked to overturn the 2020 result. A presidential pardon covers only federal crimes, and none of the people named had been charged with a federal crime over the 2020 election. A pardon does not cancel a civil judgment such as the award to Freeman and Moss, which had already been paid.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who does not get a vote" -->
The 2020 census counted 689,545 people living in the District of Columbia. That was more than Wyoming, with 576,851, and more than Vermont, with 643,077. Each of those states sends two senators and a voting representative to Congress. The District sends a delegate who cannot vote on the House floor, and it has no senators. It has three electors for president because of the Twenty-third Amendment.

The same census counted 3,285,874 people in Puerto Rico, more than lived in 21 of the 50 states in 2020. Its residents are United States citizens. While they live there, they cannot vote for president, and their Resident Commissioner in the House cannot vote on the House floor. In 2020 the census counted 153,836 people in Guam, 87,146 in the United States Virgin Islands, 49,710 in American Samoa and 47,329 in the Northern Mariana Islands, just under 339,000 in all. The Census Bureau states the rule itself: "The populations of the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico are excluded from the apportionment population because they do not have voting seats in Congress."

In 2024, about 4 million Americans could not vote because of a felony conviction, according to an estimate by the Sentencing Project, a group that studies prisons and sentencing. A felony is a serious crime. That was 1.7 percent of adults of voting age, down from 5.9 million in 2016. Among Black Americans of voting age, 1 in 22 could not vote for this reason. About 72 percent of the people counted were not in prison. They were on probation or parole, or they had finished their sentences. Each state's own laws set these rules. Maine and Vermont let people vote from prison.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="A senator convicted, 2024" -->
On September 22, 2023, federal prosecutors in Manhattan charged Senator Bob Menendez of New Jersey, his wife, Nadine Menendez, and others with bribery. According to news reports of the case in 2023, agents had found $480,000 in cash and more than $100,000 in gold bars in the Menendez home. On July 16, 2024, a jury convicted him on all counts, including acting as an agent of the government of Egypt. He resigned from the Senate in August 2024. On January 29, 2025, a judge sentenced him to 11 years in prison. He reported to prison in June 2025.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Immigration law after 2001" -->
The Homeland Security Act of 2002 created the Department of Homeland Security, which began work in 2003. The old Immigration and Naturalization Service was split into three agencies. On June 15, 2012, officials of the Department of Homeland Security began a policy called Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, or DACA. Under it, certain people brought to the country as children could get protection from being deported and permission to work. Lawsuits over DACA went on for years.
