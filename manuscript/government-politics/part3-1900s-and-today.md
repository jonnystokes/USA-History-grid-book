## 1900 to 1950

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
An amendment is a change to the Constitution. Between 1913 and 1920, members of Congress and state lawmakers added four of them. Under those four, Congress could tax incomes, voters chose their senators, alcohol was banned, and no state could deny women the vote. A fifth amendment ended the ban on alcohol in 1933. President Franklin Roosevelt was elected four times. After the Supreme Court's justices struck down parts of his New Deal, the set of laws he backed in the 1930s, he tried to add more justices to the Court. The justices upheld a Virginia law under which doctors sterilized about 8,300 people. To sterilize a person is to operate on them so that they can never have children. The justices also held that the Army's orders forcing people of Japanese descent from their homes during World War II were justified. About 122,000 people were forced into camps under those orders. In big cities, the leaders of party organizations called political machines still traded jobs and favors for votes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="george-washington-plunkitt" name="George Washington Plunkitt" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### George Washington Plunkitt

> **Who:** A New York machine politician who explained to a reporter, from a shoeshine stand at the New York County Court House, how he made his money and kept his votes.
> **When and where:** The Fifteenth Assembly District, New York City. His talks were printed in 1905. He died in 1924.

George Washington Plunkitt was a district leader for Tammany Hall, the Democratic Party's political machine in New York City. He led its work in the Fifteenth Assembly District. He also served as a state senator and as a member of the state assembly. He grew up poor. By the time he died in 1924, he was rich.

A newspaperman named William L. Riordon wrote down Plunkitt's talks and published them in 1905 as *Plunkitt of Tammany Hall*. The title page states that Plunkitt gave the talks "from His Rostrum." A rostrum is a speaker's stand, and his was the shoeshine stand at the New York County Court House. The book is not a word-for-word record. Riordon gathered and shaped what Plunkitt said.

Plunkitt split graft into two kinds. Graft is money a person gets by using a public office. Dishonest graft, he said, meant squeezing money out of gamblers and illegal businesses. Honest graft meant learning where city officials planned to build something, buying the land first and then selling it to the city at a profit. "I ain't no looter," he said. "The looter hogs it. I never hogged." His summary of himself is cut into his gravestone: "I seen my opportunities and I took 'em."

He also described the everyday work of a machine. When a building burned at night, Plunkitt and his captains, the party workers under him, came to the fire and found the families shelter and clothes on the spot. He knew employers across the city and got people jobs. His men found families in trouble before the charity groups did. He said he did it because those people remembered him on election day.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="george-washington-plunkitt" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The food laws of 1906" -->
On June 30, 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt signed two laws on the same day. The Pure Food and Drug Act banned selling food, drink or drugs across state lines if they were mislabeled or made impure. Harvey Wiley and the officials of his Bureau of Chemistry enforced it. In 1930 that office was renamed the Food and Drug Administration. The Meat Inspection Act required federal inspectors in meatpacking plants, set rules for cleanliness and banned false labels. The laws followed Upton Sinclair's book *The Jungle*, published in February 1906, and years of work by Wiley's team.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Two amendments in 1913" -->
In 1894 members of Congress put a 2 percent tax on incomes over $4,000 into a tariff bill, a law setting taxes on goods brought in from other countries. The Supreme Court's justices struck the income tax down, 5 to 4. Members of Congress passed the Sixteenth Amendment on July 2, 1909. According to the National Archives, some of the conservatives who proposed it expected it never to be ratified. An amendment is ratified, or approved, when lawmakers or special meetings in three-quarters of the states have voted for it. The Sixteenth Amendment was ratified on February 3, 1913. Under it, Congress may tax incomes "without apportionment among the several States." That meant the money did not have to be divided among the states by population.

The Seventeenth Amendment was ratified on April 8, 1913, when Connecticut became the state that completed the three-quarters needed. Before it, the members of each state's legislature chose that state's senators. After it, the voters chose them. Augustus Bacon of Georgia was the first senator elected by the voters under the new amendment, on July 15, 1913.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The grandfather clause struck down, 1915" -->
Under a 1910 amendment to Oklahoma's constitution, Black men had to pass a reading test to vote, and white men were excused from it. The amendment excused anyone who could vote "on January 1st, 1866, or at any time prior thereto." It also excused any "lineal descendant" of such a person, meaning a child or grandchild. Almost no Black man could vote before 1866, so nearly all white men were excused and Black men had to take the test. A rule like this is called a grandfather clause, because it excused a man whose grandfather could vote.

Federal prosecutors charged two Oklahoma election officers, Frank Guinn and J. J. Beal, with keeping Black citizens from voting under this rule. The justices of the Supreme Court ruled against the 1866 clause in *Guinn v. United States* on June 21, 1915. They held that it was void, meaning it had no force, because it broke the Fifteenth Amendment. That amendment bars the United States and every state from denying a citizen the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Chief Justice Edward White wrote the opinion, the Court's written explanation of its ruling.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prohibition and repeal" -->
Members of Congress passed the Eighteenth Amendment on December 18, 1917, and lawmakers in the states ratified it on January 16, 1919. It banned "the manufacture, sale, or transportation of intoxicating liquors," meaning alcoholic drinks. The ban began one year after ratification, in January 1920.

Fourteen years later, members of Congress voted to end the ban. They passed the Twenty-first Amendment on February 20, 1933. Its first section reads: "The eighteenth article of amendment to the Constitution of the United States is hereby repealed." To repeal a law is to cancel it. Members of Congress sent this amendment to special conventions, meetings held in each state to decide on it, and not to the state legislatures. It was ratified on December 5, 1933. No other amendment repeals another, and no other was sent to state conventions.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Nineteenth Amendment, 1920" -->
Members of the House passed the Nineteenth Amendment on May 21, 1919, and senators passed it on June 4, 1919. On August 18, 1920, Tennessee's lawmakers ratified it. Tennessee was the thirty-sixth state to do so, which was enough. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified it, or officially declared it part of the Constitution, on August 26, 1920. Its words are: "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Abridged means cut down. After it, no state could keep the vote for men only, as the justices had allowed in *Minor v. Happersett*.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Quotas, and citizenship for Native people, 1921 and 1924" -->
In 1921 members of Congress passed the Emergency Quota Act, the first law with a set limit on how many immigrants could come. A quota is a fixed share. Under the act, the yearly limit for each nationality was 3 percent of the people born in that country who were living in the United States in 1910.

In 1924 members of Congress passed the Immigration Act, which set a lower limit. It was 2 percent of the people born in each country who were living in the United States in 1890, as the census of that year counted them. A census is a count of all the people in the country. Under the 1924 act, fewer immigrants could come from Southern and Eastern Europe than before, and nearly all immigrants from Asia were barred. The total was capped at about 165,000 a year.

On June 2, 1924, President Calvin Coolidge signed the Indian Citizenship Act. Under it, American Indians became citizens. It did not put them on the lists of voters, because each state wrote its own rules about who could vote. An Arizona law barred people "under guardianship" from voting. In 1928 the judges of the Arizona Supreme Court held that Native people on reservations were wards of the federal government, people under its guardianship, so the law kept them from voting. New Mexico's constitution shut out "Indians not taxed." In 1948 judges struck down both bans. Justice Levi Udall of the Arizona Supreme Court wrote the ruling in *Harrison v. Laveen* on July 15. In August a federal court in New Mexico ruled in *Trujillo v. Garley* that the "Indians not taxed" rule broke the Constitution. In Utah, Native people could not vote until 1957, later than in any other state.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The anti-lynching bill, 1922" -->
Lynching is murder by a mob, carried out without any trial. Members of Congress introduced bills to make lynching a federal crime, one the national government could charge. George Henry White introduced one on January 20, 1900. Representative Dyer introduced his bill on April 8, 1918. A later House report, House Report 117-251, names White's bill as the first in one place and Dyer's in another. Members of the House passed Dyer's bill on January 26, 1922. Senators opposed to it blocked it in the Senate, and it never became law. No federal anti-lynching law passed in this era.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Teapot Dome" -->
In the Teapot Dome case, a member of the president's cabinet leased Navy oil land to two oil men who gave him money. The cabinet is the group of officials who head the big government departments. Albert B. Fall was Secretary of the Interior under President Warren G. Harding. Fall persuaded Harding to move the Navy's oil reserves, land kept for the Navy's future oil, to the Interior Department. Secretary of the Navy Edwin Denby carried out the transfer. Fall then leased, or rented out, the Teapot Dome reserve in Wyoming to Harry F. Sinclair's Mammoth Oil company and the Elk Hills reserve in California to Edward Doheny's Pan-American Petroleum. Doheny gave Fall a $100,000 "loan" with no interest. Sinclair gave him about $300,000 in cash and government bonds, papers the government promises to pay back with interest.

In October 1923 the senators on the Public Lands Committee opened an investigation. Senator John B. Kendrick of Wyoming had called for it, and Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana led it for about two years. Walsh found Doheny's $100,000. In 1927 the justices of the Supreme Court ruled that the leases had been gained by corruption. On October 25, 1929, a jury found Fall guilty of taking a bribe from Doheny. A bribe is money given to an official in return for a favor. A judge sentenced Fall to one year in prison and a $100,000 fine. He was the first person convicted of a crime he committed while serving in a president's cabinet. Doheny was found not guilty of paying the same bribe. Sinclair went to jail for several months, for contempt of Congress and for having the jury in his case followed. Contempt of Congress is the crime of refusing to obey Congress or one of its committees.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law: who decides, and who pays" -->
Oregon's Compulsory Education Act of 1922 required parents to send children aged 8 to 16 to the public school in their district. Compulsory means required. The Society of the Sisters of the Holy Names of Jesus and Mary, which ran Catholic schools, sued Oregon's governor, Pierce, over the law. A second case was brought for Hill Military Academy. The justices of the Supreme Court struck the law down in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters* on June 1, 1925. Justice James McReynolds wrote that it interfered with the liberty of parents to direct their children's education. He wrote: "The child is not the mere creature of the state." He meant that a child does not belong to the government, and that the people who raise a child have the right to guide the child's future. Under that ruling, state lawmakers may require a child to be educated, but they may not require every child to attend a government school. Private schools, church schools and, later, homeschooling are all legal because of it.

On March 21, 1925, Tennessee's governor, Austin Peay, signed the Butler Act. It made it unlawful to teach in the state's public schools "any theory that denies the story of Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals." That meant a teacher could not teach evolution, the scientific idea that living things, people included, developed over a very long time from earlier forms. Civic leaders in Dayton, Tennessee, heard that the American Civil Liberties Union would pay for a court challenge to the law. They set up a test case, a trial arranged on purpose so that a court would rule on the law. They asked John T. Scopes, a football coach and science teacher, whether biology could be taught without evolution. He said it could not, because the state's own approved textbook included it. His trial opened in Dayton on July 10, 1925, before Judge John T. Raulston. On July 21 the jury found him guilty, and he was fined $100, the smallest fine the law allowed.

In January 1927 the judges of the Tennessee Supreme Court threw out his conviction. The state constitution required a jury, not a judge, to set any fine over $50, and Raulston had set the fine himself. So the judges never ruled on the Butler Act itself. It stayed in force until 1967.

In these years, most of the money for public schools was local money, raised in thousands of small school districts. The National Center for Education Statistics counted the shares. In the school year 1919-20, local money made up 83.2 percent of the money for public schools. By 1949-50 the local share had fallen to 57.3 percent. State money made up 39.8 percent that year, and federal money 2.9 percent. The number of school districts also fell, from 119,001 in 1937-38 to 83,718 in 1949-50, as small districts were merged into larger ones.

In June 1947 Governor Earl Warren of California signed the Anderson Bill. With it, California became the first state to repeal its own law on segregated schools. Segregated schools kept children of different races apart.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Buck v. Bell, 1927" -->
In 1924 Virginia's lawmakers passed a law that allowed state officials to sterilize people they judged "feeble-minded" and likely to have "socially inadequate offspring." Sterilization is an operation that makes a person unable ever to have children. "Feeble-minded" was a label officials put on people they judged to be slow to learn. Offspring means children. Aubrey Strode wrote the law and then defended it in court.

Carrie Buck was born on July 2, 1906, in Charlottesville. She was raised by foster parents. According to Encyclopedia Virginia, she became pregnant after the foster parents' nephew raped her. It gives the rape as alleged, meaning claimed but not proved. Her foster parents believed the pregnancy showed promiscuity, meaning sex with many partners, and so showed that she was "feebleminded." On January 23, 1924, they asked a court to have her committed to a state institution, meaning sent there to live by a court's order.

Her case went to a trial court under Judge Bennett T. Gordon on November 18, 1924, then to Virginia's Supreme Court of Appeals on November 12, 1925, and then to the Supreme Court. On May 2, 1927, the justices upheld Virginia's law, 8 to 1. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote the opinion. It includes the sentence "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." Imbecile was another insulting label for a person judged slow to learn. Justice Pierce Butler dissented, meaning he disagreed with the ruling, without writing an opinion.

On October 19, 1927, Dr. John H. Bell performed the operation on Carrie Buck. It is called a salpingectomy. He cut her fallopian tubes, the tubes that carry eggs to the womb, so that she could never become pregnant again. Between 1927 and 1972, doctors in Virginia sterilized about 8,300 people under the law. The source does not say which operation was done on each of them. Virginia's lawmakers repealed the law in 1974. On May 2, 2002, Governor Mark Warner apologized for the state's program. No later group of justices has overruled *Buck v. Bell*, or cancelled it with a new ruling.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="franklin-d-roosevelt" name="Franklin D. Roosevelt" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Franklin D. Roosevelt

> **Who:** The president who lost key cases in the Supreme Court, tried to add justices to it, and was elected four times.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1933 to 1945. His plan for the courts went to Congress on February 5, 1937.

Franklin D. Roosevelt tried to change the size of the Supreme Court after its justices struck down one of his laws. On May 27, 1935, in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, the justices struck down part of the National Industrial Recovery Act. That part let the president approve "codes of fair competition," which were rules for whole industries. The justices held that members of Congress had given the president powers that Congress had no right to give away. They also held that the poultry business in the case was not trade between the states, the kind of trade Congress could regulate, or make rules for.

On February 5, 1937, Roosevelt sent Congress a plan to reorganize the federal courts. Under the plan, the president could name one new justice to the Supreme Court for each justice over the age of 70, up to six more. The Court could have grown from nine justices to fifteen.

On March 29, 1937, in *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, the justices upheld Washington State's minimum wage for women and minors. A minimum wage is the lowest pay the law allows, and minors are young people who are not yet adults. Soon after, they upheld other New Deal laws too. Legal historians still disagree about whether Roosevelt's plan caused that change. The dates fit, and the cause is not settled.

On June 14, 1937, the members of the Senate Judiciary Committee told the Senate to reject the plan. They called it "needless, futile, and utterly dangerous." On July 22, 1937, the senators rejected it, 70 to 20. According to the Federal Judicial Center, Roosevelt lost a great deal of political support for proposing it.

Roosevelt was elected four times, in 1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944. George Washington had served two terms, and presidents after him had kept that custom. Roosevelt broke it.

On June 25, 1941, he signed Executive Order 8802. An executive order is a written order from the president to the officials under him. It states: "There shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries and in Government, because of race, creed, color, or national origin." Discrimination means treating people worse because of who they are. A creed is a religion. With the order, Roosevelt created the Committee on Fair Employment Practice. After the war, members of Congress refused to make that committee permanent.

On February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It let the Secretary of War and military commanders mark off military areas and shut "any or all persons" out of them. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt, commander of the Army's Western Defense Command, used it to force people of Japanese descent from their homes. He issued Public Proclamation No. 4 on March 29, 1942. Over six months, under his orders, about 122,000 men, women and children were forced into assembly centers, which were short-term holding places. From there they were forced into ten camps in remote parts of six western states and Arkansas. Nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens, by the count of the National Archives. None was charged with a crime, and none had any way to appeal.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="franklin-d-roosevelt" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The courts and the camps" -->
A man named Korematsu was convicted of breaking the military's exclusion orders, the orders that shut people of Japanese descent out of the military areas. On December 18, 1944, in *Korematsu v. United States*, the justices of the Supreme Court upheld his conviction, 6 to 3. They held that forcing citizens out of their homes in wartime was justified. On the same day, in *Ex parte Endo*, all the justices agreed that federal officials could not hold a citizen whom they admitted was loyal. According to the National Archives, that ruling "paved the way for the end of mass confinement."

In the 1980s federal judges threw out the convictions of Korematsu and others. Judge Marilyn Hall Patel vacated, or cancelled, Korematsu's conviction on November 10, 1983. In the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, members of Congress apologized for the imprisonment and voted to pay $20,000 to each person who had been held.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Poll taxes and the white primary" -->
Under Georgia law, a voter owed a poll tax of $1 a year, and officials let no one vote who had not paid it. People under 21 or over 60, blind people, and women who did not register to vote did not have to pay. Nolan Breedlove sued the tax collector, T. Earl Suttles. All the justices of the Supreme Court upheld the tax in *Breedlove v. Suttles* on December 6, 1937. Justice Pierce Butler wrote that "the equal protection clause does not require absolute equality." The equal protection clause is the part of the Fourteenth Amendment that requires states to treat people equally under the law.

In Texas, the Democratic Party was the only party that won elections, so its primary was the election that counted. A primary is the election in which a party chooses its candidates. On May 24, 1932, the leaders of the Texas Democratic Party voted that only "white citizens" could be members. The election judges of precinct 48 in Harris County refused Lonnie Smith, a Black voter, a ballot in the primary. A precinct is one small voting area. Smith sued one of those judges, a man named Allwright, and the others. In *Smith v. Allwright*, decided on April 3, 1944, the justices held that the party's leaders were acting for the state when they ran the primary. Keeping Black voters out of it therefore broke the Fifteenth Amendment. Justice Stanley Reed wrote the opinion. Justice Owen Roberts dissented.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="1948: the armed forces, refugees and conventions on television" -->
On July 26, 1948, President Harry Truman signed Executive Order 9981. It states: "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed forces without regard to race, color, religion or national origin." By about 1953 Black and white troops served together in nearly all the armed services.

The Displaced Persons Act of 1948 was the first American law for refugees, people forced from their home countries. It first allowed 200,000 Europeans who had lost their homes in the war to come to the United States. Members of Congress raised the number later. Between 1948 and 1952 about 350,000 to 400,000 people came in under it.

In 1948 both major parties held their national conventions in Philadelphia, and television networks broadcast them. At a national convention, delegates from every state choose the party's candidate for president and write its platform, the list of positions it stands for. The National Constitution Center puts the television audience at as many as 10 million viewers on the East Coast. On July 12, 1948, after a speech by Hubert Humphrey, the Democratic delegates added a civil rights plank to their platform. A plank is one position in a platform. Civil rights are the rights every citizen has to equal treatment under the law. Two Southern delegations walked out on live television. On July 17, Southern delegates met in Birmingham, Alabama, and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party, called the Dixiecrats. They chose Strom Thurmond, the governor of South Carolina, as their candidate. Their platform stated, "We stand for the segregation of the races." Segregation means keeping people of different races apart. Thurmond won four states and 39 electoral votes. Electoral votes are the votes for president cast by electors, the people chosen in each state for that job.

## 1950 to 2000

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
From 1954 to 1977, the justices of the Supreme Court decided cases on schools, on how votes and voting districts were counted, and on how police question people they arrest. In 1964 and 1965, members of Congress passed two laws. One banned segregation in public places. Under the other, officials in some states and counties had to ask federal officials before changing any voting rule. Five amendments were added to the Constitution between 1951 and 1971. In 1974 President Richard Nixon resigned before members of the House could vote to impeach him. To impeach a president is to charge him formally with wrongdoing, which leads to a trial in the Senate. In 1998 the members of the House impeached President Bill Clinton, and the senators found him not guilty.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Five amendments, 1951 to 1971" -->
Each of the five amendments added between 1951 and 1971 governs who holds office or who votes.

Since the Twenty-second Amendment was ratified on February 27, 1951, "No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice." Before it, Washington's two terms had been a custom, and Franklin Roosevelt had broken it.

The District of Columbia, the capital city, got electors in presidential elections through the Twenty-third Amendment, ratified on March 29, 1961. The District got no senators and no voting member of the House.

Ratified on January 23, 1964, the Twenty-fourth Amendment bans poll taxes only in elections for president, vice president, senator and representative. State officials could still charge a poll tax in state and local elections. Then, on March 24, 1966, in *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections*, the justices of the Supreme Court struck down state poll taxes too. Virginia's constitution had set a poll tax of up to $1.50 a year. Justice William O. Douglas wrote that making "the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard" breaks the Equal Protection Clause. Affluence means wealth.

The Twenty-fifth Amendment was ratified on February 10, 1967. It states what happens when a president dies, resigns or is removed. It also sets out a way to fill an empty vice presidency: the president names someone, and the members of both houses of Congress vote to confirm, or approve, the choice.

Eighteen-year-olds got the vote under the Twenty-sixth Amendment. It was ratified on July 1, 1971, faster than any other amendment.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Brown v. Board of Education and all deliberate speed" -->
On May 17, 1954, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, the justices of the Supreme Court held that segregated public schools broke the Constitution. The ruling did not state how or when to end segregation.

A year later, on May 31, 1955, they decided a second case, often called *Brown* II. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for a unanimous Court, meaning every justice agreed. The justices sent the cases back to federal district judges, the judges of the lowest federal courts. They told school boards to make "a prompt and reasonable start toward full compliance." They ordered the judges to admit the children who had sued to public schools "on a racially nondiscriminatory basis with all deliberate speed." Deliberate means careful and unhurried, so the words let school boards move slowly. The order set no date. School board members who moved slowly were not clearly breaking it.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="thurgood-marshall" name="Thurgood Marshall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Thurgood Marshall

> **Who:** The lawyer who won 29 of the 32 cases he argued in the Supreme Court and later became its first Black justice.
> **When and where:** Baltimore, New York and Washington, D.C. Born 1908. Died 1993.

Thurgood Marshall held four jobs in the American legal system. He ran the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, and then he was a federal appeals judge, Solicitor General and a justice of the Supreme Court. He was the first Black justice. He was born in Baltimore, Maryland, on July 2, 1908. He wanted to study law at the University of Maryland, and officials at the law school turned him away because he was Black. He went to Howard University's law school instead and graduated first in his class in 1933. One of his first cases, *Murray v. Pearson*, ended with Maryland's judges ordering that same law school to admit Black students. The Maryland Court of Appeals ruled in the case on January 15, 1936.

From 1940 to 1961 he ran the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, a law office whose lawyers took civil rights cases to court. He argued 32 cases in the Supreme Court and won 29. Among them were *Smith v. Allwright* in 1944, *Morgan v. Virginia* in 1946 on segregated buses between states, *Sweatt v. Painter* in 1950 on the University of Texas law school, and *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954.

On October 5, 1961, President John F. Kennedy made Marshall a judge of the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, a court that hears appeals from trial courts. He did it by recess appointment, which means a president fills the seat while the Senate is away. A recess appointment ends unless the senators confirm it. Southern senators opposed Marshall, and the senators did not confirm him until September 11, 1962, eleven months later.

On August 23, 1965, he left that court to become Solicitor General of the United States, the officer who argues the government's own cases in the Supreme Court. He won 14 of the 19 cases he argued in that job.

President Lyndon Johnson nominated him, or named him, to the Supreme Court on June 13, 1967, for the seat Tom C. Clark had left. The senators confirmed him on August 30, 1967. He took the oath as a justice on October 2, 1967. He took senior status, which meant he stopped full-time work on the Court, on October 1, 1991. He died on January 24, 1993.

After his death, Chief Justice William Rehnquist said most justices are remembered for their work on the Court. "Justice Marshall, however, is unique because his contributions to constitutional law before becoming a member of the Court were so significant."
<!-- hb-story:end slug="thurgood-marshall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Prayer and the Bible in public schools, 1962 and 1963" -->
The members of the New York State Board of Regents, the officials who oversaw the state's schools, wrote a prayer for public school children to say each morning. It read: "Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee, and we beg Thy blessings upon us, our parents, our teachers and our Country." No child had to say it. On June 25, 1962, in *Engel v. Vitale*, the justices of the Supreme Court held the prayer unconstitutional anyway, 6 to 1. Unconstitutional means against the Constitution. The justices ruled against the prayer because government officials had written it. The prayer was short and a child could stay silent, and the justices held that neither fact changed the ruling. Justice Hugo Black wrote that the First Amendment's ban on establishing a religion, meaning setting up an official religion, "must at least mean that in this country it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group."

The justices decided two more cases together in *Abington School District v. Schempp* on June 17, 1963. In Pennsylvania's public schools, the school day opened with Bible readings. In the Baltimore city schools, children said the Lord's Prayer together. The justices struck down both. Justice Tom Clark wrote the opinion, and Justice Potter Stewart was the only one to dissent.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="One person, one vote" -->
Under Georgia's county unit system for statewide elections, the candidate who won a county got all of that county's unit votes. So a vote in a small rural county counted many times as much as a vote in a city. The justices struck the system down, 8 to 1, in *Gray v. Sanders* on March 18, 1963. Justice William O. Douglas wrote: "The conception of political equality from the Declaration of Independence, to Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, to the Fifteenth, Seventeenth, and Nineteenth Amendments can mean only one thing." He ended the sentence with the words "one person, one vote."

On June 15, 1964, in *Reynolds v. Sims*, the justices applied the same rule to state legislatures. Alabama's lawmakers had not redrawn the lines of the state's legislative districts, the areas that elect the state's lawmakers, since 1901. They still used the 1900 census. By 1960, 25.1 percent of Alabamians could elect a majority of the state senate. In the worst case, one senate district had about 41 times as many people as another. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the opinion. Under it, districts had to hold about equal numbers of people, and lawmakers in almost every state had to redraw their districts.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="lyndon-b-johnson" name="Lyndon B. Johnson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Lyndon B. Johnson

> **Who:** A Senate leader who kept count of how each senator would vote, then a president who used the same skill on civil rights laws.
> **When and where:** Texas and Washington, D.C., 1948 to 1969.

Lyndon Johnson reached the Senate in 1948 by 87 votes, out of nearly a million cast. That was his final margin over Coke Stevenson in the Texas Democratic runoff, a second election between the top two candidates in the party's primary. A landslide is a win by a huge margin. For the rest of his life people called him "Landslide Lyndon."

On the night of the runoff, Johnson seemed to have lost. Six days after the polls closed, the election judge of Precinct 13 in Jim Wells County, Luis Salas, reported 202 more votes: 200 for Johnson and 2 for Stevenson. He had changed the total on the tally sheet from 765 to 965. The added names were in alphabetical order, in the same ink and the same handwriting. Some of the people listed said they had not voted that day. The members of the state party's executive committee voted 29 to 28 to certify Johnson as the winner. A federal judge, T. Whitfield Davidson, blocked that result. Then Supreme Court Justice Hugo Black, acting alone, ruled for Johnson. He ruled aloud on September 28, 1948, and in writing the next day. Johnson's name went on the ballot. In 1977 Salas told a reporter, James Mangan of the Associated Press, that the local political boss, George B. Parr, had ordered about 200 votes added. No court ever ruled that the votes were forged, and Johnson was never charged.

Johnson became his party's whip in the Senate in 1951. The whip counts votes and keeps party members in line. In 1953 he became its minority leader, the leader of the smaller party, and from 1955 to 1961 he was majority leader, the leader of the larger party. At 45, still in his first term, he was the youngest party leader the Senate had ever had. The Senate's historians describe how he worked on other senators. He stood close to a senator's face and pulled "clippings, memos, statistics" out of his pockets. His tone "could be supplication, accusation, cajolery, exuberance, scorn, tears, complaint, the hint of threat." Supplication means begging, cajolery means coaxing, exuberance means high spirits and scorn means open disrespect.

In 1957 he pushed through the first civil rights law since Reconstruction, the years after the Civil War. He wanted it passed, but he feared a long filibuster would split his party. A filibuster is a way of blocking a bill by talking on and on so that no vote can happen. To get the bill through, he took out its main enforcement sections. Members of Congress passed it without them. According to the Senate's own history, very little changed because of it.

As president, Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964. On April 11, 1965, he signed the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, and he said, "no law I have signed or will ever sign means more to the future of America." On August 6, 1965, he signed the Voting Rights Act. It included the kind of enforcement tools that the 1957 law had been passed without. Federal examiners could register voters themselves, and some states had to ask federal officials before changing any voting rule.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="lyndon-b-johnson" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="How the civil rights laws passed" -->
Southern senators led by Richard Russell of Georgia tried to block the Civil Rights Act of 1964 with a filibuster. They held the Senate floor, meaning they kept talking so that no vote could happen, for 60 working days, including seven Saturdays. On June 10, 1964, the senators voted 71 to 29 for cloture, the motion that ends debate. It was the first time senators had ever cut off debate on a civil rights bill. Senator Clair Engle of California had a brain tumor and could not speak. He raised his weakened arm and pointed to his eye to vote aye, which means yes. The senators passed the bill 73 to 27 on June 19.

The act has ten parts, called titles. Title II bans segregation in places that serve the public. Its words are: "All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, and privileges ... of any place of public accommodation ... without discrimination or segregation." Title VI bans discrimination in any program paid for with federal money. Later, federal officials used Title VI to cut off money to school districts that stayed segregated. Title VII bans discrimination in jobs. In Title VII, members of Congress also created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

Section 4 of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 contains a formula for which places were covered. A state or county was covered if it had used a literacy test, a reading test for voters, or a similar device on November 1, 1964. It also had to have fewer than half of its voting-age people registered or voting in the 1964 presidential election. Under Section 5, officials in every covered place had to get permission from the Attorney General, the head of the Justice Department, or from a federal court in Washington before changing any voting rule at all. By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had been registered, one-third of them by federal examiners, according to the National Archives.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Federal money for schools, 1965" -->
On April 11, 1965, Johnson signed the Elementary and Secondary Education Act outside the former Junction Elementary School near Stonewall, Texas. Elementary and secondary schools are the schools for children from first grade through high school. Junction Elementary was the one-room school where he had started at the age of four. His first teacher, Kate Deadrich Loney, sat beside him. The printed law and the LBJ Library both give the date as April 11. The National Center for Education Statistics gives April 12.

Under Title I of the act, federal officials send money to school districts according to how many children in them come from families with low incomes. The money comes with conditions the districts have to meet. In the 2015 budget year, about $14.3 billion went out under Title I, according to the National Center for Education Statistics in 2019.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Immigration law after 1965" -->
In 1965 members of Congress passed the Immigration and Nationality Act. Under it, the national quotas set in the 1920s ended, and officials admitted immigrants on the basis of family ties and skills. The act also set the first cap on immigration from the Americas. The Refugee Act of 1980 set out a permanent system for admitting refugees. The Immigration Reform and Control Act became law on November 6, 1986. Under it, about 2.7 million people who had lived in the country for years without permission got legal status, meaning the right to stay. The act also set penalties for employers who hired workers without permission to work.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Miranda v. Arizona, 1966" -->
On March 13, 1963, police arrested Ernesto Miranda at his home in Phoenix, Arizona. They questioned him in a police interrogation room, a room for questioning suspects. After about two hours he signed a written confession. He was convicted of kidnapping and rape. At his trial the officers admitted that no one had told him he could have a lawyer with him.

The justices of the Supreme Court set a rule for police in *Miranda v. Arizona* on June 13, 1966. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the opinion. Before questioning a person in custody, meaning held by police and not free to leave, police must tell the person that they have the right to remain silent, that anything they say can be used against them in court, that they have the right to a lawyer, and that a lawyer will be appointed, or provided free, if they cannot pay for one. If the person asks for those rights, the questioning must stop.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="School law in the 1970s" -->
Wisconsin law required children to attend school until age 16. Three fathers did not send their children to high school after the eighth grade. Jonas Yoder and Wallace Miller belonged to the Old Order Amish church, and Adin Yutzy to the Conservative Amish Mennonite church. Their children were Frieda Yoder and Barbara Miller, both 15, and Vernon Yutzy, 14. Each father was fined $5. The justices of the Supreme Court held 6 to 1 in *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, on May 15, 1972, that Wisconsin officials could not make the fathers send their children to high school. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote the opinion. The justices found that the families' way of life was a religious practice, lived every day and centuries old. They held that Wisconsin's officials had not shown that two more years of school mattered enough to override that practice, which the First Amendment protects.

The Education Amendments of 1972 became law on June 23, 1972. Title IX of that law states: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." In plain words, a school or college that gets federal money may not treat a student worse because the student is a girl or a boy.

Texas schools were paid for partly with local property taxes. A property tax is a tax on the value of land and buildings. In the school year 1967-68, two school districts in San Antonio spent different amounts on each pupil. Edgewood, whose students were mostly Mexican American, spent $356 on each pupil. Alamo Heights spent $594. In *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, on March 21, 1973, the justices decided 5 to 4 that education is not a fundamental right under the Constitution. They found no right to education written in the Constitution or clearly implied by it. Justice Lewis Powell wrote the opinion. Texas officials only had to show that their way of paying for schools was rational, meaning it had a sensible reason. Keeping schools under local control counted as a sensible reason. After 1973, people who sued over unequal school money had to go to state courts, under state constitutions.

On November 29, 1975, President Gerald Ford signed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act. Handicapped is an older word for disabled. Under the act, every child with a disability had a right to a free, appropriate public education. The Department of Education's history of the law states that in 1970, schools in the United States educated only one in five children with disabilities. It also states that members of Congress were concerned about "the more than 1 million children with disabilities excluded entirely from the education system." It does not say which officials kept those children out.

In October 1970, James Ingraham was a student at Drew Junior High School in Dade County, Florida. Because he was slow to follow a teacher's instructions, he was held over a table in the principal's office and hit more than 20 times with a wooden paddle. School policy allowed one to five blows, called licks, on the buttocks with a flat wooden paddle less than two feet long, three to four inches wide and about half an inch thick. The beating raised a hematoma, a pocket of blood under the skin. He needed a doctor, and he missed school for several days. His family sued three school officials: the principal, Willie J. Wright, an assistant principal, Lemmie Deliford, and an assistant to the principal, Solomon Barnes. The Supreme Court's opinion does not say which of them held him or struck him. Another student, Roosevelt Andrews, was paddled several times for small offenses. Once he was hit on the arms and lost the full use of an arm for a week. The sources used here do not say who struck him.

The justices decided *Ingraham v. Wright* 5 to 4 on April 19, 1977. They held that the Eighth Amendment's ban on "cruel and unusual punishments" does not cover school discipline. They wrote that it "was designed to protect those convicted of crimes." They also held that school staff do not have to give a child a hearing, a chance to tell his side, before hitting him, because the child can sue afterward if the beating was too severe. The Constitution, as the justices read it, did not forbid paddling in schools. So paddling ended only in states where lawmakers voted to end it, one state at a time.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Congress and the president: war powers and Watergate" -->
In 1973 members of Congress passed the War Powers Resolution, a law that limits how long a president may keep troops fighting without the approval of Congress. President Nixon vetoed it, meaning he rejected it, on October 24, 1973. On November 7, 1973, the representatives voted 284 to 135 and the senators voted 75 to 18 to override the veto, which made it law without his signature. Under it, when a president sends troops into fighting without a declaration of war, he must report to Congress in writing within 48 hours. Within 60 days he must end the fighting unless members of Congress declare war, vote to approve it or give more time.

Early on June 17, 1972, five men broke into the offices of the Democratic National Committee at the Watergate building in Washington, D.C. Police arrested them with cash and equipment for spying on the offices. Investigators later tied them to Nixon's re-election campaign. Officials in Nixon's White House at first denied any link. The break-in and the scandal that followed are called Watergate. A scandal is wrongdoing that becomes publicly known.

On February 7, 1973, the senators voted to create a committee to investigate, led by Sam Ervin of North Carolina. A White House aide, Alexander Butterfield, told the committee that the president kept a tape recorder in rooms of the White House that switched on at the sound of a voice. In October 1973 Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliott Richardson to fire the special prosecutor, Archibald Cox, a lawyer named to investigate the case, who had demanded the tapes. Richardson and his deputy resigned in protest over Nixon's order.

In *United States v. Nixon*, decided on July 24, 1974, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled 8 to 0 that Nixon had to hand the tapes over to the prosecutor. Justice William Rehnquist took no part. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote that the president's right to keep his talks private "must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."

In late July the members of the House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment, or formal charges. They approved obstruction of justice on July 27, abuse of power on July 29 and contempt of Congress on July 30. Obstruction of justice means trying to block an investigation. Abuse of power means using the powers of an office for wrong purposes. On the morning of August 9, 1974, Nixon signed a one-sentence letter of resignation. He addressed it to the Secretary of State, because a law passed in 1792 states that a president's resignation is filed there. It took effect at 11:35 a.m., when Secretary of State Henry Kissinger initialed it, or wrote his initials on it. The members of the full House never voted on the articles.

Gerald Ford took the oath of office at noon. He said: "My fellow Americans, our long national nightmare is over." He went on to say that the Constitution worked. He also said: "I am acutely aware that you have not elected me as your President by your ballots." Vice President Spiro Agnew had resigned in October 1973. Nixon had named Ford to replace him under the Twenty-fifth Amendment, and the members of both houses had confirmed him. As president, Ford named Nelson Rockefeller as his vice president the same way. For about two and a half years, the country had a president and a vice president whom no voter had chosen for either office.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New kinds of schools, 1991 to 1996" -->
In 1991 Minnesota's lawmakers passed the first state law allowing charter schools. Under it, a local school district could grant a group a charter, a written agreement, to run a school. The law's text calls them "outcome-based schools," and it allowed only eight in the whole state. State Senator Ember Reichgott Junge was its chief author. The first one, City Academy in St. Paul, opened in 1992.

Homeschooling became legal across the country state by state during the 1980s and early 1990s, through new laws and court rulings. The usual answer to which state came last is Michigan, in 1993, and it took two steps there. On May 25, 1993, in *People v. DeJonge*, the justices of the Michigan Supreme Court struck down the state's rule that parents who taught their children at home must use certified teachers, meaning teachers licensed by the state. The ruling covered only families whose religious beliefs forbade certified teachers. On the same day, in *People v. Bennett*, the same justices upheld the rule for a family with no religious objection. Michigan's lawmakers wrote a home-education exemption into the state's school attendance law in 1995. An exemption is a rule that lets some people off a requirement. It took effect on July 1, 1996.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="An impeachment at the end of the century" -->
The members of the House of Representatives impeached President Bill Clinton on December 19, 1998. The Constitution requires the votes of two-thirds of the senators, 67 votes, to convict. On February 12, 1999, the senators voted 45 guilty and 55 not guilty on the first article. On the second article the vote was 50 to 50. Clinton stayed in office.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-time:start id="2000-today" order="10" chapter="government-politics" label="2000 to Today" state="full" progress="written" -->
## 2000 to Today

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Since 2000, judges have ruled on how votes are counted, how voting districts are drawn and whether corporations may spend money on elections. In 2000 the justices of the Supreme Court stopped a recount, a second count of the votes, in Florida during the presidential election. On January 6, 2021, while members of Congress counted the electoral votes, a crowd attacked the Capitol, the building where Congress meets. Judges also ruled on public money for religious schools and on how children are assigned to schools.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Bush v. Gore, 2000" -->
The presidential election of 2000, between George W. Bush and Al Gore, came down to Florida. The United States Supreme Court's opinion describes what the justices of the Florida Supreme Court had ordered. They had ordered a hand recount of about 9,000 ballots in Miami-Dade County that counting machines had read as blank. They had also ordered 215 votes from Palm Beach County and 168 from Miami-Dade added to the certified total, the official count. They had called for recounts of blank ballots in the counties that had not yet counted them by hand.

On December 12, 2000, the justices of the United States Supreme Court decided *Bush v. Gore* in an unsigned opinion, one that no single justice signed as its author. Seven justices saw a problem under the Constitution: different counties used different rules to decide what a ballot meant, so two identical ballots could be counted differently depending on where they were cast. Five justices voted to stop the recount instead of sending it back to be fixed, because there was no time left. Justices John Paul Stevens, David Souter, Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Stephen Breyer dissented. The opinion states that the ruling was "limited to the present circumstances." The recount ended.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Money and maps" -->
On January 21, 2010, the justices decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, 5 to 4. Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, had made a film attacking Hillary Clinton. A nonprofit corporation is a group organized like a company that does not pay profits to owners. Its leaders wanted to advertise the film during a period before an election when federal law barred corporations from spending money on elections. Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote for the majority that "the Government may not suppress political speech based on the speaker's corporate identity." Suppress means stop. In plain words, government officials may not ban a group's political speech just because the group is a corporation. The justices overruled a 1990 decision, *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce*, and part of another, *McConnell v. Federal Election Commission*.

The justices decided *Rucho v. Common Cause*, also 5 to 4, on June 27, 2019. Gerrymandering is drawing the lines of voting districts so that one side wins more seats. Two maps were before the Court: North Carolina's map for Congress drawn by Republicans in 2016, and Maryland's drawn by Democrats in 2011. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that "partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts." Partisan means favoring one political party. Justice Elena Kagan wrote in dissent: "For the first time ever, this Court refuses to remedy a constitutional violation because it thinks the task beyond judicial capabilities." After 2019, a voter who believed a map was drawn to favor one party could not take that complaint to a federal court. Judges in state courts and members of Congress could still act on it.

A map drawn by race is a separate question, governed by the Voting Rights Act. In another Voting Rights Act case, *Shelby County v. Holder*, the justices on June 25, 2013, struck down the formula in Section 4(b) of the act. That formula set which places had to ask permission before changing voting rules. As of September 2026, members of Congress had passed no new formula.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Schools and the courts" -->
The No Child Left Behind Act is named for 2001, but it became law on January 8, 2002. Members of Congress wrote it as a new version of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965, and they kept that act's method: federal money for schools, with conditions attached. The Congressional Research Service, the office that does research for members of Congress, states that No Child Left Behind began the focus on accountability for test results, meaning judging schools by how their students scored. On December 10, 2015, members of Congress replaced it with the Every Student Succeeds Act. State officials still had to set standards, lists of what students should learn, and test students every year in reading, mathematics and science. Under the new law, state officials had more say over what to do about the results. In the 2023 budget year, federal spending on all the programs under the 1965 act came to $29.0 billion, according to a Congressional Research Service report of February 2024.

Four times between 2002 and 2022, the justices of the Supreme Court ruled on public money for religious schools. On June 27, 2002, in *Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, the justices upheld Ohio's scholarships for students in Cleveland, 5 to 4. The scholarships paid up to $2,250 a year for families with low incomes. According to the Court's 2002 opinion, 82 percent of the private schools taking part were religious. The justices held that such a program is allowed if it treats religion neutrally, favoring no religion and no lack of one, and the money reaches a religious school only because a family chose that school.

In the next three rulings the justices went further. They held that when state officials pay for private schools or programs, they may not leave out religious ones. Missouri officials had refused a church preschool a grant, a gift of public money, for resurfacing its playground just because it was a church, though the grant was open to others. The justices ruled against them in *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia v. Comer* on June 26, 2017. Three years later, five justices barred Montana officials from keeping scholarship money from religious schools, in *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, decided 5 to 4 on June 30, 2020. Maine officials paid tuition, the cost of attending a school, for children from towns with no high school, but only at nonreligious schools. In *Carson v. Makin*, decided 6 to 3 on June 21, 2022, the justices held that Maine officials could not require that.

On June 28, 2007, in *Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1*, the justices struck down two plans that used a child's race to decide which school the child attended, 5 to 4. Officials of Seattle's school district had used race to break ties, meaning to choose between students who ranked the same for a high school place. School officials in Jefferson County, Kentucky, had used it in placing children in elementary schools. Chief Justice Roberts wrote: "The way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race." Only four justices joined that sentence. Justice Kennedy cast the fifth vote and wrote separately. He wrote that school officials may still work toward a mix of students. He listed ways to do it: choosing where to build new schools, drawing attendance zones, paying for special programs, recruiting students and teachers, and tracking enrollment and results by race. Attendance zones are the areas whose children go to each school. After 2007, school officials could still try to mix their schools, but they could not assign a single child to a school because of that child's race.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Counting the votes, January 6, 2021" -->
Every four years, the members of the House and Senate meet together in the House chamber to count the electoral votes for president. The vice president presides, or runs the meeting. Tellers, the members chosen to count, open each state's certificate and read out its votes. Until 2022, one member of the House and one senator together could object in writing to a state's votes. The members of the two houses then met separately, debated and voted.

In 2020, Republicans who acted as electors in some states prepared papers presented as certificates of electoral votes, and they sent them to the Senate and the National Archives. According to a Congressional Research Service report of December 2024, those papers did not carry the certificate that each state's governor or other executive must issue under federal law. So they were not presented at the count.

A crowd attacked the Capitol while the count was under way on January 6, 2021. The Government Accountability Office, a federal agency that checks the government's work, reported in 2022 that over about seven hours, members of the crowd assaulted, or attacked, about 140 police officers. About 80 of them were from the United States Capitol Police. About 114 Capitol Police officers reported injuries. The damage cost about $1.5 million. The same report states that the plans Capitol Police officials made for the day "did not reflect the potential for extreme violence aimed at the Capitol." Those officials had information that protesters might be armed and were planning to target Congress.

That day, members of Congress took up two objections. According to the Congressional Research Service report, senators rejected the objection to Arizona's votes 93 to 6, and representatives rejected it 303 to 121. The objection to Pennsylvania's votes failed 92 to 7 among senators and 282 to 138 among representatives. The members of Congress then finished the count and declared the result.

Afterward, members of Congress changed the rules. The Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act became law on December 29, 2022. Under it, an objection must have the support of one-fifth of the members of each house, about 87 representatives and 20 senators. Only the state's executive, usually the governor, may certify the state's electors. Electors must be chosen under state laws passed before election day. The President of the Senate is the vice president, and the act states: "The President of the Senate shall have no power to solely determine, accept, reject, or otherwise adjudicate or resolve disputes over the proper certificate of ascertainment of appointment of electors, the validity of electors, or the votes of electors." In plain words, the vice president cannot decide alone which electors or which votes count.

On January 20, 2025, the day he took office again, President Donald Trump signed Proclamation 10887, a formal order from the president. With it, he cut the prison sentences of fourteen named members of two groups, the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys, to the time they had already served. Among them were Stewart Rhodes and Kelly Meggs. A pardon is a president's order that forgives a federal crime, so the person is no longer punished for it. He gave "a full, complete and unconditional pardon" to everyone else convicted of crimes connected with the events at the Capitol on January 6, 2021, and he ordered everyone still in prison for them released.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Donald Trump's two impeachment trials" -->
Senators can convict a president, and so remove him from office, only if two-thirds of them, 67 votes, find him guilty. No president has ever been convicted.

After the members of the House impeached President Donald Trump, the senators voted on February 5, 2020, 48 guilty and 52 not guilty on the first article, and 47 to 53 on the second. The members of the House impeached him a second time. On February 13, 2021, the senators voted 57 guilty and 43 not guilty, ten votes short of conviction. Seven Republican senators voted guilty: Richard Burr, Bill Cassidy, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Mitt Romney, Ben Sasse and Pat Toomey.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="shaye-moss" name="Wandrea ArShaye Moss" movie="" kind="ordinary" status="verified" -->
### Wandrea ArShaye "Shaye" Moss

> **Who:** A Fulton County election worker who was publicly and falsely accused of cheating in the 2020 count by a lawyer for the Trump campaign and by Rudolph Giuliani, and who told a committee of Congress under oath what followed.
> **When and where:** Fulton County, Georgia, 2017 to 2022. Sworn testimony in Washington, D.C., June 21, 2022.

Shaye Moss worked in the Department of Registration and Elections in Fulton County, Georgia, from 2017 until 2022. She handled applications from voters and requests for absentee ballots, which are ballots mailed to voters who vote from home. She also helped count the votes. Her mother, Ruby Freeman, worked as a temporary election worker in the same county for the 2020 election.

On June 21, 2022, Moss testified under oath to the House committee that investigated January 6. She said: "I have always been told by my grandmother how important it is to vote and how people before me, a lot of people, older people in my family did not have that right." Older voters were her favorite part of the job: "the older voters like to call, they like to talk to you, they like to get my card, they like to know that every election I am here." She once drove to a hospital to bring a woman her absentee ballot application.

About five million Georgians voted in 2020. From November 11 to 19, 2020, election workers counted every ballot by hand, in 41,881 batches. In no county did the margin, the gap between the two candidates' totals, change by more than 0.73 percent. Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger certified the result on November 20, and Governor Brian Kemp certified it too. After a machine recount, requested on behalf of the Trump campaign, state officials certified the result again on December 7, 2020.

On December 3, 2020, a lawyer working for the Trump campaign played clips of a security video from the State Farm Arena at a hearing of the Georgia Senate. The lawyer said election workers there had pulled 18,000 hidden ballots from "suitcases of ballots under a table, under a tablecloth." The two women in the clips were Freeman and Moss.

The women's lawsuit, as the federal court described it in 2022, states what happened next. Georgia's state election officials and agents of the Georgia Bureau of Investigation watched the whole security tape, which ran for hours, and interviewed everyone who had been there. They found no evidence for any of the claims. Early on December 4, the state's Voting Implementation Manager posted that the video "Shows normal ballot processing." The containers held ballots that had been processed earlier that night, and no observer had been told to leave.

On December 10, 2020, Rudolph Giuliani told a hearing of the Georgia House that the two women were "quite obviously surreptitiously passing around USB ports as if they are vials of heroin or cocaine." Surreptitiously means secretly. USB ports are the sockets that computer memory sticks plug into. He said their homes and workplaces should have been searched. Moss later testified that the object her mother handed her in the video was "a ginger mint." On January 2, 2021, Trump telephoned Raffensperger and named Ruby Freeman again and again. He called her "a vote scammer, a professional vote scammer and hustler." Raffensperger answered on the call that Giuliani "or his people, they sliced and diced that video and took it out of context."

Moss testified about the messages she received: "a lot of threats, wishing death upon me, telling me that, you know, I will be in jail with my mother, and saying things like, 'Be glad it's 2020 and not 1920.'" Many were racist, she said. The record does not name the people who sent them. Strangers went to her grandmother's house and pushed their way in, saying they had come to make a citizen's arrest, an arrest by someone who is not a police officer, and needed to find Moss and her mother. Agents of the FBI, the federal police, advised Freeman to leave her home around the week of January 6, 2021, and she stayed away about two months. "I've lost my name," Freeman said, "and I've lost my reputation." Moss said she stopped giving out her business cards and stopped going to the grocery store. Asked how many of the permanent election workers and supervisors in that video were still doing the job, Moss answered: "There is no permanent election worker or supervisor in that video that is still there." She had left too.

In December 2021 Freeman and Moss sued Giuliani for defamation in federal court in Washington, D.C. Defamation is harming a person's good name with false statements. On August 30, 2023, Chief Judge Beryl Howell ruled against Giuliani on all their claims, as a penalty for refusing to hand over evidence. On December 15, 2023, a jury awarded the two women about $148 million, a figure reported by PBS. The judge later reduced the amount to about $146 million, according to NBC News. On April 15, 2024, Judge Howell refused Giuliani's request to throw out or change the verdict, the jury's decision. In January 2025 a federal judge in New York, Lewis Liman, found Giuliani in contempt of court for failing to give information about his property. A court filing showed that the judgment had been satisfied, meaning its terms had been met, on February 21, 2025.

In November 2025 President Trump pardoned Giuliani and others who had worked to overturn the 2020 result. A presidential pardon covers only federal crimes, and none of the people named had been charged with a federal crime over the 2020 election. A pardon does not cancel a civil judgment, a court's order in a lawsuit to pay money, such as the award to Freeman and Moss. That award had already been paid.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who does not get a vote" -->
In the 2020 census, Census Bureau workers counted 689,545 people living in the District of Columbia. That was more than Wyoming, with 576,851, and more than Vermont, with 643,077. The voters of each of those states elect two senators and a voting representative. The District has no voting member of the House and no senators. It has three electors for president because of the Twenty-third Amendment.

The same census found 3,285,874 people in Puerto Rico, more than lived in 21 of the 50 states in 2020. Its residents are United States citizens. While they live there, they cannot vote for president. Their Resident Commissioner, their one member in the House, cannot vote on the House floor. In 2020 the census found 153,836 people in Guam, 87,146 in the United States Virgin Islands, 49,710 in American Samoa and 47,329 in the Northern Mariana Islands, just under 339,000 in all. None of those four places has a voting member of Congress or electors for president. In all, about 3.6 million American citizens and nationals lived in 2020 in places with no voting member of Congress. Nationals are people who belong to the United States without being full citizens. The Census Bureau states the rule itself: "The populations of the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico are excluded from the apportionment population because they do not have voting seats in Congress." The apportionment population is the count used to divide the seats in the House among the states.

In 2024, about 4 million Americans could not vote because of a felony conviction, according to an estimate by the Sentencing Project. The count is the Sentencing Project's estimate, not a government count. A felony is a serious crime. That was 1.7 percent of adults of voting age. In 2016 the number had been 5.9 million. Among Black Americans of voting age, 1 in 22, or 4.5 percent, could not vote for this reason. That was more than three times the rate for everyone else, 1.3 percent. About 72 percent of the people counted were not in prison. They were on probation, which is supervision in place of prison, or on parole, which is early release from prison under supervision, or they had finished their sentences. State lawmakers and state constitutions set these rules. Under the laws of Maine and Vermont, people in prison can vote.

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Immigration law after 2001" -->
In the Homeland Security Act of 2002, members of Congress created the Department of Homeland Security. Its officials began work in 2003. Under the act, the old Immigration and Naturalization Service was split into three agencies, known by their initials as USCIS, ICE and CBP. On June 15, 2012, officials of the Department of Homeland Security began a policy called Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, or DACA. Under it, certain people brought to the country as children could get protection from being deported, or sent out of the country, and permission to work. Lawsuits over DACA went on for years.
