## 1800 to 1850

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
In 1803 the Supreme Court's justices struck down part of a law passed by Congress for the first time. From then on, American courts claimed the power to cancel laws that break the Constitution. In the same years, several states dropped the rule that a white man had to own property to vote, and some of those states took the vote away from Black men. The first national party convention met in 1831. President Andrew Jackson handed out government jobs to his supporters and signed the law that moved Native nations out of the East.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="john-marshall" name="John Marshall" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### John Marshall

> **Who:** The Chief Justice who wrote that a court may refuse to follow a law that breaks the Constitution.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1801 to 1835. *Marbury v. Madison* decided in February 1803.

John Marshall wrote the ruling that gave American courts the power to strike down laws. The Chief Justice is the head of the Supreme Court. President John Adams nominated Marshall for that post on January 20, 1801, in the last weeks of his presidency. Marshall took his seat on February 4, 1801. He held it for 34 years, until he died in Philadelphia on July 6, 1835. In that time he heard more than a thousand cases and wrote more than five hundred opinions. An opinion is a judge's written explanation of a ruling.

The case that made his name began with a small job. In 1801, in his last days in office, Adams appointed William Marbury a justice of the peace, a local judge, for Washington, D.C. The paper that gave Marbury the job, called a commission, was never delivered to him. The new Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to hand it over. Marbury asked the Supreme Court for an order forcing Madison to deliver it.

In February 1803 Marshall's Court decided the case, called *Marbury v. Madison*. The justices said the Court could not give Marbury the order he asked for. A section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 gave the Court that power, and that section went beyond what the Constitution allowed. So the justices refused to follow it. It was the first time the Supreme Court had declared a law passed by Congress and signed by the president unconstitutional.

Marshall wrote: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Province here means proper job. A law that goes against the Constitution is void, he wrote, and when the two conflict in a case, judges must follow the Constitution. This power is called judicial review. The justices did not declare another act of Congress unconstitutional until the *Dred Scott* case of 1857.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="john-marshall" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Louisiana Purchase, 1803" -->
On April 30, 1803, American diplomats signed a treaty with France to buy 828,000 square miles of land west of the Mississippi River. The price was $15 million, about 4 cents an acre. The two American negotiators, Robert Livingston and James Monroe, had been allowed to spend up to $10 million, and only for New Orleans and the Floridas.

The purchase raised a question about the Constitution. President Thomas Jefferson usually read the Constitution strictly, which means he held that the government could do only what the document said it could. He wondered whether the Constitution allowed the government to take on new land at all. In a letter in 1803 to Wilson Cary Nicholas, Jefferson wrote: "Our peculiar security is in possession of a written Constitution. Let us not make it a blank paper by construction." By construction he meant by stretching the meaning of its words. He wrote that he would rather ask the nation for more power than take it. He went ahead with the purchase anyway.

France sold its claim to the land. Thousands of Native people, in dozens of nations, lived on it. They were not part of the sale. In Article VI of the treaty, the United States promised to carry out the treaties Spain had made with "the tribes and nations of Indians" there.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Ending the import of enslaved people, 1808" -->
Under the Constitution, Congress could not ban the shipping of enslaved Africans into the country before 1808. On March 2, 1807, President Jefferson signed a law that banned it from the first day allowed. The ban took effect on January 1, 1808. From then on, bringing enslaved people across the ocean into the United States was against federal law.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Who gained the vote and who lost it" -->
Between 1807 and 1838, the lawmakers of several states rewrote their rules on voting. They let white men without property vote. In the same laws and constitutions, they shut out Black men and women.

New Jersey came first. In 1807 its legislature passed an election law that let all white male taxpayers vote and shut out everyone else. Women and free Black people in New Jersey who met the property test had voted since 1776. The new law ended that. Its authors said they wanted "uniform practice" across the state.

In 1821 the delegates who rewrote New York's constitution dropped the property test for white men. In the same document, they required a Black man to own property worth $250, free of debt, before he could vote. In 1825 only 298 of the nearly 6,000 free Black adults in New York met that test.

In 1835 the delegates to North Carolina's constitutional convention took the vote away from free Black men who had been allowed to vote until then. In January 1838 the delegates to Pennsylvania's convention added the word "white" before "freemen" in the section on who could vote. Robert Purvis of Philadelphia wrote a protest called the *Appeal of Forty Thousand Citizens, Threatened with Disfranchisement*. To disfranchise means to take away the vote. He read it aloud on March 14, 1838, at the First African Presbyterian Church in Philadelphia. Pennsylvania's voters approved the new constitution on October 9, 1838, by 113,971 votes to 112,759. Black men in Pennsylvania could not vote again until the Fifteenth Amendment in 1870.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first national party convention, 1831" -->
In September 1831 the Anti-Masonic Party held a meeting in Baltimore to choose its candidate for president. It was the first national nominating convention held by any party. A nominating convention is a meeting where a party's members choose its candidates. The Anti-Masonic Party was also the first party to give voters a platform, a written list of what it stood for. The delegates chose William Wirt. In the 1832 election he won only Vermont and its seven electoral votes.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="andrew-jackson-gov" name="Andrew Jackson" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Andrew Jackson

> **Who:** The president who signed the law to move Native nations out of the East, used the veto as a political weapon, and gave government jobs to his supporters.
> **When and where:** Washington, D.C., 1829 to 1837.

Andrew Jackson was the seventh president, from 1829 to 1837. On May 28, 1830, he signed the Indian Removal Act. The act allowed the president to make treaties with Native nations living east of the Mississippi River to move them west. The members of the House passed it on May 26, 1830, by 102 votes to 97, according to the House's own historians. The National Park Service gives the count as 101 to 97. The Senate vote was 28 to 19.

By the end of Jackson's presidency, his officials had made nearly 70 removal treaties. Federal officials moved about 50,000 Native people from the East to Indian Territory. About 25 million acres of their land were opened to white settlers. In 1838 United States Army troops and state militia forced more than 16,000 Cherokee people out of their homelands, held them in camps and then sent them west. The journey is called the Trail of Tears. The National Archives puts the Cherokee dead at about 4,000. The National Park Service counts more than a thousand who died on the way and perhaps several thousand more who died from its effects.

Georgia's lawmakers had also passed a law about white people living on Cherokee land. It required any white person living in Cherokee territory after March 1, 1831, to get a license from Georgia's governor and to swear an oath to obey Georgia's laws. Samuel Worcester, a Christian missionary, lived on Cherokee land without that license. A Georgia court convicted him and sentenced him to four years of hard labor in the state prison.

In 1832 the Supreme Court decided *Worcester v. Georgia*. Chief Justice John Marshall wrote that Georgia's law was "void, and the judgment a nullity," meaning it had no force. Georgia's officials did not carry out the ruling. In April 1832 Jackson wrote to his friend John Coffee that the Court's decision "has fell still born." Stillborn means born dead. Many schoolbooks quote Jackson saying, "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it." He never said it. The sentence first appears in a history book by Horace Greeley printed in 1864, about twenty years after Jackson died.

On July 10, 1832, Jackson vetoed a bill to renew the charter of the Second Bank of the United States, a national bank. A veto is a president's refusal to sign a bill into law. His veto message called the Bank "dangerous to the liberties of the people." He was the first president to use a veto as a weapon in a major fight over the economy. He made the veto a part of his campaign in 1832 and won a second term.

Jackson then moved the government's money out of the Bank. On March 28, 1834, the senators voted to censure him for it. To censure is to condemn someone officially. It is the only time the Senate has censured a president. In January 1837, after Jackson's party won a majority, the senators voted to expunge the censure, which means to strike it from the record.

Jackson also filled government jobs with his own supporters. He called this rotation in office. He argued that government work could be made "so plain and simple" that ordinary men could take turns at it. Senator William L. Marcy of New York gave the practice its nickname when he said "to the victor belong the spoils of the enemy." Spoils are the goods an army takes from the side it beats. The practice became known as the spoils system.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="andrew-jackson-gov" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The fight over the school tax, 1834 and 1835" -->
In 1834 Pennsylvania's lawmakers passed the Free Public School Act. It set up a system of common schools across the state, paid for by taxes. A common school was a free public school open to the children of the town.

Many voters did not want to pay the tax. In 1835 petitions signed by thousands of them reached the legislature, asking for the law to be repealed, or cancelled. No one in the state Senate spoke against repeal, and few members of the House did.

On April 11, 1835, Thaddeus Stevens, a member of the Pennsylvania House, spoke against repeal. He said: "Many complain of the school tax, not so much on account of its amount, as because it is for the benefit of others and not themselves." He pointed out that a man "cheerfully pays the tax which is necessary to support and punish convicts, but loudly complains of that which goes to prevent his fellow being from becoming a criminal." After his speech, the repeal failed, and the schools stayed. Stevens later called the speech the "crowning utility" of his life, the most useful thing he ever did.

## 1850 to 1900

<!-- hb-zoom level="era" -->
Three amendments to the Constitution, added between 1865 and 1870, ended slavery, made everyone born in the country a citizen, and barred states from denying a citizen the vote because of race. In the 1870s and 1880s Black men in many Southern states voted and held office. Starting in 1890, the delegates who rewrote Southern state constitutions added rules that kept most Black men from voting. The justices of the Supreme Court upheld those rules, and they upheld laws that kept Black and white people apart. In the same years, lawmakers in Washington passed the first laws to shut out immigrants by nationality, and to fill some government jobs by exam. States began to require children to go to school.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Laws that made children go to school" -->
In 1852 the lawmakers of Massachusetts passed the first state law that required children to go to school. Children from 8 to 14 had to attend. The school committee in each town or city had to look into every case of a child who did not. A family that broke the law could be fined up to $20. By 1900, thirty-two states had a law like it. Mississippi was the last state to pass one, in 1918. A child who had to be in school could not also be working in a mill.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Dred Scott v. Sandford, 1857" -->
On March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court decided *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, by a vote of 7 to 2. Dred Scott, an enslaved man, had sued in federal court. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney wrote the ruling. He wrote that a Black person whose ancestors were brought to the country and sold as slaves was not a citizen under the Constitution. So Scott had no right to sue in a federal court. Taney also wrote that Black people "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect."

The ruling went further. It held that Congress had no power to ban slavery in the federal territories, the lands that were not yet states. That made *Dred Scott* only the second time the justices had struck down a law passed by Congress. The first was *Marbury v. Madison* in 1803.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Secession, 1860" -->
On December 20, 1860, in Charleston, the delegates of a South Carolina convention signed an ordinance of secession, a law declaring that their state was leaving the United States. Four days later the delegates adopted a statement of their reasons. It complained of "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery." It also complained that the Northern states had joined to elect a man "whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery." The man they meant was Abraham Lincoln.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="abraham-lincoln" name="Abraham Lincoln" movie="Lincoln (2012)" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Abraham Lincoln

> **Who:** The president who suspended a basic legal right during the Civil War, was told by the Chief Justice that he had no power to do it, and kept doing it.
> **When and where:** Washington and Baltimore, April to July 1861.
> **Movie:** *Lincoln* (2012), a film about the fight in January 1865 to get the House of Representatives to pass the Thirteenth Amendment. It does not cover the events below.

In the first months of the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus. Habeas corpus is a person's right to be brought before a judge, who decides whether the government may go on holding that person. When it is suspended, the government can hold a person without taking them before a judge at all. The Constitution allows the writ to be suspended "in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion." It does not say who may suspend it.

On April 25, 1861, Lincoln wrote to General Winfield Scott. He approved "the most prompt, and efficient means" against people in Maryland who were arming against the government. Those means included "the bombardment of their cities" and, "in the extremest necessity," suspending the writ. On April 27 he gave Scott the power to suspend it anywhere along the routes the army's troops used between Philadelphia and Washington. No federal official had suspended the writ since the War of 1812.

At two in the morning on May 25, 1861, federal soldiers entered the country house of John Merryman, a planter in Baltimore County. They took him to Fort McHenry. General George Cadwalader held him there. The charge was that Merryman was an officer in a company of men who supported secession and had federal weapons. On May 26 Chief Justice Roger Taney, who also served as a judge in Maryland, ordered Cadwalader to bring Merryman to court. Cadwalader refused in a letter. He wrote that Lincoln had given officers the power to suspend the writ. A deputy marshal carried Taney's next order to the gate of the fort, and a guard would not let him in.

On May 28, with a crowd gathered outside, Taney ruled in Baltimore that the president had no power to suspend the writ, under the Constitution or any law. He said Merryman must be freed at once. Taney issued no order to make that happen. He was 84 years old. He was also the judge who had written *Dred Scott*, so few people at the time saw him as neutral.

On July 4, 1861, Lincoln explained himself to Congress. He said the power to suspend the writ had "purposely been exercised but very sparingly." Then he asked, "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" He argued that the framers could not have meant that "the danger should run its course, until Congress could be called together."

In March 1863 the members of Congress passed a law that let the president suspend the writ for the rest of the war. The dispute was settled by a vote of Congress, not by a court.

On January 31, 1865, the members of the House passed the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery, by 119 votes to 56. Lincoln signed the resolution the next day, though a president does not have to sign an amendment. The amendment was ratified on December 6, 1865.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="abraham-lincoln" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Land for colleges, 1862" -->
On July 2, 1862, the Morrill Act became law. Senator Justin Morrill of Vermont sponsored it. Under it, each state received 30,000 acres of federal land for each of its senators and representatives in Congress. The land was to pay for colleges that taught "agriculture and the mechanic arts," meaning farming and the skills of building and machines. More than 10 million acres of the land given out under the act had been taken from Native nations by the United States, through treaties, agreements and seizure. A second Morrill Act, in 1890, required each state either to set up separate land-grant colleges for Black students or to show that its colleges did not turn students away because of race.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments" -->
Members of Congress approved the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866, and it became part of the Constitution on July 9, 1868. It states that "all persons born or naturalized in the United States" are citizens. It also states that no state may "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." That means a state has to apply its laws the same way to everyone.

Members of Congress passed the Fifteenth Amendment on February 26, 1869, and it was ratified on February 3, 1870. It states that the right of citizens to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Abridged means cut back. Previous condition of servitude means having once been enslaved. The amendment does not mention sex.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="New constitutions in the South" -->
Reconstruction was the rebuilding of the Southern states' governments after the Civil War. Members of Congress passed laws for it called the Reconstruction Acts. Under those acts, a convention met in Charleston on January 14, 1868, to write a new constitution for South Carolina. Black men in South Carolina had voted for the first time in the election of its delegates. Three out of every five delegates were Black.

Article X of the constitution they wrote required a system of free public schools, "free and open to all the children and youths of the State, without regard to race or color." Schools were to be open at least six months a year. Once there were enough schools, children would have to attend for at least twenty-four months. For decades, state officials did not carry out Article X.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Tweed Ring and Tammany Hall" -->
In the 1860s and 1870s, William M. Tweed held several offices in New York at once. He was a state senator from 1867 to 1871 and chaired the state Senate's finance committee. From 1868 he was the Grand Sachem, or head, of Tammany Hall. Tammany Hall was the political machine of the Democratic Party in New York City. A political machine is a party organization that wins votes by handing out jobs and favors. Through Tammany Hall, Tweed had great power in the Democratic Party.

Tweed and the men around him, called the Tweed Ring, took money from the city in several ways. They sent the city bills for more than the work cost. Printing and supply companies they controlled overcharged the city. The cost of public buildings ran far past their budgets. A new county courthouse was expected to cost half a million dollars. It ended up costing the city's taxpayers about $13 million. Modern historians estimate that the ring stole between $30 million and $200 million.

The cartoonist Thomas Nast attacked Tweed in the magazine *Harper's Weekly* from 1869. In July 1871 *The New York Times* printed evidence from inside the city's accounts, after people who knew about the thefts passed it to the paper. In late 1873 Tweed was convicted and sentenced to twelve years in prison and a fine of $12,750. On appeal the sentence was cut to one year. He died of pneumonia in the Ludlow Street jail on April 12, 1878.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Minor v. Happersett: citizens without the vote" -->
In October 1872 Virginia Minor, a citizen of Missouri, tried to register to vote in St. Louis. The registrar, Reese Happersett, refused her because she was not a "male citizen." Minor sued. She argued that the Fourteenth Amendment made voting a right of every citizen.

The Supreme Court ruled against her, with no justice dissenting, in its October 1874 term. The Chief Justice delivered the ruling. It held that "the Constitution of the United States does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one." Suffrage means the right to vote. Under the ruling, each state could keep the vote for men only.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The first laws against immigrants by nationality" -->
The Page Act of 1875 was the first federal law to limit immigration. It barred Chinese laborers brought over under contract. In practice, most Chinese women were kept out under it too.

On May 6, 1882, President Chester A. Arthur signed the Chinese Exclusion Act. It barred Chinese laborers from coming into the country for ten years. It was the first federal law to shut out a group of people by their nationality. Members of Congress renewed it and widened it later. It was not repealed until 1943.

The federal government also took over the work of admitting immigrants. New York State had run the landing station at Castle Garden from 1855 to 1890. In 1892 the federal station at Ellis Island opened.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The election of 1876 and the end of Reconstruction" -->
The presidential election of 1876 was between Rutherford B. Hayes, a Republican, and Samuel J. Tilden, a Democrat. The electoral votes of Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina and Oregon were disputed. On January 18, 1877, Senator George Edmunds of Vermont proposed an Electoral Commission to settle the count. It had fifteen members: five representatives, five senators and five Supreme Court justices. Members of Congress passed the bill.

The commission's members voted 8 to 7, along party lines, to give every disputed electoral vote to Hayes. Hayes became president. On April 24, 1877, on his orders, federal troops left the Louisiana state house. It was the last state house in the South that federal troops had guarded. Historians date the end of Reconstruction to 1877.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-story:start slug="joseph-rainey" name="Joseph Hayne Rainey" movie="" kind="famous" status="verified" -->
### Joseph Hayne Rainey

> **Who:** A man born to enslaved parents, forced to work on Confederate defenses, who became the first Black member of the US House and the first to preside over it.
> **When and where:** Georgetown, South Carolina, and Washington, D.C. Born June 21, 1832. Sworn in December 12, 1870. Died August 2, 1887.

Joseph Hayne Rainey was the first African American to serve in the House of Representatives. He was born on June 21, 1832, in Georgetown, South Carolina. His parents were enslaved. His father, Edward L. Rainey, worked as a barber and used his earnings to buy the family's freedom in the early 1840s.

During the Civil War, Confederate officers forced Rainey to work on the defenses of Charleston. He also worked as a steward, a ship's servant, on a Confederate ship that slipped through the Union blockade. In 1862 he and his wife escaped to Bermuda. There he ran a barbershop and she ran a dress shop. They came home in 1866.

On October 19, 1870, Rainey won election to a full term in Congress with 63 percent of the vote. On November 8 he won a special election for the rest of the current term, with 86 percent. He was sworn in on December 12, 1870.

On April 1, 1871, he spoke for the Ku Klux Klan Act. The Ku Klux Klan was a vigilante group, people who act outside the law, that threatened Black Americans and their white allies in the South. The act made it a federal crime to deny anyone the rights the Constitution protects. President Ulysses S. Grant signed it on April 20, 1871. Rainey said the Constitution owed "protection to the humblest citizen, without regard to rank." He also supported the Civil Rights Act of 1875. He wanted Black and white children taught in the same schools, and he said "mixed schools" would "annihilate" prejudice. On April 29, 1874, he took the Speaker's chair and ran the House, the first African American to do so.

On January 25, 1877, Rainey spoke against the Electoral Commission. He warned it could lead to "the election of a President by one man rather than by a majority of the people of the United States." The bill passed anyway.

Rainey lost his seat in 1878, by 62 percent to 38, and left the House on March 3, 1879, after more than eight years. No Black member of Congress served longer than he had until the 1950s. He was the first of fourteen Black representatives who served during Reconstruction. After Reconstruction ended, the number of Black members of Congress fell to zero and stayed near zero for decades. Rainey worked for the Treasury Department in South Carolina and ran a banking business in Washington. He died in Georgetown on August 2, 1887.
<!-- hb-story:end slug="joseph-rainey" -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="The Pendleton Act, 1883" -->
Since Andrew Jackson's time, presidents had given most government jobs to their supporters. Then Charles Guiteau, a man who had wanted a government job and had not gotten one, killed President James A. Garfield. Chester A. Arthur became president after Garfield. Arthur had come up through New York machine politics.

On January 16, 1883, Arthur signed the Pendleton Act. Under it, some federal jobs went to people who passed a competitive exam, not to people who had worked for the winning party. At first the exam rules covered only about 10 percent of the government's 132,000 workers. The other 90 percent of the jobs were still handed out by the party in power.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Taking away the vote in Mississippi and Louisiana" -->
The Fifteenth Amendment barred states from denying the vote because of race. In 1890 the delegates who wrote Mississippi's new constitution set out to stop Black men from voting without naming race. Senator J. Z. George led the convention. Its committee on voting included its one Black delegate, Isaiah Montgomery. The committee members agreed that their task was disfranchisement.

The delegates added a poll tax, a tax of $2 a year that a man had to pay before he could vote. They required two years of living in the state and one in the county. A voter had to swear he had not committed certain crimes, crimes the committee thought Black men were more likely to have been charged with. Ballots were printed by the state and could only be used by a man who could read. A man who could not read could be read a section of the constitution. If he could explain it, he could vote. The registrar alone decided whether he had explained it well enough. The delegates put the new constitution into effect themselves, without a vote of the people.

In 1898 the Supreme Court heard *Williams v. Mississippi*. Williams was a Black man convicted of murder. Jurors in Mississippi were chosen from the lists of voters. He argued that the 1890 constitution kept Black men off those lists, so it kept them off his jury. On April 25, 1898, the justices ruled against him, with none dissenting. Justice Joseph McKenna wrote that the rules "do not on their face discriminate between the races, and it has not been shown that their actual administration was evil." He went on: "only that evil was possible under them." Mississippi's voting rules stood until the Twenty-fourth Amendment of 1964.

Louisiana's delegates wrote a new constitution in 1898 and also put it into effect without a vote of the people. It set a poll tax of $1 a year, with receipts for two past years. It set a reading test given by parish registrars with no fixed standard, and a test of owning $300 in property. It also had a grandfather clause. A man who failed the tests could still register if his father or grandfather had voted in 1867, a year when formerly enslaved men could not vote in Louisiana. Thomas Semmes chaired the convention's committee on the judiciary. He said: "We met here to establish the supremacy of the white race, and the white race constitutes the Democratic Party of this State."

By the count of the Louisiana encyclopedia *64 Parishes*, 130,344 Black voters were registered in 1897. In 1898 there were 12,902. In 1900 there were 5,320, and in 1922 there were 598.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896" -->
A Louisiana law required railroads to give Black and white passengers separate cars, or cars split by a partition. A person who broke the law could be fined $25 or jailed for 20 days. On May 18, 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, by a vote of 7 to 1. Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote the ruling. He called it a "fallacy," a mistake in reasoning, to believe that "the enforced separation of the two races stamps the colored race with a badge of inferiority."

Justice John Marshall Harlan was the only justice who dissented, or disagreed. He wrote: "Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Born here, a citizen: Wong Kim Ark, 1898" -->
Wong Kim Ark was born in San Francisco in 1873 to parents who had come from China. In August 1895 he came home from a trip to China on the ship *Coptic*. Officials refused to let him land, under the Chinese exclusion laws. On October 2, 1895, a writ of habeas corpus was filed on his behalf. On March 28, 1898, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 2 that the Fourteenth Amendment makes everyone born in the United States a citizen, whatever their parents' nationality.
<!-- /hb-zoom -->

<!-- hb-zoom level="span" label="Cumming v. Richmond County, 1899" -->
In June 1897 the members of the school board of Richmond County, Georgia, closed Ware High School, the county's high school for Black students. The board members said they could not afford a high school for 60 Black students while 300 Black children had no primary schooling. They kept paying for the high schools for white students.

J. W. Cumming, James S. Harper and John C. Ladeveze, Black taxpayers, sued. They asked the court to stop the board from spending public money on the white high schools until there was an equal school for their children. They also asked it to stop taxing them for schools their children could not attend.

On December 18, 1899, the Supreme Court turned them down. The justices found no violation of the Fourteenth Amendment and treated the closing as a question of money, not of race. Justice John Marshall Harlan wrote the ruling. Three years earlier he had been the one dissenter in *Plessy*. Here he wrote that "if it appeared that the board's refusal to maintain such a school was in fact an abuse of its discretion and in hostility to the colored population because of their race, different questions might have arisen."
