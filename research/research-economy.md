## 01 · Before 1500

- **Native economies were real economies without money:** production, specialization, and long-distance exchange, moved by barter and gift between people and nations. No coins, wages, or market prices existed here. (Illinois State Museum, "Mississippian Economy: Trade"; American Yawp ch. 1.)
- **Cahokia** (near modern St. Louis; peak about 1050–1150) was the largest city north of Mexico and a production and trade center. Farm surpluses of corn supported specialists. It controlled the making and movement of **Mill Creek chert hoe blades**, quarried in southern Illinois and found more than 400 miles away — Louisiana to Minnesota to the Arkansas River valley. (Mill Creek chert references; Illinois State Museum; Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site materials.)
- Goods that moved long distances: **marine shell** (whelk) from the Gulf coast, **copper** from Lake Superior, chert tools; shell beads were produced at Cahokia in quantity and also at small farmsteads. (Illinois State Museum; Milwaukee Public Museum, Middle Mississippians.)
- Obsidian moved across western trade networks (per the seed; general archaeological literature). Wampum-as-record is `money`'s subject — one clause at most here.
- **No documented named individual** survives from this era at story level. State plainly and move on.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-442): how the hoe blades were made, and obsidian distances

- **Chipped blades and quarry pits.** Mississippian people made hoes by knapping (flaking or chipping) Mill Creek chert, a tough stone found only in southern Illinois (Union and Alexander counties). Some of the open quarry pits at Mill Creek were dug 10 to 20 feet deep and 12 to 40 feet across. (Arkansas Archeological Survey, University of Arkansas, "Mill Creek Chert Hoe Re-Sharpening Flake", archeology.uark.edu; Illinois State Museum, "Illinois Agriculture: Native American Tools", museum.state.il.us; Wikipedia "Mill Creek chert".)
- **Obsidian.** The Alberta Obsidian Project traced 383 obsidian pieces from 96 sites in western Canada, dated between about 13,000 and 300 years ago. Most came from Bear Gulch (Idaho) and Obsidian Cliff (Wyoming) and from two British Columbia sources; some traveled as far as 750 miles from their source. (Archaeology Magazine news, 4 April 2025; CBC News Edmonton, 2025.)

## 02 · The 1500s

- Crown-funded expeditions (Spain, France, England) sought gold and silver; **in the land that became the United States they found none** — the silver was in Mexico and South America. (Standard survey sources; the seed's framing verified by absence — no U.S. gold/silver strike documented before 1799 Reed nugget, see era 05 note in parked section.)
- **First recorded fur trading with Europeans on the Atlantic coast: July 1534**, Chaleur Bay (north of Maine, in today's Canada): Mi'kmaq held up beaver furs on sticks to Jacques Cartier's crew and traded them for knives and iron goods — recorded in Cartier's own account. The fur trade reached the future United States in the next century. (Canadian Museum of History, Virtual Museum of New France; Mariners' Museum.)
- European fishing fleets worked the cod banks off the northeast coast through the century and dried catch ashore; fish, not gold, was the first steady European business in these waters. (Standard maritime histories; keep general — one sentence.)
- Honest thinness: no colonial economy because almost no colonists. Spanish outposts (St. Augustine 1565) were military posts paid for by the crown, not profit-making colonies — keep to one clause, unverified details omitted.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-243a): the "first recorded fur trading" claim is not safe

- **Challenge to the 1534 "first".** In **1524**, ten years before Cartier, Giovanni da Verrazzano's crew met Abenaki (Wabanaki) people on the coast of **Maine**, inside the future United States. The Abenaki would not let the crew land and **traded by lowering baskets of goods on a rope from a cliff** to the ship. Verrazzano called the place "the land of bad people." (Encyclopedia.com, "Abnaki", per search excerpt 2026-09-26.) The Maine Memory Network and a Wabanaki timeline note that European ships had already **taken Native people from the Maine coast** to sell as slaves, which is why the Abenaki kept the crew off shore (Maine Memory Network, "Holding up the Sky"; Maine Center for Economic Policy, "Wabanaki Sovereignty in Context: A Timeline", per search excerpt). The sources checked do not say whether furs were in the 1524 baskets. **So "the first recorded fur trading between Europeans and Native people on the coast" is not supported.** Safe wording: "one of the earliest recorded trades of furs", or drop "first".

## 03 · The 1600s

- **Tobacco saves Virginia.** John Rolfe grew imported West Indies tobacco seed by **July 1612**; his **first shipments to London in 1614** founded the export economy. Virginia tobacco exports: about **2,300 pounds in 1615-16 → about 20,000 pounds by 1617 → about 1.5 million pounds by 1629-30 → about 22 million pounds a year by 1700** (Chesapeake total to England). (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Tobacco in Colonial Virginia"; Encyclopedia.com John Rolfe entry; Historic Jamestowne.)
- Tobacco was also used as money in the Chesapeake — that telling is `money`'s (their 1600s span has payment in tobacco). One clause here.
- **The fur trade grows.** One Dutch West India Company ship, the *Arms of Amsterdam*, carried **7,246 beaver skins** (plus otter, mink, lynx, muskrat) to Amsterdam in one 1626 cargo — the Schaghen letter, the same letter that reports the purchase of Manhattan. Beaver felt hats in Europe drove the demand. (New York State Library, Schaghen letter translation; NMAI "Native New York.")
- **Mercantilism, said simply:** colonies exist to enrich the home country — supply raw materials, buy finished goods. **Navigation Acts:** 1651 (trade only in English or colonial ships), 1660 (**enumerated goods** — tobacco, sugar, cotton, indigo, ginger — could be shipped **only to England** or English colonies). (Britannica, "Navigation Acts"; NCpedia.)
- **The trade in people begins.** New England and British ships ran Atlantic circuits: goods and rum to West Africa, **enslaved people** across the Middle Passage to the Caribbean and mainland, sugar and molasses back north. Say the enslaved-people leg plainly. `slavery-freedom` leads the Middle Passage and the system; the trade-circuit economics are ours in one span. (Standard triangle-trade framing; numbers belong to `slavery-freedom`'s bank.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-243a): indentured servants, the headright, and the turn to slavery (whose labor ran the tobacco economy)

- **Who did the tobacco work at first: indentured servants.** A servant signed a contract (an *indenture*) to work for a master for a set number of years in return for the cost of the ocean crossing, food, clothing and a place to sleep. Adults usually served **four to seven years**; children sometimes served much longer. At the end the servant was owed "freedom dues" (land, tools, clothes or livestock, depending on the contract and the colony). (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Indentured Servants in Colonial Virginia"; NCpedia, "Indentured Servants"; Bay Path University open text *HIS114*, "Indentured Servants".)
- **How many.** Encyclopedia Virginia (as quoted in search excerpts; its page blocked direct download on 2026-09-26): about **50,000 servants, about three-quarters of all new arrivals, came to the Chesapeake colonies between 1630 and 1680**; of about **120,000 English emigrants to Virginia and Maryland in the 1600s, about 90,000 were indentured servants**; planters brought in **about 100,000 servants by 1700**. For all the British colonies, historian **Abbot Emerson Smith** estimated that **one-half to two-thirds of all white immigrants from the 1630s to the Revolution** came under indenture (cited by economist David Galenson, "The Rise and Fall of Indentured Servitude in the Americas," *Journal of Economic History* 44:1, 1984). These are historians' estimates from ship and court records, not a count made at the time.
- **Who profited from each servant: the headright.** Under Virginia's headright rule, **whoever paid a person's passage received 50 acres of land** for that person. A planter who paid for a servant got the servant's years of labor **and** 50 acres. (Wikipedia "Headright", summarizing Virginia Company and colony records; Encyclopedia Virginia.) This is how the large tobacco planters built their estates.
- **What the work did to servants.** Masters could whip servants, and courts added extra years to a servant's term for running away or for a woman servant's pregnancy. Virginia law required masters to give "Competent Dyet, Clothing & Lodging" and not to "exceed the Bounds of moderation in correcting them." **Many servants died of disease before their contracts ended, and most who survived stayed poor.** (NCpedia, "Indentured Servants", quoting colonial statutes; Lowcountry Digital History Initiative, College of Charleston, "New World Labor Systems: European Indentured Servants".) Under the Virginia Company's first contract form (1609 to 1619), recaptured runaways could be **hanged, burned to death or shot** (T. H. Breen, "A Changing Labor Force and Race Relations in Virginia," *Journal of Social History* 7:1, 1973, as summarized in Wikipedia "Indentured servitude in Virginia"). Say plainly who did this: Virginia Company officers in the colony.
- **The turn to slavery.** About **300 Africans** lived in Virginia by 1650, alongside about **4,000 white servants** (PBS *History Detectives*, "Indentured Servants in the U.S."). Virginia's assembly passed its first laws treating Africans as slaves for life in the **1660s** (Wikipedia "Indentured servitude in Virginia", citing PBS and Encyclopedia Virginia, dates the colony's first slave law to 1661). Near 1700, as fewer English servants came (conditions in England improved) and the Atlantic slave trade grew, planters bought **enslaved Africans instead of servants**. After Bacon's Rebellion (1676 to 1677), in which servants and enslaved Africans fought on the same side, Virginia's ruling planters passed laws "to harden Virginia's racial caste system" (Lowcountry Digital History Initiative). Galenson (1984) gives the economic reason: once enslaved people could be bought in numbers, a planter could hold a person and that person's children for life instead of for five years. `slavery-freedom` owns the laws and the lives. Ours: the planters' labor force changed from servants to enslaved people, and the change was the planters' choice.
- **Dating note for the bullet "The trade in people begins":** Brown University's report dates Rhode Island's thousand-plus slaving voyages to "the century before" 1807, so the large New England trade belongs mainly to the 1700s. See the 1700 to 1750 patch for its numbers and named traders.

### PATCH 2026-10-02 (T-487): servant women's pregnancies, the fathers, and the punishments

- **The punishment, March 1662.** Virginia's General Assembly passed "Against ffornication" in its March 1662 session. A woman servant who had a child outside marriage, "in regard of the losse and trouble her master doth sustaine by her haveing a bastard shall serve two yeares after her time by indenture is expired or pay two thousand pounds of tobacco to her master," besides the fine for the offence. (Encyclopedia Virginia, primary document "Against ffornication" (1662), from Hening's *Statutes at Large*, opened 2026-10-02.) An earlier rule, per Encyclopedia Virginia's entry, added one year or 1,000 pounds of tobacco to her master or mistress. (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Indentured Servants in Colonial Virginia", opened 2026-10-02.)
- **Masters as fathers, December 1662.** In its December 1662 session the Assembly passed "Women servants gott with child by their masters after their time expired to be sold by the Churchwardens for two yeares for the good of the parish." Its words: "each woman servant gott with child by her master shall after her time by indenture or custome is expired be by the churchwardens of the parish where she lived when she was brought to bed of such bastard, sold for two yeares." Encyclopedia Virginia's introduction: the Assembly "addressed the problem of indentured servants having children by their masters." So the law itself records that masters fathered some of these children. The woman, not the master, was sold. (Encyclopedia Virginia, primary document, Hening vol. 2, p. 167, opened 2026-10-02.)
- **Why that law was written, and sexual attacks.** Encyclopedia Virginia's entry: "If the father of an illegitimate child were a master, then, according to a 1662 law, the maidservant would, upon completion of her indenture, be sold to the local parish for two years." It notes that masters "could abuse the law by impregnating a servant" and gaining extra years of her work, and states that "Other female servants were victims of sexual assault." The entry does not use the word rape and does not count how many fathers were masters. (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Indentured Servants in Colonial Virginia", opened 2026-10-02.)

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-442): Jamestown's gold hunt, and the 1705 slave code

- **No gold, then tobacco.** John Smith wrote that the early colonists were distracted by hopes of gold: "There was no talk, no hope, no work but dig gold, wash gold, refine gold, load gold." They found no gold. Officials tried other businesses (archaeologists found evidence of metal testing, glassmaking and silk-making from local mulberry trees), and none paid. After Rolfe's tobacco experiments, tobacco became the profitable export the investors had wanted. The Virginia Company of London was a group of investors who hoped to profit from the colony. (American Battlefield Trust, "Everyday Life in the Jamestown Colony"; Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation, "History of Jamestown", jyfmuseums.org.)
- **What the post-Bacon laws did (copied from `research/research-slavery-freedom.md`, era 1700s line "Virginia, 1705"):** in 1705 Virginia's General Assembly gathered its slave laws into one code. Enslaved people were property, baptism did not free anyone, and a master who killed an enslaved person while "correcting" them faced no penalty. (Encyclopedia Virginia, per that bank.)

## 04 · 1700 to 1750

- **Colonial prosperity, measured:** economic historians **Peter Lindert and Jeffrey Williamson** (2016, *Unequal Gains*; NBER working papers) found that by 1774 **free American colonists had higher average incomes than England's people**, more equally spread than anywhere then measurable. State it as their published finding. (Princeton UP book page; NBER w19861.)
- The wealth was not everyone's: much of it, especially in the South, was produced by **enslaved people who were paid nothing**. Say plainly. (Same scholarship; `slavery-freedom` leads the system.)
- **Shipbuilding:** cheap timber made colonial ships cheaper than English-built; by the Revolution **about one-third of the British merchant fleet was American-built**. (Encyclopedia.com, "Shipbuilding Industry.")
- **What the colonies sold:** tobacco (Chesapeake); **rice and indigo** (South Carolina Lowcountry — grown and processed by enslaved workers); wheat and flour (middle colonies); fish, rum, and ships (New England). (Standard colonial-economy synthesis; indigo specifics below.)
- **Indigo:** Eliza Lucas (later Pinckney), running her father's three South Carolina plantations from age 16, made the **first successful indigo dye crop in 1744** (the 1744 crop yielded 17 pounds of dye; she shared seed with neighbors). Exports: about **5,000 pounds in 1745-46 → about 130,000 pounds by 1748**; over a million pounds a year by the mid-1750s; indigo became **second only to rice** among the colony's exports and by the Revolution more than a third of their value. Skilled dye-makers her father sent from the Caribbean, and enslaved workers, did the processing — their knowledge and labor were part of the success; say so. (South Carolina Encyclopedia, "Pinckney, Eliza Lucas"; Britannica; Encyclopedia.com.)
- **Economic control from London:** **Molasses Act 1733** (sixpence-a-gallon duty on foreign molasses — widely evaded by smuggling); **Iron Act 1750** (colonial pig and bar iron admitted duty-free to Britain, but new colonial steel furnaces, rolling and slitting mills, and plating forges banned; colonists built them anyway). (Encyclopedia.com "Molasses Act"; Britannica "Iron Act.")

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-243a): convict labor, Rhode Island's slave trade, and who did the rice work

- **Convicts sold as laborers, 1718 to 1775.** Parliament's **Transportation Act of 1718** let British courts send people convicted of lesser felonies to the American colonies for **seven years**, and people convicted of more serious crimes for **fourteen years**, instead of hanging them. About **50,000 convicts** were shipped to the North American colonies between 1718 and 1775, about **one-quarter of all British emigrants** to colonial America in those years. Most went to **Maryland and Virginia**, where they were sold at the dock as bound laborers. (A. Roger Ekirch, *Bound for America: The Transportation of British Convicts to the Colonies, 1718-1775*, Oxford, 1987; Early American Crime site, "The Transportation Act of 1718".)
- **Who profited.** The British government paid London merchant **Jonathan Forward £3 a head**, and later £5, to carry convicts (Early American Crime, "The Business of Convict Transportation"; Wikipedia "Jonathan Forward"). The Maryland firm **Stevenson, Randolph and Cheston** sold convicts at Maryland sales in 1767 to 1775: most men sold for **£11 to £14 sterling**, most women for **£9 to £10** (Ekirch, Table Ten, p. 125, from the firm's own sales books in the Cheston-Galloway Papers, Maryland Historical Society). The planters who bought them got years of unpaid work.
- **Deaths on the ships.** On 38 London convict voyages of 1718 to 1736 carrying **3,599 convicts, 385 died (10.7%)**; Ekirch notes the true rate may be **14.9%** if voyages with no recorded deaths were clerical gaps. On 12 Bristol voyages of the 1770s, 23 of 990 died (2.3%). (Ekirch, Table Nine, p. 105, from Guildhall landing certificates.)
- **Who they were.** In four Maryland county records (1719 to 1775), **about 82% of convicts were men and 18% women**. On two 1721 and 1724 ships, most convicts were aged 20 to 29 and about half were unskilled laborers. (Ekirch, Tables Two to Four.)
- **Rhode Island's slave trade, with named traders.** Rhode Islanders mounted **at least 1,000 slaving voyages, carrying over 100,000 Africans** into slavery, in the century before the U.S. ban of 1807. **About 60% of slaving voyages launched from North America, in some years more than 90%, sailed from Rhode Island.** By 1764 the colony had **about thirty rum distilleries, twenty-two of them in Newport**, turning Caribbean molasses into the rum traded on the African coast for captive people. (Brown University, *Slavery and Justice* report, 2006; digital 2nd edition, section "Slavery, the Slave Trade, and Brown".)
- **The *Sally*, 1764 to 1765.** The Providence merchant brothers **Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown** sent the ship *Sally* to West Africa under captain **Esek Hopkins**, carrying **17,274 gallons of rum** plus chains, shackles and guns. Hopkins bought **196 Africans**. **At least 109 died**: 19 before the ship left Africa, 68 on the crossing, 20 soon after landing in the West Indies. When the captives rose up, Hopkins's crew fired on them; his account book reads "Slaves Rose on us was obliged fire on them and Destroyed 8." The survivors were so sick that some sold for **£5**. (Brown *Slavery and Justice* report, quoting the *Sally* account book; Rhode Island Historical Society, EnCompass, "Account of the Slave Ship Sally".)
- **John Brown kept trading.** In 1795 his ship *Hope* took 229 Africans from the Gold Coast; 198 lived to be sold in Cuba. The **Providence Abolition Society** prosecuted him in federal court, the first American prosecution for illegal slave trading, and a jury acquitted him. (Brown *Slavery and Justice* report.) This belongs mainly to `slavery-freedom`; ours is the economics (rum, ships, profit) in a sentence.
- **Rice, and the people who knew how to grow it.** Enslaved Africans were **a majority of South Carolina's people from about 1708** and **about two-thirds by 1740** (Wikipedia "History of slavery in South Carolina" and "Stono Rebellion", following Peter H. Wood, *Black Majority*, 1974). Rice was the Lowcountry's main cash crop from the early 1700s; enslaved people "moved earth on a massive scale" to build the rice fields, and the wealth "remained concentrated in the hands of a small minority of white planters" (Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library, "Ten Things Everyone Should Know about Lowcountry Rice"). By the 1720s Carolina planters were asking slave traders for Africans from the **Rice Coast** because they already knew how to grow rice (S.C. Sea Grant Consortium, "African Roots, Carolina Gold"). Enslaved children in the Lowcountry died at especially high rates, and the enslaved population did not grow by births until the 1760s (Lowcountry Digital History Initiative, "Rice in the Lowcountry").

## 05 · 1750 to 1800

- **Paying for the Revolution:** Congress had almost no tax power, so it printed **Continental dollars** from 1775. Depreciation: about 4 paper dollars to 1 in silver by end of 1777; about 100 to 1 by 1781, when the paper collapsed — "not worth a Continental." The currency story is `money`'s lead; ours is that the war was fought on paper and debt. (Harvard Curiosity, "Continental Currency"; NY Fed Liberty Street "Crisis Chronicles.")
- **The debt:** Hamilton's **First Report on Public Credit (January 1790)** counted the national debt at **about $54 million**, with state war debts adding **about $25 million** more. His plan: pay in full, and have the federal government **assume the state debts** to bind creditors to the new government and establish its credit. (Bill of Rights Institute; Online Library of Liberty; founders.archives.gov.)
- **The dinner bargain (June 1790):** with assumption losing in the House, Jefferson hosted Hamilton and Madison; the deal — assumption passed, the permanent capital placed on the Potomac. (American Battlefield Trust; National Archives Prologue.)
- **Report on Manufactures (December 5, 1791):** Hamilton's plan to grow American manufacturing with tariffs and subsidies; **Congress never brought it to a vote**. He co-founded the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures (S.U.M.), an industrial town at the Passaic falls — Paterson, New Jersey. (Gilder Lehrman; Hamilton College/PBS materials.)
- **The whiskey tax:** 1791 excise on distilled spirits (7 cents a gallon domestic) to help carry the war debt; western Pennsylvania farmers — whose corn traveled as whiskey — resisted; **1794 Whiskey Rebellion**; Washington called up **about 13,000 militia** (some sources ~12,000-12,950 — "about 13,000") and the rebellion dissolved without a battle; about 150 arrests, 20 leaders taken. (Mount Vernon Digital Encyclopedia; TTB.gov; EBSCO.)
- The First Bank of the United States and the Mint are `money`'s stories (their Hamilton). Ours: debt, credit, taxes, manufacturing vision.
- **Hamilton film:** *Alexander Hamilton* (PBS American Experience, 2007) — two-hour documentary, Brían F. O'Byrne as Hamilton. Verified. (`immigration` uses the filmed musical *Hamilton* (2020) for their Hamilton story; use the documentary here.)

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-442): the Sally's deaths, John Brown's trial, and the Whiskey Rebellion arrests

- **The *Sally*'s deaths, from the report itself.** Brown University's *Slavery and Justice* report (section "Slavery, the Slave Trade, and Brown") says 19 Africans had died before the ship sailed, a twentieth, a woman, was "left for dead" on the day it sailed, 68 died during the crossing, and another 20 died in the days after it reached the West Indies; in all "at least 109" of the 196 died, "some in a failed insurrection, the balance through disease, suicide, and starvation." The 19 who died before sailing included "several children" and one woman who "hanged her Self between Decks." Hopkins took "more than nine months" on the African coast to buy the 196, "an exceptionally long time." Hopkins's full entry adds "and Several more wounded badly." The report's itemized numbers do not add to 109; the report gives "at least 109" as its total. (slaveryandjusticereport.brown.edu, fetched 2026-10-01; Wikipedia "Sally (1764 ship)".)
- **John Brown's trial.** The federal Slave Trade Act of 1794 forbade American citizens from owning, outfitting, investing in or serving aboard ships carrying enslaved people to foreign ports. On 5 August 1797 John Brown became the first American tried under it, in federal district court in Providence, for fitting out the *Hope*. The jury acquitted him and awarded him costs against the prosecutors of the Providence Abolition Society; he did not get the ship back. (Brown *Slavery and Justice* report; Wikipedia "Slave Trade Act of 1794"; EBSCO Research Starters, "Analysis: The Trial of John Brown".)
- **Whiskey Rebellion details.** On 6 September 1791 a disguised gang in Washington County, Pennsylvania, tarred and feathered the tax collector Robert Johnson. On 16 July 1794 about 600 armed rebels attacked the house of the tax inspector John Neville at Bower Hill; the rebel leader James McFarlane was shot and killed, and the rebels set the house on fire. On 13 November 1794 ("the Dreadful Night"), troops under General Henry Lee broke into houses and arrested about 150 people, holding them in animal pens and basements. Twenty were taken to Philadelphia for trial. Two men, Philip Wigle and John Mitchell, were convicted of treason; President Washington pardoned both. (Wikipedia "Whiskey Rebellion"; World History Encyclopedia, "Whiskey Rebellion"; History.com.)

## 06 · 1800 to 1850

- **The market revolution, plainly:** in 1800 most families grew and made most of what they used; by mid-century far more produced for sale and bought what they needed — cash crops, store goods, wage work. "Market revolution" is the historians' term; use and define it. (Standard synthesis — American Yawp ch. 8; Britannica.)
- **Transport made it possible:** after the **Erie Canal opened in 1825**, freight from Buffalo to New York City fell from **about $100 a ton by road to about $10 a ton by canal**, and transit time halved (twenty days to about ten; six days for some flour shipments). The canal itself is `transportation`'s; the price collapse is ours. (EBSCO "Erie Canal"; Britannica; NY State Museum.)
- **Lowell:** the mills turned New England farm daughters into some of the country's first big groups of cash-wage earners. The daily job, the strikes, and the mill girls' stories are `work-workers`' (they feature Harriet Hanson Robinson) — ours is one sentence on wages entering household life.
- **King Cotton:** cotton was **51.6% of U.S. export value in 1840**; it stayed above half nearly every year to the Civil War. About three-quarters of U.S. cotton was exported; between 1820 and 1860 about **80% of the world's cotton supply** came from the United States. It was planted, picked, and processed by **enslaved people**; say so in the same sentence, not a footnote. (OpenStax U.S. History, "The Economics of Cotton"; Fairfax County Schools chart of cotton as % of exports; `slavery-freedom` bank has the 1860 figures.)
- **Panic of 1837:** speculation in western land and cotton, then British credit tightening and falling cotton prices; on **May 10, 1837** New York banks stopped paying out gold and silver (the bank mechanics are `money`'s). What followed was the era's long depression: **of about 850 U.S. banks, 343 closed entirely and 62 partly failed**; hard times ran to about **1843**. Define "panic" simply: many people trying to get their money out, or sell, at the same time. (Lumen U.S. History I; Wikipedia Panic-of-1837 figures traceable to Hammond's *Banks and Politics* — the 343/850 count appears across teaching sources; state as "about 4 in 10 banks.")
- **Philip Hone** (1780–1851): New York auctioneer-merchant, mayor 1826–27, whose diary (kept 1828–1851) is a firsthand record of the panic in New York — entries describe stopped business and failures: "No goods are selling, no business stirring, no boxes encumber the sidewalks of Pearl Street." His own fortune was reduced in the crisis years. Diary published as *The Diary of Philip Hone* (ed. Tuckerman, 1889; public domain). (Wikipedia/Philip Hone with diary citations; Ephemeral New York; the diary itself.)
- **Gold Rush economics** (1848–1855): in the parked section below (verified by the migration agent) — ~80,000 forty-niners in 1849, ~300,000 people by 1855; the merchants who sold supplies made steadier money than most miners. `migration` leads the crowds; `elements` the metal.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-243a): enslaved people as the economy's capital, who profited, and who paid in the Panics of 1819 and 1837

- **Enslaved people as capital, 1860.** By 1860 enslavers valued the **about 4 million people** they held (Census 1860: 3,953,760) at **at least $3 billion**, more than all the capital invested in railroads and factories in the United States combined. Teaching sources add: about three times the value of all U.S. manufacturing, or of all railroads, taken alone. (Lowcountry Digital History Initiative, "North American Context"; NMAAHC Searchable Museum, "A Nation Bound by Slavery"; Samuel H. Williamson and Louis P. ### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-243a): sharecropping and debt, convict leasing, peonage, and who paid in the Panics of 1873 and 1893

- **Sharecropping, the economics.** After the Civil War most freedpeople had no land. A landowner gave a family a plot and "furnished" food, seed, tools and a cabin on credit; at harvest **the landowner sold the crop**, subtracted the "furnish" and interest, and gave the family its share, usually **half or less**. Because **landlords held a lien (a legal claim) on the crop and kept the books**, most sharecroppers were still in debt at every settlement, year after year. The "furnish" was lent "at usurious interest rates" (usurious means far above fair). Sharecroppers usually worked about **twenty acres** and could be put off the land at the landlord's will. (Encyclopedia.com, "Sharecroppers", from the *Dictionary of American History*; Oklahoma Historical Society, *Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture*, "Tenant Farming and Sharecropping".) `slavery-freedom` owns sharecropping as a system of lives. Ours: who got the crop money.
- **How many.** The **1930 census counted 1,831,470 tenant farmers in the South** (Oklahoma Historical Society), "almost 1.8 million tenant families", **about half the region's farmers** (Encyclopedia.com). **About 700,000** were share tenants (they owned mules or tools and kept two-thirds to three-quarters of the crop). In **1937 the President's Committee on Farm Tenancy counted about 775,000 sharecroppers in the South, almost equally white and Black** (Encyclopedia.com). By **1935, 77% of Black farmers and nearly half of white farmers** in the country owned no land (Oklahoma Historical Society). Black sharecroppers in the South peaked at **392,897 in 1930** (search excerpt attributing USDA Rural Development, *Black Farmers in America, 1865-2000*, RR194; the PDF refused download on 2026-09-26, so flag this one number as unverified).
- **Debt peonage, a named case.** Peonage means forcing a person to work to pay off a debt. Congress banned it in the **Peonage Abolition Act of 1867**, but Southern states wrote laws that did the same thing. On **December 26, 1907**, **Alonzo Bailey**, a Black farm laborer, signed a contract with **H. C. Borden, manager of the Riverside Company**, a corporate farm in Montgomery County, Alabama. Bailey got a **$15 advance** to be repaid out of pay of **$12 a month**. He quit after about a month. Under Alabama law, quitting without repaying made him a criminal: he was fined **$30** and sentenced to **136 days at hard labor**. Montgomery lawyer **Edward S. Watts**, city judge **William H. Thomas** and, secretly, **Booker T. Washington** helped carry the case to the U.S. Supreme Court, which ruled in ***Bailey v. Alabama* (January 3, 1911)** that the law violated the Thirteenth Amendment. Other states' peonage laws stood until the Court struck down Georgia's and Florida's in the 1940s. Peonage was most common in the **turpentine camps** of southern Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and the Florida panhandle and in the cotton belt. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, "*Bailey v. Alabama*"; *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219, Library of Congress U.S. Reports.)
- **Convict leasing: the state as seller, companies as buyers.** The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery "except as a punishment for crime". Southern states then **leased prisoners to private companies**. In Alabama the lease ran from **1846 to July 1, 1928**; after a state money crisis in **1875**, **warden John G. Bass** leased state prisoners to coal mines, farms and lumberyards for monthly fees. From **1883**, under **warden John Hollis Bankhead**, most prisoners went to three coal companies: the **Pratt Coal and Iron Company**, the **Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company (TCI)** and the **Sloss Iron and Steel Company**. In **1888 TCI signed a ten-year contract for all able-bodied state prisoners**, paying the state **$9 to $18.50 a month per prisoner**. **More than 95% of county prisoners and 90% of state prisoners were Black.** Contractors whipped prisoners to make them work, and coal officials faked "bad conduct" reports to lengthen sentences. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, "Convict-Lease System".)
- **Money and deaths, with counts.** Convict leasing brought Alabama **about 10% of total state revenue in 1883 and nearly 73% in 1898** (Digital History, University of Houston, "Convict Lease System", ID 3179). **In 1896, 1,710 state prisoners worked in the mines, and 233 had died in the two years before; 745 county prisoners worked in the mines, and 181 had died in the two years before.** Jefferson County health officer **Dr. Thomas D. Parke** reported in **1895** a death rate of **90 per 1,000 prisoners a year at Sloss's Coalburg mine**. In **1924** a prisoner died after being **lowered into a vat of boiling water**; Sloss kept working county prisoners in its mines until 1928. **Alabama was the last state to end convict leasing (1928), about five years after Florida.** (Encyclopedia of Alabama.) Digital History adds that **death rates among leased convicts were about ten times** those of prisoners in states without leasing, and that **in 1873, 25% of all Black leased convicts [in Alabama] died**. Record both sources' figures. `slavery-freedom` and `crime-justice` own the lives; ours is that states and companies made money from it, named.
- **Why companies leased prisoners.** The Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company **admitted that its main reason for using convict labor was to break strikes and keep unions out** (Wikipedia "Tennessee State Prison", as quoted in search excerpt; Tennessee's lease ran 1871 to 1896). Flag as secondary.
- **The Panic of 1873: who paid.** By the winter of 1873 to 1874, **about one-quarter of New York City's workers had lost their jobs** (Newspapers.com blog "The Tompkins Square Riot", citing period newspapers; label as an estimate). Over **90,000 homeless workers, almost half of them women**, slept in New York police stations (Wikipedia "1874 Tompkins Square Park riot", citing Howard Zinn; flag as secondary). On **January 13, 1874**, about **7,000** unemployed people met in **Tompkins Square Park** to ask **Mayor William F. Havemeyer** for public jobs. New York **police**, including mounted police, drove the crowd out of the square and made **46 arrests**. Wikipedia's summary names **Mayor Havemeyer and Police Commissioner Abram Duryée** as the officials on the police side. (Details of the beatings are not in the sources checked; do not add them.) (Encyclopedia.com, "Tompkins Square Rally"; Wikipedia, citing Herbert Gutman, 1965, and Burrows and Wallace, *Gotham*, 1998.) No count of the injured is given in these sources.
- **The Panic of 1893: who paid.** Economic historian **Stanley Lebergott's** later estimate puts unemployment at **18.4% in 1894**, up from about **3% in 1893** (as cited by EH.net, "The Depression of 1893"; the page refused direct download, figure from search excerpt). This sits inside the bank's existing **17 to 25%** range. No count was made at the time.

At the 1935 hearings, **Charles Hamilton Houston** testified for the NAACP that the bill would leave out "Negro sharecroppers and Negro cash tenants, who are just about at the bottom of the economic scale," and called the law "a sieve with the holes just big enough for the majority of Negroes to fall through" (same).
- **Who made the exclusion: sources disagree; record both.**
  - **The racial account:** Southern members of Congress insisted on it. **Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia** opposed a rule requiring benefits high enough for "decency and health" because it "might serve as an entering wedge for federal interference with the handling of the Negro question"; the committee's executive director **Edwin Witte** reported that Byrd's position was "supported by nearly all of the southern members of both committees." Virginia **Representative Howard Smith** argued for keeping Black sharecroppers and domestic workers dependent on their employers. (Jon C. Dubin, "The Color of Social Security," *Stanford Law & Policy Review* 35:104, 2024.)
  - **The administrative account:** SSA historian **Larry DeWitt** found the exclusion came from **Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau's** amendments, for tax-collection reasons, and wrote that "the allegations of racial bias in the founding of the Social Security program, based on the coverage exclusions, do not hold up under detailed scrutiny." Labor Secretary **Frances Perkins** and relief chief **Harry Hopkins** had wanted these workers covered. (Larry DeWitt, "The Decision to Exclude Agricultural and Domestic Workers from the 1935 Social Security Act," *Social Security Bulletin* 70:4, 2010, as quoted and summarized by Dubin 2024; the SSA page refused download on 2026-09-26.)
  - **Coverage came later:** **1950** amendments covered farm and domestic workers regularly employed by one employer; **1954** amendments covered the rest (DeWitt 2010, n.16, quoted by Dubin). Representative Smith opposed the 1950s amendments too; of eight House members who voted against them, **six were Southern Democrats**, and Senators **John Stennis** (Mississippi) and **Harry Byrd** also opposed them (Dubin 2024).
- **The AAA pushed sharecroppers off the land.** The AAA's 1934 to 1935 cotton contracts paid **landowners** to take land out of cotton (the secretary of agriculture "rented" about **three-eighths of the nation's cotton land**). The contract gave sharecroppers only about **11% of the benefit**, and landlords could keep the whole check by evicting sharecroppers and hiring day labor instead. When planters cut their acreage by **40 or 50%**, they cut their tenants by the same share. The **Southern Tenant Farmers' Union**, a union of Black and white sharecroppers founded in eastern Arkansas in **summer 1934**, protested the evictions and "was subjected to violent repression by planters." In **February 1935** AAA administrator **Chester Davis** had Agriculture Secretary **Henry Wallace** fire the AAA lawyers, led by **Jerome Frank**, who had ruled that landlords must keep the same tenants (the "AAA purge"). (Encyclopedia.com, "Sharecroppers"; Oklahoma Historical Society, "Tenant Farming and Sharecropping".) `food-farming` shares the AAA; ours is who received and who lost the money.
- **Mexican Americans expelled.** From **1929**, government officials and private employers in California and across the country "undertook an aggressive program to forcibly remove persons of Mexican ancestry." California's legislature found that **about 400,000 American citizens and legal residents of Mexican ancestry** were forced to go to Mexico from California alone, and estimated **two million** people nationally, **about 1.2 million of them born in the United States**. Local authorities sold some families' property to pay for their removal. California apologized in the **Apology Act for the 1930s Mexican Repatriation Program (SB 670, 2005)**. (California Government Code §§8720-8722, SB 670 chaptered text.) Other estimates run **500,000 to 2 million** in all (Wikipedia summary). The Immigration History site (University of Texas) says the **Border Patrol** ran detention campaigns that swept up about 2 million people. `immigration` owns this story; one line here on who paid for the Depression.
- **Outline claim check, "1.2 percent (1944), the lowest ever recorded":** the BLS annual series (Current Population Survey, "Employment status ... 1944 to date") starts in 1944, and 1.2% in 1944 is its lowest year; Debt.org calls it "the country's lowest unemployment rate" (weak source). The pre-1940 figures are later estimates. Safer wording for writers: "the lowest in the official records".

## 09 · 1950 to 2000

- **The boom:** between **1947 and 1973, inflation-adjusted median family income roughly doubled**, and gains reached poor, middle, and rich alike. (Brookings; CBPP historical-trends guide; Census P60 series.)
- **The "great compression":** economists **Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo** named the sharp narrowing of the wage gap in the 1940s that held into the 1970s. (NBER w3817.)
- **G.I. Bill economics:** by the time the original bill ended in July 1956, **7.8 million of 16 million WWII veterans** had used education or training benefits; the VA backed **about 2.4 million home loans, 1944–1952**. `education` shares the schooling; `home-family`/`city-building` the suburbs. (VA, "Born of Controversy: The GI Bill of Rights"; VA anniversary releases.)
- **Keynes in America — span, not story:** the **Employment Act of 1946** made "maximum employment, production, and purchasing power" a stated federal responsibility and created the Council of Economic Advisers — the ideas of British economist John Maynard Keynes (government spending against slumps), carried into U.S. law in weakened form (the "full employment" guarantee was stripped in Congress). (Federal Reserve History, "Employment Act of 1946"; Britannica.)
- **Stagflation:** prices and joblessness rising together, which most economists had thought could not happen. Consumer prices rose **about 11% in 1974**; unemployment hit **9.0% in May 1975**; inflation returned past **13% in 1979–80**; unemployment reached **10.8% in November–December 1982 — the highest since the Depression**. The 1973 oil embargo (oil roughly quadrupled in six months) is `energy`'s story; the Fed's interest-rate cure is `money`'s. (BLS "One hundred years of price change"; BLS/Fed history; the energy bank has the embargo numbers.)
- **Deindustrialization, the macro shift:** manufacturing was about **30% of U.S. nonfarm jobs in the early 1950s (30.6% in 1955), about 11% by 2005, about 8% by the 2020s**; service-providing industries were above **80% of nonfarm jobs by the 2000s**. Manufacturing employment peaked at 19.6 million in June 1979 (BLS; also in the work-workers bank — theirs is the daily job, `big-business` the corporate decisions, ours the shift). (FRED/BLS series; Statista chart of BLS data; StateUniversity summary of BLS.)
- **Youngstown:** **"Black Monday," September 19, 1977** — Youngstown Sheet & Tube announced the Campbell Works closure; **5,000 jobs** ended at once; **within about five years the Mahoning Valley lost about 50,000 jobs** in steel and related work. (WKBN 40th-anniversary reporting; Bill Moyers "Black Monday '77"; Belt Magazine oral history — journalism, labeled.)
- **Gerald Dickey:** steelworker and **recording secretary of USW Local 1462** (1976–79) who proposed that the workers and community **buy and reopen the Campbell Works** — the plan carried by the **Ecumenical Coalition of the Mahoning Valley** (clergy-led). The Carter administration first signaled support for loan guarantees; the government ultimately **denied the ~$100 million in federal loan guarantees** the plan needed (Economic Development Administration decision, 1979), and the mill stayed closed. The campaign spread the worker-ownership idea through the labor movement. (United Steelworkers, "A Brief History of the USW and Worker Ownership"; Ohio History Connection, Gerald Dickey collection; Staughton Lynd, *The Fight Against Shutdowns*; Architectural League "Employee Ownership in the Mahoning Valley." Sequence of federal support-then-denial differs slightly across accounts — the denial and the closure are certain.)
- **The 1980s farm crisis:** verified numbers in the parked food-farming section below (land values down up to 60% in parts of the Midwest 1981–86; ~$215 billion farm debt by 1984; ~300,000 farm defaults; ~300 agricultural bank failures; Farm Aid, September 22, 1985). `food-farming` leads the families; the finance is ours.
- **The Reagan era, both sides plainly:** the **Economic Recovery Tax Act (1981)** cut the top income-tax rate from **70% to 50%**; the **Tax Reform Act of 1986** took it to **28%**. Inflation fell from 13.5% (1980) to the 3–4% range by the mid-1980s (BLS). The **national debt roughly tripled during the 1980s**. State both facts without verdicts. (EBSCO "Economic Recovery Tax Act"; Brookings "What we learned from Reagan's tax cuts"; Wikipedia/Reagan tax cuts summarizing Treasury data.)
- **The 1990s:** expansion **March 1991 – March 2001, exactly 120 months — the longest on record until then** (NBER); unemployment fell to **3.9% (fall 2000)**; the federal budget ran **surpluses in fiscal 1998–2001**; the technology/dot-com stock boom (NASDAQ up about 600% 1995 → March 2000 — the bust lands in era 10). (NBER Business Cycle Dating announcements; CRS RL31237; Treasury/CBO historical tables for the surpluses.)
- Container shipping's trade-cost collapse: verified in the parked transportation section below ($5.86 → $0.16 a ton, Levinson's calculation) — feeds this era's trade/imports lines.

## 10 · 2000 to Today

- **Dot-com bust:** NASDAQ peaked at **5,048 on March 10, 2000** and fell **about 78% by October 2002**; the 2001 recession (March–November 2001, NBER) was short and mild next to what came later. (Britannica "dot-com bubble"; EBSCO; NBER announcements.)
- **2008, explained simply:** home prices rose for years; lenders wrote mortgages many borrowers could not repay; banks bundled the mortgages into investments sold as safe; when home prices fell, the investments failed and the biggest financial firms began to fall. **Lehman Brothers failed September 15, 2008 — the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history** (about $619 billion in debts). (Britannica; EBSCO "Collapse of Lehman Brothers"; U.S. Treasury "About TARP"; CBO TARP reports.)

Foreclosure counts at family level are `home-family`'s (their bank: ~3.8 million foreclosures 2007–2010; whole-crisis estimates 7.7–10 million); the banks and the Fed are `money`'s; the bailouts as corporate power `big-business`'s. - **2020:** the pandemic recession was **February–April 2020 — two months, the shortest on record** (NBER); unemployment hit **14.8% in April 2020**; GDP fell at a **31.4% annualized rate in Q2 2020** and rebounded at a record rate in Q3 (BEA); the **CARES Act (March 27, 2020) provided roughly $2 trillion** in relief, the largest such law to that date.
