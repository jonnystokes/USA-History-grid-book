## Era 01 — Before 1500: a managed continent

- **Native fire management shaped the eastern forests.** Penn State research (published in *Annals of Forest Science*, 2019) found forest composition in the eastern U.S. was shaped more by Native American burning than by climate: repeated low-to-moderate understory burns over millennia favored oak (and other fire-adapted species), kept woods open, regenerated food plants, and fed the game animals people relied on. Even small populations burned large areas repeatedly. European colonists dismissed the practice; after about 1940, total fire suppression became "an ecologically transformative event in all forests." (Sources: Penn State News "Eastern forests shaped more by Native Americans' burning than climate change," 2019; USDA Forest Service Treesearch, "Native Burning in Western North America"; History.com overview — journalism.)
- **The Three Sisters.** Corn, beans, and squash grown together: beans fix nitrogen, squash leaves shade and hold the soil, corn stalks carry the beans. A soil-keeping polyculture, in contrast to single-crop fields that wear out. (General agricultural-history sourcing — Smithsonian NMAI education materials; also covered in `food-farming`'s lane, which leads on the food itself.)
- **Bison.** Roughly **30 million** on the Plains in the early 1800s (the pre-collapse baseline; pre-contact estimates are less certain). Carried from the verified `native-nations` bank (Environment & Society Portal; Smithsonian; NPS "Where the Buffalo Roamed").
- **Beaver.** Population before the fur trade: estimates run **as high as 400 million** across North America (commonly quoted range 60–400 million — these are estimates, not counts; state as such). Beaver dams pond water, slow floods, and build wet meadows — an animal that engineers watersheds. (Sources: Northwest Power & Conservation Council "Beavers"; Hudson River Valley Institute fur-trade paper; nature.org/NWF summaries.)
- **Calusa watercourts** (engineered live-fish storage pens, built 1300–1400, southwest Florida) — carried from `native-nations` bank (NPS; Florida Museum). Use here only as evidence of engineered land/water management; `native-nations` leads on the Calusa.
- **No named individual survives from this era at story zoom.** The book does not invent people; the era carries wide and span zooms only (same solution as `native-nations` era 1).

## Era 02 — The 1500s: the Columbian Exchange remakes the land

- **Pigs.** Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay in **May 1539** with about 600 men and a herd of pigs — commonly given as **13 pigs**, grown to about **700** by his death in 1542 (not counting pigs eaten, given away, or escaped; escapees became the ancestors of feral hogs in the Southeast). (Sources: Texas Parks & Wildlife "Wild Pigs"; Ohio State Univ. animal-science note; World History Encyclopedia on the expedition. The 13→700 figures come from expedition chronicles — label as chronicle figures.)
- **Horses and cattle** returned/arrived with the Spanish (horses on Columbus's second voyage 1493; New Mexico colony 1598). The horse-spread dispute (traditional post-1680 account vs. the 2023 *Science* early-1600s finding) is documented in the `native-nations` bank — that chapter leads on what nations did with horses; this chapter only notes grazing animals as a new force on the land.
- **Earthworms.** The glaciers had scraped northern North America bare of earthworms; the forests that regrew after the ice (roughly the last 12,000 years) ran on a thick spongy leaf-litter "duff" layer with **no earthworms in it**. European worms arrived hidden in ship ballast soil and the root balls of imported plants, then spread west with wagons, horses, and farming. Where they invade northern forests they eat the duff layer that native wildflowers and seedlings grow in. (Sources: Minnesota DNR "Earthworms"; National Wildlife Federation "Too Much Wiggle Room," 2021; EarthSky/Smithsonian coverage.)
- **The Great Dying regrew forests — one research team's estimate.** Koch et al. 2019 (*Quaternary Science Reviews*, University College London): epidemics, war, and famine after 1492 dropped the Indigenous population of the Americas from an estimated 60 million to about 6 million within roughly a century; abandoned farmland regrew — about **55.8 million hectares** of reforestation — pulling enough carbon from the air (atmospheric CO2 fell 7–10 ppm) to contribute an estimated ~0.15°C of global cooling, possibly deepening the Little Ice Age. **Label clearly as one study's estimate; the starting population is itself disputed (the `native-nations` bank carries the 1–18 million North-of-Mexico range).** (Sources: UCL press release Feb 2019; ScienceDirect abstract.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242e): who carried the diseases and made the wars after 1492 (Koch et al. 2019, the paper's own words)

- **Who brought the diseases.** Koch, Brierley, Maslin and Lewis, "Earth system impacts of the European arrival and Great Dying in the Americas after 1492," *Quaternary Science Reviews* 207 (2019), pp. 13-36 (open-access text, UCL Discovery 10068488): "The arrival of Europeans in the Americas in 1492 CE marks the onset of disease epidemics resulting in the loss of the majority of indigenous people living in the Americas over the subsequent century." The epidemics "were introduced by European settlers and African slaves and were passed on to an indigenous population that had not been previously exposed to these pathogens." The diseases named: "smallpox, measles, influenza, the bubonic plague, and later malaria, diphtheria, typhus and cholera." (African slaves here means enslaved Africans whom Europeans carried to the Americas. The paper uses the words "African slaves.")
- **The wars and the rest.** Same paper: "Direct effects of the European conquest included warfare and the exploitation of indigenous populations ... including their forced relocation of people and slavery." It also names famine "from crop failure due to epidemic-driven labor shortages." It counts "warfare, the enslavement of indigenous people, and hunger" as causes of "even larger population losses," but its model uses the epidemics "as the main driver." (Koch et al. 2019.)
- **Use:** the existing bullet's "epidemics, war, and famine after 1492" can now name the actors: European settlers and enslaved Africans carried the diseases (the Africans carried them without choosing to travel), and European conquerors made the wars and enslaved Native people.

## Era 03 — The 1600s: two ideas of land meet

- **The fur trade and the beaver.** European hat-makers wanted beaver felt; the trade ran on Native trappers and European goods. From the pre-contact estimate (up to ~400 million), the long decline began in the Northeast in this century and ran until beaver were nearly gone from the continent by 1900 (**about 100,000 left**, mostly in Canada). Recovery today: roughly **15 million**. (Sources: NW Power & Conservation Council; Hudson River Valley Institute; inkl/AP-style summaries — the 1900 and today figures are widely repeated estimates; label as estimates.)
- **Property in land.** English colonists brought fixed boundaries, fences, deeds, and livestock; Native nations used land seasonally (planting grounds, fishing stations, hunting territories) without treating it as a permanent exclusive possession. Free-ranging colonial livestock in Native planting fields was a running cause of conflict. Scholarly anchor: William Cronon, *Changes in the Land* (1983) — the standard history of this collision in New England. (Named as scholarship, not quoted.)
- **Miantonomi's recorded complaint, 1642.** The Narragansett sachem Miantonomi (c. 1600–1643), speaking to the Montaukett on Long Island in 1642, as recorded by the English soldier Lion Gardener (written down in his *Relation*, 1660): "our fathers had plenty of deer and skins and our plains were full of game and turkeys" — but the English "with scythes cut down the grass, and with axes fell the trees; their cows and horses eat the grass, and their hogs spoil our clam banks, and we shall all be starved." An environmental observation set down within one generation of settlement. **Caveat: recorded by an Englishman years after the fact — the wording is Gardener's; historians treat the substance as credible but the exact words as filtered.** Miantonomi was executed in 1643 after capture by the Mohegans (with Connecticut's approval). (Sources: Plimoth Patuxet Museums excerpt; Rhode Tour (RI Council for the Humanities); Oxford primary-source collections; Wikipedia "Miantonomoh" cross-checked for dates.)
- **First colonial limits on taking.** Plymouth Colony ordinance, **1626**: no cutting or selling timber off colony land without official consent — among the earliest conservation-type laws in the colonies. William Penn's rules for Pennsylvania, **1681**: leave **one acre of trees for every five acres cleared** (oaks and mulberries named, for silk and shipping) — widely disregarded in practice. (Source: Forest History Society, "In Colonial Days 1607–1782.")

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242e): Miantonomi's killing, who decided it and who did it (with a correction note)

- **Correction note on the bank line "executed in 1643 after capture by the Mohegans (with Connecticut's approval)."** The approval came from the **commissioners of the United Colonies of New England**, the joint council of Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut and New Haven, and not from Connecticut alone. Uncas, sachem of the Mohegans, captured Miantonomi in 1643 and brought him to Hartford. The commissioners, meeting at Boston in September 1643, referred the case to a group of ministers (clergymen), then sentenced him to death at the hands of Uncas. (Encyclopaedia Britannica 1911, "Miantonomo," via Wikisource; Wikipedia "Miantonomoh," cross-checked; Rhode Tour, Rhode Island Council for the Humanities, "Canonicus and Miantonomi.") Governor John Winthrop's *Journal* for 1643 records of the commissioners that "they all agreed that he ought to be put to death," because "Uncas could not be safe while Miantonomo lived." (Winthrop's *Journal*, quoted in Robert A. Geake, "Uncas, Miantonomo, and Historical Memory," rifootprints, 2011.)
- **Who killed him and how.** Mohegan men took Miantonomi back to the ground near present-day Norwich, Connecticut, where he had been captured. Wawequa, Uncas's brother, killed him with a hatchet (tomahawk) blow to the head. Benjamin Trumbull, *History of Connecticut* (1797): "one of Uncas' men, who marched behind Miantonomoh, split his head with a hatchet, killing him with a single stroke." The name Wawequa comes from Mohegan tradition and later histories (Rhode Tour; Britannica 1911). Historian Neal Salisbury writes that "several Englishmen would accompany the party to see that the execution was actually carried out" (quoted in Geake 2011). Rhode Tour says the commissioners' return of Miantonomi to the Mohegans "was likely a strategy to pit Native communities against each other."
- **Not for use:** Trumbull (1797) adds that Uncas cut and ate a piece of Miantonomi's shoulder. That claim comes from one writer 150 years later, and no source checked here confirms it. Do not use it.

## Era 04 — 1700 to 1750: taking more than the land replaces

- **The King's pines.** The Massachusetts Bay charter of **1691** reserved large white pines (24 inches diameter and up) for the Royal Navy's masts; the **White Pine Act of 1722** made it illegal in New Hampshire (and neighboring colonies) to cut white pines over **12 inches** without license. Marked with the King's **Broad Arrow** (three hatchet slashes). Enforcement was loose until the 1760s. (Sources: Forest History Society colonial-era history; NH PBS NatureWorks; AmericanRevolution.org "Pine Tree Riot" background. The 1691/24-inch and 1722/12-inch figures appear consistently across these.)
- **Tobacco wears out the soil.** A tobacco field gave good crops for only **three to four years**; planters then cleared new ground and let old fields go to scrub. Land around Jamestown was depleted early; planters kept moving up the rivers — the Rappahannock and Potomac by about 1650 and onward through the 1700s. Cheap new land made exhaustion a habit, not a crisis. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "Tobacco in Colonial Virginia"; virginiaplaces.org; NPS "Rise of the Colonial Plantation System.")
- **Whaling begins.** New England shore whaling (right whales from beaches) came first; Nantucket's deep-sea sperm whaling **by tradition began in 1712**, when Christopher Hussey's boat was blown offshore and killed a sperm whale — the story was first written down in Obed Macy's 1835 history and may be legend; the Nantucket Historical Association treats the date as traditional. What is certain: by the mid-1700s Nantucket ran a deep-ocean sperm-whale fishery. (Sources: Nantucket Historical Association "When Did Nantucketers Begin Whaling?"; New Bedford Whaling Museum "Yankee Whaling"; Smithsonian magazine.)
- No documented individual found at story zoom for this era from this chapter's angle (the whaling crews are `work-workers`/`economy` material when named; planters' account books exist but no single verified land-story stood out). Era marked thin, honestly.

## Era 05 — 1750 to 1800: the land measured, divided, and sold

- **The Pine Tree Riot, April 14, 1772, Weare, New Hampshire.** Sawmill owner **Ebenezer Mudgett** was fined for cutting the King's white pines. On April 13 Sheriff Benjamin Whiting rode into South Weare with an arrest warrant; Mudgett said he would pay in the morning. At dawn he returned with 20–30 men, faces blackened with soot; they beat the sheriff and his deputy with rods and drove them out of town. Rioters were later fined lightly (20 shillings). Some historians credit the riot as a forerunner of the Boston Tea Party (Dec 1773) — label as "some credit," not established causation. After it, the mast law went largely unenforced. (Sources: Weare Historical Society; New England Historical Society; Journal of the American Revolution (allthingsliberty.com); AmericanRevolution.org. The sheriff's first name appears as Benjamin in most accounts.)
- **The Land Ordinance of May 20, 1785.** The Confederation Congress ordered the public lands surveyed **before sale** into a rectangular grid: **townships six miles square**, each of **36 sections of one square mile (640 acres)**, numbered, with **section 16 reserved to support public schools**. The system (refined 1796) carried the grid across the continent to the Pacific; the checkerboard is still visible from any airplane window over the Midwest. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia primary document; Indiana Historical Bureau; encyclopedia.com; National Archives context.)
- Independence voided the Broad Arrow reservations; clearing accelerated as settlement crossed the Appalachians (Northwest Territory opened by the 1785–87 ordinances — the settlers are `migration`'s story; the grid itself is ours).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): whose land the 1785 grid was laid on, and how it was taken

Source for all of this: George W. Knepper, *The Official Ohio Lands Book* (Ohio Auditor of State), pp. 3-10.
- **Who owned it first.** In 1785 the land north and west of the Ohio River was the home of the Shawnee, Delaware, Wyandot, Miami, Ottawa, Chippewa, Potawatomi and others. To Congress, "Indian claims to that land had to be cleared."
- **Treaty of Fort McIntosh, January 1785.** U.S. commissioners met representatives of several nations at Fort McIntosh (1784-85) and concluded a treaty confining most Ohio Indians to a reserve between the Cuyahoga and Maumee rivers. "Most Ohio lands would now be open for settlement." The Shawnee especially "refused to abide by a treaty negotiated under duress by minor tribal chiefs who lacked authority to speak for their people." In 1786 U.S. commissioners "coerced" the Shawnee into another treaty at Fort Finney. (Knepper.)
- **The first survey.** On **September 30, 1785**, Thomas Hutchins, Geographer of the United States, and his crew began the first federal survey, the **Seven Ranges** in eastern Ohio. They ran the Geographer's Line 42 miles west from Pennsylvania and cut it into ranges, townships and sections, with section 16 kept for schools. Surveyors worked from Fort McIntosh, "guarded by its troops" (Beaver Area Heritage Foundation, "Treaty of Fort McIntosh," via search). The land was surveyed for sale while the nations who lived on it still rejected the treaty. (Knepper.)
- **The war that settled it.** The nations formed a confederacy to stop settlement. In 1790 Maj. Gen. Josiah Harmar led an army against them and lost to warriors led by the Miami war chief **Little Turtle (Meshekinoquah)**. In 1791 Maj. Gen. Arthur St. Clair, governor of the Northwest Territory, led "the worst defeat ever inflicted upon the U.S. Army by Indian warriors" (Knepper; the place later called Fort Recovery). In **1794** Maj. Gen. **Anthony Wayne** defeated the confederacy at **Fallen Timbers**. (Knepper.)
- **Treaty of Greenville, August 3, 1795.** Eleven northwestern nations ceded **16,930,417 acres** (Knepper's table, "Main Treaties Ceding Indian Lands in Ohio"). The Greenville Treaty Line confined them to Ohio's northwest quarter and beyond. Further Ohio cessions followed: Fort Industry, 1805 (2,726,812 acres); Detroit, 1807 (345,600); Fort Meigs, 1817 (4,554,459); St. Marys, 1818 (three treaties). (Knepper.)
- **For the writer:** the bank's line that the grid ignored "everyone already living there" now has a mechanism. Commissioners took treaties signed under duress, surveyors worked under army guard, and the army defeated the nations who refused. Settlers' journeys stay in `migration`, and the nations' side of the war is `native-nations`' and `war`'s. This chapter needs only the chain: treaty, survey, war, cession, grid.

## Era 06 — 1800 to 1850: rivers put to work, forests at low ebb, first protests

- **Dams stop the fish.** The first dam across the Connecticut River (Turners Falls, Mass., **1798**) blocked salmon from their spawning water; **by the War of 1812 no salmon entered the river**. The **Great Stone Dam** at Lawrence, Mass. (built 1847–48) walled off the Merrimack **30 miles from the sea**. Atlantic salmon runs were gone from southern New England rivers **by 1865**; shad collapsed too. A New Hampshire report of 1857 blamed dams, unregulated harvest, and mill machinery killing young fish. (Sources: NOAA Fisheries "Industry vs Diadromy: the Merrimack"; NH Fish & Game "Restoring Anadromous Fish"; USFWS Atlantic salmon history.)
- **Eastern deforestation peaks.** By 1850, **estimates for New England run from about 60% to 80% cleared** (some local areas higher; one commonly quoted lower figure says as little as 25% remained forested — the estimates genuinely vary; state the range). One driver with a number: the Merino **sheep boom** — about **4 million sheep in New England by 1840**. Forest cover across the eastern U.S. bottomed out around **1872** at roughly **half** its 1620 extent; afterward farm abandonment for the West let forests regrow. (Sources: Forest Society (NH) "Keeping Forests as Forests"; *PLOS One* "Four Centuries of Change in Northeastern United States Forests," 2013; Inside Climate News 2026 — journalism.)
- **George Catlin proposes "a nation's park," 1832.** The painter, traveling the upper Missouri and fearing the destruction of the bison and the Plains nations together, wrote for "a magnificent park … a nation's park, containing man and beast, in all the wild and freshness of their nature's beauty." Forty years before Yellowstone. (Sources: NPS park-history online books (Runte); Buffalo Bill Center of the West.)
- **John James Audubon** (1785–1851). *The Birds of America* — **435 life-size bird portraits**, published 1827–1838, about 18 years of work behind it. His 1813 passenger-pigeon account: riding to Louisville, Kentucky, he passed under a single flock that darkened the sky for **three days**; his own arithmetic put one flock above **1 billion birds**. Documentary: ***John James Audubon: Drawn from Nature* (PBS American Masters, 2007, dir. Lawrence Hott)** — a person-doc, verified. (Sources: Audubon.org; Smithsonian; IMDb/PBS for the film.)
- **Henry David Thoreau** (1817–1862). Lived in a self-built cabin at **Walden Pond, Concord, Mass., July 4, 1845 – September 6, 1847** (two years, two months, two days); *Walden* published **1854** (publication date falls in era 7 — state the residency here, note the book year honestly). Worked as a land surveyor — the man who argued for wild land measured woodlots for a living. No widely established person-documentary verified; Movie line omitted. (Sources: Britannica "Walden"; Walden Woods Project.)

## Era 07 — 1850 to 1900: the biggest taking, and the first protection

- **The bison slaughter.** Baseline roughly **30 million** (early 1800s) → **fewer than 1,000 by 1900**; one 1884 count found **325**. Industrial hide-hunting exploded after the transcontinental railroad (1869) gave hunters access and shipping; the Army encouraged the kill because the herds fed the Plains nations. Today about **420,000 bison in commercial herds** and roughly **20,500 in conservation herds** (~500,000 total; figures vary by count — state as rounded). Carried from the verified `native-nations` bank (Environment & Society Portal; Smithsonian; NPS) plus USFWS "Plains Bison" and National Bison Association for today's numbers. **`native-nations` tells what the collapse did to the nations; we tell the animal and the grassland.**
- **Frank H. Mayer**, buffalo runner **1872–1878**. His memoir *The Buffalo Harvest* (told at about age 100 to Charles B. Roth; published 1958, four years after Mayer died at Fairplay, Colorado in February 1954, aged 104) describes the arithmetic of the trade — rifle, wagons, skinners, hides at a dollar or two each — and ends in regret at what the runners did. **Caveats: a memoir told in extreme old age; his numbers are his own; even his age has been questioned. Use as a documented voice, flag the reliability.** (Sources: Denver Public Library holds a Frank H. Mayer oral history collection; Goodreads/Google Books for the book; The Flume (Park County, CO newspaper) profile — journalism.)
- **The pigeon collapse.** Peak population estimated **3–5 billion** — possibly 4 of every 10 birds in North America. The **1871 Wisconsin nesting**: an estimated **136 million breeding birds over about 850 square miles**. The **1878 Petoskey, Michigan** nesting — one of the last great ones — was worked by professional netters and shooters at a reported **50,000 birds a day for months**, the kill shipped to cities by rail; the telegraph told hunters where every big nesting was. Wild flocks effectively gone by the mid-1890s. (Sources: Smithsonian "Once There Were Billions"; Wisconsin Historical Society / PBS Wisconsin; Audubon magazine "Why the Passenger Pigeon Went Extinct.")
- **Yosemite Grant, June 30, 1864** — Lincoln signed the first federal set-aside of land for preservation and public use (granted to California to manage; Yosemite became a national park October 1, 1890). **Yellowstone, March 1, 1872** — Grant signed the act making it **the first national park**, run by the federal government, "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." (Sources: National Archives milestone documents; NPS "Birth of a National Park"; Library of Congress research guide.)
- **George Perkins Marsh, *Man and Nature* (1864)** — the Vermont scholar-diplomat's argument that civilizations fall when they destroy their forests and soils; influenced the **Forest Reserve Act of 1891**, which let presidents set aside public forest land (the start of the national forests). (Sources: NPS "George Perkins Marsh"; Biodiversity Heritage Library; Forest History Society.)
- **John Muir** (1838–1914). ### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): who killed the bison, and the officials' and officers' own words

Fills the unnamed actor in "the Army encouraged the kill." Main scholarly source: David D. Smits, "The Frontier Army and the Destruction of the Buffalo: 1865-1883," *Western Historical Quarterly* 25:3 (Autumn 1994), pp. 312-338, which cites each quotation below to its archive. Quotations are given as Smits prints them.
- **The dispute, stated first.** Historians agree that army commanders encouraged the hide hunters. They disagree on whether the army had an official extermination policy. Robert M. Utley (*Frontier Regulars*, 1973): there "was never any such policy," but "both civil and military officials concerned with the Indian problem applauded the slaughter, for they correctly perceived it a crucial factor that would force the Indian onto the reservation." Robert Wooster (1988): the army "did not make the virtual extermination of the American bison part of its official policy." Smits (1994) argues for a deliberate, mostly unwritten policy. William A. Dobak answered Smits in "The Army and the Buffalo: A Demur," *WHQ* 26:2 (May 1995), disputing how much the army caused. **No written order to exterminate the buffalo has been found** (Smits). The hide market was the engine. What officials said and did is documented, as below.
- **Who did the killing.** Commercial hide hunters, who called themselves "buffalo runners," did most of it. In 1871 a Pennsylvania tannery found a way to turn buffalo hides into commercial leather, used for harnesses and machine belts. Each hide sold for **$1 to $3**. The Kansas Pacific and the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe railroads carried hides east. (Smits 1994.)
- **The count, 1872-1874, southern Plains.** Lt. Col. Richard Irving Dodge estimated that the three railroads carried **1,378,389 hides** in those three years. He counted 459,463 on the Santa Fe line and assumed each of the other two lines carried as many, so the total is itself an estimate. Dodge reckoned that before 1874 hunters wasted so many hides that one hide shipped meant about three buffalo killed. His totals, as reported by William T. Hornaday (Smithsonian, *The Extermination of the American Bison*, 1889): **3,158,730 killed by white hunters** and about **1,215,000 by Native hunters**, **4,373,730** in all. These are Dodge's estimates, not counts. (Source: Hornaday 1889 as summarized in *American Heritage*, Aug. 1956, "How They Killed the Buffalo.")
- **General William T. Sherman** (commanding general of the Army from 1869), letter to General Philip Sheridan, **May 10, 1868**: "as long as Buffalo are up on the Republican the Indians will go there. I think it would be wise to invite all the sportsmen of England and America there this fall for a Grand Buffalo hunt, and make one grand sweep of them all. Until the Buffalo and consequent[ly] Indians are out [from between] the Roads we will have collisions and trouble." (Sheridan Papers, Library of Congress, reel 17, via Smits.) The *Army and Navy Journal*, **June 26, 1869**, reported that Sherman said "the quickest way to compel the Indians to settle down to civilized life was to send ten regiments of soldiers to the plains, with orders to shoot buffaloes until they became too scarce to support the redskins." (Via Smits.)
- **General Philip H. Sheridan**, letter to Sherman, **October 15, 1868**: "The best way for the government is to now make them poor by the destruction of their stock, and then settle them on the lands allotted to them." By "stock" he meant their buffalo and horses (Smits). Lt. Col. Luther P. Bradley's journal, September 13, 1868, records his orders: "to kill all the buffalo we find, and drive the Arapahoes and Cheyennes south, and the Sioux north." (Via Smits.)
- **The Sheridan "medal" speech, 1875. DISPUTED; label it.** Texas legislators introduced a buffalo-protection bill in 1875. The Texas State Historical Association states that Sheridan protested it, "contending that peace with the Indians could be maintained only if their food supply was eliminated," and that "the bill failed to pass" (TSHA Handbook, "Buffalo"). The famous words come from one source only: the former hide hunter John R. Cook, *The Border and the Buffalo* (1907), pp. 163-64, written about 32 years later. Cook says Sheridan told the legislators to give each hunter "a medal of bronze, with a dead buffalo on one side and a discouraged Indian on the other," and said: "let them kill, skin, and sell until the buffaloes are exterminated." **No official record of Sheridan speaking to the legislature has been found** (Smits). Smits judges Cook "credible." *Harper's* (Christopher Ketcham, June 2008, journalism) calls the speech "believed to be apocryphal." A writer may quote it only as what Cook wrote that Sheridan said.
- **Lt. Col. Richard Irving Dodge**, 1867, to the Englishman W. F. Butler, who had apologized for killing over thirty bulls: "Kill every buffalo you can! Every buffalo dead is an Indian gone." (Butler's autobiography, 1911, p. 97, via Smits. A second-hand record, written down by Butler decades later.) In 1873 hide hunters asked Dodge, then commanding Fort Dodge, what he would do if they hunted south into Texas, on hunting grounds the 1867 Medicine Lodge Treaty had reserved for the Kiowa, Comanche, Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho. Hunter J. Wright Mooar recalled Dodge's answer: "Boys, if I were a buffalo hunter I would hunt buffalo where buffalo are." (Mooar, 1930, via Smits.) Dodge also described Kansas in fall 1873: "Where there were myriads of buffalo the year before, there were now myriads of carcasses. The air was foul with a sickening stench."
- **Secretary of the Interior Columbus Delano**, annual report for 1873 (primary text on Wikisource, "Report of the Secretary of the Interior/1873"): "While I would not seriously regret the total disappearance of the buffalo from our western prairies, in its effect on the Indians, regarding it rather as a means of hastening their sense of dependence upon the products of the soil and their own labors, yet these encroachments by the whites upon the reservations set apart for the exclusive occupancy of the Indian is one prolific source of trouble." In 1871 Delano refused a Sioux delegation's request to keep white hunters out of Sioux country (Smits).
- **President Ulysses S. Grant, 1874.** Both houses of Congress passed a bill making it unlawful for any non-Indian to kill a female buffalo, or more males than needed for food, with a **$100 fine per animal**. Grant let it die unsigned, a **pocket veto** (a bill dies when Congress adjourns and the president has not signed it). (Smits, citing Hornaday 1889 and *Congressional Record*, 43d Cong., 1st sess., 1874.) Grant had also promised a Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho delegation that hunters would be kept out of their country. Their agent John D. Miles wrote on **September 30, 1874** that "they looked in vain." (National Archives, via Smits.)
- **Free army ammunition.** Frank H. Mayer (already banked above) in *The Buffalo Harvest* (1958), pp. 29-30: "army officers in charge of plains operations encouraged the slaughter of buffalo in every possible way ... ammunition, free ammunition, all you could use ... All you had to do to get it was apply at any frontier army post ... I received thousands of rounds in this way." Mayer quotes an unnamed "high ranking officer": "either the buffalo or the Indian must go ... if we kill the buffalo we conquer the Indian." (Unnamed in the source. Do not name him.) Army posts (Forts Griffin, Dodge, Concho, Richardson) also gave hunters protection, supplies and shipping. After the 1874 attack on the hunters' post at Adobe Walls, three cavalry companies from Fort Dodge escorted the hunters back to Dodge City. (Smits.)
- **Col. Nelson A. Miles**, commanding in the Red River War (1874-75), December 1874 plan: "I would leave as few Indians or Buffalo behind, and as little for them to subsist on as possible." His memoir (1896): "the buffalo, like the Indian, stood in the way of civilization and in the path of progress, and the decree had gone forth that they must both give way." Richard Henry Pratt (later founder of Carlisle Indian School) recalled in retirement that "the general destruction of the buffalo was ordered as a military measure." (Via Smits. Pratt's is a late recollection; no written order survives.)
- **A Cheyenne leader's words.** Col. Edward Wynkoop, former Cheyenne agent, reported to Henry Bergh of the ASPCA (1872) that Little Robe, a Cheyenne chief, told him: "What do you think my people ought to do when they see their cattle, the buffaloes, killed by your race when they are not hungry?" (*Congressional Globe*, 42d Cong., 2d sess., 1872, via Smits. Second-hand, recorded by Wynkoop.)
- **Officers who opposed it.** Col. William B. Hazen to Bergh, January 20, 1872: "The theory that the buffalo should be killed to deprive the Indians of food is a fallacy ... wicked and wanton waste." (Via Smits.) Useful so the prose does not say "every officer."

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): Native people driven from the land that became Yosemite and Yellowstone

The bank tells the parks as "set aside for the people." These are the people who were already there, who removed them and how. Glacier is under era 08, Yosemite's last village under era 09.
- **Yosemite, 1851: the Mariposa Battalion.** California Governor John McDougal issued proclamations calling for volunteers after attacks on miners and traders. The volunteers formed the Mariposa Battalion: about **200 mounted men**, mustered **January 24, 1851**, commanded by **Major James D. Savage**, a trader. The state supplied camp gear and supply trains, and federal money paid for the battalion under the U.S. Indian commissioners. (Primary: Lafayette H. Bunnell, a battalion member, *Discovery of the Yosemite and the Indian War of 1851*, 1880, text at archive.org "discoveryofyosem00bunn.") The NPS dates the battalion's first entry into Yosemite Valley to **March 27, 1851**. NPS: the battalion "systematically burned villages and food supplies and forced men, women, and children away from their homes." It marched Tenaya, headman of the Ahwahneechee, and his people to a reservation on the Fresno River. (NPS Yosemite, "Destruction and Disruption"; NPS Yosemite timeline, via search; Bunnell.)
- **The food they burned.** Bunnell records that in Captain John Boling's company, footmen were detailed "to burn any caches they might find." He writes that the huts and "adjacent supplies of acorns, were at once given to the flames," and estimates the acorns destroyed "at from eight hundred to one thousand bushels," plus pine nuts (Bunnell, pp. 128-30; this passage is from the upper San Joaquin, next to Yosemite). Acorns were the people's main winter food (NPS "Their Lifeways"). In Yosemite Valley Bunnell found "nearly all the acorns had been burnt." (Bunnell, pp. 143-44.)
- **Tenaya's youngest son, killed 1851.** Primary account, Bunnell, pp. 164-66. Battalion men held Tenaya's youngest son and another young man tied to a tree as hostages. The guards let the two untie themselves "in order to fire upon them, expecting to kill them both." One guard shot Tenaya's son in the back. Bunnell found "the dead body of old Ten-e-ya's youngest son. The warm blood still oozing from a wound in his back." The other young man escaped. **Captain John Boling told the guards "that they had committed murder."** Bunnell: "no punishment was ever inflicted upon the perpetrators." The shooter is described only as "a young man" influenced by "an old Texan." **Bunnell names neither. Do not name them.** Tenaya himself died in 1853 in a fight with Mono Lake Paiutes (NPS; accounts of the cause differ).
- **The count of the dead and missing.** NPS: "By 1910, over 90% of the original Ahwahneechee inhabitants were dead or missing." (NPS Yosemite, "Surviving Communities.") A survivor who witnessed it: **To Tu Ya (Maria Lebrado Ydrte)**, called by the NPS "the last survivor of the Mariposa Battalion's 1851 raid." As a child she saw "the invasion and destruction of her home in Yosemite Valley and the murder of her uncle." She returned to Yosemite in the late 1920s and told her story. (NPS "Destruction and Disruption.") Named, documented, and usable at story zoom if a writer wants a Native voice for this era.
- **The Yosemite Grant (1864) and park (1890)** let the managers remove people who built homes without permission. NPCA: the 1890 park came "with authorization to remove anyone making homes without permission." (NPCA, "Reclaiming Wahhoga," 2025/26, advocacy group, label.) Ahwahneechee families came back and lived in the valley anyway, working for the hotels and park.
- **Yellowstone, 1872 onward.** Before the park the Tukudika ("Sheep Eaters"), a band of Mountain Shoshone, "lived for thousands of years in the area that would become Yellowstone National Park." They "were forcibly removed to reservations" (NPS Yellowstone, "The Tukudika Indians"). NPS: "Early park superintendents actively discouraged tribes from visiting the newly established park to hunt or collect resources." (NPS "Historic Tribes.") Tribes with ties to the park land include the Shoshone, Bannock, Crow, Blackfeet and others (NPS "Associated Tribes").
- **Superintendent Philetus W. Norris (1877-1882)** did the removing. In fall 1879 he found a recently left Tukudika camp near Bunsen Peak. Historian Mark David Spence (*Dispossessing the Wilderness: Indian Removal and the Making of the National Parks*, Oxford, 1999): "Norris turned for help to the agent at Fort Washakie, who responded by sending a party of Shoshone to escort the Tukudika to new homes on the Wind River Reservation." E. S. Topping (1888): "In 1879 some Shoshones were sent out by the interior department to hunt them up. They were found and brought in to the Shoshone Agency at Little Wind River." In spring 1880 Norris met in Washington with a Crow delegation that was being asked to give up 1.6 million acres, and he got a "solemn promise" from people at the Lemhi agency and the Wind River Reservation to stay out of the park. (All via John Clayton, *Mountain Journal*, March 10, 2024, journalism, quoting Spence and Topping.) In his reports Norris called the Tukudika a "pigmy tribe" and "feeble and harmless," and wrote that Native people avoided the geysers out of "superstitious awe." Historians and Shoshone scholars treat that claim as false. It helped support the story that the park had been empty. (Clayton 2024; Norris's reports, 1877-1881.)
- **Ward v. Race Horse, 1896.** Race Horse, a Bannock man, hunted elk in Wyoming under the 1868 Fort Bridger Treaty's right to hunt on "unoccupied lands." Wyoming arrested him. The U.S. Supreme Court (Justice Edward Douglass White writing) ruled for Wyoming. It called the treaty right "temporary and precarious" and held that statehood ended it. The ruling made treaty hunting in and around Yellowstone a crime under state law. The Court overruled it in **Herrera v. Wyoming, May 20, 2019**, holding that Wyoming statehood did not end the Crow treaty hunting right. (Sources: Supreme Court opinion 17-532, Herrera v. Wyoming, 2019; WyoHistory.org "Who gets to hunt Wyoming's elk?"; Wikipedia "Ward v. Race Horse," cross-checked.)
- **The pattern, in one line a writer can use:** George Catlin's 1832 "nation's park" was to contain "man and beast" (era 06). The parks that were built removed the people. (Spence 1999 is the standard scholarly account.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): the great taking, how the land passed from Native nations to "public land" to private owners

- **The total.** From 1776 to 1887 the U.S. government took **over 1.5 billion acres** from Native nations by treaty and executive order. Historian Claudio Saunt (University of Georgia) counts this in his map *The Invasion of America*, built on Charles C. Royce's *Indian Land Cessions in the United States* (Bureau of American Ethnology, 18th Annual Report, part 2, 1899), which maps every cession from 1784 to 1894 in 67 maps (Library of Congress; Saunt, *Aeon*, "How were 1.5 billion acres of land so rapidly stolen?"). Royce's maps are "the foundation of cartographic testimony in Indian land claims litigation" (search summary of LOC/Wikipedia). One scholar's count from Royce, so attribute it.
- **The railroads, 1862.** President Lincoln signed the **Pacific Railway Act on July 1, 1862**. It gave the railroad companies "every alternate section of public land, designated by odd numbers, to the amount of five alternate sections per mile on each side of said railroad." It also ordered: "The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." (National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Pacific Railway Act.") From 1850 to 1871 federal grants to railroads came to about **129 to 131 million acres** (sources differ, and some count up to 175 million including grants later forfeited). States granted about 51 million more. The Northern Pacific's grant alone was about 44 million acres. (Encyclopedia.com "Railroads, Federal Land Grants to"; historian John Stover as cited there; Wikipedia "Railroad land grants in the United States," cross-checked. Record the range.)
- **The Homestead Act, May 20, 1862.** Lincoln signed it. Over its life it moved **270 million acres, 10 percent of the area of the United States**, from public land to private individuals in claims of up to 160 acres, to a claimant who lived on or farmed the land for five years (fees not banked; do not call it free without a source) (NPS "Homestead Act of 1862" transcription; NPS, "The Homestead Act"; NPS Homestead National Historical Park). NPS: much of the land offered for homesteading "was seized from the Native Americans who previously inhabited it," and "this 'free' land came at a great cost to Native Americans." (NPS Homestead NHP, "Native Americans and the Homestead Act," via search summary.) `migration` owns the settlers. This chapter states whose land it was.
- **The Morrill Act, July 2, 1862 (the land-grant colleges).** The act gave each state 30,000 acres of public land "for each senator and representative in Congress" to sell for colleges of "agriculture and the mechanic arts." The National Archives now notes that it "resulted in the expropriation of over 10 million acres from Native American tribal lands" (National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Morrill Act"). *High Country News* (Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone, "Land-grab universities," March 30, 2020, investigative journalism) traced **nearly 11 million acres (10.7 million)** of it to land taken from **nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities** through **more than 160 "violence-backed" cessions**. The government paid **less than $400,000** in all to end Native title to it. The land raised **$17.7 million** for 52 university endowments by the early 1900s. Universities still held **over 500,000 acres** in 2020, earning **$5.4 million** in fiscal 2019. Example: in summer 1851 four Dakota bands signed treaties at Mendota and Traverse des Sioux, Minnesota, under "withholding of rations, the threat of violence, enforced starvation." They received "less than 2.4¢ per acre." The University of Minnesota's share of the resulting land sales was "nearly $580,000." (HCN 2020.)
- **Allotment (carried from the verified `native-nations` bank and confirmed by NPS).** The Dawes Act (1887) cut reservations into 160-acre family farms or 320-acre grazing plots and sold off the "surplus." "Reservation lands went from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres in 1934! That is a loss of 65 percent" (NPS Homestead NHP, "Native Americans and the Homestead Act"). The Meriam Report gives 137 million to 47 million. The loss is about 90 million acres either way (`native-nations` bank).
- **Ownership:** `native-nations` leads on dispossession as the nations lived it. This chapter tells the land's side, turning people's homelands into survey squares, railroad sections, homesteads and college endowments. One or two numbers per era are enough. Do not re-tell the wars.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): California makes Native burning a crime, 1850 (source for "outlawed")

## Era 08 — 1900 to 1950: conservation becomes federal work; the Plains show the cost of failure

- **Theodore Roosevelt's record, 1901–1909:** about **230 million acres** put under federal protection — **150 national forests, 5 national parks, the first 18 national monuments, 51 federal bird reservations, 4 national game preserves** (some sources say 55 bird reservations and game preserves combined — counts vary slightly by source; state "about"). **Pelican Island, Florida, March 14, 1903** — first federal bird reservation, the seed of the National Wildlife Refuge System. **U.S. Forest Service created 1905**, Gifford Pinchot first chief. **Antiquities Act signed June 8, 1906**; first monument **Devils Tower, September 24, 1906**. The **1903 Yosemite camping trip**: Roosevelt spent three nights in the open with John Muir (under the Grizzly Giant sequoia, in a snowstorm near Sentinel Dome, near Bridalveil Fall). Documentary: ***The Roosevelts: An Intimate History* (PBS, Ken Burns, 2014)** — about the family (TR, FDR, Eleanor); label as such. (Sources: USFWS "The Conservation President"; Theodore Roosevelt Association; NPS; PBS.)
- **Gifford Pinchot** (1865–1946). First American-trained professional forester; first chief of the Forest Service (1905–1910, fired by Taft in 1910 in the Ballinger dispute); later twice governor of Pennsylvania. His definition of conservation: "the greatest good to the greatest number for the longest time" — **use the land carefully forever** vs. Muir's **leave the best of it wild**. The two were friends before Hetch Hetchy split them. Documentary: ***Seeking the Greatest Good* (WVIA/PBS, 2013)** — about Pinchot's conservation legacy; verified. (Sources: Forest History Society; U.S. Forest Service history pages; PBS/WVIA.)
- **Hetch Hetchy.** San Francisco (rebuilding after the 1906 earthquake and fire) sought Hetch Hetchy Valley in Yosemite National Park for a reservoir. Muir and the Sierra Club fought it for years; Pinchot backed the city. Congress passed the **Raker Act; Wilson signed December 19, 1913**. Muir died a year later (December 24, 1914). **O'Shaughnessy Dam finished 1923**; the valley is under water today. The **National Park Service Organic Act (August 25, 1916)** — a bureau to protect the parks — passed partly in reaction. (Sources: EBSCO research starters; Earth Island Journal; NPS "Quick History"; Raker Act references.)
- **The last passenger pigeons.** Last confirmed wild bird: **March 24, 1900, Pike County, Ohio** — shot by **Press Clay Southworth**, age 14, with his family's 12-gauge after asking his mother's permission; the family had the bird mounted (button eyes — hence "**Buttons**"); it is held by the Ohio History Connection, Columbus. Southworth told the story himself decades later (recounted in Joel Greenberg, *A Feathered River Across the Sky*, 2014). A few unconfirmed wild sightings ran to about 1902. **Martha**, the last of the species, died at the **Cincinnati Zoo, September 1, 1914** (about age 29); her body was frozen in a 300-pound block of ice and shipped to the Smithsonian, where the mount still exists. Zoo general manager **Sol Stephan** cared for her and saved her feathers in a cigar box — but his accounts of her origin changed over the years; the zoo's own records are inconsistent. **State the inconsistency.** Documentary: *From Billions to None: The Passenger Pigeon's Flight to Extinction* (2014, dir. David Mrazek) — **topic doc**, not about one person. (Sources: Smithsonian Institution Archives "Martha, A Cold and Lonely Last Migration"; Smithsonian magazine; Wikipedia "Martha (passenger pigeon)" cross-checked; Mark Avery "24 March 1900"; abc6 Columbus — journalism.)
- **The Dust Bowl as an ecological event.** Wheat prices (WWI) and tractors plowed up tens of millions of acres of native shortgrass; drought began 1931; the sod that held the Plains was gone. By December 1934, about **35 million acres** of farmland were ruled essentially destroyed for crops, **100 million acres** had lost all or most topsoil, another 125 million acres losing it fast (USDA Yearbook figures). In 1935 alone an estimated **850 million tons** of topsoil blew off the southern Plains. **Black Sunday, April 14, 1935** — the worst single storm, a black wall moving at 60 mph. That spring soil scientist **Hugh Hammond Bennett** was testifying in Washington when Plains dust darkened the sky outside — "This, gentlemen, is what I've been talking about" (accounts date the moment to late March or early April 1935 — sources disagree on the exact day; state "that spring"). Congress declared soil erosion "a national menace"; the **Soil Conservation Service** was created **April 27, 1935** with Bennett directing. Contour plowing, shelterbelts, grass restoration followed. (Sources: NWS Norman "The Black Sunday Dust Storm"; NRCS "A Brief History"; PBS American Experience Dust Bowl timeline; WETA Boundary Stones — journalism.) **`migration` owns the exodus (250,000+ to California, "Okies," Route 66 — verified in its bank); `disasters` owns the storms as calamity; `food-farming` owns farm practice. ### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-242a): Glacier National Park and the Blackfeet

- **Starvation Winter, 1883-84.** By 1880 the bison were nearly gone. Congress cut the money it spent on annuities, the food and goods owed to tribes by treaty as payment for land. Early storms held up supplies. In December a wagonload of bacon arrived at the agency near Browning, "so old it was contaminated with maggots." "Approximately one-quarter of the Blackfeet tribe, 600 people, died from starvation." The Assiniboine lost about 300. (Montana Historical Society, *Montana: Stories of the Land*, ch. 11, "Starvation Winter: Trading More Land for Food.") Other counts: "nearly 600," and in some secondary accounts 500 to 800 (Old Agency historical marker via search summary; lower confidence). The Indian agent at the Blackfeet agency, called Old Agency, from 1879 was **John Young** (marker text via search; not seen directly). Record the range. The MHS figure is the anchor.
- **The 1895 sale.** "After Starvation Winter, the tribes sold the only resource they had left: land." In 1895 the Blackfeet sold a strip that prospectors hoped held gold. "When no gold was found there, most of this land became Glacier National Park." (MHS textbook, ch. 11.) The strip was about **800,000 acres**, sold for **$1.5 million** (NPCA, "In My Country"; Intermountain Histories, "Blackfeet Removal from Glacier National Park"). Many Blackfeet say it was offered as a **99-year lease**, not a sale, and that a surveying error took another 45,000 acres (NPCA). The agreement "guaranteed the Blackfeet the right to hunt, fish, cut wood and gather plants on the land" (NPCA). White Calf, a Piikani (Blackfeet) chief, of Chief Mountain: "Chief Mountain is my head. Now my head is cut off." (MHS textbook, fig. 11.6.) Little Dog told the commissioners that the Blackfeet "did not ask the government to come and buy their land." (Intermountain Histories.)
- **1897 and 1910.** The land became a forest reserve in 1897 and **Glacier National Park on May 11, 1910** (NPS Glacier fact sheet and "The Development of Glacier National Park": established by act of Congress May 11, 1910). The reserved rights "essentially vanished after Glacier National Park was created in 1910" (NPCA). "In 1932, a US District Court ruled conclusively that they had no rights to hunt in the park." In 1973 a judge ruled that Blackfeet could enter the park without paying the entrance fee. (Intermountain Histories, Brigham Young University / Charles Redd Center project.)
- **Earlier violence tied to the same land.** In 1870 U.S. Army troops attacked Heavy Runner's Piikani camp on the Marias River and killed more than 200 men, women and children (NPCA). This is `native-nations`' event (the Marias Massacre). Use it here in one line at most, only as background to the 1895 sale.

## Era 09 — 1950 to 2000: pollution becomes visible, then becomes law

- **DDT and the eagle.** DDT in wide use from the mid-1940s; it thinned raptor eggshells until eggs broke in the nest. Bald eagles down to **417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 by 1963**. (Sources: USFWS bald eagle fact sheet; American Eagle Foundation.)
- **Rachel Carson** (1907–1964). Marine biologist and former U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service writer-editor; *The Sea Around Us* made her famous; ***Silent Spring* (September 1962)** documented what persistent pesticides were doing to birds, soil, water, and people. The chemical industry attacked her; she answered with evidence — including on **CBS Reports, April 3, 1963**, already sick with the breast cancer that killed her **April 14, 1964, Silver Spring, Maryland**. She did not live to see: the **EPA ban on DDT (1972)**. Documentary: ***Rachel Carson* (PBS American Experience, January 24, 2017)** — person-doc, verified (an earlier American Experience film, "Rachel Carson's Silent Spring," aired 1993). (Sources: ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark "Silent Spring"; Britannica; NOAA NCEI profile; PBS.)
- **The Cuyahoga fire, June 22, 1969.** An oil slick and debris on the river in Cleveland caught fire (about 20–30 minutes, controlled quickly) — **at least the thirteenth fire on the river since 1868**. The 1952 fire did $1.3 million damage; the 1912 fire killed five men. What changed in 1969: *Time* magazine (August 1, 1969) put the burning river before the country, and Cleveland's mayor **Carl Stokes** — the first Black mayor of a major U.S. city — held a press conference at the river the next day demanding state and federal action. Also 1969: the **Santa Barbara oil spill** (January) blackened California beaches. (Sources: Case Western Reserve Encyclopedia of Cleveland History; NPS "Carl B. Stokes and the 1969 River Fire"; Smithsonian magazine.)
- **The environmental decade.** **Earth Day, April 22, 1970** — an estimated **20 million Americans** took part (organized by Sen. Gaylord Nelson with Denis Hayes). **NEPA signed January 1, 1970**; **EPA opened December 2, 1970**; **Clean Air Act 1970**; **DDT banned 1972**; **Clean Water Act 1972**; **Endangered Species Act signed December 28, 1973**. Results with numbers: bald eagle to about 10,000 pairs by 2007 (delisted that year) and roughly **71,400 pairs** by the 2020s. (Sources: EPA history pages; LOC Today in History; History.com; USFWS.)
- **Love Canal.** Hooker Chemical buried about **21,000 tons** of chemical waste in an abandoned canal in Niagara Falls, N.Y., **1942–1953**, then sold the covered site to the school board for $1; a school and about 100 homes went up on and beside it. Gibbs founded the Center for Health, Environment & Justice and spent her life helping other neighborhoods; Goldman Environmental Prize 1990. (Sources: EPA Superfund history; Goldman Prize profile; National Geographic — journalism; Levin Center "Congress and the Love Canal"; IMDb/Rotten Tomatoes for the film.)

(Sources: UNC Library exhibition "We Birthed the Movement"; NCpedia; Wikipedia "1982 North Carolina PCB Protest" cross-checked.)
- **Wolves return.** Gray wolves reintroduced to **Yellowstone, January 1995** (first shipments from Canada; 31 wolves relocated by end of 1996; 41 total 1995–97 counting the 1997 additions — the 1995–96 figure of 31 is the standard one; state simply "starting January 1995"). ## Era 10 — 2000 to Today (current through August 2026)

- **The warming.** **2024 was the warmest year on record globally** (NOAA: 1.29°C above the 20th-century average; NASA, Copernicus, Berkeley Earth agree) **and the warmest on record for the contiguous U.S.** (55.5°F, 3.5°F above the 20th-century average; 17 states set records). Say it plainly and factually; `science` owns the climate science, `energy` owns the fuels and the shift, `disasters` owns individual catastrophes. (Sources: NOAA news releases Jan 2025; NASA.)
- **Fire comes back — bigger.** A century of suppressed fire left the woods loaded with fuel; heat and drought dried it. **Camp Fire, November 8, 2018**: 85 people killed, the town of Paradise, California essentially destroyed (~19,000 structures) — deadliest U.S. wildfire in a century. **2020 California season: about 4.2–4.3 million acres burned (~4% of the state)** — the modern record; the **August Complex** passed **1 million acres**, called the first "gigafire" in modern state history. Agencies now set intentional fires: prescribed burning is policy again. (Sources: Britannica "Camp Fire of 2018"; NIST timeline; CAL FIRE 2020 archive; CalMatters — journalism.)
- **Margo Robbins and cultural fire.** Yurok tribal member; co-founded the **Cultural Fire Management Council** (first community burn 2012, organization formed 2013) to bring traditional burning back to Yurok land on the Klamath River — hazel patches burned so the shoots grow straight for basket-weaving (her stated first goal: baskets to carry her grandchildren); the council now runs training exchanges (TREX) teaching cultural burning to fire crews; deer returned to burned ground at Weitchpec. The full circle of the chapter: the practice outlawed and suppressed for a century is now taught to the government's own firefighters. (Sources: The Nature Conservancy magazine "Indigenous Tribes Restore Prescribed Burns in California"; PPIC interview; National Wildlife Federation "Good Fire," 2025; elementalfilm.com expert page — she appears in the film *Elemental: Reimagine Wildfire* (2022) but it is a topic film, not about her; no Movie line.)
- **The shrinking river.** 2000–2021 was the **driest 22-year stretch in the Southwest in at least 1,200 years** (tree-ring study, *Nature Climate Change*, Feb 2022, UCLA/Columbia; ~42% of the drought's severity attributed to human-caused climate change). **Lake Mead and Lake Powell hit record lows** (2021–22; Mead about a quarter full in mid-2022). **May 2023**: Arizona, California, and Nevada agreed to cut at least **3 million acre-feet** through end of 2026 — about 10% of the lower-basin allocation — with federal compensation. Forty million people and much of the country's winter vegetables depend on the river. (Sources: UCLA Newsroom; NBC/PBS coverage — journalism; Washington Post explainer — journalism; Bureau of Reclamation via those pieces.)

Richard, a schoolteacher, led a roughly **13-year campaign** (air monitoring with buckets, shareholder meetings, trips to Shell in the Netherlands); in **2002 Shell agreed to fund full voluntary relocation**, cut emissions 30%, and fund a $5 million community fund. **April 2004: first African American awarded the Goldman Environmental Prize.** (Sources: Goldman Prize profile; Grist interview — journalism; Wikipedia "Margie Eugene-Richard" cross-checked.)
