### 5. 1750 to 1800
- **The tax:** 1791 federal excise on distilled spirits, proposed by Hamilton to help carry the Revolutionary War debt — **7 cents a gallon on domestic spirits** (10 cents on spirits from foreign materials). Small frontier distillers paid effectively higher rates than big eastern ones and had to pay in scarce cash — the grievance behind the revolt. (EBSCO "Whiskey tax of 1791"; TTB.gov "The Whiskey Rebellion.")
- **The rebellion:** resistance climaxed **July 1794** — an armed crowd besieged and burned tax inspector John Neville's home at Bower Hill (a two-day fight). Washington called up militia from four states — **about 13,000 men** (sources give 12,000–13,000) — and rode out with them part of the way, the only time a sitting president has led an army in the field. The rebels dispersed without a battle; about 150 arrests, 20 leaders taken east; two convicted of treason and pardoned by Washington. (Mount Vernon Digital Encyclopedia "Whiskey Rebellion"; Gilder Lehrman; TTB.gov.)
- **Whiskey as money:** backcountry farmers turned corn into whiskey because a jug traveled where a wagon of corn could not; whiskey served as a medium of exchange in cash-poor western settlements — your lead (also noted in `food-farming`'s 1750–1800 section). `economy` keeps one span on the tax as war-debt revenue; `government-politics` the federal-power proof.

## 01 · Before 1500

### Tobacco: the oldest evidence, and what kind of use it was
- **Oldest evidence of human tobacco use in the Americas: about 12,300 years ago**, at the Wishbone site in "North America's desert west": burned tobacco in a hearth, "9,000 years earlier than previously documented." Duke D, Wohlgemuth E, Adams KR et al., "Earliest evidence for human use of tobacco in the Pleistocene Americas," *Nature Human Behaviour* 2022 (online Oct 2021), doi 10.1038/s41562-021-01202-9 (abstract opened via Europe PMC). The authors only "suggest ways that the tobacco may have been used": how it was used is not settled. The site is in the Great Salt Lake Desert, Utah (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **A pipe from central Washington State, about 1,430 years old**, held nicotine and strong evidence of smoking *Nicotiana quadrivalvis* (a wild native tobacco) mixed with smooth sumac (*Rhus glabra*). A pipe from after contact held the trade tobacco *Nicotiana rustica*. Brownstein KJ, Tushingham S, Damitio WJ, Nguyen T, Gang DR, *Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences* 2020, doi 10.3389/fmolb.2020.00133 (abstract opened via Europe PMC).
- **Traditional tobacco versus commercial tobacco.** The National Native Network's "Keep It Sacred" program (Inter-Tribal Council of Michigan) defines traditional tobacco as tobacco or plant mixtures "grown or harvested ... for ceremonial or medicinal purposes," today the plant *Nicotiana rustica*. It is "used as an offering to the Creator or to another person, place, or being." The smoke carries "thoughts and prayers to the spirit world." It may be burned in a fire or smoked in a pipe, and "the smoke is generally not inhaled." "When used appropriately, traditional tobacco is not associated with addiction and adverse health impacts." (keepitsacred.itcmi.org, "Traditional Tobacco Use," opened 2026-09-27.) Truth Initiative, "The difference between commercial tobacco and sacred tobacco," makes the same distinction (unconfirmed: search summary only). Practices differed from nation to nation. No source here says every nation used tobacco the same way.

### Caffeine drinks: the Black Drink at Cahokia, cacao at Chaco
- **Black Drink at Cahokia, A.D. 1050 to 1250.** Chemists found theobromine, caffeine and ursolic acid (markers for holly, *Ilex*) in residues inside beaker cups from Cahokia and nearby sites in Illinois. Black Drink was brewed from the leaves of yaupon or dahoon holly, which grow far south of Cahokia, so people carried the leaves north. In the historic period "men consumed Black Drink in portions of the American Southeast for ritual purification." Crown PL, Emerson TE, Gu J, Hurst WJ, Pauketat TR, Ward T, "Ritual Black Drink consumption at Cahokia," *PNAS* 109 (2012), doi 10.1073/pnas.1208404109 (abstract opened via Europe PMC). Eight beakers tested: ScienceDaily, 6 Aug 2012 (unconfirmed: search summary only). Black Drink is a stimulant (caffeine), not alcohol and not a hallucinogen.
- **Cacao at Pueblo Bonito, Chaco Canyon, New Mexico, about A.D. 1000 to 1125.** Theobromine residue in cylinder jars: the "first known use of cacao drinks north of the Mexican border," which shows trade with cacao growers in Mesoamerica (Mexico and Central America). Crown PL, Hurst WJ, *PNAS* 2009, doi 10.1073/pnas.0812817106 (abstract opened via Europe PMC).
- **Stimulant drinks at 18 sites** in the US Southwest and northern Mexico, about A.D. 750 to 1400, from southern Colorado to northern Chihuahua, made with cacao or holly. Crown PL et al., "Ritual drinks in the pre-Hispanic US Southwest and Mexican Northwest," *PNAS* 2015, doi 10.1073/pnas.1511799112 (abstract opened via Europe PMC).
- Cacao DNA also found in 11th to 12th century pottery at Etowah, Georgia (King A et al., *PLoS ONE* 2026, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0353607, abstract opened via Europe PMC). Optional detail.

### Plants that change the mind: peyote and datura
- **Peyote, about 5,700 years ago, Lower Pecos, Texas.** Two dried peyote "buttons" (the tops of the cactus *Lophophora williamsii*) in the Witte Museum, San Antonio, "presumably found in Shumla Cave No. 5 on the Rio Grande," date to 3780 to 3660 BC and still contained mescaline, the drug in peyote. The authors call them "the oldest plant drug ever to yield a major bioactive compound upon chemical analysis," and say the find shows Native North Americans knew peyote's effects "as long as 5700 years ago." El-Seedi HR, De Smet PA, Beck O, Possnert G, Bruhn JG, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 2005 (PubMed 15990261, abstract opened via Europe PMC). Three more Shumla specimens dated 4045 to 3960 BC (Terry M et al., "Lower Pecos and Coahuila peyote: new radiocarbon dates," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 2006, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Datura at Pinwheel Cave, California.** Chewed wads (quids) of *Datura wrightii* pressed into the ceiling of a painted cave held scopolamine and atropine, drugs that cause hallucinations. "Native Californians are historically documented to have used *Datura* to enter trance states." The cave was used from late prehistory into the colonial period, so the quids are not firmly dated to before 1500. Robinson DW et al., *PNAS* 2020, doi 10.1073/pnas.2014529117 (abstract opened via Europe PMC).

### Alcohol before contact: the Southwest, and scattered use elsewhere
- Patrick J. Abbott, M.D., "American Indian and Alaska Native Aboriginal Use of Alcohol in the United States," *American Indian and Alaska Native Mental Health Research* 7(2), 1996 (PDF opened, Colorado School of Public Health journal archive): "Prior to White contact, the use of alcoholic beverages in the United States was primarily confined to American Indian groups in the Southwest. However, there was scattered use in other parts of the country." "Most American Indians and Alaska Natives knowledge and use of alcohol coincided with White contact (16th and 17th century)." The drinks were beer and wine only. Distilling was unknown north of the Aztec.
- **Saguaro wine (Pima and Papago, today the Akimel O'odham and Tohono O'odham):** in late summer the women gathered the red fruit of the saguaro cactus, used most of it for jams and the last of the harvest for wine, which was drunk at rain ceremonies held at their New Year, late in the summer. "Today, many of the Papago villages continue to carry out these sacred ceremonials." (Abbott 1996, citing LaBarre 1938, Price 1975, Waddell and Everett 1980.)
- **Tiswin (also tulapai or tulpi):** Apache corn beer, learned from peoples of northern Mexico (the Tarahumara made the corn beer tesguino). Abbott gives the Apache arrival in the Southwest as between 1000 and 1550 A.D. (citing Josephy 1991), so tiswin before 1500 is not firmly dated. Tiswin "was used primarily to mediate social and secular relationships," occasionally for ceremony. (Abbott 1996.)
- **Rules.** Abbott's rules about drunkenness (punished outside ceremonies, allowed to the elderly) are Aztec, in Mexico, and do not belong in this land's cell. For the O'odham the rule the source shows is timing: the wine was made for the yearly rain ceremony.
- **The "firewater myth"** (the false belief that Native people cannot handle alcohol) began at contact. Abbott gives two origins of the word "firewater": traders mixing alcohol with tobacco juice, hot peppers or opium, and the custom of testing its strength by throwing it on a fire (Abbott 1996, citing Leland 1976, MacAndrew and Edgerton 1969, Williams 1985). For eras 02 and 03.
- Alcohol was also traded "in exchange for furs, land, and sexual favors" after contact (Abbott 1996). For eras 03 to 05.

### Tobacco goes to Europe
- Jean Nicot (1530 to 1600 or 1604), French ambassador to Portugal from 1559, learned of tobacco in Lisbon from Damião de Góis. **In 1560** he sent tobacco seeds, with a letter about its medical uses, to Queen Catherine de Médicis of France. On his return in 1561 he gave her leaves and showed her how to crush them into a powder to sniff for headaches. The remedy became popular at the French court. Carolus Linnaeus named the tobacco genus *Nicotiana* after Nicot in 1753, and the word **nicotine** comes from his name. (Britannica, "Jean Nicot," fetched 2026-09-27.)

### Thomas Hariot at Roanoke, 1585 to 1586 (story `thomas-hariot-drugs-alcohol`, marked verified: the facts below back it)
- Born about 1560 (he matriculated at Oxford at 17 in 1577). Joined the 1585 Roanoke expedition, which "failed and returned to England the next year." He learned the Algonquian language from two Virginia Indians, Wanchese and Manteo, and made his own alphabet to record its sounds. *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* was "first published in 1588." "Hariot died in London on July 2, 1621, of cancer of the nose, probably caused by heavy tobacco use." (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Thomas Hariot (ca. 1560–1621)," opened 2026-09-27.)
- **Land:** the Outer Banks, which the Algonquian people called Ossomocomuck, were the land of the Roanoke, Secotan and Croatoan peoples, whose leading werowance (chief) in the 1580s was Wingina. Wingina let the English build their fort on the north end of Roanoke Island in 1585. On 1 June 1586 Ralph Lane's men attacked his village. Wingina (by then called Pemisapan) was shot, chased down and beheaded by an Irishman named Edward Nugent. A week later the English left the island. (NPS, Fort Raleigh, "Wingina," opened 2026-09-27.) The killing belongs to `native-nations` and `war`. Here it gives only the land and why the colony ended.
- **What he wrote about tobacco** (*A Briefe and True Report*, 1590 de Bry edition, DocSouth, UNC, opened 2026-09-27): an herb called "Vppowoc," "sowed a part by it selfe." The people dried the leaves, made them into powder, and drew the smoke through clay pipes. They believed it "purgeth superfluous fleame & other grosse humors" and "openeth all the pores & passages of the body," and so kept them healthy. They threw the powder into "hallowed fires" as a sacrifice, and on the water in storms, and after escaping danger. "We our selues during the time we were there vsed to suck it after their maner, as also since our returne," and "the vse of it by so manie of late, men & women of great calling as else, and some learned Phisitions also" shows its spread in England.

## 03 · The 1600s

### What colonists drank, and the water question (the seed's "the water is not trusted")
- Ed Crews, "Drinking in Colonial America," *Colonial Williamsburg Journal*, Holiday 2007 (opened via Wayback Machine snapshot of research.colonialwilliamsburg.org, 7 Oct 2025): "The first beverages of choice were cider and beer. Both were simple to make." Beer was made from "corn, wheat, oats, persimmons, and green cornstalks." "Americans thought alcohol was healthful. To their minds, drink kept people warm, aided digestion, and increased strength." "Water, on the other hand, could make you sick. Though the New World had plenty of fresh, unspoiled water, incautious Americans sickened and sometimes died by drinking from polluted sources." At Jamestown George Percy wrote that the colonists' drink was "cold water taken out of the River, which was at a floud verie salt, at a low tide full of slime and filth, which was the destruction of many of our men." "In Europe, where polluted waterways were a bigger problem, people substituted alcohol. It was an easy example for the colonists to follow." European grapevines died of American pests and diseases, so vineyards failed in Virginia, New England, Maryland, South Carolina, Pennsylvania and Georgia.

### Drinking laws: Virginia 1619 and Massachusetts
- **Virginia, first General Assembly, 1619** (text: Teaching American History, "Laws Enacted by the First General Assembly of Virginia," opened 2026-09-27): "Against drunkeness be it also decreed that if any private persons be found culpable thereof, for the first time he is to be reproved privately by the minister, the second time publicly, the third time to lie in bolts 12 hours in the house of the provost marshal and to pay his fees, and if he still continue in that vice to undergo such severe punishment as the Governor and Council of Estate shall thinke fit to be inflicted on him." An officer: first a reproof from the governor, second a public reproof in church by the minister, third "committed and then degraded." "Lie in bolts" means held in iron shackles (the plain meaning of bolts; no dictionary opened). The same assembly made it a hanging offense to sell or give Indians guns, shot or powder. It set no rule on selling them alcohol.
- **Massachusetts, *The Laws and Liberties* (1648, collecting orders of 1633 to 1647)** (text: "Ch. 3.4 Primary Source: The Laws and Liberties of Massachusetts Bay, part 2," *American Legal History to the 1860s*, University of Wisconsin Pressbooks, opened 2026-09-27):
  - No one could keep a tavern or sell "wine, ale, beer or strongwater" by retail without the approval of the town's selectmen and a license from the county court: fine £5.
  - A licensed keeper could not let anyone "drink excessively, that is: above half a pint of wine for one person at one time," or keep "tippling above the space of half an hour," or after nine at night: fine 5 shillings.
  - A person "found drunken, that is, so that he be thereby bereaved or disabled in the use of his understanding, appearing in his speech or gesture" forfeited 10 shillings. Excessive drinking 3s 4d, tippling over half an hour 2s 6d, after nine at night 5s. Anyone who could not pay was jailed or "set in the stocks one hour or more ... not exceeding three hours at one time." The stocks: a wooden frame with holes that locked a person's ankles while they sat in public (standard definition; no dictionary opened).
  - Taverns had to show a sign. Beer could not cost more than 2 pence the ale-quart.
  - Tavern keepers near the meeting-house had to clear their houses of everyone able to go to the weekday lecture (sermon).
  - **Tobacco:** in 1633 constables were told to report "unprofitable fowlers and tobacco takers" as idlers. After earlier laws against tobacco were repealed and "the same is more abused than before," the court (1638, 1647) banned smoking within twenty poles (about 330 feet) of any house, barn, corn or haystack, fine 10 shillings, and in an inn except in a private room, fine 2s 6d.
- Samuel Adams Drake, *Old Boston Taverns and Tavern Clubs* (1886, enlarged 1917, Project Gutenberg #42999, opened): in 1634, when prices were set by law, "sixpence was the legal charge for a meal, and a penny for an ale quart of beer, at an inn, and the landlord was liable to ten shillings fine if a greater charge was made." The traveler John Josselyn reported that a constable in a Boston ordinary would stop a stranger ordering more drink than the constable thought right.

### Robert Coles and the red D, 1631 to 1634 (story `robert-coles-drugs-alcohol`, marked verified: the facts below back it)
- Source: Wikipedia, "Robert Coles (settler)" (opened 2026-09-27), citing the court records: John Noble, ed., *Records of the Court of Assistants of the Colony of the Massachusetts Bay, 1630–1692*, vol. 2 (Boston, 1904), pp. 18 to 41, and Winthrop's Journal. Primary pages not opened this pass.
- Born about 1600 in England. Came to Massachusetts Bay in 1630 with the Winthrop Fleet. Settled at Roxbury, then Agawam (Ipswich) in 1633.
- 1631: fined five marks (about £3 6s 8d) for drunkenness aboard the ship *Friendship* and at Winnisimmet (now Chelsea). The *Friendship* carried two hogsheads of metheglin (spiced honey wine).
- 1633, at Agawam, charged with drunkenness a third time: fined £10 "for multiple offenses: drunkenness, encouraging Shatswell's wife to drink," and enticing her "to incontinency" (sexual misconduct). He was sentenced "to stand with a white sheet of paper on his back wherein a drunkard shall be written in great letters." John Shatswell was fined £2.
- 1634, for drunkenness at Roxbury: the court ordered that he "shall be disfranchized, weare about his necke & soe to hange upon his outward garment a D made of redd clothe & sett upon white, to contynue this for a yeare & not to leave it off at any tyme when hee comes amongst company." Disfranchised means he lost his right to vote as a freeman. Winthrop's Journal: "Robert Cole, having been oft punished for drunkenness, was now ordered to wear a red D about his neck for a year."
- He was restored as a freeman about two months later (14 May 1634) and was never charged with drunkenness again. He was later excommunicated by the Roxbury church (Wikipedia, citing the Roxbury church records).
- He moved to Providence by 1637, where he was one of its 13 original proprietors and co-wrote the Providence Combination of 1640. Providence was on land Roger Williams got in 1636 from Canonicus and Miantonomi, chief sachems of the Narragansett. He died in Warwick, Rhode Island, in 1655.
- Some scholars link his red letter to Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) (Wikipedia, citing Pennell 2018). No film about Coles.

### Samuel Cole's inn, Boston, 1634 (story `samuel-cole-drugs-alcohol`, marked verified: resolves the seed's target tavern keeper)

### The alcohol trade with Native nations, from about 1650
- Mancall, "Indians and Alcohol" (Dictionary of American History, Encyclopedia.com, opened): the real trade began in the mid-1600s, "when British and French colonists recognized that sugar produced in the West Indies could be distilled in the Western Hemisphere and sold as liquor in North America. From 1650 onward, alcohol became a common item in the fur trade. Native Americans who had developed a taste for alcohol purchased rum from the English and brandy from the French." Observers, Native and colonial, reported that drinkers "fought with each other and with members of their families ... fell into fires or off cliffs or drowned; and they at times murdered others." Young men who controlled the furs from the hunt traded them for liquor, "and as a result, poverty became more widespread." "Each colony passed laws to prohibit the commerce in liquor. More important, Native peoples organized opposition to the trade." The belief that Native people are genetically more likely to become alcoholics has no evidence behind it (same).
- **New Netherland ordinances** (Laws and Ordinances of New Netherland, 1638–1674, E. B. O'Callaghan, 1868, on Wikisource, pages for 1643, 1645, 1647, opened 2026-09-27):
  - **18 June 1643**, Director General Willem Kieft and Council: "Whereas large quantities of strong liquors are daily sold to the Indians, whence serious difficulties have already arisen," all tapsters and inhabitants were forbidden to sell any liquors to Indians. Fine 25 guilders, doubled the second time, the third time "arbitrarily corrected."
  - **21 November 1645**: the same ban repeated, now naming "Wine, Beer or other Strong liquors" and forbidding passing them "by the third or fourth hand."
  - **1 July 1647**, Director General Petrus Stuyvesant and Council: the ban repeated a third time, fine raised to 500 Carolus guilders plus the damage caused. (Willem Kieft signs the 1645 ordinances in the collection. Stuyvesant heads the 1647 ones, with "the late Director W. Kieft" listed as present. The 1643 page does not print the Director General's name, but Kieft was still in office in 1645.)
  - The same 1647 ordinances closed taverns on Sunday until 2 p.m. (4 p.m. when there was a sermon) and every night after the bell at about nine. They taxed Spanish wine, brandy and liquor at 2 stivers a pot.
- The ban was passed three times in five years. The ordinances themselves say sales continued "daily."

### Rum
- The first known name for rum is from Barbados, about 1651: a planter wrote that "the chiefe fudling they make in the Iland is Rumbullion, als Kill-Divill, and this is made of suggar cones distilled a hott hellish and terrible liquor." Search summaries name Giles Silvester as the writer (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Rum trade "began in the New England colonies in the seventeenth century": New England ships took lumber and fish to the West Indies for molasses, which was made into rum, "one of the earliest of New England's industries." The rum trade became part of a "triangular trade" of molasses to New England, rum to Africa, and enslaved Africans to the West Indies, which kept the northern colonies prosperous "throughout the eighteenth century." ("Rum Trade," *Dictionary of American History*, Encyclopedia.com, opened.) Staten Island distillery 1664 and Boston 1667 as the first in the colonies: drinks-industry websites only (unconfirmed: search summary only). Do not state either "first."

### Tobacco: the cash crop and the habit
- Encyclopedia Virginia, "Tobacco in Colonial Virginia" (opened 2026-09-27): the Virginia Indians' tobacco, *Nicotiana rustica*, "tasted dark and bitter to the English palate." John Rolfe, "an ardent smoker," got seeds of Spanish tobacco (*Nicotiana tabacum*) from Trinidad and Caracas and was growing it by July 1612. Tobacco "had first been brought to England in 1565, perhaps from Florida by Sir John Hawkins." Samples reached England on 20 July 1613. 20,000 pounds were shipped to England in 1617, about 40,000 in 1618. In 1622 the crop was 60,000 pounds. In 1709 the colony grew an all-time high of 29 million pounds. Planters called it "sot-weed." Indentured servants did the work at first, and "By 1700, enslaved Africans, bound for life, outnumbered white indentured servants." After 1622 "the English took over cleared Indian land" (Powhatan land) for tobacco. The 1619 General Assembly passed the first tobacco inspection law.
- **King James I, *A Counterblaste to Tobacco*, 1604** (Project Gutenberg #17008, opened): he called smoking "A custome lothsome to the eye, hatefull to the Nose, harmefull to the braine, dangerous to the Lungs, and in the blacke stinking fume thereof, neerest resembling the horrible Stigian smoke of the pit that is bottomelesse." He mocked the English for copying "the barbarous and beastly maners of the wilde, godlesse, and slauish Indians." (His words about Native people are contempt, and a writer quoting him must say so.)
- The labor (servants, then the enslaved) is told in `work-workers`, `economy`, `slavery-freedom`. The crop is `food-farming`'s. This chapter's slice: the smoking habit, the king's attack on it, and the laws about where people could smoke.

### Rum: how much, how cheap, and the Molasses Act of 1733
- Rum was made from molasses shipped in from the Caribbean sugar plantations, "Sometimes the raw material arrived legally; sometimes it was smuggled" (Ed Crews, "Drinking in Colonial America," *Colonial Williamsburg Journal*, Holiday 2007, opened through Wayback http://web.archive.org/web/20251007000412/https://research.colonialwilliamsburg.org/Foundation/journal/Holiday07/drink.cfm).
- Price, Philadelphia, 1740: a gallon of American rum cost 1 shilling 8 pence, a gallon of Caribbean rum 2 shillings 5 pence. Crews: "With prices for domestic alcohol so low, almost anybody could afford it."
- Molasses Act, 1733 (6 Geo. 2 c. 13): Parliament put a tax of sixpence a gallon on molasses from non-British colonies (the French islands), "largely at the insistence of large plantation owners in the British West Indies" (Wikipedia, "Molasses Act," opened). Mount Vernon Digital Encyclopedia, "Rum" (Emily Niekrasz, opened through Wayback http://web.archive.org/web/20260701181455/https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/rum): colonial merchants smuggled cheaper molasses from the French colonies, "particularly Saint Domingue (Haiti)." After the act "large-scale smuggling of molasses became even more commonplace. While the Molasses Act should have raised 25,000 pounds each year, British officials collected only 259 pounds in 1735." Actors: the merchants who bought and smuggled the molasses, and the distillers. The economy bank has the act too (Encyclopedia.com).
- "During the colonial era, rum was the preferred alcoholic drink of American colonists" (Mount Vernon, "Rum").
- Rum and the slave trade in this era: Crews says rum "was integral to slaving. Rum for that business was distilled several times to make a concentrated product. This saved storage space on ships, as captains could cut their cargo with water upon arrival in Africa." The triangle (molasses to New England, rum to Africa, enslaved Africans to the West Indies) is recorded in "Rum Trade," *Dictionary of American History* (era 03 section above). Named voyages with counts (the *Sally*, 1764) fall in era 05 and are told in `economy`. The traders and the enslaved people's side belong to `slavery-freedom` and `economy`.

### Drink and elections: Virginia's 1705 law
- Encyclopedia Virginia, "Elections in Colonial Virginia" (opened 2026-09-27): "Voters in some places also expected treats in the form of liquor and food to be served by prospective candidates." Under "a 1705 law," candidates were not to make promises to voters. "Legal treats could be served to voters before the sheriff read the election writ and after individuals voted on election day, but the weeks in between were to remain 'dry.' Clever candidates got around such restrictions by absenting themselves from the event and having treats provided by a friend or servant or even their wives." Enslaved people, free Black people, Native people, indentured servants, landless white men and all women could not vote (same). Voters were male landholders aged 21 or more, and each voter said his choice out loud in front of the crowd (Mount Vernon, "House of Burgesses," via Wayback http://web.archive.org/web/20260910031734/https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/house-of-burgesses).
- The 1705 act: "An act for regulating the Elections of Burgesses; for settling their Privileges; and for ascertaining their allowances," October 1705 session. Its ban on giving voters "money, meat, drink or provision" to get votes, with the penalty that the winner could not sit and his election was void: search summary of the Encyclopedia Virginia primary-document page (unconfirmed: search summary only). The EV entry above confirms the law and the "dry" weeks.

### Georgia bans rum, 1735 to 1742
- Georgia's first colonists arrived at Yamacraw Bluff (now Savannah) in February 1733. Tomochichi, head of a Yamacraw town of about 200 Creek and Yamasee people, gave James Oglethorpe land on the bluff (Wikipedia, "Tomochichi," opened). Land: Yamacraw (Creek) land.
- Georgia Encyclopedia, exhibition "Wrestling Temptation: The Quest to Control Alcohol in Georgia" (opened): "The Yamacraw leader Tomochichi noticed an increase in alcohol abuse among the native population as well, and in 1734, he asked James Oglethorpe and the Trustees to forbid the sale of rum. The Trustees responded the following year by issuing a decree against strong liquors, naming an enforcement officer, and adopting a series of graduated penalties for violators." Taverns had to get licenses to sell "light liquor" (beer and wine). "Accusations of corruption in this new system surfaced quickly, and angry colonists protested the restrictions. A faction known as the Malcontents argued that new measures hurt trade": without rum to trade for timber and other exports to the British West Indies, trade fell. "On September 29, 1742, the Trustees overturned the act and once again allowed the importation of rum for use in the lumber trade." The same page says Georgia's colonists "became unruly and mutinous under the influence of so much strong drink."
- Georgia Encyclopedia, "Prohibition in Georgia" (Kaylynn Washnock, opened): "To curb public drunkenness among both colonists and Native Americans, the Trustees issued a decree forbidding the sale of strong liquor in 1735," overturned seven years later after merchants protested that it hurt the colony's trade.
- Wikipedia, "Trustee Georgia" (opened): in 1735 the Trustees put three acts before the Privy Council, and the king and council approved them: an Indian trade act requiring Georgia licenses to trade west of the Savannah River, an act banning rum, and an act outlawing slavery. South Carolina objected to the Indian act and to the Trustees' order to stop rum passing up the Savannah River. Oglethorpe "disagreed with the relaxation of the ban on rum in 1742."
- The act's wording (historical marker at the American Prohibition Museum, Savannah, HMdb 133125, opened): "Whereas it is found by Experience that the use of Liquors called Rum and Brandy, in the Province of Georgia are more particularly hurtful and pernicious to Man's Body and have been attended with dangerous Maladies and fatal distempers NO Rum or Brandy nor any other kind of Spirits or Strong Waters ... shall be imported or brought to shore." The marker calls it "The First Act of Alcohol Prohibition in America." That "first" is the marker's claim only. New Netherland had banned selling liquor to Native people in 1643 (era 03). Write "a ban on all strong liquor in one colony," not "the first."
- Oglethorpe's report of August 1733 that several people in Savannah had died from drinking rum: search summary only (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Oglethorpe, letter of 9 June 1737, on the Creek (quoted in Wikipedia, "Tomochichi"): "Drunkenness, which they learnt from our Traders," was one of "the two greatest Obstacles to their being truly Christians ... with respect to drinking of Rum, I have weaned those near me a good deal from it." (A colonist's view, written in support of a mission.)

### How much colonists drank: the first estimates (clears the seed's per-person [VERIFY])
- 1770: "the colonies had more than 140 rum distilleries, making about 4.8 million gallons annually. That was on top of the 3.78 million gallons imported each year. Production was concentrated in the Northeast." One historian estimates "that during the 1770s the average adult male may have consumed as much as three pints weekly" (Crews 2007, Wayback, opened).
- "By one estimate, colonists consumed 3.7 gallons [of rum] annually per head by the time of the American Revolution" (Mount Vernon, "Rum," citing Ian Williams, *Rum: A Social and Sociable History*, 2005, p. 166, opened via Wayback).
- "colonists consumed far more alcohol than Native Americans—perhaps seven shots of distilled beverages each day by 1770 according to one estimate" (Mancall, "Indians and Alcohol," Encyclopedia.com, opened). Three estimates, three different measures. Give each with its source. None is a count.
- 1790: "United States government figures showed that annual per-capita alcohol consumption for everybody over fifteen amounted to thirty-four gallons of beer and cider, five gallons of distilled spirits, and one gallon of wine" (Crews). Crews does not name the government source.
- Today, for comparison (perishable, checked 2026-09-27): "Per capita consumption of ethanol from all alcoholic beverages combined in 2023 was 2.48 gallons," for people aged 14 and older, about 529 standard drinks a year (NIAAA Surveillance Report #122, https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/surveillance-reports/surveillance122, opened. #122 is the newest per-capita report listed; #123 is on liver cirrhosis). Units differ: the 1790 figures are gallons of each drink, the 2023 figure is gallons of pure alcohol. Do not convert without a source that does.
- Crews also: Americans thought alcohol healthful. "They took whiskey for colic and laryngitis. Hot brandy punch addressed cholera ... Pregnant women and women in labor received a shot to ease their discomfort." Madeira fortified with brandy "About 1750" became "the preeminent wine in British North America." Benjamin Franklin collected more than 200 words for drunk.

### Rum and the slave trade, with names and counts
- By 1764 Rhode Island had "about thirty rum distilleries, twenty-two of them in Newport," making rum traded on the African coast for captive people. Rhode Islanders mounted at least 1,000 slaving voyages carrying over 100,000 Africans in the century before 1807 (Brown University, *Slavery and Justice* report, 2006, as filed in research/research-economy.md, which opened it).
- The *Sally*, 1764 to 1765: Nicholas, John, Joseph and Moses Brown of Providence sent the ship to West Africa under captain Esek Hopkins with 17,274 gallons of rum and chains, shackles and guns. Hopkins bought 196 Africans. At least 109 died. When the captives rose up, Hopkins's crew fired on them: "Slaves Rose on us was obliged fire on them and Destroyed 8" (same, quoting the *Sally* account book). `economy` tells this voyage. This chapter takes one line: the rum was the money.
- Sugar Act, 1764: Parliament "lowered taxation on molasses from six pence per gallon to three, yet encouraged the British Navy to put an end to smuggling. Upon discovering any contraband, the navy was permitted to seize half of the findings" (Mount Vernon, "Rum"). The act and its politics: `government-politics` and `economy`.
- After the Revolution, "rum production continued in the British West Indies, but America's consumption of the drink significantly decreased. Instead, Americans turned toward whiskey" (Mount Vernon, "Rum"). Crews: the war and the Royal Navy made molasses "expensive and irregular," so Americans turned to whiskey, made mostly by Scotch-Irish settlers in Pennsylvania, Maryland, western Virginia and western North Carolina from rye and corn. Kentucky distillers were shipping whiskey by river to New Orleans before 1800.

### Native nations and the liquor trade, 1750 to 1800
- 1754: the Catawba headman Hagler told North Carolina's emissaries, "You Rot Your grain in Tubs, out of which you take and make Strong Spirits." He and other Native leaders argued that colonists should stop, because the trade caused violence and despair (Mancall, "Indians and Alcohol," opened).
- 1764: Sir William Johnson, British superintendent of Indian affairs for the northern colonies, told the Lords of Trade that the rum trade might cause problems, but "the Trade will never be so extensive" without rum (Mancall). He was the official in charge, and he wanted the trade kept going.
- After the Revolution the trade "spread farther west. Wherever traders went, alcohol followed" (Mancall). Traders watered the liquor to have more to sell (Mancall).
- Handsome Lake (Seneca, born about 1735 at Canawaugus on the Genesee River, died 10 August 1815): after the Sullivan-Clinton campaign of 1779, when U.S. troops burned Haudenosaunee villages, and after the loss of most Haudenosaunee land in New York and Pennsylvania, many Haudenosaunee, "including Handsome Lake himself," drank heavily. "In 1799, after a period of illness due to many years of excessive alcoholism, Handsome Lake had the visions that gave him the power to become a prophet ... Shortly after Handsome Lake's first vision, he ceased drinking alcohol ... He preached against drunkenness." His teaching became the Code of Handsome Lake, the Longhouse Religion, "still practiced today" (Wikipedia, "Handsome Lake," opened). Mancall names him among the Native leaders who "convinced their followers to abandon alcohol." Shared with `religion` (the Longhouse Religion) and `native-nations`.
- Land: the Catawba lived in the Carolina piedmont; the Seneca on the Genesee River in western New York.

### Benjamin Rush and the first American argument that drunkenness is a disease (story `benjamin-rush-drugs-alcohol`, marked verified)
- `health` tells Rush for the 1793 yellow fever (slug `benjamin-rush`). This chapter's slug is `benjamin-rush-drugs-alcohol` and its slice is the *Inquiry*.
- Crews: Rush, "a Philadelphia physician and signer of the Declaration of Independence," wrote *An Inquiry into the Effects of Ardent Spirits Upon the Human Body and Mind*, "published in 1785." "Rush saw alcoholism as a disease ... and an addiction. As he put it: 'The use of strong drink is at first the effect of free agency. From habit it takes place from necessity.' He said the only cure was abstinence." His advice: "Taste not, handle not." It "had little impact at the time." Before Rush, "Most Americans saw excessive drinking as a simple lack of will."
- Smithsonian (Megan Gambino, 27 March 2015, opened): *Inquiry* 1785. A 1790 edition added "A Moral and Physical Thermometer," a chart with water and milk at the top. Punch led to "idleness, sickness and debt." Toddy and egg rum led to "peevishness, puking" and jail. Gin, brandy and rum taken every day led to "murder, madness" and the gallows. Historian Mark Lender: "The conception we have of addiction today can generally be traced back to Benjamin Rush."
- Date dispute: Crews and Smithsonian give 1785. A search summary cites a 1789 publication date for a version with the thermometer (unconfirmed: search summary only). Library catalogues often give 1784 (not opened). Write "in the mid-1780s" or "1785 (some sources say 1784)."
- Sales of more than 170,000 copies (CMAJ, "The temperate doctor," search summary only, unconfirmed: search summary only). Do not use without opening.
- Whether Rush opposed all alcohol or only spirits: a search summary says he was "not an alcohol abolitionist but instead proposed temperance in the sense of moderation" (unconfirmed: search summary only). Crews quotes "Taste not, handle not" as his advice to alcoholics. T-266b: open the *Inquiry* text (Rutgers RUcore PDF, https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/68704/PDF/1/play/) if era 06 needs it.

### The whiskey tax and the Whiskey Rebellion, 1791 to 1794 (see also the economy parking at the top of this file)
- January 1791: Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton proposed an excise (a tax on a product made at home) "upon spirits distilled within the United States" to pay the Revolutionary War debt, and Congress passed it (Mount Vernon, "Whiskey Rebellion," Peter Kotowski, opened via Wayback http://web.archive.org/web/20260905224218/https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/whiskey-rebellion).
- Why western farmers made whiskey: shipping grain east "was dangerous because of poor storage and dangerous roads. As a result, farmers frequently distilled their grain into liquor which was easier to ship and preserve" (Mount Vernon). Coins were scarce on the frontier, the law required payment in coin, and "whiskey often served as a medium of exchange, which for poorer people who were paid in whiskey meant the excise was essentially an income tax" (Wikipedia, "Whiskey Rebellion," wikitext opened 2026-09-27).
- Unequal rates: large distillers could pay a flat fee per still, which came to "as low as 6 cents" a gallon by Hamilton's figure, and small western distillers ended up paying about 9 cents a gallon (Wikipedia). The economy parking gives the statutory rate as 7 cents a gallon on domestic spirits (EBSCO, TTB). Record all three.
- 6 September 1791 (Slaughter 1986 says 11 September): "a recently appointed tax collector named Robert Johnson was tarred and feathered by a disguised gang in Washington County. A man sent by officials to serve court warrants to Johnson's attackers was whipped, tarred, and feathered" (Wikipedia). Tarring and feathering: the attackers poured hot tar on the person's skin and stuck feathers to it (definition for the writer: hard-subjects §3b; the Wikipedia text does not describe Johnson's injuries).
- 15 July 1794: U.S. Marshal David Lenox and General John Neville, the regional tax inspector, served writs. Warning shots were fired at them at the Miller farm, about 10 miles south of Pittsburgh (Wikipedia).
- 16 July 1794: at least 30 Mingo Creek militiamen surrounded Neville's house, Bower Hill. "Neville responded by firing a gunshot that mortally wounded Oliver Miller, one of the rebels." Enslaved people on Neville's place helped defend the house (Wikipedia: "who had his slaves' help to defend the house").
- 17 July 1794: nearly 600 rebels under Major James McFarlane, a Revolutionary War veteran, returned. After about an hour of firing McFarlane called a ceasefire, "As McFarlane stepped into the open, a shot rang out from the house, and he fell mortally wounded." The rebels burned the house, "including the slave quarters." Deaths at Bower Hill: McFarlane and one or two other militiamen, and possibly one U.S. soldier; the count "is unclear" (Wikipedia). Who fired the shot that killed McFarlane: the records name no one (see SEARCHED, NOT FOUND). Mount Vernon's figure: "nearly 400 whiskey rebels ... set fire to the home of John Neville." Record both (400 and nearly 600).
- The army: Washington called up 12,950 militiamen from New Jersey, Maryland, Virginia and Pennsylvania and led them west (Mount Vernon; Wikipedia). The rebels scattered. About 12 soldiers died of illness or accidents (Wikipedia infobox).
- 13 November 1794, "the Dreadful Night": troops broke into houses at night and seized suspects. "No distinction was made between rebels and witnesses. Captives were driven, in their nightclothes and barefoot, over muddy roads and streams, to be held in floorless animal pens and basements. Some had their health ruined, and at least one died" (Wikipedia, citing Slaughter 1986 and Crytzer 2023). About 150 arrested. The soldiers' officers are not named in the passage opened.
- Twenty prisoners were marched to Philadelphia and "paraded through the city and down Broad Street as trophies" on Christmas Day 1794 (Patrick Grubbs, "Whiskey Rebellion Trials," Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, opened). ### Philip Vigol (Wigle), convicted of treason (story `philip-vigol-drugs-alcohol`, resolves the seed's target Whiskey Rebellion farmer, marked verified)

- Court report: *United States v. Indictment for high treason, "in levying war against the United States." "The prisoner was one of the most active of the insurgents in the Western Counties of Pennsylvania, and had accompanied the armed party, who attacked the house of the Excise Officer, (Reigan's) in Westmoreland, with guns, drums, &c. insisted upon his surrendering his official papers, and extorted an oath from him, that he would never act again in the execution of the Excise Law. The same party then proceeded to the house of Wells, the Excise Officer in Fayette county, swearing that the Excise Law should never be carried into effect, and that they would destroy Wells and his house. On their arrival, Wells had fled and concealed himself; whereupon they ransacked the house; burned it, with all its contents, including the public books and papers; and afterwards discovering Wells, seized, imprisoned, and compelled him to swear, that he would no longer act as Excise Officer."
