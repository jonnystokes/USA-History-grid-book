# Research Notes: Chapter 8, Technology (`technology`)

Research bank for the chapter. Angle: **the tools and machines that changed how people lived and worked — the applied invention.** Keep out: the science behind it (`science`), vehicles as systems (`transportation`), fuels and the grid (`energy`), news media (`news-communication`). No invented facts; every date web-verified 2026-08-08 with source named inline. Journalism labeled. Organized by the ten standard time sections. ## 1. Before 1500

- **The bow replaces the atlatl — late, and not everywhere at once.** A 2026 study in the *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* dates the bow and arrow's arrival across western North America to roughly A.D. 600–700 (about 1,400 years ago). South of the 55th parallel the atlatl (spear-thrower) drops out of the archaeological record soon after the bow appears — a fast replacement; north of that line the two weapons were used side by side for more than 1,000 years. (Source: *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* 2026, reported by phys.org and Discover — journalism reporting an academic paper.)
- **Boylston Street Fishweir, Boston.** Under Boston's Back Bay, alignments of wooden stakes — fish weirs — were built in tidal channels roughly 4,700 to 3,700 years ago and used across more than a thousand years; the complex was abandoned around 1550 B.C. as the bay silted in. Fence-like traps caught fish moving with the tide. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Boylston Street Weir"; Massachusetts Ancient Fishweir Project; *Quaternary Research* radiocarbon study.)
- **Cahokia's Woodhenge.** At Cahokia (Illinois, the largest city north of Mexico before 1500), five successive circles of upright posts were raised about A.D. 900–1100 — 24 to 72 posts each, 73 to 145 meters across, with posts marking sunrise positions through the year. An engineered calendar in wood. (Sources: Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site; corroborated by UC Berkeley ORIAS.)
- **Technique as technology.** Interplanted corn-beans-squash agriculture (the Three Sisters), controlled-fire land management, hide tanning, pottery, basketry, and the birchbark canoe were engineered systems matched to place — technology without metal machinery. The canoe as a built craft is this chapter's; travel on the water is `transportation`'s. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI education materials, incl. Native Knowledge 360; NativeTech.)
- **Lake Superior copper** worked cold for thousands of years — `elements` leads that story (see its bank).
- No named individual inventors are recorded for this era; the honest story slot is a documented present-day practitioner of a traditional technique (target).

## 2. The 1500s

- **European metal arrived ahead of most Europeans.** Smelted European copper and brass reached the East coast by the 1500s through down-the-line trade from coastal contacts; in 1524 Verrazzano recorded sheets of worked copper among Native people of the Northeast coast. (Sources: NativeTech "Contact and Precontact Copper & Brass Metalwork"; pXRF sourcing study, *Journal of Archaeological Science* via ScienceDirect.)
- **Kettles became raw material.** Native smiths cut and reworked European kettle metal into arrow points, knives, pipe stems, bells, and ornaments — adopting the material into their own toolkits rather than the European use. Hammered-flat brass points outperformed stone. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI "Native New York — Journey of Trade Goods"; NativeTech; *Journal of Archaeological Science* study of 17th-century Illinois metal-forming.)
- Era is **thin** from this chapter's angle: tools arrived and were adapted; no workshops or mills existed yet in the future United States. Spanish expeditions passed through with iron weapons and tools but left no lasting tool trade.

## 3. The 1600s

- **Falling Creek, Virginia — the first ironworks, destroyed before it shipped.** The Virginia Company sent ironworkers in 1619; the blast furnace at Falling Creek was ready about 1621–22 with a first iron shipment planned for April 1622. In the coordinated Powhatan attacks of March 22, 1622, 27 people were killed at the works and the facility was destroyed; it was not rebuilt. (Sources: Virginia Department of Historic Resources site 020-0063; Colonial Williamsburg journal "The Works at Falling Creek.")
- **Saugus, Massachusetts — the first sustained, integrated ironworks in British colonial America.** Operated on the Saugus River 1646–1670: blast furnace, forge, and a rolling and slitting mill, all driven by waterwheels. Reconstructed on the site today as Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site. (Source: National Park Service, Saugus Iron Works NHS.) `elements` tells the iron; this chapter's slice is the water-powered machinery. Workers are partly documented in the site's records — ordinary-story target.
- **The first printing press in the English colonies, Cambridge, 1638.** The Rev. Jose Glover bought a press and hired locksmith Stephen Daye to run it; Glover died on the crossing, and the press was set up in Cambridge, Massachusetts under his widow Elizabeth Glover's ownership, with Daye operating it. Its first full book, the *Bay Psalm Book* (1640), was the first book printed in what is now the United States. (Sources: Library of Congress exhibition "First Among Many: The Bay Psalm Book and Early Moments in American Printing"; Britannica "Stephen Day.") The PRESS as a machine is this chapter's; the press as news is `news-communication`'s.
- **Grist mills and sawmills** — water power doing the colonies' two heaviest jobs, grinding grain and sawing lumber — spread through the century wherever streams could be dammed. (General framing; the Saugus waterwheels above are the documented anchor.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **The Franklin stove.** Franklin designed his open metal-lined fireplace-stove in 1741–42 (sources give both years; the Franklin Institute uses 1742) to put more of a fire's heat into the room and less up the chimney. He refused to patent it — or any of his inventions — on the stated principle that ideas should serve everyone freely. (Sources: The Franklin Institute, "Benjamin Franklin's Inventions"; Environment & Society Portal.)
- **Franklin the printer-inventor** — a working Philadelphia printer whose shop trade made him the era's most famous colonial craftsman-inventor. His almanac and newspaper belong to `news-communication`; his electrical science to `science`; the stove and (next era) the lightning rod are this chapter's.
- **Colonial ironworks multiplied** in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia through the early 1700s — furnaces and forges, still water- and charcoal-powered (framing; detailed iron economics belong to `economy`/`elements`).
- Verified film for the Franklin story: ***Benjamin Franklin* (2022)** — two-part PBS documentary by Ken Burns, premiered April 4–5, 2022. (Sources: PBS; kenburns.com.)

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The lightning rod — the applied half of Franklin's electricity.** Franklin described protecting buildings from "electrical fire" in a 1750 letter to Peter Collinson; after the 1752 demonstrations (the French sentry-box experiment, then his own kite) the first lightning rods went up in Philadelphia. (Source: The Franklin Institute.) The kite and the science are `science`'s; the rod on the roof is this chapter's.
- **The cotton gin, 1793–94.** Eli Whitney, a Massachusetts-born Yale graduate visiting a Georgia plantation, built a machine in 1793 that pulled cotton fiber off its seeds; he applied for the patent in 1794 (granted March 14, 1794). (Sources: Mass Moments; National Archives education resources.)
- **What the gin did to slavery — the honest consequence.** Cleaning upland cotton by hand had been the bottleneck; the gin removed it. U.S. cotton production went from under 9,000 bales a year around 1790 to over 200,000 by 1800 and about 4 million by 1860. The enslaved population counted in the census grew from 697,897 (1790) to nearly 4 million (1860), and slavery spread west across the cotton belt — the machine that was supposed to save labor instead multiplied the demand for enslaved labor. State plainly, then hand off: the system and the lives inside it are `slavery-freedom`'s. (Sources: National Archives "Eli Whitney's Patent for the Cotton Gin"; DPLA primary-source set "Cotton Gin and the Expansion of Slavery"; EH.net "Cotton Gin.")
- **Interchangeable parts — the myth and the record.** Whitney took a federal musket contract in 1798 and promoted the idea of machine-made, interchangeable parts; the famous 1801 demonstration before officials is part of the legend. Historian Robert S. Woodbury showed in 1960 that the credit is a myth: the idea originated in Europe (French armories) before Whitney, Whitney's own factory never achieved true interchangeability, and the best-documented first American production use is John H. Hall's rifle works at Harpers Ferry armory, assembling firearms from interchangeable parts by 1819. Tell the armory version, not the legend. (Sources: Woodbury, "The Legend of Eli Whitney," *Technology and Culture* 1960, summarized by Linda Hall Library "Eli Whitney" and *Commonplace* "How Eli Whitney Single-handedly Started the Civil War... and Why That's Not True.")
- **Slater's mill, 1790.** British law barred exporting textile machinery and the emigration of skilled textile workers. Samuel Slater, trained in an Arkwright-system mill in Derbyshire, left England in 1789 without declaring his trade, and rebuilt Arkwright's carding and spinning machines from memory in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, with local craftsmen — capital and building from the merchants Almy & Brown. The mill spun its first yarn in December 1790: the start of the American factory textile industry. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Slater's Spinning Mill"; Encyclopedia.com "Samuel Slater Builds the First Factory.") Child workers in Slater-system mills are `work-workers`' story.

## 6. 1800 to 1850

- **The telegraph, May 24, 1844.** Samuel Morse sent "What hath God wrought" (Numbers 23:23) from the U.S. Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore over the government-funded Washington–Baltimore line. The message was chosen by Annie G. Ellsworth, daughter of the Patent Commissioner. The paper tape survives. (Sources: U.S. Senate historical office; Smithsonian NMAH object record "'What Hath God Wrought' Telegraph Message.")
- **Vail's role — say it honestly.** Alfred Vail built and improved the instruments at the Speedwell Ironworks shop and received the first public message in Baltimore. Historians disagree about how much of the dot-dash alphabet is his: accounts range from "Morse invented, Vail improved" to crediting Vail with the practical letter frequencies (short codes for common letters). Morse held the patents and the fame. State the disagreement; do not resolve it. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAH documentation of Vail's instruments; HistoryNet "Morse's Partner Argued He Invented Famous Code.")
- **The sewing machine and the first big patent war.** Elias Howe patented the two-thread lockstitch machine September 10, 1846. Isaac Singer's more practical machine sold better; Howe sued, and in 1854 the courts upheld Howe's patent — makers owed him royalties (as much as $4,000 a month at peak). In 1856 Howe and the major makers (Singer, Wheeler & Wilson, Grover & Baker) pooled their patents in the Sewing Machine Combination, which controlled the trade until 1877. (Sources: Library of Congress business-history guide "Elias Howe Issued a Patent for the Sewing Machine"; ABA Journal "Sept. 10, 1846: The Sewing Machine Patent War"; Mass Moments.)
- **Vulcanized rubber, 1839 → patent 1844.** Charles Goodyear's rubber-and-sulfur mixture met a hot stove in Woburn, Massachusetts in 1839 and charred like leather instead of melting. Accounts differ on exactly how the rubber hit the stove (dropped, or flung during an animated sales pitch) — the accident is documented, the details vary; say so. It took him five more years to make the process repeatable; patent 1844. (Sources: Britannica "Charles Goodyear"; ConnecticutHistory.org.)
- **The reaper and the steel plow** — McCormick's reaper (first public demonstration 1831, patent 1834) and John Deere's self-scouring steel plow (1837): see parked farm-machine notes at bottom, already verified (Britannica; ASME landmark; Virginia Museum of History & Culture).
- **The camera arrives, 1839.** News of Daguerre's process reached the U.S. in late August–September 1839; Philadelphians rebuilt it within weeks, and Robert Cornelius's October/November 1839 self-portrait is the earliest surviving American portrait photograph (Library of Congress holds it). The camera as a machine is this chapter's; photography as art is `art`'s. (Sources: American Philosophical Society "Robert Cornelius daguerreotypes"; Library of Congress.)

## 7. 1850 to 1900

- **The telephone and the same-day filing, February 14, 1876.** Bell's attorney filed his application "Improvement in Telegraphy" that morning (5th filing of the day); Elisha Gray's caveat for a very similar instrument arrived hours later (39th). Both described a liquid transmitter. Patent 174,465 issued to Bell March 7, 1876; Gray, on his lawyers' advice, withdrew. Bell's notebook records the first spoken sentence by telephone on March 10, 1876 ("Mr. Watson — come here — I want to see you"). The dispute over who deserved priority has been argued ever since, including a 2020 IEEE *Proceedings* re-examination — present the same-day filing as fact, Bell as the patent holder, and the controversy as real. (Sources: Library of Congress "Who is credited with inventing the telephone?"; IEEE Xplore, "The Bell Versus Gray Telephone Dispute" (2020); Bell family papers at LOC.)
- **Emma Nutt — the first woman telephone operator.** Hired September 1, 1878 by the telephone dispatch company in Boston, away from a telegraph office; paid $10 a month for a 54-hour week. Her sister Stella followed the same day. Companies replaced teenage boy operators (rude, prank-prone) with women, and by the late 1880s operating was an almost entirely female occupation — one of the first big technology jobs for women. She worked as an operator for over 30 years (accounts of the exact span vary, roughly 33–37 years). (Sources: New England Historical Society; Boston Magazine; Time.) The operator's working day at scale is `work-workers`' angle.
- **Edison — improver and system-builder, not the light bulb's sole inventor.** Inventors had worked on incandescent lamps for 40 years before 1879 (Swan in England among them). Edison's Menlo Park team — Charles Batchelor, John Kruesi, Francis Upton and others — produced in October 1879 a carbonized cotton-thread filament lamp that burned about 14.5 hours, demonstrated publicly at Menlo Park on New Year's Eve 1879; longer-lived bamboo filaments followed. What Edison actually built that others had not: the whole working SYSTEM — lamp, dynamo, wiring, metering — and the Pearl Street station (September 4, 1882, 257 Pearl Street, Manhattan) that lit the first paying district. Phonograph: John Kruesi built the tinfoil machine to Edison's design in early December 1877; Edison recorded "Mary had a little lamb" on December 6, 1877. (Sources: U.S. Department of Energy "The History of the Light Bulb"; Smithsonian NMAH 1879 lamp object record; IEEE Milestone "Pearl Street Station, 1882"; Rutgers Thomas A. Edison Papers "Tinfoil Phonograph.") The current itself and the AC-vs-DC fight are `energy`'s (its Edison/Westinghouse-Tesla stories exist).
- **Lewis Latimer — the honest role.** Self-taught draftsman (son of parents who escaped slavery); drew the patent drawings for Bell's 1876 telephone application while at a patent law firm. At Hiram Maxim's U.S. Electric Lighting Co. he received patent 252,386 (January 17, 1882) for a "Process of Manufacturing Carbons" — a better way to MAKE carbon filaments, more uniform and longer-lasting; he did not invent the filament or the bulb. Joined Edison's operation in 1884: patent drawings, expert-witness work, and supervising lamp-plant and lighting installations (New York, Philadelphia, Montreal, London). The only Black member of the Edison Pioneers (1918). (Sources: Rutgers Edison Papers, "Inventing a Better Life: Latimer's Technical Career"; National Inventors Hall of Fame; Lewis Latimer House Museum; Smithsonian collections.)
- **Granville T. Woods.** Black inventor working in Cincinnati; patented the Synchronous Multiplex Railway Telegraph November 29, 1887 — induction-based messages between moving trains and stations, so dispatchers knew where trains were. Won patent interference proceedings twice against claims pressed by Edison's side, and turned down an offer to join Edison; dozens of electrical and railway patents across his career. (Sources: National Inventors Hall of Fame; APS News "Granville Woods Patents Induction Telegraph"; Encyclopedia.com; New York Transit Museum digital exhibit.)
- **Tesla's induction motor.** Nikola Tesla (Serbian immigrant, arrived 1884) patented the AC induction motor (patent 381,968, May 1, 1888, filed October 1887); in July 1888 Westinghouse licensed Tesla's polyphase patents for $60,000 in cash and stock plus a $2.50-per-horsepower royalty. The motor — electricity turned into rotating work without brushes — is this chapter's machine; the grid war is `energy`'s. (Sources: IEEE Spectrum "May 1888: Tesla Files His Patents for the Electric Motor"; The Henry Ford, Westinghouse induction motor artifact.)
- **The typewriter.** Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden, and Samuel Soule patented their machine June 23, 1868; E. Remington & Sons began production March 1, 1873, and the Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer sold from 1874 at $125 — carrying the QWERTY keyboard still in use. (Sources: Library of Congress business-history guide; Britannica.)
- **Barbed wire** — Glidden's patent No. 157,124, November 24, 1874: see parked notes at bottom (National Archives education lesson).

## 8. 1900 to 1950

- **The Wright Flyer, December 17, 1903.** Four flights near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina: Orville first, 120 feet in 12 seconds; Wilbur's fourth flight, 852 feet in 59 seconds. Five witnesses; lifesaving-station member John T. Daniels took the famous photograph. Behind the machine: the brothers' own wind-tunnel tests of wing shapes, three-axis control worked out on gliders, and a light gasoline engine built in their Dayton bicycle-shop workshop. (Sources: Library of Congress "Today in History — December 17"; National Park Service, Wright Brothers National Memorial; Britannica "Wright flyer of 1903.") Flight as travel is `transportation`'s. Verified film: ***Wright Brothers' Flying Machine* (2003)**, PBS NOVA (documentary about the machine, first aired November 11, 2003).
- **Ford's moving assembly line, 1913.** At Highland Park, flywheel-magneto assembly moved onto a line in spring 1913; on October 7, 1913 the chassis line started — 140 assemblers along 150 feet, the chassis pulled past the workers. Chassis assembly fell from over 12 hours to under 3, eventually about 93 minutes. In January 1914 Ford announced the $5, eight-hour day (against about $2.34 for nine hours) — partly to keep workers who hated the line's pace. The line as manufacturing method is this chapter's; the line as a job (the speed-up) is `work-workers`'; the Model T as a car is `transportation`'s. (Sources: The Henry Ford, K-12 assembly-line guide and Five Dollar Day photograph record; Detroit Historical Society, "Ford Highland Park Plant"; History.com "Moving assembly line debuts.") Verified film: ***Henry Ford* (2013)**, PBS American Experience, dir. Sarah Colt.
- **Radio as a device.** Lee de Forest's Audion (1906) — the first triode vacuum tube — made weak radio signals amplifiable, the part that made home receivers practical. Broadcasting and what radio did to news belong to `news-communication` (KDKA 1920 is theirs). (Sources: Britannica "Lee de Forest"; National MagLab "Audion – 1906.")
- **Television as a device.** Philo Farnsworth, age 21, transmitted the first all-electronic television image — a straight line — on September 7, 1927 at his Green Street lab in San Francisco, using his image-dissector camera tube. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Philo T. Farnsworth"; FoundSF; California Office of Historic Preservation, Farnsworth Green Street lab listing.)
- **The refrigerator reaches the kitchen.** GE's Monitor Top (1927, about $300) was the first widely sold home electric refrigerator — over a quarter million sold by mid-1929. What appliances did to housework is `home-family`'s. (Sources: Albany Institute of History and Art object record; Powerhouse Museum.)
- **Bakelite, 1907–09.** Leo Baekeland (Belgian-born American chemist) made the first fully synthetic plastic from phenol and formaldehyde in 1907; process patent filed July 1907, granted December 7, 1909. ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark as the first commercial synthetic plastic. Nylon (DuPont, Carothers) follows in the 1930s — Carothers is `styles`' story; production-side note parked there. (Sources: Science History Institute "Leo Hendrik Baekeland"; Britannica; ACS landmark program.)
- **ENIAC, February 1946.** The first general-purpose electronic computer, unveiled at the University of Pennsylvania. Six women programmed it — Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty Mauchly Antonelli, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence, Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum — and were left out of the celebration (not invited to the demonstration dinner); their story stayed untold for decades until Kathy Kleiman's ENIAC Programmers Project recovered it. Verified film: ***The Computers* (2014)**, documentary short (premiered Seattle International Film Festival 2014). (Sources: Penn Today; ENIAC Programmers Project.)
- **The transistor, December 1947.** At Bell Labs, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain first amplified a signal with a solid-state device on December 16, 1947 — the point-contact transistor — in William Shockley's group; Shockley's junction transistor design followed. Announced publicly June 30, 1948; the name "transistor" won an internal ballot. Nobel Prize in Physics 1956 to all three. The semiconductor physics is `science`'s; the device that replaced the vacuum tube is this chapter's. (Sources: Computer History Museum, "The Silicon Engine — 1947"; IEEE Milestone, "Invention of the First Transistor at Bell Telephone Laboratories"; NobelPrize.org.)
- **The cyclotron** (Lawrence, 1930–31) — see parked Elements notes at bottom (LBL; APS News). **Fordson/Farmall tractors and Birdseye flash freezing** — see parked food-farming notes at bottom.

## 9. 1950 to 2000

- **The integrated circuit — two inventors, honestly.** Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) demonstrated the first integrated circuit September 12, 1958 — germanium, wired by hand. Robert Noyce (Fairchild Semiconductor) in 1959 designed the planar silicon version whose flat, photolithographed wiring could be mass-produced — the form every later chip took. Both are credited as co-inventors; Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics 2000 (Noyce died in 1990; Kilby publicly said Noyce deserved to share it). (Sources: Britannica "Jack Kilby"; DPMA "65 Years Integrated Circuit"; NobelPrize.org.)
- **Grace Hopper.** Mathematician (b. December 9, 1906, New York); Navy WAVES lieutenant assigned in July 1944 to the Harvard Mark I, one of its first programmers; wrote the first extensive computer programming manual (1946). At Remington Rand's UNIVAC she built the A-0 compiler (1951–52) — the idea that programs could be written closer to human language and translated by the machine — then FLOW-MATIC (1955–59), the English-like business language that shaped COBOL. Retired 1986, age 79, then the Navy's oldest active-duty officer, as a rear admiral; died January 1, 1992. (Sources: IEEE Milestone "A-0 Compiler, 1951–1952"; Britannica; Naval History and Heritage Command.) Verified film: ***The Queen of Code* (2015)**, short documentary, dir. Gillian Jacobs, FiveThirtyEight *Signals* series.
- **The personal computer.** The MITS Altair 8800 kit (announced in *Popular Electronics*, January 1975) triggered the hobby; the Homebrew Computer Club first met March 5, 1975 in Gordon French's Menlo Park garage — about 30 people in a rainstorm, gathered to see an Altair. Steve Wozniak credited the club as the seedbed of the Apple I. Apple Computer founded April 1, 1976 (Jobs, Wozniak, and briefly Ron Wayne); the Apple II (introduced June 1977) put a finished, plug-in computer in homes; the IBM PC (August 12, 1981) made the office standard. (Sources: Computer History Museum, Homebrew reunion materials; Library of Congress business-history guide "The Founding of Apple Computer, Inc."; IEEE Spectrum "How the IBM PC Won.")
- **Lee Felsenstein** — Homebrew's longtime meeting moderator, an engineer whose oral history is preserved by the Computer History Museum; a documented ordinary-side figure of the hobbyist wave beyond the famous founders. (Source: Computer History Museum, Homebrew Computer Club 2013 reunion.)
- **Films for the Jobs/Wozniak story:** ***Steve Jobs* (2015)** — Danny Boyle/Aaron Sorkin **dramatization** (label it as drama, from Isaacson's book); documentaries: ***Steve Jobs: The Man in the Machine* (2015)**, dir. Alex Gibney; ***Something Ventured* (2011)** covers Apple's early venture funding. All verified (IMDb/Wikipedia/Zeitgeist Films).
- **ARPANET, October 29, 1969.** Student programmer Charley Kline at UCLA (Leonard Kleinrock's lab) sent the first host-to-host message to Stanford Research Institute: the system crashed after "L-O" of "LOGIN," making the first message "LO"; the full login worked about an hour later. ARPANET was Defense-Department-funded university infrastructure — the seed of the internet. The ARPANET adopted the internet's shared protocol (TCP/IP) on January 1, 1983. (Sources: UCLA Samueli "Internet 50"; ICANN "The First Message Transmission"; Kleinrock's own account, lk.cs.ucla.edu.)
- **The web is NOT an American invention — say so.** Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist at CERN in Switzerland, invented the World Wide Web (proposal 1989; files opened to the public August 6, 1991). America's precise role: the underlying internet grew from U.S.-funded ARPANET, and the first widely used graphical browser — Mosaic — was released April 22, 1993 by Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina, and colleagues at the University of Illinois' NCSA, which put the web in ordinary hands. (Sources: CERN "A short history of the Web"; EBSCO; NCSA, University of Illinois; NSF.)
- **GPS.** First Navstar satellite launched February 22, 1978; the 24-satellite constellation reached Full Operational Capability in 1995; on May 1, 2000 the U.S. ended Selective Availability, giving civilians full-precision signals — the moment consumer GPS became sharp enough for turn-by-turn navigation. (Sources: Penn State GEOG 862 course materials; 2000 presidential directive, widely documented.)
- **The handheld cell phone.** Motorola engineer Martin Cooper made the first handheld cellular call April 3, 1973, walking in Manhattan — calling Joel Engel, his rival at Bell Labs, to tell him. The commercial Motorola DynaTAC 8000X followed in 1983: $3,995, 28 ounces, about 30 minutes of talk. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "First Cellular Phone Call"; 50th-anniversary coverage by NPR/ABC 2023 — journalism.)
- **The americium smoke detector** — see parked Elements notes at bottom (EPA RadTown; LANL).

## 10. 2000 to Today (current through 2026 — state the cutoff)

- **The iPhone.** Announced by Steve Jobs January 9, 2007; on sale June 29, 2007 ($499/$599 with contract); over one million sold in 74 days. The smartphone folded the computer, camera, phone, GPS receiver, and music player into one pocket machine. (Sources: EDN "Apple iPhone goes on sale"; MacRumors retrospective — journalism.)
- **Social media as platforms** changed how the phone was used; what the feed did to news is `news-communication`'s story. Keep to one line here.
- **AI, plainly.** In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet (Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Geoffrey Hinton, University of Toronto) won the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a wide margin (15.3% top-5 error vs 26.2% for second place), setting off the deep-learning era. On November 30, 2022 OpenAI released ChatGPT; about one million users in five days and an estimated 100 million monthly users within two months — reported figures from press coverage, label as journalism. Current AI systems write, draw, and code from plain-language requests; their limits and errors are documented and should be stated plainly. (Sources: the AlexNet result is in the 2012 NeurIPS paper "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks"; ChatGPT dates: History.com "ChatGPT released"; TechCrunch — journalism.)
- **3D printing.** Chuck Hull invented stereolithography in 1983–84 (patent application August 8, 1984; granted March 11, 1986) — objects built up in thin hardened layers; hobby and industrial printers spread widely in the 2010s. (Sources: National Inventors Hall of Fame "Charles Hull"; The Henry Ford, SLA-1 artifact.)
- **The battery in the electric car — an honest, partly-foreign story.** Rechargeable lithium batteries began with M. Stanley Whittingham's work at Exxon in New Jersey in the 1970s; John Goodenough (American chemist, then at Oxford in England) found the lithium-cobalt-oxide cathode in 1980; Akira Yoshino in Japan built the safe carbon-anode prototype (1985), and Sony (Japan) sold the first commercial lithium-ion battery in 1991. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019 to all three. The EV as a vehicle is `transportation`'s; the grid it plugs into is `energy`'s. (Sources: NobelPrize.org 2019 press release and popular-science background.)
- **Limor Fried.** MIT-trained engineer (BS 2003, MEng 2005) who founded Adafruit Industries in her dorm room in 2005 — open-source electronics kits and tutorials for the maker generation; first female engineer on the cover of *Wired*; her company is 100% woman-owned with over 100 employees. A documented ordinary-side story of who builds with technology now. (Sources: Adafruit "About"; The Manufacturing Institute profile; E&T magazine interview.)
- **CRISPR** gene editing (2012) is noted here only as a shared event — the science and medicine belong to `science` and `health`.

## Parked from Ch27 Elements research (2026-07-23) — the machines behind the elements, and the elements inside the machines
Ch27 tells the element discoveries; the machines as machines belong here. Facts source-checked in `research/research-elements.md`.

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **The cyclotron (1930–31).** Ernest O. Lawrence's particle accelerator — his first model, built at Berkeley of glass, sealing wax, and bronze, was small enough to hold in one hand and boosted hydrogen ions to 80,000 electron volts. Successive machines (11-inch, 27-inch, 37-inch, and the 60-inch of 1939 with its 220-ton magnet) whirled particles in a spiral to smash atoms — the tool that made artificial elements, radioisotopes for medicine, and Lawrence's 1939 Nobel Prize. Ch27 uses it as the element factory; the machine's own story — how it works, how it grew, what machines came after (the HILAC, the 88-Inch Cyclotron still running today) — belongs here. (Sources: LBL "Ernest Lawrence's Cyclotron: Invention for the Ages"; APS News "Lawrence and the First Cyclotron.")
- **Copper as the wire metal.** Electric light and telephone networks turned copper into infrastructure (also → Ch9 for the mining economy).

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **The americium smoke detector.** Household ionization smoke detectors contain about a third of a millionth of a gram of americium-241 (a Manhattan Project-era element, discovered 1944–45): its alpha particles ionize air in a small chamber; smoke disturbs the tiny current and trips the alarm. A cheap, mass-made life-safety device with a synthetic element inside — a strong "machine in every home" story. (Sources: EPA RadTown "Americium in Ionization Smoke Detectors"; LANL "United States of Americium.")
- **Silicon and the chip (light from Ch27).** The element silicon (discovered in Sweden, 1824 — no U.S. first) became the material of the transistor age; "Silicon Valley" is named for it. The transistor/computer story is this chapter's own subject; Ch27 keeps only the element name.

## Parked from food-farming research (2026-08-07) — farm machines as machines
`food-farming` tells what the machines meant for farms and food; the machines themselves belong here. Facts web-verified in `research/research-food-farming.md`.

### 6. 1800 to 1850
- **John Deere's steel plow, 1837** (Grand Detour, Illinois): polished steel moldboard scoured itself clean in sticky prairie soil where iron plows gummed up; by 1846 Deere sold near 1,000 a year. (Sources: Britannica "John Deere"; ASME landmark.)
- **Cyrus McCormick's reaper**: first successful public demonstration at Steele's Tavern, Virginia, **1831**; patent 1834; first sales 1840. (Sources: Virginia Museum of History & Culture; Science Museum Group; Encyclopedia.com.)

### 7. 1850 to 1900
- **Glidden barbed wire patent No. 157,124, November 24, 1874** (DeKalb, Illinois) — the commercially successful design among nine competing wire-fence patents; the Barbed Wire Patent Case followed. (Sources: National Archives education lesson; EBSCO; Northern Public Radio Illinois history.)
- **Swift's refrigerated railcar**: engineer **Andrew Chase** designed the practical ice-bunker car for Gustavus Swift in **1878** (ice compartments at top, chilled air falling); first refrigerated dressed-beef shipments east 1877–78. (Sources: Britannica "Gustavus Swift"; Swift Refrigerator Line references.)

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Fordson tractor, 1917** — cheap mass-market tractor; ~75% of U.S. tractors built in the 1920s were Fordsons. **Farmall, 1924** (International Harvester) — tricycle row-crop tractor that could also cultivate, removing the horse's last farm job. 1920 census: 246,000 tractors vs ~26 million horses/mules (horse peak ~1919). (Sources: Farm Collector; Wessels Living History Farm; NBER working paper 7947 on tractor diffusion.)
- **Clarence Birdseye's flash freezing** — double-belt freezer process; first retail sale of frozen foods **March 6, 1930, Springfield, Massachusetts** (18 stores). (Sources: National Inventors Hall of Fame; Mass Moments.)

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **The 1954 Census of Agriculture was the first with more tractors than horses+mules on U.S. farms** (~4.6M tractors; horses/mules down from ~25–26M in 1920 to ~4M). (Source: USDA Census of Agriculture, widely reported.)
