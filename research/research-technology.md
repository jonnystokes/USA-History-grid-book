## 1. Before 1500

- **The bow replaces the atlatl — late, and not everywhere at once.** A 2026 study in the *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* dates the bow and arrow's arrival across western North America to roughly A.D. 600–700 (about 1,400 years ago). South of the 55th parallel the atlatl (spear-thrower) drops out of the archaeological record soon after the bow appears — a fast replacement; north of that line the two weapons were used side by side for more than 1,000 years. (Source: *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* 2026, reported by phys.org and Discover — journalism reporting an academic paper.)
- **Boylston Street Fishweir, Boston.** Under Boston's Back Bay, alignments of wooden stakes — fish weirs — were built in tidal channels roughly 4,700 to 3,700 years ago and used across more than a thousand years; the complex was abandoned around 1550 B.C. as the bay silted in. Fence-like traps caught fish moving with the tide. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Boylston Street Weir"; Massachusetts Ancient Fishweir Project; *Quaternary Research* radiocarbon study.)
- **Cahokia's Woodhenge.** At Cahokia (Illinois, the largest city north of Mexico before 1500), five successive circles of upright posts were raised about A.D. 900–1100 — 24 to 72 posts each, 73 to 145 meters across, with posts marking sunrise positions through the year. An engineered calendar in wood. (Sources: Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site; corroborated by UC Berkeley ORIAS.)
- **Technique as technology.** Interplanted corn-beans-squash agriculture (the Three Sisters), controlled-fire land management, hide tanning, pottery, basketry, and the birchbark canoe were engineered systems matched to place — technology without metal machinery. The canoe as a built craft is this chapter's; travel on the water is `transportation`'s. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI education materials, incl. Native Knowledge 360; NativeTech.)
- **Lake Superior copper** worked cold for thousands of years — `elements` leads that story (see its bank).
- No named individual inventors are recorded for this era; the honest story slot is a documented present-day practitioner of a traditional technique (target).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246): Wayne Valliere, Ojibwe birchbark canoe builder (fills the `traditional-technique-practitioner` slot)
- **Who.** Wayne Valliere, a member of the Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Ojibwe in Wisconsin; birchbark canoe builder, Ojibwe language and culture teacher at Lac du Flambeau Public School. 2020 National Heritage Fellow of the National Endowment for the Arts. (NEA, "Wayne Valliere (Lac du Flambeau Ojibwe)," https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/wayne-valliere-lac-du-flambeau-ojibwe.)
- **How he learned.** From elders including Joe Chosa, Marvin DeFoe and Ojaanimigiizhig, and by studying "museum pieces and different writings and different photographs" (NEA). NEA says he began at 16. A search summary of other coverage says he first saw canoe making at 14 and built his first canoe two years later (unconfirmed: search summary only). Record both; the NEA figure is the one to use.
- **The technique.** Thick birchbark for the hull, straight-grained cedar for the ribs, spruce roots for the stitching, and pitch mixed with oak ash and deer tallow to seal the seams. Each material is harvested in a different season, so builders work "on nature's time" (NEA). A search summary says he puts the learning time at four years at least, and decades to learn to harvest and prepare the materials (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Passing it on.** He has taught more than 100 students and trained at least one independent canoe builder, with a second apprentice in progress (NEA).
- Use: the Before 1500 cell's craft span names the birchbark canoe as built technology. Valliere shows the same technique practiced by a named, documented person today. No invented claim of unbroken detail from before 1500; NEA describes the tradition as handed down over many generations.
- **Movie:** NEA made a short tribute video (NEA, "NEA National Heritage Tribute Video: Wayne Valliere," https://www.arts.gov/stories/video/nea-national-heritage-tribute-video-wayne-valliere). It is a tribute clip, not a film. No Movie line.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-455): how the Boylston Street weirs caught fish, and what a National Heritage Fellowship is (era 1)
- **The weir mechanism.** EBSCO Research Starters, "Boylston Street Weir" (https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/boylston-street-weir), with Wikipedia "Boylston Street Fishweir": the weirs were rows of upright stakes, one to two meters long, driven into the tidal mud, with bundles of brush woven between them to make a wall. Fish came in over the flats at high tide, and the brush wall trapped them as the tide went out. People then took the fish with baskets, dip nets, pronged spears or their hands. Herring in the spring run were a main catch (Wikipedia, citing the excavation reports).
- **National Heritage Fellowship.** NEA, "National Heritage Fellowships" (https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage): the nation's highest honor in the folk and traditional arts, given each year since 1982; 502 fellowships awarded through the 2026 class. The National Endowment for the Arts is a federal agency that funds and honors the arts.

## 2. The 1500s

- **European metal arrived ahead of most Europeans.** Smelted European copper and brass reached the East coast by the 1500s through down-the-line trade from coastal contacts; in 1524 Verrazzano recorded sheets of worked copper among Native people of the Northeast coast. (Sources: NativeTech "Contact and Precontact Copper & Brass Metalwork"; pXRF sourcing study, *Journal of Archaeological Science* via ScienceDirect.)
- **Kettles became raw material.** Native smiths cut and reworked European kettle metal into arrow points, knives, pipe stems, bells, and ornaments — adopting the material into their own toolkits rather than the European use. Hammered-flat brass points outperformed stone. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI "Native New York — Journey of Trade Goods"; NativeTech; *Journal of Archaeological Science* study of 17th-century Illinois metal-forming.)
- Era is **thin** from this chapter's angle: tools arrived and were adapted; no workshops or mills existed yet in the future United States. Spanish expeditions passed through with iron weapons and tools but left no lasting tool trade.

## 3. The 1600s

- **Falling Creek, Virginia — the first ironworks, destroyed before it shipped.** The Virginia Company sent ironworkers in 1619; the blast furnace at Falling Creek was ready about 1621–22 with a first iron shipment planned for April 1622. In the coordinated Powhatan attacks of March 22, 1622, 27 people were killed at the works and the facility was destroyed; it was not rebuilt. (Sources: Virginia Department of Historic Resources site 020-0063; Colonial Williamsburg journal "The Works at Falling Creek.")
- **Saugus, Massachusetts — the first sustained, integrated ironworks in British colonial America.** Operated on the Saugus River 1646–1670: blast furnace, forge, and a rolling and slitting mill, all driven by waterwheels. Reconstructed on the site today as Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site. (Source: National Park Service, Saugus Iron Works NHS.) `elements` tells the iron; this chapter's slice is the water-powered machinery. Workers are partly documented in the site's records — ordinary-story target.
- **The first printing press in the English colonies, Cambridge, 1638.** The Rev. Jose Glover bought a press and hired locksmith Stephen Daye to run it; Glover died on the crossing, and the press was set up in Cambridge, Massachusetts under his widow Elizabeth Glover's ownership, with Daye operating it. Its first full book, the *Bay Psalm Book* (1640), was the first book printed in what is now the United States. (Sources: Library of Congress exhibition "First Among Many: The Bay Psalm Book and Early Moments in American Printing"; Britannica "Stephen Day.") The PRESS as a machine is this chapter's; the press as news is `news-communication`'s.
- **Grist mills and sawmills** — water power doing the colonies' two heaviest jobs, grinding grain and sawing lumber — spread through the century wherever streams could be dammed. (General framing; the Saugus waterwheels above are the documented anchor.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246): Joseph Jenks, the Saugus blacksmith and the first machine patent (fills the `saugus-ironworker` slot)
- **Who.** Joseph Jenks (also spelled Jenckes, Jinks), born about August 1599, baptized in the parish of St. Anne, Blackfriars, London; son of John Jenks, a cutler, and Sarah Fulwater, daughter of an immigrant German cutler. He worked in the 1630s for the swordsmith Benjamin Stone, whose water-powered sword mill stood at Hounslow; a basket-hilted broadsword inscribed "IENCKES IOSEPH / ME FECIT HOVNSLO" survives in the Powysland Museum, Welshpool, Wales. His wife Jone was buried at Isleworth on February 29, 1634/35 and a daughter in November 1638. On August 7, 1639 he petitioned the Earl of Northumberland for ground at Worton Bridge to build "a new invented engine or blade mill." He next appears in court records in the Kittery area of Maine. (Source: Curtis White, "The Jenks Area and the Tailrace," ch. 7 of *Saugus Iron Works: The Roland W. Robbins Excavations, 1948-1953*, National Park Service, pp. 173-176, https://www.nps.gov/sair/learn/historyculture/upload/08Chapter7-508x.pdf.) Wikipedia gives his departure from England as about 1641 and first New Hampshire record as 1642 (unconfirmed beyond Wikipedia, "Joseph Jenckes Sr.").
- **The patent, May 1646.** In May 1646 Jenks petitioned the Massachusetts General Court for the sole right to "Build a Mill for making of Sithes; and alsoe a new Invented Saw Mill, and divers other Engines for making of edge tooles," for fourteen years. He told the court he had "knowledge in Making and erecting of Engines of Mills to goe by water for the speedy dispatch of much worke with few mens labour in litle time," and that without protection "Another when he seeth it makes the like; and soe I loose the benefit." The magistrates granted it "so far as concerns any newe such Inventions," keeping the power to restrain exports and prices. The petition is dated May 10, 1646 (Massachusetts Archives vol. 59, Manufacturers 1639-1773, p. 26). (Source: NPS, White ch. 7, pp. 177-178.) NPS: "Jenks is credited by the United States Patent Office with having the first machine patent in what would eventually become the United States." (NPS, "Jenks Blacksmith Shop," https://www.nps.gov/places/jenks-blacksmith-shop.htm.) **Date dispute:** Britannica and Wikipedia-derived pages give March 6, 1646 (unconfirmed: search summary only, Britannica returned 403). The archive-dated petition is May 10, 1646. Record both. A second 7-year grant in 1655 for an improved grass scythe "for the more speedy cutting of grass" is reported by Wikipedia only (unconfirmed).
- **The shop and the work.** On January 20, 1647 ironworks agent Richard Leader agreed to let Jenks "build & erect a mill or hamer for the forging and making of sithes or any other Iron ware by water at the taile of ye furnace," using the furnace's waste water. The company owned the shop and Jenks paid rent. He forged saw blades, felling saws, steelyards (weighing scales) and drew fine wire (NPS White ch. 7, pp. 178-181; NPS "Jenks Blacksmith Shop"). The ironworks burned over 3,600 cords of wood a year, cut with axes and saws made on site (White, p. 178). A 1652 letter from Emmanuel Downing records Jenks sending two saw blades for John Winthrop Jr.'s water-powered sawmill in Connecticut (p. 181). After the Company of Undertakers went bankrupt, Jenks bought a share of the shop and corn mill on September 12, 1656, and the rolling and slitting mill on October 27, 1656, on a mortgage from Simon Bradstreet (p. 181). In October 1667, "very weak in estate," he petitioned the court for 50 pounds to start making iron wire (pp. 181-183). He died in March 1683 (White ch. 7). Archaeologist Roland Robbins excavated his shop in 1952: overshot and undershot waterwheels, supports for a tail-helve hammer, an anvil base, a stone forge (p. 183, fig. 7.6).
- **The coin-die claim is tradition only.** An 1898 marker at the site says Jenks "in 1652 made the dies for the first silver money coined in New England," and a 1930 state marker says he "invented the modern type of scythe" (NPS White ch. 7, p. 173). The NPS chapter treats these as "pronouncements" to test. Wikipedia: coin dies credited by tradition, "lacking direct documentation." Do not state as fact.
- **His son.** Joseph Jenks Jr. began work at Saugus in 1649 at 16 and in 1672 built his own forge at Pawtucket, Rhode Island (NPS Teaching with Historic Places lesson 30, "Saugus Iron Works: Life and Work at an Early American Industrial Site," https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/upload/TwHP-Lessons_30saugus.pdf).
- **Movie:** none found.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246): who worked at Saugus, what the work did to them, whose land (era 3)
- **Whose land.** NPS: "The Native Americans who were in the area at the time of contact with Europeans are known as the Pawtucket." In the 1640s "a group of Englishmen and Massachusetts residents built the Saugus Iron Works to make iron for the new colony and to make money for themselves" (NPS Saugus, "People," https://www.nps.gov/sair/learn/historyculture/people.htm). NPS TwHP lesson 30: "Saugus was a Native American settlement that the English originally called Lynn." Epidemics of 1616 and 1633 that emptied Pawtucket communities (unconfirmed: search summary only of the NPS Native American collections page; the page text did not load).
- **The workforce.** About 100 people were employed and housed in the company village of Hammersmith, named for the English town many ironworkers came from; skilled workers were recruited from English ironmaking regions (NPS TwHP lesson 30). John Winthrop Jr. found about two dozen investors by 1643 for the "Company of Undertakers of the Iron Works in New England" (same). Colonists from nearby farms did unskilled labor. "Company records show that at least two Indians chopped wood for the same wages as other workers" (same). The names of those two men are not given there.
- **The Scots.** NPS TwHP: "In 1651 about 35 Scottish prisoners of war were brought to New England to work at the ironworks"; they received clothing, food and tobacco and many cut wood for charcoal. The elements bank (T-241a, T-241e) has the fuller record: captured by Oliver Cromwell's army at Dunbar, September 1650; 150 delivered on Council of State orders by Sir Arthur Haselrig to agents of company investors John Becx and Joshua Foote; shipped on the *Unity* from Gravesend November 11, 1650; survivors unknown; the company kept 62, 36 at Saugus (Hamilton 2009 via SPOWS). **Count and year disagree:** TwHP says about 35 in 1651; Hamilton says 36 arrived December 1650. Record both. NPS: seven-year indentures, worked as wood cutters, colliers or laborers (NPS, "Scottish Prisoners at the Iron Works").
- **Named Scots.** The Scottish Prisoners of War Society prints a list of 35 Scots at the works in 1653, including James Adams, John Clark, Alexander Innes (also Ennis), Peter Grant, John Stewart and Thomas Tower; Innes appears on a November 1653 inventory of the works valued at 10 pounds (SPOWS, "Saugus Iron Works," https://spows.org/new-england/saugus-iron-works/, citing John Giffard's record books and Harvard Baker Library papers). The listing of a man with a price in an inventory of the works is from that source only. NPS's Saugus Facebook page says John Stewart's indenture was sold to William Pynchon and he became Springfield's first blacksmith (unconfirmed: search summary only, Facebook).
- **The danger of the work.** NPS TwHP lesson 30: at the forge "Flying sparks and pieces of hot metal constantly threatened men working in the forge. The noise of the 500-pound hammer cost many workers their hearing." Seven waterwheels drove the works; the furnace bellows were 18 feet long; the furnace reached about 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit; 10 to 12 men worked the forge. No death count at Saugus was found in these sources.
- **Why it failed.** The works ran about 22 years (TwHP gives 1646 to 1668; the bank's NPS entry says to 1670) and closed through debt, high costs and imported competition; creditors sued. Workers such as James Leonard went on to run forges elsewhere (TwHP lesson 30).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246, bank check): Falling Creek, who did the work, who died, why, and a correction
- **The workers.** The Virginia Company hired Captain Benjamin Bluett as ironmaster in 1619 and sent him with 80 men; company records also show plans to send 150 men for three ironworks, but only Falling Creek was begun. Bluett died on the voyage or soon after arriving, and others of his men also died of "seasoning," the illness and death that killed 50 to 90 percent of early newcomers (as the county page states it). The survivors could not finish the blast furnace. In 1621 the company hired John Berkeley on the same terms; he sailed with his son Maurice and 25 others and promised iron by Whitsuntide, June 9, 1622. (Chesterfield County, Virginia, "Falling Creek Ironworks Park," https://www.chesterfield.gov/Facilities/Facility/Details/Falling-Creek-Ironworks-Park-79.) A county tourism page says the company sent 150 men in 1619 (Experience Chesterfield, "History Happened Here: Falling Creek Ironworks"). **Numbers disagree:** 80 men under Bluett (county page) against 150 (tourism page, and the county page's figure for the planned three works). A search summary gives Berkeley's party as 20 ironworkers; the county page says 25 others. Record both.
- **The attack, March 22, 1622.** Powhatan warriors, in the colony-wide attack planned by Opechancanough, killed 27 English people at the works and left it in ruins; "At Falling Creek, 2 children survived." Maurice Berkeley, away from the works that day, survived and tried to restart it and failed; a third try by Sir John Zouch in 1634 to 1636 also failed. The county page states the Powhatan aim as "to force the English to leave the Powhatan territory." (Chesterfield County page.) Wikipedia says two women and three children were among the 27 dead (unsourced there; unconfirmed). John Berkeley, the ironmaster, was among the dead (elements bank T-241e, via DHR/Wikipedia search summary). Why Opechancanough attacked: English tobacco planters taking Powhatan land without payment from 1618 to 1620 and Virginia Company pressure to take Powhatan children for English schooling (elements bank T-241e, Virtual Jamestown). Colony-wide dead: about 350 (NPS), 347 (Virtual Jamestown).
- **Whose land.** Powhatan territory, at the falls of the James River near present-day Richmond (Chesterfield County page; elements bank).
- **Correction to the bank's era-3 entry ("destroyed before it shipped").** Virginia DHR: "Workers completed a portion of the ironworks and produced iron in 1620," and calls Falling Creek "the first successful integrated iron production facility in English North America" (Virginia DHR, https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/historic-registers/020-0063/). The county page says Bluett's men could not finish the furnace and Berkeley was still promising iron for June 1622. **The sources disagree on whether Falling Creek made any iron.** The "first" claims also collide: DHR calls Falling Creek the first successful integrated ironworks, NPS calls Saugus the first sustained, integrated one. Tell it as: Falling Creek was the first ironworks begun in the English colonies; Saugus was the first that ran for years. Do not write "never made iron."

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **The Franklin stove.** Franklin designed his open metal-lined fireplace-stove in 1741–42 (sources give both years; the Franklin Institute uses 1742) to put more of a fire's heat into the room and less up the chimney. He refused to patent it — or any of his inventions — on the stated principle that ideas should serve everyone freely. (Sources: The Franklin Institute, "Benjamin Franklin's Inventions"; Environment & Society Portal.)
- **Franklin the printer-inventor** — a working Philadelphia printer whose shop trade made him the era's most famous colonial craftsman-inventor. His almanac and newspaper belong to `news-communication`; his electrical science to `science`; the stove and (next era) the lightning rod are this chapter's.
- **Colonial ironworks multiplied** in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia through the early 1700s — furnaces and forges, still water- and charcoal-powered (framing; detailed iron economics belong to `economy`/`elements`).
- Verified film for the Franklin story: ***Benjamin Franklin* (2022)** — two-part PBS documentary by Ken Burns, premiered April 4–5, 2022. (Sources: PBS; kenburns.com.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246, bank check): who worked the colonial ironworks of 1700 to 1750 (the "furnaces and forges multiplied" line)
- **Principio and Baltimore.** British investors set up the Principio Company in Cecil County, Maryland, in 1719. Five Maryland planters founded the Baltimore Iron Works in 1731 on the Patapsco River: Dr. Charles Carroll (who put in 2,200 acres and was first manager), Charles and Daniel Carroll, Daniel Dulaney and Benjamin Tasker. The partners supplied money and enslaved laborers. It ran from 1731 to 1799. (Mount Clare Museum, "From Baltimore Iron Works to Bethlehem Steel," https://www.mountclare.org/historic-site/industry/baltimore-iron-works.)
- **Who did the work.** "The Baltimore Iron Works opened with a workforce of 89, including 42 enslaved individuals. By 1785, it advertised a workforce of 'more than 200 Negroes.'" Laborers, "many of whom were enslaved or transported convicts from Britain" (convicts shipped from British prisons to work out their sentences), worked 12-hour shifts hauling charcoal, oyster shells and ore to the top of the furnace, around the clock, six or seven days a week. Managers preferred enslaved workers over convicts. Many enslaved ironworkers were Igbo people who brought metalworking skill from West Africa. (Mount Clare, same page.) Mount Clare's "Enslaved People" page: "more than 200 enslaved workers toiled at the Baltimore Iron Works" (https://www.mountclare.org/history/slave_industrialworks.html).
- **What was done to them.** The owners "failed to provide enough food or replacements for the depleted workforce"; in 1775 manager Clement Brooke wrote to the partners, "I cannot do my job unless you send more slaves and more corn," and the Mount Clare page states "The workers and animals were starving." Some workers ran away (a freedom-seeker advertisement in the *Maryland Gazette*, August 1754), others worked slowly. In spring 1771 an indentured servant, Thomas Livy, and an enslaved blacksmith named Anthony were jailed for robbing a store, likely of food; overseer William Hammond wrote, "I cannot tell how we can carry on the Forge business without him." (Mount Clare, "Anthony, Blacksmith," https://www.mountclare.org/people/enslaved-people/anthony-blacksmith.)
- **Who profited.** Each of the five owners took one-fifth of the pig iron; in good years the partners made a 55 percent annual return. In 1810 the remaining 30,000-plus acres and 200 enslaved people were auctioned for 30 heirs of the original shareholders. (Mount Clare, Baltimore Iron Works page.)
- **Principio.** Its early labor force was mainly British indentured servants plus some enslaved and free workers; by the mid-1700s more enslaved African Americans held skilled jobs there (Wikipedia "Principio Furnace," unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Whose land: the Mount Clare pages fetched do not name the Native nation of the Patapsco valley; not supplied. Shared with: `slavery-freedom` (the system), `work-workers` (the work), `elements` (the iron).

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-303a): Franklin's own reason for refusing a patent, and how the stove worked
- The Franklin Institute, "Benjamin Franklin's Inventions" (https://fi.edu/en/science-and-education/benjamin-franklin/inventions): in 1742 Franklin made a metal-lined fireplace set a few inches out from the chimney, with a hollow baffle at the back that mixed the fire's heat with air, and an inverted siphon that drew off more heat. It made less smoke than an ordinary fireplace. Franklin's stated reason for not patenting, quoted on the same page: "That as we enjoy great Advantages from the Inventions of others, we should be glad of an Opportunity to serve others by any Invention of ours; and this we should do freely and generously." (The words are from his Autobiography.)

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-455): the Franklin stove's inverted siphon, and who shipped convicts to Maryland (era 4)
- **Inverted siphon.** Wikipedia, "Franklin stove" (with the Franklin Institute page already in the bank): the inverted siphon was a U-shaped flue behind the stove. The hot gases of the fire passed up and over the hollow iron baffle, then down behind it and up the chimney, so the smoke took a longer path and the iron baffle took in more of its heat. Room air entered the bottom of the baffle box, was heated, and came out of side holes near its top. Franklin advertised the stove in a 1744 pamphlet, *An Account of the New Invented Pennsylvanian Fire-Places*.
- **Convict transportation.** Encyclopedia Virginia, "Convict Labor during the Colonial Period" (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/convict-labor-during-the-colonial-period/): Parliament passed the Transportation Act of 1718. English courts sentenced convicts to transportation; merchants under contract with the British government shipped them (Jonathan Forward held the first contract). In the colonies planters, merchants and iron manufacturers bought them, most for seven years and some for up to fourteen. Between 1700 and 1775 about 52,200 convicts sailed for the colonies, more than 20,000 of them to Virginia. A search summary adds that about 80 percent went to Maryland and Virginia (unconfirmed: search summary only).

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The lightning rod — the applied half of Franklin's electricity.** Franklin described protecting buildings from "electrical fire" in a 1750 letter to Peter Collinson; after the 1752 demonstrations (the French sentry-box experiment, then his own kite) the first lightning rods went up in Philadelphia. (Source: The Franklin Institute.) The kite and the science are `science`'s; the rod on the roof is this chapter's.
- **The cotton gin, 1793–94.** Eli Whitney, a Massachusetts-born Yale graduate visiting a Georgia plantation, built a machine in 1793 that pulled cotton fiber off its seeds; he applied for the patent in 1794 (granted March 14, 1794). (Sources: Mass Moments; National Archives education resources.)
- **What the gin did to slavery — the honest consequence.** Cleaning upland cotton by hand had been the bottleneck; the gin removed it. U.S. cotton production went from under 9,000 bales a year around 1790 to over 200,000 by 1800 and about 4 million by 1860. The enslaved population counted in the census grew from 697,897 (1790) to nearly 4 million (1860), and slavery spread west across the cotton belt — the machine that was supposed to save labor instead multiplied the demand for enslaved labor. State plainly, then hand off: the system and the lives inside it are `slavery-freedom`'s. (Sources: National Archives "Eli Whitney's Patent for the Cotton Gin"; DPLA primary-source set "Cotton Gin and the Expansion of Slavery"; EH.net "Cotton Gin.")
- **Interchangeable parts — the myth and the record.** Whitney took a federal musket contract in 1798 and promoted the idea of machine-made, interchangeable parts; the famous 1801 demonstration before officials is part of the legend. Historian Robert S. Woodbury showed in 1960 that the credit is a myth: the idea originated in Europe (French armories) before Whitney, Whitney's own factory never achieved true interchangeability, and the best-documented first American production use is John H. Hall's rifle works at Harpers Ferry armory, assembling firearms from interchangeable parts by 1819. Tell the armory version, not the legend. (Sources: Woodbury, "The Legend of Eli Whitney," *Technology and Culture* 1960, summarized by Linda Hall Library "Eli Whitney" and *Commonplace* "How Eli Whitney Single-handedly Started the Civil War... and Why That's Not True.")
- **Slater's mill, 1790.** British law barred exporting textile machinery and the emigration of skilled textile workers. Samuel Slater, trained in an Arkwright-system mill in Derbyshire, left England in 1789 without declaring his trade, and rebuilt Arkwright's carding and spinning machines from memory in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, with local craftsmen — capital and building from the merchants Almy & Brown. The mill spun its first yarn in December 1790: the start of the American factory textile industry. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Slater's Spinning Mill"; Encyclopedia.com "Samuel Slater Builds the First Factory.") Child workers in Slater-system mills are `work-workers`' story.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246, bank check): the cotton gin, who else is credited, who did the work, who profited, whose land
- **How the gin came to be built.** After Yale (1792) Whitney was travelling south to be a tutor when Catherine Littlefield Greene, widow of General Nathanael Greene, invited him to her plantation north of Savannah, Georgia (Mulberry Grove). It grew mostly rice, and some cotton. Removing the sticky seeds from cotton "was done by hand by enslaved laborers," and one worker needed many hours to clean one pound of fiber. Whitney's machine used two cylinders that combed the cotton through small holes, leaving the seeds behind. One small gin could clean 50 pounds a day, "the equivalent of several hundred hours of hand labor." (Eli Whitney Museum and Workshop, "The Cotton Gin and Its Legacies," https://www.eliwhitney.org/cotton-gin-and-its-legacies.)
- **Credit dispute.** The Eli Whitney Museum: "It has been suggested that Catherine Greene also contributed ideas to the design." Matilda Joslyn Gage's 1883 pamphlet *Woman as Inventor* first claimed Greene, not Whitney, invented the gin; later writers credit her with the idea of the brush that strips fiber off the teeth and with wire teeth (unconfirmed: search summary only, Encyclopedia.com and New England Historical Society entries). Greene and Phineas Miller, Whitney's partner, paid for his patent lawsuits (same, unconfirmed). Say: Whitney held the patent; some historians credit Greene with part of the design; the record does not settle it. No source found credits a named enslaved person with the gin; do not claim one.
- **Who did the work and what it did to them.** The Eli Whitney Museum: enslaved workers "picked around 200 pounds a day of cotton under the summer sun, and faced violence if they did not meet expected quotas." Enslaved workers also ran the gins and presses: "the machines had many hazardous moving parts, and the small cotton fibers expelled into the air caused lung damage." About 700,000 people were enslaved in 1790; about 4 million by 1860, an increase of more than 550 percent; about 2.5 million in the cotton-growing regions from eastern Maryland to Texas (same page; census figure 697,897 for 1790 already in bank).
- **Who profited.** The museum: the gin "increased southern plantation owners' economic and political power," fed cotton mills in the North and in Britain, and by 1830 finished cotton goods were the country's largest industrial export. Whitney himself lost most of his gin profits in patent-infringement suits (Wikipedia via search summary, unconfirmed).
- **Whose land.** The museum: in the 1830s the demand for new cotton land was "a major factor" in the removal of the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole nations to Indian Territory; "at least 60,000 people made the journey, with more than 8,000 people dying on the way," and their land went to white landowners. The museum names "the American government" as the actor; the named actors (President Andrew Jackson, the 1830 Indian Removal Act, General Winfield Scott in 1838) are in `native-nations` and the elements bank. Lead: `native-nations`.
- **A number to avoid copying.** The museum page converts "less than 9,000 bales" to "about 45,000 pounds." At its own 500 pounds a bale that is about 4.5 million pounds. Use bales only.

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-303a): how the lightning rod works, in Franklin's own instructions
- Franklin's instructions were printed in *Poor Richard's Almanack* for 1753 under the heading "How to secure Houses, &c. from Lightning" (Founders Online, Franklin Papers vol. 4, https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-04-02-0148, page did not render; text via search summary of the Founders and bibliomania reprints, and the Franklin Institute page below). Provide a small iron rod. Put one end three or four feet into moist ground. Let the other end rise six or eight feet above the highest part of the building. Fasten a foot of brass wire, the thickness of a knitting needle and sharpened to a fine point, to the top. Hold the rod to the house with small staples. Franklin: a house so furnished "will not be damaged by Lightning, it being attracted by the Points, and passing" through the metal into the ground. Full text confirmed on the bibliomania reprint of Franklin's *Autobiography, Letters and Other Writings* (http://www.bibliomania.com/2/9/77/124/21475/1.html), which ends the sentence "passing thro the Metal into the Ground without hurting any Thing." The same text says a long house or barn may have a rod and point at each end joined by a wire along the ridge, and a ship may carry a pointed rod on its mast with a wire down to the water.
- The Franklin Institute, "Benjamin Franklin's Inventions" (https://fi.edu/en/science-and-education/benjamin-franklin/inventions): Franklin proposed "upright rods of iron made sharp as a needle" on the highest parts of buildings. "Franklin's pointed lightning rod design proved effective and soon topped buildings throughout the Colonies." (Confirmed on the page.)
- Use: the rod gives lightning a metal path from above the roof down into the ground, so the electricity passes through the rod instead of through the wooden building.

- **Owner of the first steam engine.** Richard P. ## 6. 1800 to 1850

- **The telegraph, May 24, 1844.** Samuel Morse sent "What hath God wrought" (Numbers 23:23) from the U.S. Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore over the government-funded Washington–Baltimore line. The message was chosen by Annie G. Ellsworth, daughter of the Patent Commissioner. The paper tape survives. (Sources: U.S. Senate historical office; Smithsonian NMAH object record "'What Hath God Wrought' Telegraph Message.")
- **Vail's role — say it honestly.** Alfred Vail built and improved the instruments at the Speedwell Ironworks shop and received the first public message in Baltimore. Historians disagree about how much of the dot-dash alphabet is his: accounts range from "Morse invented, Vail improved" to crediting Vail with the practical letter frequencies (short codes for common letters). Morse held the patents and the fame. State the disagreement; do not resolve it. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAH documentation of Vail's instruments; HistoryNet "Morse's Partner Argued He Invented Famous Code.")
- **The sewing machine and the first big patent war.** Elias Howe patented the two-thread lockstitch machine September 10, 1846. Isaac Singer's more practical machine sold better; Howe sued, and in 1854 the courts upheld Howe's patent — makers owed him royalties (as much as $4,000 a month at peak). In 1856 Howe and the major makers (Singer, Wheeler & Wilson, Grover & Baker) pooled their patents in the Sewing Machine Combination, which controlled the trade until 1877. (Sources: Library of Congress business-history guide "Elias Howe Issued a Patent for the Sewing Machine"; ABA Journal "Sept. 10, 1846: The Sewing Machine Patent War"; Mass Moments.)
- **Vulcanized rubber, 1839 → patent 1844.** Charles Goodyear's rubber-and-sulfur mixture met a hot stove in Woburn, Massachusetts in 1839 and charred like leather instead of melting. Accounts differ on exactly how the rubber hit the stove (dropped, or flung during an animated sales pitch) — the accident is documented, the details vary; say so. It took him five more years to make the process repeatable; patent 1844. (Sources: Britannica "Charles Goodyear"; ConnecticutHistory.org.)
- **The reaper and the steel plow** — McCormick's reaper (first public demonstration 1831, patent 1834) and John Deere's self-scouring steel plow (1837): see parked farm-machine notes at bottom, already verified (Britannica; ASME landmark; Virginia Museum of History & Culture).

## 7. 1850 to 1900

- **The telephone and the same-day filing, February 14, 1876.** Bell's attorney filed his application "Improvement in Telegraphy" that morning (5th filing of the day); Elisha Gray's caveat for a very similar instrument arrived hours later (39th). Both described a liquid transmitter. Patent 174,465 issued to Bell March 7, 1876; Gray, on his lawyers' advice, withdrew. Bell's notebook records the first spoken sentence by telephone on March 10, 1876 ("Mr. Watson — come here — I want to see you"). The dispute over who deserved priority has been argued ever since, including a 2020 IEEE *Proceedings* re-examination — present the same-day filing as fact, Bell as the patent holder, and the controversy as real. (Sources: Library of Congress "Who is credited with inventing the telephone?"; IEEE Xplore, "The Bell Versus Gray Telephone Dispute" (2020); Bell family papers at LOC.)
- **Emma Nutt — the first woman telephone operator.** Hired September 1, 1878 by the telephone dispatch company in Boston, away from a telegraph office; paid $10 a month for a 54-hour week. Her sister Stella followed the same day. Companies replaced teenage boy operators (rude, prank-prone) with women, and by the late 1880s operating was an almost entirely female occupation — one of the first big technology jobs for women. She worked as an operator for over 30 years (accounts of the exact span vary, roughly 33–37 years). (Sources: New England Historical Society; Boston Magazine; Time.) The operator's working day at scale is `work-workers`' angle.
- **Edison — improver and system-builder, not the light bulb's sole inventor.** Inventors had worked on incandescent lamps for 40 years before 1879 (Swan in England among them). Edison's Menlo Park team — Charles Batchelor, John Kruesi, Francis Upton and others — produced in October 1879 a carbonized cotton-thread filament lamp that burned about 14.5 hours, demonstrated publicly at Menlo Park on New Year's Eve 1879; longer-lived bamboo filaments followed. What Edison actually built that others had not: the whole working SYSTEM — lamp, dynamo, wiring, metering — and the Pearl Street station (September 4, 1882, 257 Pearl Street, Manhattan) that lit the first paying district. Phonograph: John Kruesi built the tinfoil machine to Edison's design in early December 1877; Edison recorded "Mary had a little lamb" on December 6, 1877. (Sources: U.S. Department of Energy "The History of the Light Bulb"; Smithsonian NMAH 1879 lamp object record; IEEE Milestone "Pearl Street Station, 1882"; Rutgers Thomas A. Edison Papers "Tinfoil Phonograph.") The current itself and the AC-vs-DC fight are `energy`'s (its Edison/Westinghouse-Tesla stories exist).
- **Lewis Latimer — the honest role.** Self-taught draftsman (son of parents who escaped slavery); drew the patent drawings for Bell's 1876 telephone application while at a patent law firm. At Hiram Maxim's U.S. Electric Lighting Co. he received patent 252,386 (January 17, 1882) for a "Process of Manufacturing Carbons" — a better way to MAKE carbon filaments, more uniform and longer-lasting; he did not invent the filament or the bulb. Joined Edison's operation in 1884: patent drawings, expert-witness work, and supervising lamp-plant and lighting installations (New York, Philadelphia, Montreal, London). The only Black member of the Edison Pioneers (1918). (Sources: Rutgers Edison Papers, "Inventing a Better Life: Latimer's Technical Career"; National Inventors Hall of Fame; Lewis Latimer House Museum; Smithsonian collections.)
- **Granville T. Woods.** Black inventor working in Cincinnati; patented the Synchronous Multiplex Railway Telegraph November 29, 1887 — induction-based messages between moving trains and stations, so dispatchers knew where trains were. Won patent interference proceedings twice against claims pressed by Edison's side, and turned down an offer to join Edison; dozens of electrical and railway patents across his career. (Sources: National Inventors Hall of Fame; APS News "Granville Woods Patents Induction Telegraph"; Encyclopedia.com; New York Transit Museum digital exhibit.)
- **Tesla's induction motor.** Nikola Tesla (Serbian immigrant, arrived 1884) patented the AC induction motor (patent 381,968, May 1, 1888, filed October 1887); in July 1888 Westinghouse licensed Tesla's polyphase patents for $60,000 in cash and stock plus a $2.50-per-horsepower royalty. The motor — electricity turned into rotating work without brushes — is this chapter's machine; the grid war is `energy`'s. (Sources: IEEE Spectrum "May 1888: Tesla Files His Patents for the Electric Motor"; The Henry Ford, Westinghouse induction motor artifact.)
- **The typewriter.** Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden, and Samuel Soule patented their machine June 23, 1868; E. Remington & Sons began production March 1, 1873, and the Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer sold from 1874 at $125 — carrying the QWERTY keyboard still in use. (Sources: Library of Congress business-history guide; Britannica.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-246, bank check): Ned, the enslaved inventor, and the 1858 ruling that no Black inventor could hold a patent
- **The invention.** Sometime before August 25, 1857, near Holmesville, Pike County, Mississippi, an enslaved blacksmith named Ned invented an improved "double Cotton Scraper, and two plows" that could "Scrape both Sides of the Cotton ridge at the same time, and plough out the middles." All that is known of Ned comes from letters by the man who claimed to own him, Oscar J. E. Stuart, a white Mississippi planter and lawyer; Ned's second name, if he had one, is not recorded, and how his life ended is not known. (Kara W. Swanson, "Race and Selective Legal Memory: Reflections on Invention of a Slave," *Columbia Law Review* 120, no. 4 (2020), pp. 1077-1116, https://columbialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Swanson-Race_and_Selective_Legal_Memory.pdf, citing Stuart's letters reprinted by Yancy 1984 and Boyle 1960, originals in the National Archives.)
- **What Stuart did.** Stuart applied for the patent November 15, 1857, swearing that he owned Ned and that Ned was the inventor. He threatened to "correct" Ned "according to our Southern usage" if Ned contacted the Patent Office himself. Patent Commissioner Joseph Holt returned the application: it lacked the inventor's oath, and he held that an enslaved man could not take it. Stuart complained to Interior Secretary Jacob Thompson, who sent the question to Attorney General Jeremiah S. Black. (Swanson.)
- **SEARCHED, NOT FOUND 2026-10-02 (T-487): what "correct ... according to our Southern usage" meant in practice.** Swanson, "Race and Selective Legal Memory", *Columbia Law Review* 120:4 (2020), p. 1085 (PDF opened and text-extracted): "Oscar threatened to 'correct' Ned 'according to our Southern usage' should he dare contact the patent office himself." Her note 46: the words are in Stuart's letter to Interior Secretary Jacob Thompson (printed in the source at her note 39, pp. 791-94), also quoted in Portia James, *The Real McCoy* (1989), p. 49. Swanson gives no gloss of the phrase. HistoryNet, "Whose Patent Is It?" (opened) does not quote the threat. A search-engine summary glossed it as whipping, with no source page behind the gloss: not used. Prose keeps "To correct meant to punish" and adds that the threat was in Stuart's later letter to the secretary of the interior and did not say what the punishment would be.
- **The ruling.** Black's 1858 opinion, "Invention of a Slave," 9 Op. Att'y Gen. 171, held that "a machine invented by a slave, though it be new and useful, cannot, in the present state of the law, be patented." Black relied on the Supreme Court's *Dred Scott* decision of 1857 that African Americans were not citizens. Without citizenship, an enslaved inventor could not swear the oath, and the owner could not patent it either because only the "original and first inventor" could. Swanson: the opinion "created a formal racial barrier to the United States patent system, which free African Americans had previously accessed." (Swanson.)
- **After.** Stuart twice petitioned Congress and failed; a 1859 Senate bill backed by Mississippi Senator Albert Gallatin Brown to let enslavers patent enslaved people's inventions did not advance. Stuart sold the "Stuart Double Plough and Scraper" anyway. The Confederate patent law of May 21, 1861 let enslavers patent inventions of enslaved people. Benjamin Montgomery, enslaved in Mississippi by Joseph Davis, invented an improved boat propeller; Joseph Davis tried to patent it through his brother, Senator Jefferson Davis, and failed. After freedom Montgomery tried to patent it in his own name and failed (per his son Isaiah Montgomery). The end of slavery and the Reconstruction amendments ended the bar. In 1913 Henry E. Baker, a Black patent examiner, published *The Colored Inventor* for the NAACP's *Crisis* to mark 50 years of emancipation. (Swanson and her notes.)

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-303a): how the telephone and the electric lamp work
- Bell's own 1876 description, "Bell's Articulating Telephone," *Journal of the Society of Telegraph Engineers*, 1876, pp. 519-525 (reprinted at https://earlyradiohistory.us/1876bel.htm): "When a note or a word is sounded into the mouthpiece of the transmitter, its membrane vibrates in unison with the sound." "By October 1879, Edison's team had produced a light bulb with a carbonized filament of uncoated cotton thread that could last for 14.5 hours." ## 8. 1900 to 1950

- **The Wright Flyer, December 17, 1903.** Four flights near Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina: Orville first, 120 feet in 12 seconds; Wilbur's fourth flight, 852 feet in 59 seconds. ### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-303b): how the Wright control, the Audion and the transistor worked; ENIAC's job and size; the cause of the 1908 crash
- **The Wrights' three kinds of control.** NASA Glenn Research Center, "Aircraft Control - 1902 Glider" (https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/aircraft-control-1902-glider/): "A rolling motion is an up and down movement of the wing tips of the aircraft." The Wrights used "wing warping to achieve roll control. Through a series of cables, the Wrights twisted the wing tips up or down relative to the rest of the wing." "A pitch motion is an up or down movement of the nose of the aircraft." "During the flights of 1901 the Wrights included a movable elevator at the front of the aircraft to control pitch." "A yaw motion is a movement of the nose of the aircraft from side to side." "The movable rudder was added to the 1902 aircraft to provide yaw control."
- **The 1908 crash, cause.** Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum image caption "First Fatal Crash in an Airplane" (https://airandspace.si.edu/multimedia-gallery/image/5840hjpg): "On September 17, 1908, with Army observer Lt. Thomas E. Selfridge on board, the airplane experienced a mechanical malfunction involving one of the propellers and crashed." Orville hospitalized seven weeks, Selfridge died several hours later (army.mil search summary, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **The Audion.** National MagLab, "Audion - 1906" (https://nationalmaglab.org/magnet-academy/history-of-electricity-magnetism/museum/audion-1906/): "like a light bulb, the Audion contained a metal plate (the anode) and a filament (the cathode) inside of glass housing," with "a piece of bent wire called the control grid" between them. By "applying a small electric current to the grid" one could "modulate and amplify the signal between the other two electrodes." It was "the first solely electronic component to make amplification of electromagnetic signals possible." It "detected radio signals."
- **The point-contact transistor.** Computer History Museum, The Silicon Engine, "1947: Invention of the Point-Contact Transistor" (https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-point-contact-transistor/): Bardeen and Brattain "applied two closely-spaced gold contacts held in place by a plastic wedge to the surface of a small slab of high-purity germanium." "The voltage on one contact modulated the current flowing through the other, amplifying the input signal up to 100 times." Shockley's group was seeking "semiconductor replacements for unreliable vacuum tubes and electromechanical switches."
- **ENIAC's job and size.** National Museum of the United States Army, "ENIAC Computer" (https://www.thenmusa.org/armyinnovations/innovationeniaccomputer/): the Army provided the resources; its purpose was "artillery firing tables, charts that helped Soldiers determine the best aim for their weapons"; "ENIAC contained 17,468 vacuum tubes," "weighed more than 27 tons, occupied 1800 square feet." Penn Today, "The world's first general purpose computer turns 75" (https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/worlds-first-general-purpose-computer-turns-75): "Designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert" at Penn's Moore School; it "had no internal storage, it had to be programmed manually for each new set of calculations"; the programmers had to "adjust ENIAC's switches"; Kathy Kleiman learned of the six while researching her undergraduate thesis, and her work led to their "1997 Hall of Fame induction" (Penn Today does not name the hall in the passage fetched, so the prose does not name it).

## 9. 1950 to 2000

- **The integrated circuit — two inventors, honestly.** Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) demonstrated the first integrated circuit September 12, 1958 — germanium, wired by hand. Robert Noyce (Fairchild Semiconductor) in 1959 designed the planar silicon version whose flat, photolithographed wiring could be mass-produced — the form every later chip took. Both are credited as co-inventors; Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics 2000 (Noyce died in 1990; Kilby publicly said Noyce deserved to share it). (Sources: Britannica "Jack Kilby"; DPMA "65 Years Integrated Circuit"; NobelPrize.org.)
- **Grace Hopper.** Mathematician (b. December 9, 1906, New York); Navy WAVES lieutenant assigned in July 1944 to the Harvard Mark I, one of its first programmers; wrote the first extensive computer programming manual (1946). At Remington Rand's UNIVAC she built the A-0 compiler (1951–52) — the idea that programs could be written closer to human language and translated by the machine — then FLOW-MATIC (1955–59), the English-like business language that shaped COBOL. Retired 1986, age 79, then the Navy's oldest active-duty officer, as a rear admiral; died January 1, 1992. (Sources: IEEE Milestone "A-0 Compiler, 1951–1952"; Britannica; Naval History and Heritage Command.) Verified film: ***The Queen of Code* (2015)**, short documentary, dir. Gillian Jacobs, FiveThirtyEight *Signals* series.
- **The personal computer.** The MITS Altair 8800 kit (announced in *Popular Electronics*, January 1975) triggered the hobby; the Homebrew Computer Club first met March 5, 1975 in Gordon French's Menlo Park garage — about 30 people in a rainstorm, gathered to see an Altair. Steve Wozniak credited the club as the seedbed of the Apple I. Apple Computer founded April 1, 1976 (Jobs, Wozniak, and briefly Ron Wayne); the Apple II (introduced June 1977) put a finished, plug-in computer in homes; the IBM PC (August 12, 1981) made the office standard. (Sources: Computer History Museum, Homebrew reunion materials; Library of Congress business-history guide "The Founding of Apple Computer, Inc."; IEEE Spectrum "How the IBM PC Won.")
- **Lee Felsenstein** — Homebrew's longtime meeting moderator, an engineer whose oral history is preserved by the Computer History Museum; a documented ordinary-side figure of the hobbyist wave beyond the famous founders. (Source: Computer History Museum, Homebrew Computer Club 2013 reunion.)
- **Films for the Jobs/Wozniak story:** ***Steve Jobs* (2015)** — Danny Boyle/Aaron Sorkin **dramatization** (label it as drama, from Isaacson's book); documentaries: ***Steve Jobs: The Man in the Machine* (2015)**, dir. Alex Gibney; ***Something Ventured* (2011)** covers Apple's early venture funding. All verified (IMDb/Wikipedia/Zeitgeist Films).
- **ARPANET, October 29, 1969.** Student programmer Charley Kline at UCLA (Leonard Kleinrock's lab) sent the first host-to-host message to Stanford Research Institute: the system crashed after "L-O" of "LOGIN," making the first message "LO"; the full login worked about an hour later. ARPANET was Defense-Department-funded university infrastructure — the seed of the internet. The ARPANET adopted the internet's shared protocol (TCP/IP) on January 1, 1983. (Sources: UCLA Samueli "Internet 50"; ICANN "The First Message Transmission"; Kleinrock's own account, lk.cs.ucla.edu.)
- **The web is NOT an American invention — say so.** Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist at CERN in Switzerland, invented the World Wide Web (proposal 1989; files opened to the public August 6, 1991). America's precise role: the underlying internet grew from U.S.-funded ARPANET, and the first widely used graphical browser — Mosaic — was released April 22, 1993 by Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina, and colleagues at the University of Illinois' NCSA, which put the web in ordinary hands. (Sources: CERN "A short history of the Web"; EBSCO; NCSA, University of Illinois; NSF.)
- **GPS.** First Navstar satellite launched February 22, 1978; the 24-satellite constellation reached Full Operational Capability in 1995; on May 1, 2000 the U.S. ended Selective Availability, giving civilians full-precision signals — the moment consumer GPS became sharp enough for turn-by-turn navigation. (Sources: Penn State GEOG 862 course materials; 2000 presidential directive, widely documented.)
- **The handheld cell phone.** Motorola engineer Martin Cooper made the first handheld cellular call April 3, 1973, walking in Manhattan — calling Joel Engel, his rival at Bell Labs, to tell him. The commercial Motorola DynaTAC 8000X followed in 1983: $3,995, 28 ounces, about 30 minutes of talk. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "First Cellular Phone Call"; 50th-anniversary coverage by NPR/ABC 2023 — journalism.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-246, bank check): surveillance, a face-recognition wrongful arrest
