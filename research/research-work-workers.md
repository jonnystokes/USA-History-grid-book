## 01 · Before 1500 — work without wages (thin)

- Work in Native North America was organized by season and by household, not by wages, employers, or clocks. In Eastern Woodlands communities, women farmed (corn, beans, squash) and gathered; men hunted and fished; men and women joined together on big jobs such as harvests, maple sugaring, and canoe building. Labor gathered into larger groups at planting and harvest. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Laborers, Native American, Eastern Woodland"; American Yawp ch. 1; Kids Discover / Lenape teaching materials.)
- Where farming supported towns, some people could specialize — traders, stoneworkers, religious leaders. (Source: American Yawp ch. 1.) Keep general; no invented detail about specific workshops.
- Honest framing for the cell: the work is real, but this chapter's subject — the job you are hired for, paid for, and can lose — did not exist here yet. No documented individual at story zoom from this angle; era marked **thin**.

## 02 · The 1500s — the first organized work by newcomers was forced (thin)

- **Encomienda:** the Spanish crown's labor system let settlers extract forced labor and tribute from Native people in exchange for "protection and religious instruction." Applied from Florida across the Southwest. (Sources: Britannica "encomienda"; EBSCO "Native America–White Relations—Spanish Colonial".)
- **Repartimiento** replaced/supplemented it from the mid-1500s: colonial officials allocated rotating drafts of Native workers to projects; workers were nominally paid. In New Mexico, governors used the repartimiento — and illegal slave labor — in agriculture and commerce. (Sources: Britannica "repartimiento"; Wikipedia "Repartimiento" cross-checked with Britannica.)
- The enslavement itself is `slavery-freedom`'s (its bank already carries the 1542 New Laws detail). Our slice: this was the era's only organized "employment" by newcomers — forced, unpaid or barely paid. Era **thin**.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): forced work as a sentence, Acoma 1599 (pointer)
- After Spanish soldiers under **Juan de Oñate** attacked Acoma Pueblo (December 1598 to January 1599), Oñate sentenced the survivors in February 1599: **men over 25** to have one foot cut off and to **twenty years of personal servitude**, and **males 12 to 25 and females over 12** to twenty years of servitude. "Servitude" here meant being made to work for Spanish households, not free to leave, for twenty years. (Carlson 2008, citing the trial record; EBSCO; NLM Native Voices; all in `research-native-nations.md`, which leads on Acoma.) ## 03 · The 1600s — bound labor

- **Indentured servitude was the era's labor system.** Between one-half and two-thirds (some scholars say up to three-quarters) of European immigrants to the colonies arrived as indentured or redemptioner servants — state the range. Typical terms: four to seven years. At the end came "freedom dues": at various times and places land, clothing, corn, tobacco, tools, a musket, blankets, or some combination. (Sources: EH.net "Indentured Servitude in the Colonial U.S."; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History; PBS History Detectives.)
- Apprenticeship bound children and teenagers to masters to learn a trade (era 04 has the Franklin story). Household production — spinning, weaving, brewing, candle-making — was the rest of the working world, told by `home-family` from the household side.
- Enslaved labor begins in the English colonies in this century (1619 pointer only — the system is `slavery-freedom`'s).
- **Richard Frethorne (ordinary story, verified).** Indentured servant at Martin's Hundred, Virginia; arrived December 1622 on the *Abigail*, likely a young teenager. In March–April 1623 he wrote letters begging his parents in England to buy out his indenture or send food: since leaving the ship he had eaten nothing but peas and "loblollie" (water gruel), and "when we are sick there is nothing to comfort us." A muster list compiled by February 1624 records him ("Frethram") among the dead at Martin's Hundred. (Sources: Virtual Jamestown transcription; Encyclopedia Virginia / Wikipedia cross-check; Teaching American History document text.) No film.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): what bound servants' work was like, and what was done to runaways
- About **50,000** servants, **three-quarters of all new arrivals**, came to the Chesapeake colonies between **1630 and 1680**, about six men for every woman in the 1630s. They worked tobacco "from dawn until dusk, six days a week through the growing season." Adult indentures ran four to seven years. The "seasoning" sickness of the first summer killed a majority of newcomers, and one writer of the time said immigrants died "like cats and dogs." A woman servant who became pregnant had time added to her term or owed her master 1,000 pounds of tobacco. Freedom dues for a man: ten bushels of corn, thirty shillings and a musket. For a woman: fifteen bushels of corn and forty shillings. (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Indentured Servants in Colonial Virginia," opened.) This adds a third estimate (three-quarters, Chesapeake 1630 to 1680) to the range above.
- **Runaways.** On **July 9, 1640** the Virginia General Court ordered two runaway servants of **Hugh Gwyn**, "Victor, a Dutchman" and **James Gregory**, a Scotsman, to be whipped with **thirty lashes** each and to serve extra years. The third runaway, **John Punch**, "a negro," was ordered to serve his master "for the time of his natural Life." On **July 22, 1640** the court ordered thirty lashes for other runaways and the letter **R** branded on Christopher Miller, Peter Wilcocke and a Black man named Emanuel, who was also made to work in shackles. (Encyclopedia Virginia primary document, "General Court Responds to Runaway Servants and Slaves (1640)," opened.) The **March 2, 1643** law: a runaway served **double** the time he was gone, and a repeat runaway was "branded in the cheek with the letter R." (Encyclopedia Virginia primary document, "Runaway Servants (1643)," opened.) **Clinical-word note (hard-subjects §3b):** the sources give the letter and the place (the cheek) but do not state the method. A writer must source the method (a hot iron) before stating it. Otherwise write "marked for life with the letter R on the cheek" and say the records here do not describe how.
- John Punch and the start of slavery for life are `slavery-freedom`'s. Work angle here: the same court gave white servants extra years and a Black servant his whole life.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): whose land Martin's Hundred was, and the attack of 1622
- Martin's Hundred was a plantation of about **20,000 acres** on the James River, founded **1617** by the Society of Martin's Hundred, London investors led by attorney **Richard Martin**. Its town was **Wolstenholme Towne**, about seven miles downriver from Jamestown. The land lay inside **Tsenacomoco**, the Powhatan paramount chiefdom, an alliance of Algonquian-speaking nations whose land ran from the James River to the Potomac and west to the fall line, about 15,000 people (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Tsenacomoco (Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom)," search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only). On **March 22, 1622** Powhatan warriors attacked the English settlements and killed about **347** colonists, about a third of the colony. At Wolstenholme Towne **73** were killed, the most of any settlement. (Wikipedia "Martin's Hundred" and "Wolstenholme Towne"; Williamsburg Yorktown Daily, March 22, 2022, search summaries: unconfirmed, search summary only.) Frethorne arrived that December, nine months after the attack.

## 04 · 1700 to 1750 — the craft ladder, the docks, and the growing enslaved workforce

- **The craft ladder:** apprentice → journeyman (a wage-earner paid by the day) → master with a shop. This was the standard path in printing, shoemaking, carpentry, smithing.
- **Benjamin Franklin as a bound apprentice (famous story, verified).** Apprenticed at 12 (1718) to his brother James, a Boston printer; in September 1723, with about four years left on his indenture, he ran away — first to New York, then Philadelphia, arriving October 6, 1723, nearly penniless. He later admitted he may have been "too saucy and provoking," but also described James's beatings. Classic runaway-apprentice story of the era. (Sources: Massachusetts Historical Society; UPenn University Archives biography; ushistory.org.) **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022), Ken Burns two-part PBS documentary (aired April 4–5, 2022) — verified via PBS/kenburns.com.
- **Sailors and the waterfront:** the other big wage-earning workforce. Impressment — the Royal Navy seizing men for forced naval service — fell on this class. **Knowles Riot, Boston, beginning November 17, 1747:** after Commodore Charles Knowles's press gangs seized 46 men (boatmen, mariners, even carpenters), a crowd of mostly working-class Bostonians rioted for three days, seized naval officers as hostages, and forced the release of the pressed men in exchange. (Sources: Wikipedia "Knowles Riot" cross-checked with New England Historical Society and J.L. Bell's Boston 1775.)
- The enslaved workforce grew fast in the Chesapeake and Lowcountry; enslaved men also worked skilled trades in towns and were "hired out" for wages their owners kept. Daily-labor detail (gang/task systems, sunup-to-sundown) is told in `slavery-freedom`'s bank — do not repeat; one work-angle line with Shared-with.

## 05 · 1750 to 1800 — working people begin acting together (thin at story zoom)

- **Philadelphia journeymen printers, 1786 — the first authenticated strike** by employees against an employer in the U.S. in a single trade. They struck (beginning June 2, 1786 per labor-history accounts) against a wage cut and won a minimum of **$6 a week**; they also created the first strike-benefit fund on record, pledging mutual support. (Sources: Berkeley IRLE "Important Events in American Labor History 1778–1978" [a U.S. DOL bicentennial compilation]; ExplorePAhistory; Rosemont, *American Labor's First Strike*.)
  - **Dispute to state honestly:** journeymen tailors in New York struck in 1768, and earlier colonial work stoppages exist; the 1786 printers are called the first *authenticated* strike of hired workers against an employer. Say "often called the first" and name the 1768 claim.
- **Federal Society of Journeymen Cordwainers** (shoemakers), Philadelphia, organized May 1794 — the first sustained trade union in the U.S., calling "turn-outs" (strikes) to hold a standard wage. (Sources: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia "Cordwainers Trial of 1806"; JURIST.)
- No verified individual worker story found at story zoom for this era; the era carries spans only and is marked **thin** for stories. (Franklin sits in era 04 by the boundary rule — his apprenticeship and escape date 1718–1723.)

## 06 · 1800 to 1850 — the factory, the bell, and the ten-hour fight

- **The cordwainers' conspiracy trial, 1806 (Commonwealth v. Pullis, Philadelphia Mayor's Court):** after the November 1805 turn-out, eight union shoemakers were tried and found guilty "of a conspiracy to raise their wages," fined $8 each plus costs. Unions were treated as criminal conspiracies. (Sources: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Encyclopedia.com "Philadelphia Cordwainers Trial: 1806".)
- **Commonwealth v. Hunt (Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, 1842):** Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw ruled that trade unions were not criminal conspiracies as such and could lawfully strike and organize. (Sources: Britannica; EBSCO.)
- **Lowell, Massachusetts:** the model factory town. Young New England women lived in company boardinghouses and worked 12 to 14 hours a day, timed by the factory bells. (Sources: Britannica "Sarah G. Bagley"; NPS Lowell; historymatters.gmu.edu.)
  - **February 1834:** mill managers announced wage cuts of 12–25%; about 800 women "turned out" (struck). The strike failed. (Source: historymatters.gmu.edu / Who Built America.)
  - **October 1836:** about 1,500 struck over a boardinghouse rate hike (an effective wage cut), forming the Factory Girls' Association; this one was better organized and partly succeeded. (Sources: historymatters.gmu.edu; AFL-CIO labor history.)
  - **Lowell Female Labor Reform Association**, organized winter 1844–45 (Britannica says December 1844) under president **Sarah Bagley** — petitioned for the ten-hour day and pushed the Massachusetts legislature into an investigation of mill conditions. (Sources: Britannica; NPS "Sarah Bagley".)
- **The ten-hour movement:** the **1835 Philadelphia general strike** — begun by Schuylkill dock coal heavers; by June 10 over forty trades and nearly 20,000 workers out — won the ten-hour day and raises from the city; the first general strike in the U.S. (Sources: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia "General Trades Union Strike (1835)"; Wikipedia cross-check.) **President Van Buren's executive order, March 31, 1840:** ten-hour maximum day for laborers on federal public works (private employers unaffected). (Source: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.)
- Child labor was normal and unremarkable in law; children tended machines and did "doffing" (swapping bobbins). Canal and railroad construction gangs — heavily Irish immigrant labor — did the digging and grading (the lines and canals themselves are `transportation`'s).
- **Harriet Hanson Robinson (ordinary story, verified).** Born February 8, 1825; began work in a Lowell mill at age 10 as a doffer; during the October 1836 turnout, aged 11, she led the hesitating girls of her room out ("I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether anyone else does or not"). Worked in the mills until 1848; published the memoir *Loom and Spindle, or Life Among the Early Mill Girls* in 1898; died 1911; later a suffrage advocate. (Sources: historymatters.gmu.edu (memoir excerpt); Internet Archive edition of *Loom and Spindle*; Freedom's Way National Heritage Area biography.) No film.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): Lowell, whose land, whose cotton, and the working day
- **Land.** The mills at Lowell were built at **Pawtucket Falls** on the Merrimack River, a fishing and gathering place of the **Pennacook** people. In the 1600s two Native towns stood inside today's Lowell, **Pawtucket and Wamesit**, under the sachems **Passaconaway** and his son **Wannalancet**. During King Philip's War (1675 to 1676) Wannalancet and his people moved north to St. Francis in Canada, and by the war's end they were no longer living in the lower Merrimack valley. (University of Massachusetts Lowell Libraries, "Pawtucket & Wamesit Indians" guide, opened, and UML "Native Americans and First Contact" guide, search summary: unconfirmed for the "forced off their land by English colonists" wording.)
- **Owners and cotton (from `economy`).** The Boston merchants called the "Boston Associates" (Francis Cabot Lowell, Nathan Appleton, Patrick Tracy Jackson and others) owned the mills. Their mills spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South and also made cheap "negro cloth" sold to plantations to clothe enslaved people (NPS Lowell National Historical Park, "Lowell's Southern Connection," via `research-economy.md`).
- **The day.** Most mill girls were **15 to 25**, signed on for short stints, averaged about **three years** in the mills, and had to work at least a year, live in a company boardinghouse and attend church (NPS, "Lowell, Story of an Industrial City: The Mill Girls," opened). An average of **73 hours a week**, from **5:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.**, with fines for lateness; pay in the 1830s **$2.40 to $3.20 a week** plus room and board (Encyclopedia.com "Lowell Mills"; Wikipedia "Lowell mill girls"; Massachusetts Historical Society, Boott Cotton Mills regulations: search summaries, unconfirmed). The outline's "12 to 14 hours a day" (Britannica, NPS, above) is consistent.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): Duffy's Cut, 1832, the diggers' deaths
- In late June 1832, **57** Irish immigrants from Donegal, Tyrone and Derry, average age **22**, arrived on the ship *John Stamp* and went to work for contractor **Philip Duffy** digging a cut and building an earth fill at **mile 59 of the Philadelphia and Columbia Railroad** in Chester County, Pennsylvania. By early August **cholera** had reached their camp. All 57 died. Local people shunned the camp. Excavations from **2009 to 2012** found that of the remains dug up, workers had "sustained blows to their skulls" at the time of death, and one had a bullet in his skull fired at close range. None had wounds from defending themselves. The historian who led the dig points to "fear of cholera and perhaps even nativist sentiment" (hatred of immigrants). (William E. Watson, "Duffy's Cut," Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, opened.) Forensic counts: seven sets of remains examined, four with skull trauma (Wikipedia and Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia search summaries: unconfirmed, search summary only). In May 2025 the same team found remains suggesting a second mass grave of Irish railroad workers in Downingtown, 11 miles west (The Conversation, 2025, search summary: unconfirmed).
- Shared with: `transportation` (the railroad) · `immigration` (the Irish arrivals) · `health` (cholera).

## 07 · 1850 to 1900 — industrial work at its harshest; the first national unions

- **Knights of Labor:** founded December 9, 1869, Philadelphia, by tailor Uriah Smith Stephens as a secret order; under Terence V. Powderly it opened up and grew from under 10,000 members (1879) to roughly **700,000–730,000 by 1886** — state as "about 700,000." Admitted women and (unevenly) Black workers; wanted broad reform, not just wages. (Sources: LOC business-history guide; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Britannica.)
- **The eight-hour fight and Haymarket:** national strikes for the eight-hour day began May 1, 1886. At a rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square on **May 4, 1886**, someone (never identified) threw a dynamite bomb at police; the blast and the shooting that followed killed **seven police officers and at least four civilians**. Eight anarchists were convicted of conspiracy although none was shown to have thrown the bomb; **four were hanged November 11, 1887**, one died by suicide in his cell; in **1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned** the three still in prison, finding the trial unfair (biased judge, packed jury, fabricated evidence). The Knights collapsed in the backlash. (Sources: Library of Congress business-history guide "Haymarket"; Britannica "Haymarket Affair"; famous-trials.com.)
- **American Federation of Labor:** founded **December 8, 1886, Columbus, Ohio**; craft unions of skilled workers bargaining for wages and hours — "pure and simple" unionism. (Sources: LOC guide; Britannica.)
- **Samuel Gompers (famous story, verified):** born January 27, 1850, Spitalfields, London; cigar maker from boyhood, rolling cigars in New York tenement shops; first AFL president, serving 1886–1924 except one year (1895); the AFL grew from about 140,000 members (1886) to nearly 3 million (1924). (Sources: AFL-CIO history; Britannica; EBSCO.) No verified film about him — omit Movie.
- **Homestead, July 6, 1892:** at Carnegie Steel's Homestead, Pennsylvania works, 300 Pinkerton agents on barges tried to land and retake the struck plant; in the gun battle **seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed** (some accounts count more — state the range); 8,000 state militia arrived July 12; by November the strike and the union were broken. Company side (Frick, Carnegie, private guards) belongs to `big-business`. (Sources: LOC business-history guide "1892 Homestead Strike"; Britannica; AFL-CIO history.)
- **Pullman, 1894:** the Pullman Palace Car Company cut wages roughly 25–35% without cutting rents in its company town. The American Railway Union (founded June 1893, Terre Haute — the first industrial union, all railroad workers in one union) voted a national boycott of Pullman cars; the federal government broke it with an injunction and federal troops; about **30 people were killed** nationwide (some counts to 34 — state as "about 30"). **Eugene V. Debs served six months** (1895) in the Woodstock, Illinois jail for contempt. Six days into the strike's climax, **Congress and President Cleveland made Labor Day a federal holiday (signed June 28, 1894)** — the holiday itself is `holidays`' material (parked there). (Sources: Britannica "Pullman Strike"; EBSCO "In re Debs"; U.S. DOL "History of Labor Day".)
- **Jennie Curtis (ordinary story, verified):** born 1871; upholstery seamstress in Pullman's repair shops; earnings fell from **$1.50–$2.25 a day before the 1893 depression to about 80 cents** (many girls averaging 40–50 cents), with two wage cuts in a single week in November 1893; president of ARU "girls' local" 269 (125 members); her speech to the June 1894 ARU convention helped persuade delegates to vote the Pullman boycott; testified to the U.S. Strike Commission about wages, rents, and abusive supervisors; died 1917. In her Strike Commission testimony she said the company collected back rent her late father owed after 13 years' Pullman service — use carefully, citing the testimony. (Sources: NPS "Jennie Curtis" biography; 1894 U.S. Strike Commission testimony excerpts (Macmillan/Studocu document reprints); WTTW Chicago Stories.) No film.
- **Eugene V. Debs (famous story, verified — our slug `eugene-debs-work-workers`; `big-business` features him from the company-power side):** locomotive fireman from Terre Haute; officer of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen; founded the ARU 1893; jailed six months after Pullman; later ran for president five times as a Socialist, the last time (1920) from federal prison — about 900,000–1 million votes as Convict 2253, while serving a ten-year sentence for a 1918 anti-war speech (freed by Harding's commutation, Christmas 1921 — the politics belong elsewhere; our angle is the union man). (Sources: Britannica "American Railway Union"; EBSCO "Eugene V. Debs"; Zinn Education Project date-check.) **Movie:** *American Socialist: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs* (2017), documentary directed by Yale Strom — verified via IMDb/Metacritic.
- **The work itself:** sweatshops in garment lofts and tenements; **breaker boys** picking slate from coal on the breaker benches of the anthracite fields (documented into the next era — see 1902 commission below); **domestic service was the largest single women's occupation** — the 1870 census (the first to systematically count women's work) recorded 867,354 domestic servants, about **half of all employed women**, and it stayed the largest women's occupation at every census 1870–1940. (Sources: U.S. Women's Bureau Bulletin 218 via FRASER (St. Louis Fed); 1940 Census comparative occupation statistics.)
- **Chinese railroad workers' strike, June 25, 1867:** several thousand Chinese workers (estimates run 2,000–5,000 — state the range) building the Central Pacific through the Sierra struck for eight days for pay equal to white workers ($35 vs $40 a month), shorter hours, and an end to whipping; the company cut off their food supply and the strike ended without formal concessions, though wages quietly improved. Largest labor action in the country to that date. Shared with `transportation` (the line) and `immigration` (who came); parked in their banks. (Sources: LOC business-history guide "1867 Sierra Strike"; PBS American Experience.)
- **Convict leasing** — Southern states leasing prisoners, overwhelmingly Black men arrested under the new codes, to mines, railroads, and planters — is noted in one line with a Shared-with; the system is `slavery-freedom`'s (their bank carries it).

- **Railroad brakemen and the coupler (from `transportation`).** Coupling cars by hand meant standing between moving cars to guide an iron link into a socket. Sparks from the ventilating furnace set it on fire and **110** died, including **five boys** and two men who tried to rescue the workers. Pennsylvania's Mine Safety Act of 1870 then required more than one exit from every mine (Rachel Crowell, *EARTH Magazine*, August 3, 2018; see `research-energy.md`). Owner: Steuben Coal Company, controlled by the Delaware, Lackawanna and Western Railroad (unconfirmed: search summary only).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): children at work, 1870 to 1904
- The 1870 census found about **1 in 8** children employed. By 1900 more than **1 in 5**. From 1890 to 1910 "no less than 18 percent of all children ages 10 to 15 worked." In 1900, **25,000** of the nearly 100,000 textile workers in the South were children under 16. By 1904 the number was **50,000**, and **20,000** of them were under 12. Mill children worked "12-hour days, 6 days a week." Mill owners said adult farm workers' fingers were too "knotted and gnarled" for mill work. In Philadelphia, children's pay made up **28 to 46 percent** of household income, depending on the family's ethnic group. (Michael Schuman, "History of child labor in the United States, part 1," BLS *Monthly Labor Review*, January 2017, opened.) The 1900 census count of about **1.75 million** working children aged 10 to 15 (about 18 percent) appears in Census Bureau teaching materials (unconfirmed: search summary only. The BLS 18 percent figure above is confirmed).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): the Great Railroad Strike of 1877
- In July 1877 **B&O Railroad president John W. Garrett** cut wages again after several earlier cuts. Workers at **Martinsburg, West Virginia** walked off, and the strike spread through West Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, Missouri and Illinois. **Governors called up militia**, and **President Rutherford B. Hayes** issued proclamations for West Virginia (July 18), Maryland (July 21) and Pennsylvania (July 23) and sent federal troops. In **Pittsburgh** the official death toll was **26**, "likely more." In **Reading, Pennsylvania** "at least ten were killed." By September 1877 state and federal troops had broken the strike, and the workers won nothing at once. (Library of Congress, "This Month in Business History: Great Railroad Strike of 1877," guides.loc.gov, opened.) The size of the wage cut (10 percent) and a national total of **about 100 dead** come from Britannica (unconfirmed: search summary only, britannica.com returned 403). State "about 100 people, by one count."

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): who did what at Haymarket, Homestead and Pullman
- **Haymarket.** On **May 3, 1886** police fired on strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine Company plant in Chicago and killed **at least two**. That killing was the reason for the May 4 rally. On May 4, **176 officers under Inspector John Bonfield** marched on the meeting, and **Captain William Ward** ordered it to disperse. Then someone threw the bomb. The four hanged on **November 11, 1887** were **August Spies, Albert Parsons, Adolph Fischer and George Engel**. Governor John P. Altgeld pardoned the three still alive in **June 1893**. (Chicago History Museum, "The Haymarket Affair," chicagohistory.org/haymarket, opened, for the May 3 shooting, the 176 officers under Bonfield and the bomb. Captain Ward's order and the names of the hanged: Gilder Lehrman Institute and famous-trials.com, unconfirmed: search summary only.) **Count dispute:** the Chicago History Museum says **eight** police died and sixty were wounded, and "an undetermined number of the crowd were killed or wounded." LOC (above) says seven officers and at least four civilians. Record both. The Museum also says about **35,000** Chicago workers struck on May 1, 1886. The fifth condemned man, Louis Lingg, died in his cell before the hanging (sourced above: "one died by suicide in his cell").

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): the first federal child-labor law, and the Court that struck it down
- In **1916** Congress passed the **Keating-Owen Act**, which banned shipping across state lines goods made in factories that employed children under 14, or children 14 to 16 for more than eight hours a day, at night, or more than six days a week (Wikipedia "Keating-Owen Act," search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only). **Roland Dagenhart**, whose sons worked with him in a cotton mill in North Carolina (Charlotte), sued. On **June 3, 1918**, in *Hammer v. Dagenhart*, the Supreme Court struck the law down, ruling that Congress's power over interstate commerce "did not include production of goods." Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes dissented. Congress passed a second child-labor law in 1919, and the Court struck that down too. In **1941** the Court reversed itself and upheld the child-labor rules of the 1938 Fair Labor Standards Act. (U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, "Hammer v. Dagenhart, dissenting opinion by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes, June 3, 1918," opened.) The vote was **5 to 4** (Britannica, search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only). The sons' names, **Reuben and John**, appear only in search summaries (unconfirmed: search summary only).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): Hawks Nest tunnel, 1930 to 1932
- Union Carbide had a tunnel dug near Gauley Bridge, West Virginia, largely between **1930 and 1932**, to carry water to a power plant for its alloy works. The contractor was **Rinehart and Dennis** of Charlottesville, Virginia. **Almost 5,000** men worked on it, local men and a majority of migrant workers, **most of them Black men from the South**. The tunnel went through high-grade **silica** sandstone, and a third of it was widened to mine the silica. The work had no dust control and no breathing protection, and poor air underground. Breathing the rock dust caused **silicosis**: the fine glass-like dust scars the lungs so that the worker slowly suffocates, and it cannot be cured (definition from the e-WV entry and the Hawks Nest summaries: "a debilitating and incurable disease that... leads to patients suffocating," unconfirmed wording: search summary only; the no-protection facts are from e-WV). **Death counts disagree:** Union Carbide's own reports admitted **109** (unconfirmed: search summary only); U.S. House hearings in 1936 put it at **476**; epidemiologist Martin Cherniack's 1986 study "as many as **764**"; NIOSH in 2002 "close to **1,000**." **538** lawsuits ended in an out-of-court settlement of **$200,000**, with single awards from **$30 to $1,600**. (Martin G. Cherniack, "Hawks Nest Tunnel Disaster," e-WV: The West Virginia Encyclopedia, opened.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250): Frank Lumpkin and the Wisconsin Steel closing (fills the `laid-off-industrial-worker` target; slug `frank-lumpkin`)
Chosen because `economy` already tells Gerald Dickey (Youngstown) and Ed Neufeldt (Elkhart). Lumpkin is in no other chapter (searched `outlines/`, `manuscript/`, `research/` 2026-09-27).
- **Who.** Frank Lumpkin, **1916 to 2010**, African American steelworker (Chicago History Museum finding aid, "Frank and Beatrice Lumpkin papers," CARLI digital collections, opened). Born **1916 in Washington, Georgia**, to a sharecropping family (parents Hattie and Elmo Lumpkin). Hauled stone for an uncle at age 6; left school at 15 for work on citrus plantations; boxed professionally as "KO" Lumpkin. Moved north to **Buffalo, New York in 1940**: construction, an aircraft factory, Bethlehem Steel. Merchant Marine veteran. (People's World obituary, John Bachtell, March 10, 2010. People's World is the Communist Party USA's paper, and the Lumpkins were party members: label it as a partisan source. Merchant Marine service also in the finding aid, series 10, and UW System "Profile in Black History," 2016.)
- **The job.** Began at **Wisconsin Steel** (107th Street and Torrence Avenue, South Deering, Chicago) **in the 1950s** (finding aid). Worked there about **30 years** as a **"chipper, scarfer and millwright"** (People's World). No fetched source defines the three jobs. A writer should say only "worked in the mill for about 30 years" unless a definition is sourced.
- **The closing.** International Harvester owned the mill from 1902 and in **1977** sold it to **Envirodyne**, a firm with no steel experience that bought it with a loan from International Harvester itself (Chicago Public Library blog, "Deindustrialization of Chicago's Southeast Side," 2025, citing the Southeast Chicago Historical Society and the *Daily Calumet*). The mill **closed abruptly on March 28, 1980** (finding aid; People's World says March 1980. CPL gives March 27, 1980: state "late March 1980" or give both). Workers lost jobs, health benefits, back pay and pensions (finding aid). **Chase Manhattan Bank told workers in April that their final paychecks were worthless** (CPL). **Workers thrown out: 3,300 (CPL), "over 3,000" (People's World), 3,500 (other secondary summaries).** State "more than 3,000."
- **What he did.** Lumpkin co-founded and chaired the **Wisconsin Steel Save Our Jobs Committee** with his wife **Beatrice (Bea) Lumpkin**: a coalition of **African American, Mexican American and other steelworkers and their families** that sued International Harvester/Navistar and Envirodyne and ran food, medical and utility help for families left with no income (finding aid). The committee first tried to get the mill reopened, then turned to recovering lost pay and benefits (Chicago History Museum blog, Dan Murrieta, September 2, 2024). A 1987 committee newsletter counted **600 former workers dead** in the seven years since the closing, and a rally of **over 400** people (Chicago History Museum blog, quoting the newsletter). Lawyer **Thomas Geoghegan** won a **$14.5 million class-action settlement in 1988** against International Harvester/Navistar (finding aid). Over **17 years** the committee won settlements **totaling about $17 million, about one-third of the $40 million owed** (CPL. People's World also says $17 million). State "$14.5 million in 1988, about $17 million in all."
- He ran unsuccessfully for the Illinois legislature (finding aid, series 5). Died **March 1, 2010**, aged 93 (People's World). Book: Beatrice Lumpkin, *"Always Bring a Crowd!" The Story of Frank Lumpkin, Steel Worker* (1999) (finding aid). No film.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, unit 2): parked material folded into this era
- **Braceros at work (from `immigration`, plus new sourcing).** Men seeking contracts passed through contracting centers in Mexico and, later, U.S. Immigration and Naturalization Service reception centers at the border, such as **Rio Vista** farm in El Paso. Men who passed through Rio Vista remembered "poor food, humiliating medical examinations, and the fumigation." **The men were stripped of their clothing, organized into lines, and fumigated with DDT**, an insect-killing white powder, because officials believed Mexican workers carried insects and germs. From **1948** growers could recruit directly at the border, which let them skip transport costs and government oversight. Braceros worked **six- or seven-day weeks** in **26 states**, on farms and railroads. Some faced low pay, poor housing, and verbal and physical abuse by supervisors, and they worked with pesticides later found to be very harmful. A bracero could complain of a broken contract to a Mexican consulate. The program ended in **1964**, after pressure from unions, the spread of farm machines, and public attention to the conditions. (UTEP Institute of Oral History, "Brief History of the Bracero Program," opened.) A photograph in the Bracero History Archive shows "Braceros... fumigated with DDT while others stand in line at the Hidalgo Processing Center, Texas" (braceroarchive.org item 2998, search result title). **Antonio Garcia** (born June 13, 1937, Tepehuanes, Durango) altered his military ID to join at 17 and fell seriously ill within a week in the Texas cotton fields (Bracero History Archive item 162, parked by `immigration`, which features him as `antonio-garcia-bracero`). In **July 1955** about **88** braceros died of sunstroke during a federal round-up in 112-degree heat (Mae Ngai via NPR, November 11, 2015, in `research-immigration.md`. Those deaths were during Operation Wetback, a deportation drive, and belong to `immigration`: pointer only).
- **Farmington and black lung (from `energy`).** **November 20, 1968**, Consol No. 9 mine, Farmington, West Virginia: **78** miners killed. Congress passed the **Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969** (MSHA: "enacted March 30, 1970"): federal inspectors had to inspect every underground coal mine four times a year, could shut a mine down for life-threatening hazards, and the law set up federal benefits for black lung. **Black lung** is lung disease from breathing coal dust: the dust scars the lungs until, in the worst form, masses of scar tissue leave the miner "gasping for every breath." **More than 76,000 miners have died of black lung since 1968** (U.S. Department of Labor, via *Smithsonian Magazine*, May 15, 2017). Coal miners killed at work: 311 in 1968, 260 in 1970, 133 in 1980, 38 in 2000 (MSHA). Full sourcing in `research-energy.md`.
- **Flight attendants (from `transportation`).** Into the late 1960s U.S. airlines fired stewardesses who turned 32 (some lines 35) or married, and ran weight checks and girdle checks. **Barbara "Dusty" Roads** of American Airlines (flying from 1950) lobbied Congress and co-founded the Association of Professional Flight Attendants. The rules fell in the Title VII era (Civil Rights Act of 1964 sex-discrimination cases). Film about the stewardesses: *Fly With Me* (PBS American Experience, 2024). `transportation` features Roads.
- **The checkout (from `marketplace`).** June 26, 1974, 8:01 a.m., Marsh supermarket, Troy, Ohio: cashier **Sharon Buchanan** scanned a ten-pack of Juicy Fruit gum, the first barcode sale. Scanning ended price-keying by hand and gave managers hour-by-hour sales records later used for scheduling (GS1; *Smithsonian Magazine*; Smithsonian NMAH).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): farmworkers and pesticides
- Farm workers in steady contact with pesticides had high rates of birth defects, cancer and burns. Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta campaigned to ban DDT on grape and lettuce ranches. - A 1967 UFW contract with a grape grower banned **DDT, aldrin and dieldrin**, five years before the EPA's 1972 DDT ban, and by **1970** contracts with about **150** table-grape growers banned DDT, parathion, aldrin and other pesticides (Humanities for the Environment, "Environmental Justice Legacy of the UFW," search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only). In **1978** the EPA restricted methyl parathion after finding it had caused more than **600** poisonings and about **100** farm-worker deaths since 1966 (unconfirmed: search summary only). Chavez fasted **36 days** in **1988** against pesticide poisoning (UFW, search result title: unconfirmed, search summary only). The UFW ended its grape boycott in **November 2000** (unconfirmed: search summary only).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250): Raef Lawson, Grubhub delivery driver (fills the `gig-driver` target; slug `raef-lawson`)
Chosen instead of Barbara Ann Berwick, whom `transportation` features. Our angle: pay, hours and whether an app worker is an employee.
- **The work.** Lawson, "an aspiring actor, writer, and director in Los Angeles," signed up to deliver food through Grubhub in **August 2015**. He signed up for Grubhub "blocks" (shifts of **two to five hours**, scheduled around mealtimes, chosen weekly) on **69 days between October 25, 2015 and February 14, 2016**, and made deliveries on **59** of them. Grubhub paid him for blocks totaling **35 hours in November, 105 in December, 60 in January and 43 in the first two weeks of February**. Grubhub guaranteed a "true up" hourly pay for a block if the driver accepted a high percentage of the deliveries offered. **Grubhub did not repay him for fuel or his cell phone.** In 2016 Grubhub had **4,000 delivery drivers in California, all classed as independent contractors**. (Ninth Circuit opinion, *Lawson v. Grubhub, Inc.*, No. 18-15386, filed September 20, 2021, 13 F.4th 908, facts as found by the district court; PDF at cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov, opened.)
- **Fired by the company.** On **February 15, 2016**, Grubhub ended its agreement with him "because he had not performed delivery services during a high proportion of the delivery blocks for which he had signed up." The trial court also found he sometimes "gamed" the app (logging on late, switching his phone to airplane mode so no new orders came). (Ninth Circuit opinion, above.) State both sides.
- **The case.** He sued under the California Labor Code for **minimum wage, overtime and expense repayment**, claiming he was an employee. Only **two** of Grubhub's thousands of California drivers had opted out of its arbitration and class-action waiver, so the court refused to certify a class. **Magistrate Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley** held a **bench trial in September 2017** and ruled on **February 8, 2018** that he was an independent contractor under the older *Borello* test: the first gig-worker classification case decided at trial (Holland & Knight client alert, February 2018, law-firm source). Three months later the California Supreme Court adopted the "ABC test" (*Dynamex*, 2018), which makes the company prove a worker is not an employee. The Ninth Circuit (Judges William Fletcher, Paul Watford, Daniel Collins) vacated the judgment on **September 20, 2021** and sent it back. (Ninth Circuit opinion.)
- **The result.** On **March 30, 2023** Judge Corley ruled that under the ABC test Lawson had been an employee for his minimum-wage and overtime claims, because "food delivery is not outside the usual course of" Grubhub's business. She awarded him **$65.11** in unpaid minimum wages for the four months. No overtime, because his paid hours never passed 40 in a week. His lawyer Shannon Liss-Riordan called it possibly "the first court ruling in the U.S. to deem a gig worker an employee for wage purposes" (her claim, attribute it). (Gizmodo, Lauren Leffer, March 31, 2023, journalism, opened; ruling confirmed in the court's order of March 13, 2026, Dkt. 449, govinfo.gov, opened.)
- **The settlement.** The court limited his representative claim to **December 3, 2014 to December 16, 2020** (the day Proposition 22 took effect). Grubhub agreed to pay **$24,750,000** for California drivers, shared by miles driven, **at least $25** each, including $2,000,000 in PAGA penalties (75% to the state labor agency). A second plaintiff, **Rejenna Marshall** (a Grubhub driver since 2021), joined to represent drivers after Prop 22. (Order granting preliminary approval, March 13, 2026, Dkt. 449, govinfo.gov, opened.) **Final approval July 30, 2026**; about **60,000** drivers in the class; fees cut to **$6.18 million**; Lawson's service award **$10,000** (the motion had asked $100,000). Judge Corley called it "my oldest case." A Grubhub spokesperson said that thanks to Prop 22 drivers "will continue to enjoy the freedom and flexibility of working as independent contractors." (Courthouse News Service, Carly Nairn, July 30, 2026, journalism, opened.)
- No film.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-250, bank check): heat, children and warehouses, 2000 to today
- **Heat deaths.** **43** workers died of environmental heat on the job in 2019. From 2011 to 2019, **345** died, from a low of 18 (2014) to a high of 61 (2011). **144** of them (41.9 percent) died while doing construction, repair or cleaning work. (BLS, *The Economics Daily*, "43 work-related deaths due to environmental heat exposure in 2019," 2021, opened.) From 1992 to 2022, 986 workers died of heat, about 34 a year (National Safety Council Injury Facts, citing BLS: unconfirmed, search summary only).
- **The federal heat rule, not yet final.** OSHA published a proposed rule to protect indoor and outdoor workers from heat on **August 30, 2024**. Comments closed January 14, 2025, the public hearing ran **June 16 to July 2, 2025**, and post-hearing comments closed **October 30, 2025**. OSHA's page gives no date for a final rule. (OSHA, "Heat Injury and Illness Prevention... Rulemaking," opened.) The Labor Department's regulatory plan lists a final rule for **April 2027** (EHS Leaders, July 2026, search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only). As of September 2026 there is no federal heat standard.
- **Children cleaning slaughterhouses.** In February 2023 the U.S. Labor Department found that **Packers Sanitation Services**, a Wisconsin-based cleaning company, had employed **102** children aged **13 to 17** on overnight shifts at **13** meatpacking plants in **8** states. They cleaned back saws, brisket saws and head splitters with caustic chemicals that could burn them. At least **three** were injured. The company paid **$1.5 million**, the most the law allows. Plants included JBS in Grand Island, Nebraska (27 children) and Cargill in Dodge City, Kansas (26). Jessica Looman of the Wage and Hour Division called the violations "systemic" and "a corporate-wide failure." (Steve Vockrodt, KCUR/NPR Kansas City, February 17, 2023, journalism, opened.) The legal maximum was **$15,138** per child (DOL release, search summary: unconfirmed, dol.gov returned 403). A federal judge in Nebraska had entered a consent order in December 2022 (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Amazon warehouses.** A U.S. Senate HELP Committee staff report (Chairman Bernie Sanders, **December 2024**, "The 'Injury-Productivity Trade-off'") found that Amazon warehouses recorded **over 30 percent more injuries** than the warehousing average in 2023, that Amazon workers were **nearly twice as likely** to be injured as other warehouse workers in each of the past seven years, and that more than two-thirds of Amazon warehouses exceeded the industry injury rate. Workers repeat the same movements "hundreds and thousands of times" in **10- to 12-hour shifts** under rates tracked by computer, with automatic discipline that can end in firing. Committee staff interviewed **135** workers. It is a report by the committee's majority staff, one party's staff: label it so. (HELP Committee report, help.senate.gov PDF, opened.) Amazon disputed the findings (CNN, NPR, December 16, 2024, search summaries: unconfirmed, search summary only).
- **Wage theft.** In the 10 most populous states, **2.4 million** workers lost **$8 billion** a year to being paid less than the minimum wage, about **$3,300** a year each for year-round workers, "nearly a quarter of their earned wages." It affected **17 percent** of low-wage workers. (David Cooper and Teresa Kroeger, Economic Policy Institute, "Employers steal billions from workers' paychecks each year," May 10, 2017, opened. EPI is a labor-aligned research group: label it.) EPI's national extension of the same data is **$15 billion** a year (EPI press release title, search summary: unconfirmed, search summary only).

## Parked from `transportation` (2026-08-08) — the deadliest jobs on the rails and in the cabin
`transportation` tells the vehicles and systems; the jobs are this chapter's. Web-verified 2026-08-08, sources in `research/research-transportation.md`.

### 7. 1850 to 1900
- **Brakemen and the link-and-pin coupler:** coupling cars by hand meant standing between moving cars guiding an iron link into a socket — crushed hands and missing fingers were called "the badge of the experienced worker," and thousands died. Eli Janney patented the automatic coupler in 1873 (ASME landmark) and the Westinghouse air brake existed from 1869, but railroads did not adopt them at scale until the **Safety Appliance Act of 1893** (effective 1900) forced it. Coupling accidents fell from about **11,000 in 1892 to about 2,000 in 1902** while railroad employment grew. One of the clearest documented cases of a known safety fix delayed for decades because workers, not equipment, bore the cost. (Sources: ASME Janney Coupler landmark; Railroad Safety Appliance Act histories; Linda Hall Library "Couplers & Brakes.") `transportation` carries the equipment; the job and the fight for the law are yours.

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **Flight attendants' job rules:** into the late 1960s U.S. airlines fired stewardesses for turning 32 (some lines 35) or for marrying; weight checks and girdle checks were routine. **Barbara "Dusty" Roads** (American Airlines, flying from 1950; died 2023) lobbied Congress on her own Washington runs and staged a press challenge — pick the 32-year-olds out of a lineup; she co-founded the Association of Professional Flight Attendants. Age and marriage rules fell in the Title VII era. Documented in ***Fly With Me* (PBS American Experience, 2024)**, which interviews the survivors of those rules. `transportation` features Roads for the jet-age cabin; the job rules and union side are yours; the sex-discrimination law fight is also parked to `rights-movements`. (Sources: Wikipedia "Barbara Roads" + obituaries; PBS American Experience.)

## Parked from `landmarks` (2026-08-08) — the men on the steel, and what the big jobs cost
`landmarks` tells the structures; the jobs, photographs, and safety story are yours. Web-verified 2026-08-08, full sourcing in `research/research-landmarks.md`.

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Lewis Hine's *Men at Work* (1931–32):** Hine photographed the Empire State Building's "sky boys" — riveting gangs on open steel, up to 3,400 workers a day, frame rising ~4.5 floors a week, building done in about 410 days. **Five workers died** (official count; "hundreds" is legend). Among the gangs: Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake (tradition from the 1880s; documented individual Harold McComber in NFB's *High Steel*, 1965). (History.com ESB construction; Britannica.)
- **Golden Gate Bridge safety story:** chief engineer Joseph Strauss required hard hats and spent ~$130,000 on the first big safety net in U.S. bridge-building; it saved 19 men — the self-named **"Halfway to Hell Club."** Eleven died, ten on February 17, 1937 when a falling platform tore the net. Documented member: **Al Zampa** (fell Oct 19, 1936, net sagged to rocks, four broken vertebrae, returned to ironwork; the Carquinez span was named the Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge in 2003 — a bridge named for a working ironworker). (Half Way to Hell Club sources; Zampa foundation; CA SCR-97.)
- **Hoover Dam toll, honestly:** Bureau of Reclamation's official count 96 industrial fatalities; excludes "pneumonia" deaths many historians read as carbon-monoxide poisoning of tunnel crews (42) — true toll higher, exact number unknown. Nobody buried in the concrete: poured in small shallow lifts with crews over every pour. (USBR "Fatalities at Hoover Dam.")

## Parked from `economy` (2026-08-08) — Ford's five-dollar day, the job side
### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Turnover at Highland Park hit 370% in 1913** after the moving assembly line (installed from December 1913) reduced Model T assembly from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes of one man's repeated motions — workers quit in masses; the five-dollar day (announced January 5, 1914) was the fix. ## Parked from `marketplace` research (2026-08-08) — what changed the retail job
`marketplace` leads the way of selling; what it did to the work is yours.
- **Self-service, from September 6, 1916:** Clarence Saunders' first Piggly Wiggly in Memphis (patent 1,242,872, granted October 9, 1917) sent customers along a fenced path past every shelf and had them pick their own goods. The point was fewer clerks per store; the clerk who fetched goods from behind a counter was replaced by a checkout operator and a shelf stocker. (Tennessee Encyclopedia; *Smithsonian Magazine*; the patent record.)
- **The barcode, June 26, 1974, 8:01 a.m.:** cashier **Sharon Buchanan** at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio scanned a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum (67 cents) for Clyde Dawson — the first UPC sale. It removed price-keying from the cashier's job and gave managers hour-by-hour records of what sold, which later shaped scheduling. (GS1; History.com; *Smithsonian Magazine*; Smithsonian NMAH "Supermarket Scanner.")
- **Door-to-door selling as women's paid work:** David McConnell hired **Persis Foster Eames Albee** in **1886** as the first traveling agent for the California Perfume Company (later Avon); she sold house to house across New England and recruited other women, 34 years before American women could vote. Company-sourced, so verify any earnings figures independently. (Avon corporate history; *Keene Sentinel* local history — company and journalism, labeled.)

**Useful and confirmed, if this chapter wants it.** The five New Jersey plaintiffs by name:
**Grace Fryer, Katherine Schaub, Quinta Maggia Macdonald, Albina Maggia Larice and Edna Hussman**,
suit filed by **Raymond Berry in May 1927**. Grace Fryer worked at the Orange plant **1917 to
1920**; in **1924** the dentist **Theodor Blum** found the atrophy of her jaw bone he had never
seen before. Mechanism: "Radium, which is in the same alkaline earth family as calcium, would be

necrosis of the jaw, and osteosarcomas"; autopsies found **99 percent of the radioactivity in the
