## Era 01 — Before 1500

- **No draft animals north of Mexico before the horse returned with Europeans.** Human muscle carried and dragged loads; dogs pulled the travois on the Plains; canoes moved bulk. (Smithsonian NMAI "A Song for the Horse Nation" exhibit materials; standard museum framing.)
- **Deliberate Native burning as energy/land management:** NPS, "Indigenous Fire Practices Shape Our Land" — fire used on regular cycles to clear underbrush, maintain habitat, and support food plants; documented burning cycles among the Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa in California (NPS; The Nature Conservancy; HISTORY.com survey — journalism). Colonists later suppressed the practice; suppression story belongs to `native-nations`/`land-environment`.
- Wood + fire = heat, cooking, light. No mills, no draft animals, no fossil fuel use beyond surface finds.

## Era 02 — The 1500s

- **Horses arrived with Spanish expeditions in the 1500s** (Southeast and Southwest entradas). The first non-human muscle power on the continent in thousands of years.
- **First water-powered mills in what is now the U.S.: 1598, New Mexico** — Juan de Oñate's colonists built small "tub mills" (horizontal wheel flat in the water, direct-drive to the stones); grindstones freighted up the Camino Real. Source: *Santa Fe New Mexican*, "Early New Mexico had hundreds of mills" — **journalism about local history, labeled in outline.** Taos later a mill center.
- Honest framing kept: for nearly everyone on the continent, muscle/fire/wood were unchanged all century.

## Era 03 — The 1600s

- **Windmill, 1621, Virginia:** Sir George Yeardley erected the first recorded windmill in English America at Flowerdew Hundred ("Windmill Point") on the James. **Gristmill, 1621, Jamestown-area:** first recorded North American English gristmill the same year. (Colonial Williamsburg research reports; encyclopedia entries via NCpedia/e-WV.)
- **1634 Newichawannock (South Berwick, Maine):** John Mason sent three carpenters, William Chadbourne among them, to build a gristmill and sawmill at the falls of the Piscataqua tributary; operating 1634. Multiple "first sawmill in America" claims exist among Piscataqua-region towns — the outline states the date and says "among the first," refusing the trophy. (Old Berwick Historical Society; Tide Mill Institute "Sawmills and the Economy of Early New England"; Historic New England, *Merchant and Millwright*.)
- **William Chadbourne** — named 1634 millwright, documented in Old Berwick Historical Society materials (Stackpole 1926 and site histories). Story status: candidate (primary town-record confirmation wanted at prose time).
- **Colonial firewood burn rate — a RANGE, honestly stated:** estimates run from about **15 cords/year** (one 17th-century New England estimate) to **30–40 cords/year** for heating + cooking with open hearths; open fireplaces lost most heat up the chimney. State as "historians' estimates range from about 15 to 40 cords." (Shipley Energy/Stacker 250-year heating survey; Farmers' Museum (Cooperstown) blog; author-compiled colonial estimates — secondary, hence the range.)
- **Horses in Native hands earlier than the textbook story:** 2023 *Science* study (Taylor et al., with Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee co-authors) — archaeological horses in Wyoming/Kansas/New Mexico show riding, bridling, corn feeding **by the early 1600s**, before the Pueblo Revolt. The Revolt (1680) still mattered: perhaps **1,500+ horses** passed into Native hands at once, the largest single transfer (Smithsonian NMAI). Outline states both — new finding + Revolt transfer. (Science News; AAAS; PBS NewsHour; CU Boulder release; Smithsonian NMAI.)
- **Shore whaling / whale oil from ~1690:** NPS New Bedford Whaling NHP ("Whale Products"): whale oil a primary American lamp fuel for ~150 years beginning about 1690. Long Island/New England shore whaling preceded deep-sea voyages. (NPS; Nantucket Historical Association — the 1712 Hussey deep-sea origin story is *tradition first written down in 1835*, already told with that caveat by `land-environment`; we do not retell it.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247): William Chadbourne confirmed, and whose land the mills stood on
- **The three carpenters, from the society's own account.** "In July 1634, William Chadbourne, James Wall and John Goddard, three English carpenters under contract with Capt. John Mason's Laconia Company, arrived in present-day South Berwick, Maine, from England aboard the vessel 'Pied Cow.'" Their contract "called for them to build a saw mill and grist mill" on the Asbenbedick or Little Newichawannock River. The sawmill, "thought to be the first over-shot water-powered site in America," stood in the "Rocky Gorge" below today's Great Works bridge on Brattle Street. (Old Berwick Historical Society, "Great Works," updated December 2020, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/trades-occupations/great-works.html.) An overshot wheel is one where the water falls onto the top of the wheel and its weight turns it. "Thought to be the first" is the society's hedge: keep it as a claim, never as a fact.
- **The primary record behind it.** James Wall, carpenter and millwright, gave a sworn statement (deposition) on May 21, 1652, in a lawsuit over Mason's land: about 1634 he and his partners William Chadbourne and John Goddard came over and ran a sawmill and a "stamping mill for corn" at Asbenbedick Falls for three or four years, and Wall and Chadbourne each built a house there. (Old Berwick Historical Society timeline, Rick Coughlin and Norma Keim, 2004, lists "Depositions of James Wall ... about the location of John Mason's 1634 mills; law suit" under 1652, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/trades-occupations/timeline-of-the-great-works-mills.html. The deposition's wording is from genealogy pages (WikiTree Chadbourne-4; rootsweb) that quote it (unconfirmed: search summary only).) The same timeline records a "model of a saw-mill sent to Newichawannock on ship Pied Cow" in a May 1631 letter from Thomas Eyre.
- **Story status: verified.** Chadbourne's name, trade, year, ship, employer and job are stated by the Old Berwick Historical Society and rest on Wall's 1652 deposition. His birth and death years vary across genealogy sites (before 1582 to after 1652): leave them out.
- **Whose land.** The river's name, Newichawannock, is Abenaki (Old Berwick Historical Society). "South Berwick's First People" (Norma Keim, OBHS, 2012, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/people/17th-century/south-berwicks-first-people.html): the people there were Abenaki (Wabanaki), Algonquian speakers, and those at Quamphegan were likely western Abenaki. "Between 1616 and 1618, seven out of ten natives died" of European diseases such as measles or smallpox, before the English carpenters came. The sagamore (leader) Rowls survived. On March 19, 1650, Rowls sold his village land, Quamphegan, to Thomas Spencer "for Five Pounds Sterling" (York Deeds, quoted by OBHS). No record found says Mason's men asked anyone's leave to build the 1634 mills.

## Era 04 — 1700 to 1750

- **Charcoal iron:** furnaces ran on charcoal made by colliers in earthen pits/mounds; the quantified appetite is documented at Hopewell (era 05 entry below) — era 04 text keeps the practice general to avoid borrowing later numbers. Saugus (1646–1670) is told by `elements`/`technology`; our angle is only the fuel.
- **Whale-oil lamps and spermaceti candles:** spermaceti (head-matter wax of the sperm whale) made the brightest, cleanest, most expensive candles — "the most valuable product of the Yankee whaling industry" (New Bedford Whaling Museum; NPS "Whale Products"; Emily Irwin, "The Spermaceti Candle and the American Whaling Industry," *Historia* 2012).
- **Franklin stove, 1741:** Franklin's "Pennsylvania fireplace," cast iron, designed for more heat and less smoke from less wood; sold poorly until David Rittenhouse's improvements. The stove-as-machine → `technology` (which already carries Franklin); the wood saved is ours. (Britannica "Franklin stove"; ushistory.org; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.) Boundary rule: 1741 → this era, not 1750–1800.

## Era 05 — 1750 to 1800

- **First steam engine in America — Schuyler copper mine, North Arlington, NJ:** mine flooding led John Schuyler to order a Newcomen-type pumping engine from England **1748**; parts arrived **1753** with engine-builder **Josiah Hornblower**; engine pumping **1755** (some accounts say trial runs from 1754; the 1755 in-service date is standard). First steam engine on the continent. (Richard P. McCormick, "The First Steam Engine in America," *Journal of the Rutgers University Libraries*; HistoryOfInformation.com; Engines of Our Ingenuity #1085.) Hornblower (1729–1809) stayed in New Jersey; later public offices — story kept to the engine.
- **Hopewell Furnace fuel appetite (founded 1771; NPS):** in blast, up to **800 bushels of charcoal a day ≈ an acre of forest cut per day**; annually **6,000–7,000 cords ≈ 200 acres of woodland**; the works controlled 4,000–8,000 acres of forest. Payroll detail (1835–37): 112 of 213 workers were woodcutters — that datum chronologically belongs to era 06; parked for `work-workers`. (NPS Hopewell Furnace NHS "Teaching with Historic Places"; Forest History Today 2010.)
- **Whale oil toward peak:** fleet growth through the late 1700s; peak comes 1846 (era 06). Spermaceti candles remain the premium light. (NPS; New Bedford Whaling Museum.)

## Era 06 — 1800 to 1850

- **Jesse Fell, February 11, 1808, Wilkes-Barre:** burned anthracite successfully in an open **iron grate** at his tavern — the demonstration that "stone coal" could heat homes (anthracite needs bottom draft; the grate provided it). Spurred the anthracite industry. Earlier: the Gore brothers (blacksmiths) used anthracite in forges from 1769. (Jesse Fell historical marker/HMDB; Wyoming Valley History; explorePAhistory; Fell's own 1826 letter via Hopkin Thomas Project.)
- **Lehigh Navigation organized 1818**; anthracite floated to Philadelphia in "arks," then by canal; anthracite stoves/furnaces spread in the 1820s–40s. (Lehigh Coal & Navigation histories; Hopkin Thomas Project.)
- **Gaslight — Baltimore first:** Rembrandt Peale lit a room of his Holliday Street museum with coal gas in **1816**; **Gas Light Company of Baltimore chartered June 17, 1816** (first U.S. gas company; ancestor of BGE); **first public gas street lamp lit February 7, 1817** at Market and Lemon Streets. (American Oil & Gas Historical Society "Illuminating Gaslight"; Atlas Obscura marker record; *Baltimore Sun* retrospective — journalism; *Chronicles of Baltimore* 1881.)
- **Whaling peak, 1846:** American fleet **735 vessels** of roughly 900 worldwide; New Bedford the leading port (400+ vessels from New Bedford at its height); industry peak variously given as 1846–1852 — outline uses the 1846 fleet-count peak. (New Bedford Whaling Museum timeline; Whaling in the United States surveys; Springer *Natural Resources Research* whale-oil study.)
- **Who could afford whale oil:** whale oil ran **$1.30–$2.50/gallon**; cheaper lights = tallow, lard oil, camphene. Sperm oil lit the prosperous, lighthouses, public rooms. (AOGHS "Camphene to Kerosene Lamps"; Nantucket Historical Association.)
- **Nelson Cole Haley (1832–1900):** New Bedford; to sea at ~12 (1844); **harpooner on the *Charles W. Morgan* 1849–1853**; memoir ***Whale Hunt*** published by Mystic Seaport (1948; reissued 1990/2013). The *Morgan* survives at Mystic Seaport — last wooden whaleship. Boundary: voyage began 1849 → this era. (Mystic Seaport Museum shop/collections; Internet Archive edition; NEH feature on the Morgan's 2014 voyage.)

## Era 07 — 1850 to 1900

- **Coal passes wood ~1885** as the largest U.S. energy source (EIA, "History of energy consumption in the United States, 1775–2009" and the 2026 250-year update). Coal was 9% of consumption in 1850, 27% by 1870.
- **Drake well:** struck oil **August 27, 1859**, Titusville, PA, at **69.5 feet**; drilled for the Seneca Oil Co. of New Haven; product target = kerosene. **The "Colonel" is a marketing invention:** investor James Townsend addressed letters to "Colonel E. L. Drake" to impress Titusville locals; Drake held no commission. **Drake's end:** never patented the drive-pipe method, lost his money, invalid in later years; friends raised ~$4,000 in the late 1860s; **Pennsylvania granted a $1,500/year pension in 1873**; died November 1880, Bethlehem, PA. (Drake Well Museum; AOGHS "Edwin Drake and his Oil Well"; Britannica.)
- **Drake film (real, verified):** *Born in Freedom: The Story of Colonel Drake* (1954) — 30-minute Technicolor **industry-sponsored dramatization** (American Petroleum Institute), Vincent Price as Drake; premiered Titusville March 29, 1954. Labeled as sponsored drama in the outline. (IMDb tt0048663; AOGHS petroleum-history-videos; Letterboxd.)
- **Kerosene vs whale oil — the honest version (dispute stated):** whaling's decline began **before** kerosene: fleet peaked 1846, catches falling through the 1850s. Whale oil was a rich household's light at $1.30–$2.50/gal; the mass-market lamp fuels were camphene and lard oil. The **Civil War alcohol tax (up to $2.08/gal, 1862–64)** destroyed camphene while kerosene was taxed at 10¢ — petroleum took the lamp market in the 1860s. "Kerosene saved the whales" is an oversimplification historians actively contest. (AOGHS "Camphene to Kerosene Lamps"; environmentalhistory.org "The whale oil myth"; UH Cullen College "The Whale Oil Myth"; Breakthrough Institute essay — the last labeled as argument/essay.)
- **Pearl Street (parked entry confirmed + expanded):** service began **September 4, 1882**, 257 Pearl Street, Manhattan; six dynamos; DC; First District ≈ 1/4 square mile; **day one: ~85 customers, ~400 lamps**; within a year **513 customers, 10,000+ lamps**; Edison closed the circuit ceremony from Drexel, Morgan & Co.; station ran to January 2, 1890 with one 3-hour outage. (IEEE Milestone / ETHW "Pearl Street Station"; IEEE Power & Energy Magazine history; Smithsonian NMAH model notes.)
- **Tesla–Westinghouse (parked entry confirmed):** Tesla AC/polyphase motor patents issued **May 1888**; **Westinghouse license July 1888 — $60,000 cash+stock plus $2.50 per horsepower royalty**. (IEEE Spectrum; The Henry Ford.)
- **The smear campaign:** Harold P. Brown, covertly supported by Edison interests, staged AC animal electrocutions at West Orange (1888) and engineered AC into the first electric chair. **William Kemmler executed August 6, 1890** — first electric-chair execution; botched (first jolt failed; second prolonged; witnesses described burning). Edison's side promoted "Westinghoused" as a verb for electrocution. (Smithsonian Magazine "Edison vs. Westinghouse: A Shocking Rivalry"; McGill OSS; Harold P. Brown records.)
- **AC wins:** Westinghouse's **$399,000 winning bid** lit the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition with ~**100,000 incandescent lamps** (May 1893); **Niagara Adams Plant first AC power August 26, 1895**; **Buffalo received Niagara power November 1896** (dates given as Nov 15/16 in different sources — "November 1896" in outline). Conventionally the end of the current war. (ETHW; World's Columbian Exposition histories; Tesla Universe timeline — enthusiast site, corroborated by ETHW.)
- **Film:** *The Current War* (2017) — Hollywood **dramatization**; Cumberbatch (Edison), Shannon (Westinghouse), Hoult (Tesla), **Tom Holland as Samuel Insull**; dir. Gomez-Rejon; TIFF premiere Sept 9, 2017, wide release 2019 (Director's Cut). Labeled drama. (Wikipedia/IMDb; Deadline.)

## Era 08 — 1900 to 1950

- **Spindletop:** **January 10, 1901**, Gladys City/Spindletop hill, ~3 miles south of Beaumont, Texas; Lucas gusher blew in from **1,139 feet**; oil column over 100 feet high; flowed an estimated **100,000 barrels/day**; **capped January 19 — nine days**. Context: all Texas produced 836,000 barrels in 1900. Gulf Oil and Texaco trace to the boom (their story → `big-business`). (Texas State Historical Association "Spindletop Oilfield"; Lamar University Spindletop museum; AOGHS; HISTORY.com.)
- **Samuel Insull:** Edison's private secretary → **took over Chicago Edison 1892**, built **Commonwealth Edison** and a holding-company pyramid (85+ companies) serving millions; mass small-investor ownership of utility securities; **collapse 1932** in the Depression; fled to Europe (Greece/Turkey), **extradited 1934, tried three times (fraud, federal bankruptcy-law violations, embezzlement), acquitted all three**; FDR's 1932 campaign line named "the Ishmael or Insull"; died 1938 Paris. His empire's fall fed the Public Utility Holding Company Act framework (that law's fight → `big-business`/`government-politics`). (ETHW "Samuel Insull"; EBSCO Research Starters; Britannica; Loyola Univ. Chicago Insull Papers.)
- **TVA:** act signed **May 18, 1933**; Norris Dam construction began **October 1933**; before TVA about **3 in 100** Tennessee Valley families had electricity. (Library of Congress business-history guide; TVA "Life Before TVA"; National Archives milestone documents.)
- **Hoover Dam power:** first generator **began transmitting to Los Angeles October 9, 1936** over **266 miles** of line; the unit is also recorded reaching **full operation October 26, 1936** — sources use both dates for "first"; outline says "October 1936" and notes the two dates. Dam structure/construction → `landmarks`. (HISTORY.com this-day entry; EDN engineering-history note; Reclamation records.)
- **REA / rural electrification (parked entry confirmed + expanded):** farms with central-station electricity **<11 in 100 (1935)** → **~50% (1945)** → **~9 in 10 (1950)** → **~97% (1960)** (EH.net Encyclopedia; Living New Deal; Britannica). **REA created by Executive Order 7037, May 11, 1935; Rural Electrification Act signed May 20, 1936** — long-term low-cost loans to farmer-owned cooperatives. (Encyclopedia.com REA entries; electric.coop history.)
- **"The next greatest thing" quote:** a Tennessee farmer, **recorded 1930**: "The greatest thing on earth is to have the love of God in your heart, and the next greatest thing is to have electricity in your house." Documented in **David Nye, *Electrifying America*** (MIT Press); it titled the NRECA's 50th-anniversary history ***The Next Greatest Thing* (Pence & Dahl, NRECA, 1984)**. Speaker unnamed in the record — say "a Tennessee farmer," no invented name.
- **Louisan Mamer and the REA "electric circus":** REA home-electrification specialist who toured farm towns demonstrating appliances and electric cooking, 1930s–40s. (Smithsonian NMAH "Power from the people" story; HISTORY.com feature — journalism.)
- **Pedernales/Hill Country first light (context for the era, LBJ thread):** Pedernales Electric Cooperative energized **October 27, 1939** — 1,800 miles of line, first 422 customers, Burnet County substation; families returning at night saw their first lit farmhouses; documented in NPS LBJ materials and Caro, *The Path to Power* ("The Sad Irons"). (NPS "The LBJ Legacy: Pedernales Electric Co-op.")
- **Pearl Yates / Lena Boyce / Sam Oswalt — named REA voices:** from **44 oral histories gathered by the North Carolina Association of Electric Cooperatives in the 1980s** (REA 50th anniversary), held in **UNC's Southern Oral History Program**; retold with quotes in HISTORY.com journalism ("These Women Taught Depression-Era Americans to Use Electricity"). Pearl Yates: wired **1939**, walked room to room with her children ("we'd stop and look and they were so pretty"). Lena Boyce: first electric light showed her the kitchen walls were dirty. Sam Oswalt: electric pump ended hauling horses to the stream; "to think of setting over here in the dark for years and trying to read by oil lamp." **Status candidate: pull the primary SOHP interviews at prose time.**
- **WWII oil:** of ~**7 billion barrels** consumed by the Allies in WWII, ~**6 billion** were American (AOGHS "Oil in War"). **Big Inch** (24-inch, East Texas→Illinois→East) completed August 1943; with **Little Big Inch** carried **350+ million barrels** East before V-E Day; built because U-boats were sinking coastal tankers; Ickes called it one of the country's "most potent weapons of war" (1945). (TSHA "Big Inch and Little Big Inch"; NIST "The Big Inch"; UVA/The Conversation history — journalism-adjacent academic essay.)

## Era 09 — 1950 to 2000

- **EBR-1:** **December 20, 1951**, NRTS Idaho — first usable electricity from nuclear energy; lit **four 200-watt bulbs**; ASME/IEEE landmarks. (Idaho National Laboratory; DOE "9 Notable Facts"; ASME.)
- **Arco, Idaho:** **July 17, 1955** — first town lit entirely by nuclear power, from **BORAX III**, ~1,200 residents, about an hour on reactor power alone. (Argonne/AEC 1955 press release; INL; BoiseDev retrospective — journalism.)
- **Atoms for Peace:** Eisenhower UN General Assembly speech **December 8, 1953**. (DOE history; Eisenhower Library.)
- **Shippingport:** construction from 1954; **first electricity to Duquesne Light's grid December 18, 1957; full rated 60,000 kW December 23, 1957**; opening usually dated December 2, 1957 (first criticality). **Precise claim:** first **full-scale** U.S. nuclear power plant devoted **exclusively to peacetime/civilian** electricity — NOT the first nuclear electricity (EBR-1 1951), not the first grid nuclear power anywhere (UK's Calder Hall 1956), and smaller/dual-use U.S. units preceded it. Outline words the "first" carefully. (DOE "December 23, 1957: Shippingport"; ASME landmark; ANS Nuclear Newswire; EBSCO.)
- **1973–74 embargo:** OAPEC embargo on the U.S. announced **October 1973** (embargo decisions Oct 17–20), tied to U.S. resupply of Israel in the Yom Kippur War; lifted **March 1974**. Oil ~**$2.90 → $11.65/barrel** (quadrupling over ~six months). Gas lines; **odd-even license-plate rationing** in many states; **national 55 mph speed limit** (signed into law January 1974). (U.S. State Dept. Office of the Historian "Oil Embargo, 1973–1974"; Federal Reserve History "Oil Shock of 1973–74"; Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine retrospective — journalism.)
- **The energy laws:** **Energy Policy and Conservation Act signed December 22, 1975** — created the **Strategic Petroleum Reserve** and the first **CAFE fuel-economy standards** (cars to 27.5 mpg by 1985, roughly a doubling); **Department of Energy activated October 1, 1977** (Organization Act signed August 4, 1977). (EBSCO EPCA entry; Congress.gov S.622 (94th); DOE "Our History"; Britannica CAFE.)
- **Three Mile Island:** **March 28, 1979**, TMI-2, partial core meltdown — worst U.S. commercial nuclear accident. **March 30:** Gov. Dick Thornburgh advised **pregnant women and preschool-age children within 5 miles** to leave and those within 10 miles to stay indoors; ~**140,000** residents left voluntarily amid conflicting official information. **Health outcome:** small radioactive release; NRC and 12+ epidemiological studies since 1981 found **no detectable health effects; no deaths attributed** — while the fear and the evacuation are documented fact. **Industry effect:** new-reactor orders ended; no newly built U.S. reactor entered service until Vogtle 3 (2023). (NRC Backgrounder; World Nuclear Association; PBS American Experience "Meltdown at Three Mile Island"; Dickinson College TMI archive.) **Films (parked to `disasters`, their lead):** *Meltdown: Three Mile Island* (2022, Netflix, 4-part documentary, dir. Kief Davidson).
- **Carter's solar panels:** **32 solar water-heating panels dedicated on the West Wing roof June 20, 1979**; used ~7 years; **removed 1986 during roof repairs under Reagan and never reinstalled**; stored, then to **Unity College, Maine, 1991** (16 reinstalled for cafeteria hot water); one donated to the **Smithsonian NMAH (2009)**; others at the Carter Library and the Solar Science and Technology Museum, Dezhou, China. State the removal plainly (roof repairs, not reinstated) — no theatrical "tore them down" claim. (Smithsonian NMAH object record; Yale Climate Connections; SEJ/NYT retrospective — journalism.)
- **Natural gas & petroleum kept growing post-war** (context line): EIA, "History of energy consumption in the United States, 1775–2009."

## Era 10 — 2000 to Today (cutoff: 2026)

- **Fracking:** George Mitchell / Mitchell Energy worked the **Barnett Shale** from the early 1980s, ~two decades of trial; engineer **Nick Steinsberger's slickwater fracturing (late 1990s)** cut costs and made shale gas commercial; combined with horizontal drilling in the 2000s. **Shale gas ~5 Bcf/d (2007) → ~80 Bcf/d (2023)**; U.S. became the **world's largest natural-gas producer (2011)** and **passed Saudi Arabia in 2018 as largest crude producer**. (TSHA George P. Mitchell entry; ClearPath/industry histories; AEI/IER profiles — advocacy-adjacent, cross-checked; EIA production data.) **Debates, both sides, honestly:** cheaper energy, jobs, coal displacement (gas burns cleaner than coal) VS. **induced earthquakes tied to wastewater injection (USGS — Oklahoma)**, groundwater-contamination concerns, methane leakage. Damage detail → `land-environment` (parked).
- **Gas passes coal for electricity:** first full year **2016** (natural gas 34% of generation; monthly first in April 2015). (EIA Today in Energy id=31672/27072.)
- **Wind + solar:** record **~17% of U.S. electricity in 2025** (EIA Today in Energy id=67367); **all renewables 24.2% of generation in 2024** (EIA data via Electrek — journalism aggregating EIA). Solar the fastest-growing source.
- **Jobs:** BLS 2024–34 projections — **wind-turbine service technicians +49.9%, solar photovoltaic installers +42.1%**, the two fastest-growing U.S. occupations; combined they add <20,000 jobs (state the small absolute number honestly). (BLS Employment Projections 2024–34; BLS TED.)
- **EVs:** EV + hybrid = **20% of new light-vehicle sales in 2024**; battery-only EVs **8.1% (2024)** vs 7.8% (2023). (EIA Today in Energy id=62924/63904; CNBC/Cox Automotive — journalism.) Demand growth from data centers/AI noted in BLS/EIA projections.
- **Texas, February 2021 (winter storm Uri) — journalism + official records, labeled:** generation of all types failed in the cold (gas, coal, nuclear, wind); **>4.5 million households / ~10 million people** lost power, some for days; ERCOT shed 20,000 MW — largest manually controlled load-shed in U.S. history; grid was **4 minutes 37 seconds** from total collapse (ERCOT statement via Houston Public Media/KUT); **official state death toll 246 (Texas DSHS, 2022)** — researchers (e.g., BuzzFeed News excess-death analysis) argue substantial undercount; **$195 billion+** damage estimates. (FERC/NERC final report; UT Energy Institute report; Houston Public Media; EBSCO; Texas Tribune.) Storm-and-response as event → `disasters` (parked).
- **Nuclear's small revival:** **Vogtle Unit 3 commercial July 31, 2023; Unit 4 commercial April 29, 2024** (1,114 MW each) — first newly constructed U.S. reactors in over 30 years; TMI Unit 1 restart deal and SMR plans are in progress as of 2026 — keep dated and revisit. (EIA Today in Energy id=57280/61963; DOE.)
- **"Energy independence," defined honestly:** **2019 — U.S. energy production 101.0 quads > consumption 100.2 quads, first time since 1957** (EIA Monthly Energy Review). The U.S. **still imports crude** (Nov 2019: 5.8M b/d imported crude vs 3.0M exported) while net-exporting petroleum products; "independence" = producing more than consuming overall, not zero imports. (EIA Today in Energy id=43515; EIA FAQ; Forbes/R. Rapier explainer — journalism.)

## Disputes and honest-framing log
1. **"First sawmill/gristmill" claims** — multiple towns claim firsts; outline gives dates (1621 Virginia wind/grist; 1634 Piscataqua water mills) and says "among the first."
2. **Firewood consumption** — estimates 15 vs 30–40 cords/year; range stated.
3. **Horse-spread chronology** — 2023 Science study (pre-1680 Native horse use) vs older Pueblo-Revolt-diffusion narrative; both stated.
4. **Nantucket 1712 Hussey story** — tradition recorded 1835, may be legend; `land-environment` already carries the caveat; we don't retell.
5. **"Kerosene killed whaling / saved the whales"** — oversimplification; whaling declined first, camphene was the mass fuel, the Civil War tax pivoted the market. Stated with the tax numbers.
6. **Drake's "Colonel"** — invented by James Townsend for marketing; stated.
7. **Hoover Dam "first power" date** — Oct 9, 1936 (first transmission to LA) vs Oct 26, 1936 (full operation); both recorded.
8. **Shippingport's "first"** — first full-scale exclusively-civilian U.S. plant; EBR-1 (1951), BORAX III/Arco (1955), Calder Hall UK (1956) all precede in narrower senses; worded precisely.
9. **TMI health effects** — NRC/epidemiology: no attributable deaths or detectable effects; community fear and 140,000 self-evacuations equally documented; both stated.
10. **Carter panels removal** — removed 1986 during roof repairs under Reagan, never reinstated; no evidence of a theatrical order to rip them out; stated plainly.
11. **Uri death toll** — official 246 (DSHS) vs researchers' higher excess-death estimates; both stated, journalism labeled.
