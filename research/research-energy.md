## Era 01 — Before 1500

- **No draft animals north of Mexico before the horse returned with Europeans.** Human muscle carried and dragged loads; dogs pulled the travois on the Plains; canoes moved bulk. (Smithsonian NMAI "A Song for the Horse Nation" exhibit materials; standard museum framing.)
- **Deliberate Native burning as energy/land management:** NPS, "Indigenous Fire Practices Shape Our Land" — fire used on regular cycles to clear underbrush, maintain habitat, and support food plants; documented burning cycles among the Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa in California (NPS; The Nature Conservancy; HISTORY.com survey — journalism). Colonists later suppressed the practice; suppression story belongs to `native-nations`/`land-environment`.
- Wood + fire = heat, cooking, light. No mills, no draft animals, no fossil fuel use beyond surface finds.

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-302a): the travois, and what cultural burning was for
- **Travois.** "Travois were two large poles attached together at one end creating an acute angle." "American Indians used dogs to drag travois before the introduction of horses to the Plains." "Horses were able to drag travois farther, faster, and carry more weight than dogs." (NPS, "North American Indian Migration into North Dakota" lesson plan, https://www.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/north-american-indian-migration-into-north-dakota-lesson-plan.htm.) Note for writers: the bank's "no draft animals north of Mexico" line conflicts with dogs dragging loads. Write "no horses, oxen or mules" and let dogs stand as the one hauling animal.
- **What cultural burning is and what it was for (NPS, "Indigenous Fire Practices Shape our Land," https://www.nps.gov/subjects/fire/indigenous-fire-practices-shape-our-land.htm).** Cultural burning is "the intentional lighting of smaller, controlled fires to provide a desired cultural service, such as promoting the health of vegetation and animals that provide food, clothing, ceremonial items and more." Native people "used fire to clear areas for crops and travel, to manage the land for specific species of both plants and animals, to hunt game." In the East, "oak and chestnut trees, dominant in many forests, are the result of targeted burning to achieve specific nut crops." The NPS page does not name the Karuk, Yurok or Hupa.
- **Karuk and Yurok burning (US Forest Service research, Marks-Block, Lake, Bliege Bird and Curran, 2021, "Revitalized Karuk and Yurok cultural burning to enhance California hazelnut for basketweaving," https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62061).** Karuk and Yurok "cultural fire regimes with high burn frequencies (e.g., three to five years)" gave the best hazelnut growth for basketry stems. One growing season after a burn, the plots produced "a 13-fold increase in basketry stems." (The study measured present-day burns that revive the old practice.)

## Era 02 — The 1500s

- **Horses arrived with Spanish expeditions in the 1500s** (Southeast and Southwest entradas). The first non-human muscle power on the continent in thousands of years.
- **First water-powered mills in what is now the U.S.: 1598, New Mexico** — Juan de Oñate's colonists built small "tub mills" (horizontal wheel flat in the water, direct-drive to the stones); grindstones freighted up the Camino Real. Source: *Santa Fe New Mexican*, "Early New Mexico had hundreds of mills" — **journalism about local history, labeled in outline.** Taos later a mill center.
- Honest framing kept: for nearly everyone on the continent, muscle/fire/wood were unchanged all century.

## Era 03 — The 1600s

- **Windmill, 1621, Virginia:** Sir George Yeardley erected the first recorded windmill in English America at Flowerdew Hundred ("Windmill Point") on the James. **Gristmill, 1621, Jamestown-area:** first recorded North American English gristmill the same year. (Colonial Williamsburg research reports; encyclopedia entries via NCpedia/e-WV.)
- **1634 Newichawannock (South Berwick, Maine):** John Mason sent three carpenters, William Chadbourne among them, to build a gristmill and sawmill at the falls of the Piscataqua tributary; operating 1634. Multiple "first sawmill in America" claims exist among Piscataqua-region towns — the outline states the date and says "among the first," refusing the trophy. (Old Berwick Historical Society; Tide Mill Institute "Sawmills and the Economy of Early New England"; Historic New England, *Merchant and Millwright*.)
- **William Chadbourne** — named 1634 millwright, documented in Old Berwick Historical Society materials (Stackpole 1926 and site histories). Story status: candidate (primary town-record confirmation wanted at prose time).
- **Colonial firewood burn rate — a RANGE, honestly stated:** estimates run from about **15 cords/year** (one 17th-century New England estimate) to **30–40 cords/year** for heating + cooking with open hearths; open fireplaces lost most heat up the chimney. State as "historians' estimates range from about 15 to 40 cords." (Shipley Energy/Stacker 250-year heating survey; Farmers' Museum (Cooperstown) blog; author-compiled colonial estimates — secondary, hence the range.)
- **Horses in Native hands earlier than the textbook story:** 2023 *Science* study (Taylor et al., with Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee co-authors) — archaeological horses in Wyoming/Kansas/New Mexico show riding, bridling, corn feeding **by the early 1600s**, before the Pueblo Revolt. The Revolt (1680) still mattered: perhaps **1,500+ horses** passed into Native hands at once, the largest single transfer (Smithsonian NMAI). Outline states both — new finding + Revolt transfer. (Science News; AAAS; PBS NewsHour; CU Boulder release; Smithsonian NMAI.)
- **Shore whaling / whale oil from ~1690:** NPS New Bedford Whaling NHP ("Whale Products"): whale oil a primary American lamp fuel for ~150 years beginning about 1690. Long Island/New England shore whaling preceded deep-sea voyages. (NPS; Nantucket Historical Association — the 1712 Hussey deep-sea origin story is *tradition first written down in 1835*, already told with that caveat by `land-environment`; we do not retell it.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247): William Chadbourne confirmed, and whose land the mills stood on
- **The three carpenters, from the society's own account.** "In July 1634, William Chadbourne, James Wall and John Goddard, three English carpenters under contract with Capt. John Mason's Laconia Company, arrived in present-day South Berwick, Maine, from England aboard the vessel 'Pied Cow.'" Their contract "called for them to build a saw mill and grist mill" on the Asbenbedick or Little Newichawannock River. The sawmill, "thought to be the first over-shot water-powered site in America," stood in the "Rocky Gorge" below today's Great Works bridge on Brattle Street. (Old Berwick Historical Society, "Great Works," updated December 2020, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/trades-occupations/great-works.html.) An overshot wheel is one where the water falls onto the top of the wheel and its weight turns it. "Thought to be the first" is the society's hedge: keep it as a claim, never as a fact.
- **The primary record behind it.** James Wall, carpenter and millwright, gave a sworn statement (deposition) on May 21, 1652, in a lawsuit over Mason's land: about 1634 he and his partners William Chadbourne and John Goddard came over and ran a sawmill and a "stamping mill for corn" at Asbenbedick Falls for three or four years, and Wall and Chadbourne each built a house there. (Old Berwick Historical Society timeline, Rick Coughlin and Norma Keim, 2004, lists "Depositions of James Wall ... about the location of John Mason's 1634 mills; law suit" under 1652, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/trades-occupations/timeline-of-the-great-works-mills.html. The deposition's wording is from genealogy pages (WikiTree Chadbourne-4; rootsweb) that quote it (unconfirmed: search summary only).) The same timeline records a "model of a saw-mill sent to Newichawannock on ship Pied Cow" in a May 1631 letter from Thomas Eyre.
- **Story status: verified.** Chadbourne's name, trade, year, ship, employer and job are stated by the Old Berwick Historical Society and rest on Wall's 1652 deposition. His birth and death years vary across genealogy sites (before 1582 to after 1652): leave them out.
- **Whose land.** The river's name, Newichawannock, is Abenaki (Old Berwick Historical Society). "South Berwick's First People" (Norma Keim, OBHS, 2012, https://www.oldberwick.org/history-articles/people/17th-century/south-berwicks-first-people.html): the people there were Abenaki (Wabanaki), Algonquian speakers, and those at Quamphegan were likely western Abenaki. "Between 1616 and 1618, seven out of ten natives died" of European diseases such as measles or smallpox, before the English carpenters came. The sagamore (leader) Rowls survived. On March 19, 1650, Rowls sold his village land, Quamphegan, to Thomas Spencer "for Five Pounds Sterling" (York Deeds, quoted by OBHS). No record found says Mason's men asked anyone's leave to build the 1634 mills.

## Era 04 — 1700 to 1750

- **Charcoal iron:** furnaces ran on charcoal made by colliers in earthen pits/mounds; the quantified appetite is documented at Hopewell (era 05 entry below) — era 04 text keeps the practice general to avoid borrowing later numbers. Saugus (1646–1670) is told by `elements`/`technology`; our angle is only the fuel.
- **Whale-oil lamps and spermaceti candles:** spermaceti (head-matter wax of the sperm whale) made the brightest, cleanest, most expensive candles — "the most valuable product of the Yankee whaling industry" (New Bedford Whaling Museum; NPS "Whale Products"; Emily Irwin, "The Spermaceti Candle and the American Whaling Industry," *Historia* 2012).
- **Franklin stove, 1741:** Franklin's "Pennsylvania fireplace," cast iron, designed for more heat and less smoke from less wood; sold poorly until David Rittenhouse's improvements. The stove-as-machine → `technology` (which already carries Franklin); the wood saved is ours. (Britannica "Franklin stove"; ushistory.org; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.) Boundary rule: 1741 → this era, not 1750–1800.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247b): who hauled the charcoal in this era, the Baltimore Iron Works
- **Owners.** "Five wealthy and politically-connected planters ... founded the Baltimore Iron Works in 1731." Dr. Charles Carroll, a surgeon, gave 2,200 acres on the Patapsco River and was the first manager. His cousins Charles and Daniel Carroll, Daniel Dulany and Benjamin Tasker "provided money and enslaved laborers." Laborers, "many of whom were enslaved or transported convicts from Britain, worked 12-hour shifts hauling cartloads of charcoal, oyster shells, and iron ore to the top of the furnace." Work ran "around the clock, six or seven days a week." The owners "failed to provide enough food or replacements for the depleted workforce." Some workers ran away, and others worked slowly on purpose. In good years the partners made a 55 percent return. (Same source.) A transported convict was a person convicted of a crime in Britain and shipped to the colonies to be sold into years of forced work.

## Era 05 — 1750 to 1800

- **First steam engine in America — Schuyler copper mine, North Arlington, NJ:** mine flooding led John Schuyler to order a Newcomen-type pumping engine from England **1748**; parts arrived **1753** with engine-builder **Josiah Hornblower**; engine pumping **1755** (some accounts say trial runs from 1754; the 1755 in-service date is standard). First steam engine on the continent. (Richard P. McCormick, "The First Steam Engine in America," *Journal of the Rutgers University Libraries*; HistoryOfInformation.com; Engines of Our Ingenuity #1085.) Hornblower (1729–1809) stayed in New Jersey; later public offices — story kept to the engine.
- **Hopewell Furnace fuel appetite (founded 1771; NPS):** in blast, up to **800 bushels of charcoal a day ≈ an acre of forest cut per day**; annually **6,000–7,000 cords ≈ 200 acres of woodland**; the works controlled 4,000–8,000 acres of forest. Payroll detail (1835–37): 112 of 213 workers were woodcutters — that datum chronologically belongs to era 06; parked for `work-workers`. (NPS Hopewell Furnace NHS "Teaching with Historic Places"; Forest History Today 2010.)
- **Whale oil toward peak:** fleet growth through the late 1700s; peak comes 1846 (era 06). Spermaceti candles remain the premium light. (NPS; New Bedford Whaling Museum.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247b): how charcoal was made, who made it, and a correction to the Hopewell cord count
- **Correction note beside the Hopewell entry above (6,000–7,000 cords).** NPS "Charcoal Making" (https://www.nps.gov/hofu/learn/historyculture/charcoal-making.htm) gives "5000 to 6000 cords of wood, or more than 200 acres of woodlands each year." The Teaching with Historic Places lesson (bank source) gives 6,000–7,000. Both are NPS. Write "5,000 to 7,000 cords, more than 200 acres a year" or state both figures.
- **The method (NPS "Charcoal Making").** The collier cleared and levelled the ground, drove an 18-foot green pole (the "fagan") into the center, built a three-cornered chimney around it, and stacked the wood in three tiers. "The pit is covered with a layer of leaves and dust." He lit it by shovelling red coals from his cooking fire into the chimney, then walked on the pit ("jumping the pit") to find soft spots and fill air holes. "In about 10 days to 2 weeks, the pit 'comes to foot' meaning that the wood has been turned into charcoal." "The average charcoal hearth consumed 30 cords of wood to produce around 1100 bushels of charcoal, enough to keep the furnace operating for 1 1/2 days." The furnace used "800 bushels of charcoal per day." A collier "would live in a makeshift hut with one or two helpers who would tend up to 8 or 9 pits at one time." Woodcutters, "using only axes," averaged two cords a day. (Charcoal is wood heated with too little air to burn away, which drives off water and gases and leaves nearly pure carbon that burns hotter than wood.)
- **The work (NPS, "Hopewell Furnace: A Pennsylvania Iron-making Plantation," Teaching with Historic Places, https://www.nps.gov/articles/hopewell-furnace-a-pennsylvania-iron-making-plantation-teaching-with-historic-places.htm).** "During the 1830s, the furnace paid approximately 20 colliers, each tending eight or nine pits." NPS "Charcoal Making" gives "nearly 250 workers on the payroll from 1835 to 1837, more than 100 were wood-cutters" (the bank's older line says 112 of 213: two NPS figures, state both or say "more than 100").

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-302a): how the Schuyler engine worked, what became of it, and whose land Hopewell stood on
- **How a Newcomen engine worked (ASME, "The Newcomen Memorial Engine," International Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark brochure no. 70, https://www.asme.org/wwwasmeorg/media/resourcefiles/aboutasme/who%20we%20are/engineering%20history/landmarks/70-newcomen-engine.pdf).** "Steam is generated at atmospheric pressure in the boiler and fills the cylinder during the upward stroke of the piston. The steam valve is then closed and the steam is condensed by a jet of cold water causing a vacuum under the piston. The atmospheric pressure acting on the top of the piston forces it down ... and this constitutes the working stroke. The piston is raised again by the over balancing weight of the pump rods." Science Museum Group (https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co50900/newcomen-atmospheric-engine): the pressure of the air drove the piston down "and, through the rocking beam, lifted the heavy pump rods hanging within the mine shaft." John H. Lienhard, *Engines of Our Ingenuity* no. 1085 (https://engines.egr.uh.edu/episode/1085): the first Newcomen engines were "two stories high" and "delivered around ten horsepower"; Schuyler's mine near Passaic was "shut down by flooding" in 1748, and Schuyler paid Jonathan Hornblower (Josiah's father) 1,000 pounds to ship a "fire engine" and mechanics.
- **The engine's arrival and fate (Richard P. McCormick, "The First Steam Engine in America," *Journal of the Rutgers University Library*, https://jrul.libraries.rutgers.edu/index.php/jrul/article/view/1246).** In September 1753 the ship *Irene* reached New York after twelve weeks from London carrying "the first Newcomen or Cornish 'fire engine' to reach America," with "a young British engineer, Josiah Hornblower." It was "to be employed in pumping the deep shafts free of water." "By the early part of 1755" it was "assembled, and placed in operation, and for the next two decades it gave intermittent service." The *New York Mercury* of March 22, 1762, reported that the engine house "took Fire and was burnt to the Ground," and the engine works, said to have cost "near Ten Thousand Pounds," were destroyed. A second fire destroyed the works on July 17, 1768 (*Pennsylvania Chronicle*, July 25, 1768). Hornblower leased the mine in 1761. In his letter of July 23, 1768, he wrote that late in 1767 he "was oblig'd to stop the Engine for want of Fuel." Mining stopped in 1773. The engine "was broken up around 1800, and half of the cylinder eventually came to rest in the Smithsonian Institution." (Lienhard says the engine was repaired in 1793 and pumped into the 1800s. The two accounts differ: write neither.)
- **Hopewell's land (Robinson & Associates, *Hopewell Furnace National Historic Site Historic Resource Study*, for NPS, December 1, 2004, pp. 14-18, https://npshistory.com/publications/hofu/hrs.pdf).** The people of the Delaware region, "in which Hopewell Furnace would be built, spoke the Unami dialect and called themselves the Lenni Lenape." After William Penn left in 1701 and died in 1718, under his agent James Logan "land transactions with the Lenni Lenape were often unscrupulous and sometimes fraudulent. By the middle of the eighteenth century, nearly all Native American peoples had been driven out of the lower Delaware and Susquehanna watersheds, moving westward to the Allegheny, Monongahela, and Ohio river valleys."

## Era 06 — 1800 to 1850

- **Jesse Fell, February 11, 1808, Wilkes-Barre:** burned anthracite successfully in an open **iron grate** at his tavern — the demonstration that "stone coal" could heat homes (anthracite needs bottom draft; the grate provided it). Spurred the anthracite industry. Earlier: the Gore brothers (blacksmiths) used anthracite in forges from 1769. (Jesse Fell historical marker/HMDB; Wyoming Valley History; explorePAhistory; Fell's own 1826 letter via Hopkin Thomas Project.)
- **Lehigh Navigation organized 1818**; anthracite floated to Philadelphia in "arks," then by canal; anthracite stoves/furnaces spread in the 1820s–40s. (Lehigh Coal & Navigation histories; Hopkin Thomas Project.)
- **Gaslight — Baltimore first:** Rembrandt Peale lit a room of his Holliday Street museum with coal gas in **1816**; **Gas Light Company of Baltimore chartered June 17, 1816** (first U.S. gas company; ancestor of BGE); **first public gas street lamp lit February 7, 1817** at Market and Lemon Streets. (American Oil & Gas Historical Society "Illuminating Gaslight"; Atlas Obscura marker record; *Baltimore Sun* retrospective — journalism; *Chronicles of Baltimore* 1881.)
- **Whaling peak, 1846:** American fleet **735 vessels** of roughly 900 worldwide; New Bedford the leading port (400+ vessels from New Bedford at its height); industry peak variously given as 1846–1852 — outline uses the 1846 fleet-count peak. (New Bedford Whaling Museum timeline; Whaling in the United States surveys; Springer *Natural Resources Research* whale-oil study.)

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-302a): Fell's grate, how coal gas was made and who could pay for it, and how whale oil was made
- **Jesse Fell (ExplorePAhistory, "Jesse Fell" marker page, https://explorepahistory.com/hmarker.php?markerId=1-A-C9).** Marker: "At his tavern here on Feb. 11, 1808, Fell burned 'stone coal' successfully in an open grate." Anthracite, "the type found in northeastern Pennsylvania, was considered too difficult to burn for use as a household product." Fell built "an L-shaped grate of 'Small Iron rods'" and wrote: "I set it up in my common room fireplace, and on first lighting found it to burn excellently well." He wrote: "I had for some time entertained the Idea that if a sufficient body of [anthracite] was ignited it would burn." Anthracite was "cheaper and more fuel-efficient than wood" (page's summary).
- **Coal gas (Christopher Castaneda, California State University Sacramento, "Manufactured and Natural Gas Industry," EH.net Encyclopedia, https://eh.net/encyclopedia/manufactured-and-natural-gas-industry/).** "A rather simple process of heating coal, or other organic substance, produces a flammable gas," a mixture of carbon monoxide, hydrogen and other gases. It was stored in holders called gasometers and sent through pipes. The Gas Light Company of Baltimore lit its first street lamp on February 7, 1817, at Market and Lemon Streets. By 1850 about 50 urban areas had gas works. In the early 1800s "gas lighting was expensive and beyond the means of most Americans": streets, businesses and richer homes in larger cities. - **How whale oil was made, and Haley (Charlotte Bruce Harvey, "What Happens When a Nineteenth-Century Whaler Makes a Twenty-First-Century Voyage," *Humanities*, NEH, September/October 2014, https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2014/septemberoctober/feature/what-happens-when-nineteenth-century-whaler-makes-twenty-fi).** "In 1844, Nelson Cole Haley, a twelve-year-old boy from New Bedford, Massachusetts, ran off to sea. Haley's own words from *Whale Hunt*: "I raised the iron, and with all the force I was capable of, I sent it to the socket into the vast mass of blubber and meat that was now only a foot or two clear of the boat. Then "the second mate slayed the whale with his lance, and the crew rowed the massive carcass back to the ship to be butchered and rendered in the morning." The crew stood on the carcass "peeling away strips of blubber that were hoisted onboard and carved into what the whalers called 'bibles,' book-like chunks." These "were tossed in two huge metal pots atop the tryworks, a massive brick furnace on the deck. Once 'tried out,' or rendered, the liquid whale oil was funneled down below into barrels. The remaining cracklings ... were then burned for fuel in the tryworks." Whaleships "were at sea for three to five years at a time." The *Morgan* was built in New Bedford in 1841 and made 37 voyages between 1841 and 1921, and "carried sailors from fifty countries."

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-302a): lamp fuels, Drake's pipe, Pearl Street's coal, AC and DC, Kemmler, and Avondale's furnace
- **Lamp fuels (B.A. Wells and K.L. Wells, "Camphene to Kerosene Lamps," American Oil & Gas Historical Society, https://aoghs.org/products/camphene-to-kerosene-lamps/).** Camphene "combined one part turpentine with four parts alcohol and a small amount of camphor for aroma." Its alcohol "contained highly volatile vapors," which caused "flash fires." Prices per gallon: whale oil $1.30 to $2.50, lard oil about 90 cents, camphene about 50 cents, kerosene about 50 cents. "The Internal Revenue Act imposed a $2.08 per gallon tax on alcohol between 1862 and 1864" to pay for the Union Army. The law was meant for drinking alcohol but "did not specifically exempt industrial uses, including camphene, which was about 75 percent high-proof alcohol." Kerosene came from "simple distillation of oil."
- **Drake's well (AOGHS, "First American Oil Well," https://aoghs.org/petroleum-pioneers/american-oil-history/).** To stop Oil Creek water seeping in and the hole caving in, Drake "conceived the idea of driving a pipe down to the rock through which to start the drill." William "Uncle Billy" Smith ran the rig, "drilling an average of three feet per day through rock and shale," powered by "a six-horsepower steam engine." The Seneca Oil Company of New Haven, Connecticut, employed Drake. Later he returned to Pennsylvania with "little money" and "his health failing." In 1873 Titusville residents secured him an annual "$1,500 pension."
- **Pearl Street's fuel (IEEE Milestone, "Pearl Street Station, 1882," ETHW, https://ethw.org/Milestones:Pearl_Street_Station,_1882).** "Four coal-fired boilers" drove "Armington & Sims reciprocating steam engines" that turned "six dynamos" of "about 100 kW" each. Day one: "85 customers having a total of about 400 lamps." After a year: "about 10,000 lamps serving 513 customers." Area: "one-quarter square mile." Edison "was standing in the office of J. Pierpont Morgan of Drexel, Morgan & Company." A "low-voltage dc system has inherent disadvantages, the chief one being high line losses that limit the distance that the dc electric power can be economically transmitted."
- **AC and DC (Allison Lantero, "The War of the Currents: AC vs. DC Power," US Department of Energy, November 18, 2014, https://www.energy.gov/articles/war-currents-ac-vs-dc-power).** DC is "current that runs continually in a single direction, like in a battery." AC "reverses direction a certain number of times per second, 60 in the U.S., and can be converted to different voltages relatively easily using a transformer." DC "is not easily converted to higher or lower voltages." Westinghouse's $399,000 bid for the 1893 Chicago fair undercut General Electric's $554,000. "On November 16, 1896, Buffalo was lit up by the alternating current from Niagara Falls." (DOE says Edison himself electrocuted animals. Smithsonian, below, says Brown ran the tests at Edison's laboratory with Edison paying him. Use the Smithsonian version.)
- **The electric chair (Gilbert King, "Edison vs. Westinghouse: A Shocking Rivalry," *Smithsonian Magazine*, October 11, 2011, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/edison-vs-westinghouse-a-shocking-rivalry-102146036/).** Harold Brown, an electricity salesman, was hired by New York State to build the electric chair, and "Edison was paying him behind the scenes to use alternating current in his design." Stray dogs were bought from neighborhood boys in Orange, New Jersey, for 25 cents each for the tests. During the execution, Kemmler's fist clenched until blood ran from his palm, and the back of his coat briefly caught fire. Westinghouse afterward: "It has been a brutal affair. They could have done better with an ax."
- **Kemmler's crime and execution (Historical Society of the New York Courts, "People ex rel. Kemmler v. Durston," https://history.nycourts.gov/case/kemmler-durston/).** Kemmler killed his common-law wife, Matilda "Tillie" Ziegler, "striking her more than 25 times on the head, neck and shoulders with [a] hatchet during a drunken row." Convicted May 1889. Governor David B. Hill proposed replacing hanging. A commission of Elbridge T. Gerry, Matthew Hale and Dr. A. P. Southwick studied methods, and in 1888 New York lawmakers amended the Code of Criminal Procedure to require death by electricity. The New York Court of Appeals called electrocution "unusual" but not cruel, and the US Supreme Court declined to overturn it (*In re Kemmler*, 1890). August 6, 1890: a 17-second current, a doctor declared him dead, he groaned and breathed again, a second current took "anywhere between one to four minutes" to kill him. The room filled with the smell of burning flesh. Several witnesses fainted. Newspapers called it "a disgrace to civilization."
- **Avondale's furnace and owner (ExplorePAhistory, "Avondale Mine Disaster" marker page, https://explorepahistory.com/hmarker.php?markerId=1-A-ED).** The "Steuben Coal Company" built and ran the Avondale Colliery. Mine companies "kept small fires burning at the bottom of shafts to create drafts that promoted better air circulation." "Sparks from the Avondale ventilating furnace set fire to the timbers in the shaft, and the flames engulfed the breaker at the surface." "The fire had asphyxiated 108 men and boys, and two of the men who attempted to rescue them." The dead included "five boys between the ages of twelve and seventeen." ### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247b, bank check): who ran Three Mile Island and what failed
- World Nuclear Association, "Three Mile Island Accident" (https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/safety-and-security/safety-of-plants/three-mile-island-accident): TMI-2 was operated by **General Public Utilities** (GPU). The chain: a minor fault in the secondary cooling circuit, then a pilot-operated relief valve stuck open and let cooling water escape. Operators had no instrument that showed the valve's real position. Believing the system was too full, they cut the flow of replacement water. The core was uncovered and overheated, and "at least 45% of the core, 62 tonnes, had melted." Average dose to people within 10 miles: 0.08 millisieverts, and no more than 1 millisievert to anyone. The plant's licensee was Metropolitan Edison, a GPU company (unconfirmed: search summary only).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247b, bank check): black lung, Farmington and the 1969 law
- **Farmington, November 20, 1968.** An explosion at **Consol No. 9** mine, Farmington, West Virginia, killed **78** coal miners (MSHA disasters fact sheet). It prompted the **Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969**, enacted March 30, 1970, which required federal inspections of every coal mine (four times a year underground, twice a year for surface mines), let inspectors shut a mine down when they found life-threatening hazards, "added health protections, including for black lung diseases," and "established federal benefits for victims of black lung disease." In 1970, 260 coal miners were killed at work. In 2014, 16 were, "the lowest ever recorded" (MSHA, "Federal Coal Mine and Safety Act of 1969," https://www.msha.gov/federal-coal-mine-and-safety-act-1969). MSHA's table gives 311 killed in 1968, 203 in 1969, 133 in 1980, 38 in 2000 (MSHA coal fatalities table).
- **What black lung is.** Black lung is several lung diseases caused by breathing coal mine dust. The dust sets off the body's defenses, which cannot break down the mineral grains. The defending cells burst and release chemicals that damage the lung. Over time the lungs form scar tissue and hard lumps. Miners cough up "dark, inky phlegm." The worst form, progressive massive fibrosis, fills the lungs with large masses of scar tissue and "leaves its victims gasping for every breath." **Count:** "more than 76,000 miners have died of black lung since 1968," according to the U.S. Department of Labor. From the early 2000s the disease came back: NIOSH found 60 cases of the worst form in 20 months at one small Kentucky clinic in 2016, and an NPR investigation found 962 cases of advanced black lung in that decade, "more than double the 441 cases NIOSH had documented in the last 40 years" (Maya Wei-Haas, "Why Black Lung Disease Is Deadlier Than Ever Before," *Smithsonian Magazine*, May 15, 2017, updated February 8, 2018, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/black-lung-disease-deadlier-than-ever-before-180963303/, journalism). Shared with: `health` (the disease course) · `work-workers` (the miners' fight for the 1969 law).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-247b, bank check): Garrison Dam and the Three Affiliated Tribes; Navajo uranium and Church Rock
- **Garrison Dam, North Dakota.** Congress funded the dam in 1947 (Public Law 296) under the Army Corps of Engineers' **Pick-Sloan Plan** for the Missouri River, which promised flood control and hydroelectric power (Terri Berman, "For the Taking: The Garrison Dam and the Tribal Taking Area," *Cultural Survival Quarterly*, February 22, 2010, https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/taking-garrison-dam-and-tribal-taking-area). The dam was finished in 1953. Its reservoir, Lake Sakakawea, flooded the bottomlands of the **Fort Berthold Reservation** of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara (the Three Affiliated Tribes). **Acres, three figures:** 152,360 acres (search summary of Wikipedia), 153,000 (Cultural Survival), "about 155,000 acres of good farmland" (North Dakota Studies, State Historical Society of North Dakota, https://www.ndstudies.gov/gr4/american-indians-north-dakota/part-4-reservations-north-dakota/section-2-fort-berthold-reservation). "Farms, homes, schools, a hospital, businesses, churches, parks, cemeteries, roads, and entire towns were destroyed," including Elbowoods, Sanish and Van Hook. By 1954 the Elbowoods school building was "under 50 feet of water" (ND Studies). **People moved, counts differ:** 300 families, "more than 90 percent of the population" (Cultural Survival, which gives the 1951 population as 356 families); "more than 300 families and 1,700 residents, 80 percent of the membership" (search summary of Wikipedia). In 1948 tribal chairman **George Gillette** said at the signing: "The truth is, as everyone knows, our Treaty of Fort Laramie ... and our constitution are being torn to shreds by this contract" (Cultural Survival). In 1950, 525 of 991 adults voted to accept the act's terms (Cultural Survival). Shared with: `native-nations` (lead: the nation and its land) · `land-environment`.

- **Uranium for reactors: Navajo land (energy angle; full account in `elements`, T-241a and T-241e patches).** From 1944 to 1986 "nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands" (EPA, via research-elements.md). About 10,000 people worked in uranium, about a quarter of them Navajo, and Navajo miners report "they were not educated about the hazards of uranium mining and were not provided with protective equipment or ventilation" (Brugge and Goble, *American Journal of Public Health*, 2002, via research-elements.md). The Atomic Energy Commission was the only buyer until 1966, and after that uranium was sold for power reactors (EPA search summary, via research-elements.md). **Church Rock, July 16, 1979:** a United Nuclear Corporation tailings dam broke near Church Rock, New Mexico, and sent about 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid and 1,100 tons of solids down the Rio Puerco past about 1,700 Navajo people. ## Parked from `home-family` (2026-09-26, T-245): how homes were heated and lit, by census count
- 1940 Census: over half of US housing units heated mainly with coal and another quarter with wood. Coal fell steadily after 1940. Natural gas grew, and electric heat grew from 1960 (James D. Lutz, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, "Lest We Forget, a Short History of Housing in the United States", ACEEE Summer Study 2004, from Census data).
- Households with electricity: 8 percent in 1890, 94 percent in 1950. Dwellings with electric lights in 1940: 83 percent (Emanuela Cardia, NBER Summer Institute paper, July 2008, from Census data). home-family keeps the kitchen and washday. The fuels and the wires are yours.
