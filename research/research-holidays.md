## Era 01: Before 1500

### Haudenosaunee ceremonial year and the Thanksgiving Address
- Onondaga Nation lists its ceremonies "throughout the lunar year": Midwinter, Maple Sap, Planting, Bean, Strawberry, Green Corn, Harvest. "People responsible for ensuring that our ceremonies are still being performed ... are commonly called 'Faith Keepers'. These people refer to the seasons and the lunar calendar to decide on when our ceremonies are to take place." Faith Keepers are "men and women." "MidWinters lasts for 21 days. Each day we are giving thanks for many different things." (Onondaga Nation, "Ceremonies", https://www.onondaganation.org/culture/ceremonies/)
  - A search summary gave Midwinter as about eight days in the second week of January (unconfirmed: search summary only, source not identified). The Onondaga Nation's own figure is 21 days. Use the Onondaga figure and name it as Onondaga's.
- Thanksgiving Address, "Greetings to the Natural World". Sections in order: The People, The Earth Mother, The Waters, The Fish, The Plants, The Food Plants, The Medicine Herbs, and on to the Creator and Closing Words. Each section ends "Now our minds are one." Credit line: "This translation of the Mohawk version of the Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address was developed, published in 1993, and provided, courtesy of: Six Nations Indian Museum and the Tracking Project." English version: John Stokes and Kanawahienton (David Benedict, Turtle Clan/Mohawk). Mohawk version: Rokwaho (Dan Thompson, Wolf Clan/Mohawk). Original inspiration: Tekaronianekon (Jake Swamp, Wolf Clan/Mohawk). (NMAI PDF, https://americanindian.si.edu/environment/pdf/01_02_Thanksgiving_Address.pdf)
- The Address is known as "The Words That Come Before All Else" and in Mohawk as Ohen:ton Karihwatehkwen. It is spoken at the start and end of gatherings, celebrations and council meetings (unconfirmed: search summary only, desertreport.org and indigenousvalues.org).
- Age: no source opened gives a date of origin for the Address or the cycle. Do not say "before 1500" of any specific wording. The era cell presents the cycle as the nations' own practice, documented now.

### Wampanoag thanksgivings
- Nancy "Smiling Dove" E. Eldredge (Nauset Wampanoag), "Wampanoag Traditions of Giving Thanks", Plimoth Patuxet Museums *Almanack*, November 1998: "From time immemorial, Wampanoag and other Indigenous people of the northeast have held ceremonies to give thanks": in early spring "for the hope of a good growing season" and "in the fall, for a successful harvest." "Annual ceremonies mark the thirteen moons of the lunar year, and Wampanoag people give thanks for the seasonal harvests of strawberries and cranberries." Also ceremonies for "the birth of a child or a marriage." (https://plimoth.org/yath/unit-4/wampanoag-traditions-of-giving-thanks-for-the-children-of-the-earth)
- Cranberry Day, Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah): "an annual celebration and harvest". 2019 program, Tuesday 8 October: Greeting of the Day at Sunrise, Gift to Squant, harvesting of the cranberry, sharing of the meal at noon, contribution to the elders' portion, community potluck (https://wampanoagtribe-nsn.gov/member-events/2019/10/8/cranberry-day). Held the second Tuesday in October, an excused school absence for Wampanoag children on the island (unconfirmed: search summary only, UConn "Cranberry Thanksgiving").

### Green Corn Ceremony (Muscogee Creek and Southeast)
- Eric E. Bowne (Wake Forest University), "Green Corn Ceremony", Encyclopedia of Alabama: "held in mid-summer when the early corn became ripe"; "generally lasted either four or eight days"; fasting "between one-and-a-half and two days. The men practiced a strict fast, but women, children, and the elderly were often allowed to eat certain foods"; villagers "extinguished all the fires in the town"; "The societies at large forgave all crimes except murder"; James Adair gave "a detailed description of a southeastern Indian celebration from the mid-eighteenth century", and William Bartram and Benjamin Hawkins also wrote accounts; "Many Creek and Yuchi towns in Oklahoma maintain their stomp grounds and continue to celebrate the ceremony." (https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/green-corn-ceremony/)
- "Busk" is a traders' word from Creek *puskita* (pusketv), "a fast" (Britannica "Busk festival" and Wikipedia via search summary; Britannica returned 403) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **William Bartram, *Travels* (Philadelphia, 1791), Part IV ch. III, pp. 509 to 510** (Documenting the American South, https://docsouth.unc.edu/nc/bartram/bartram.html), read in full on the page:
  - "THEY have besides, feasts or festivals almost for every month in the year, which are chiefly dedicated to hunting and agriculture."
  - "THE busk or feast of first fruits is their principal festival; this seems to end the last, and begin the new year. IT commences in August, when their new crops of Corn are arrived to perfect maturity: and every town celebrates the busk seperately, when their own harvest is ready."
  - New clothes, pots, pans; "collect all their worn out clothes and other despicable things, sweep and cleanse their houses, squares, and the whole town ... with all the remaining grain and other old provisions, they cast together in one common heap, and consume it with fire; after having taken medicine, and fasted for three days, all the fire in the town is extinguished."
  - "A general amnesty is proclaimed, all malefactors may return to their town, and they are absolved from their crimes."
  - "ON the fourth morning, the high priest, by rubbing dry wood together, produces new fire in the public square, from whence every habitation in the town is supplied." Women bring new corn and fruits to the square. Dancing through the night for three days, then four days of visits from neighbouring towns.
  - **Sources differ on the fast:** Bartram says three days. Bowne says one and a half to two days for men. State both if the fast's length is used.
- Bartram traveled 1773 to 1777 (the book's title page dates; standard). Age before 1500: no source opened dates the ceremony's origin. The written accounts are from the 1700s.

### Hopi and Pueblo sun-watching
- High Altitude Observatory (NCAR), "Hopi Tawa Kátsina": "These ceremonies begin with the Soyala ritual at the winter solstice in December and continue in a series of monthly rituals in each village until the Niman or Home Dance in late July." "In present-day pueblos, Sun watching is the responsibility of the Sun-priest. He typically carries out his observations at sunrise, from an inconspicuous location located not far from the pueblo." "The timing of these and other ceremonies is established by the Sun-priest on the basis of horizon observations of the Sun." "The primary purpose of Sun watching is the setting of the planting calendar, as well as religious ceremonies." (https://www2.hao.ucar.edu/education/prehistoric-southwest/hopi-tawa-katsina)
- Scholarly source for follow-up: Stephen C. McCluskey, "The Astronomy of the Hopi Indians", *Journal for the History of Astronomy* 1977 (https://doi.org/10.1177/002182867700800302). Alexander Stephen's *Hopi Journal* (1936) is the classic primary record (not opened).
- Soyal as up to 16 days long (Kachina House, a shop blog, via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Not used.

### First Salmon Feast (Columbia River)
- CRITFC, "First Salmon Feast": "The Celilo Longhouse First Salmon Feast is generally on the second weekend in April." Order: drummers sing, "the bell is rung as a signal for everyone to stand and join in a prayer song," water is blessed and drunk, salmon and other foods served, closing song, final water blessing. Tradition: "when the Creator was preparing to bring forth people onto the earth," salmon "offered his body to feed the people." (https://critfc.org/salmon-culture/tribal-salmon-culture/first-salmon-feast/)
- CRITFC's member tribes: Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, Nez Perce. Ceremonial fishing opens the season before open fishing (CRITFC search summary; CRITFC home page). The page does not date the practice's origin.

### Makahiki (Hawaii)
- Kaʻanoʻi Walk, "Makahiki: An Overview", Kaʻiwakīloumoku, Kamehameha Schools, November 2013: "The rising of Makaliʻi (the constellation Pleiades) at sunset marks the beginning of the Hawaiian new year"; "Makahiki usually begins mid-November and ends in late January or February"; "War between the aliʻi was forbidden"; the akua loa (staff image of Lono) "led the procession around the mokupuni (island). Offerings of food and other products were collected at the borders of the districts"; games: heihei kūkini (racing), mokomoko (boxing), hākōkō (wrestling), pūhenehene, kōnane. (https://kaiwakiloumoku.ksbe.edu/article/essays-makahiki-an-overview)
- Peter T. Young, Images of Old Hawaiʻi, "Makahiki": rising of Makaliʻi at sunset after the new moon, "beginning in late-October or early-November". Quotes David Malo: "There were four months devoted to the observances of the Makahiki, during which time the ordinary religious ceremonies were omitted." "Before they could play, the taxes for the King – the pig, the taro, the sweet potatoes, the feathers, the kapa, the mats, all things that were made – had to be brought together and offered on the altars of Lono." "An image of Lono was carried around the island by the priests. At each of the ahupuaʻa, the chief of that district presented the gifts." (https://imagesofoldhawaii.com/makahiki/)
- David Malo, about 1793 to 1853, Native Hawaiian historian, *Moolelo Hawaii* (*Hawaiian Antiquities*), full text at https://archive.org/details/hawaiianantiquit00malouoft (Wikipedia via search summary for dates).
- Kapu system ended November 1819 when Liholiho (Kamehameha II) ate with women at a feast during the makahiki season (Images of Old Hawaiʻi "Abolition of the Kapu", via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). For era 6 if used.
- Captain James Cook reached Kealakekua Bay on 17 January 1779, during Makahiki (Images of Old Hawaiʻi). Whether Hawaiians took Cook for Lono is a scholarly dispute (Marshall Sahlins against Gananath Obeyesekere, not researched here). Era 5 fact, not used in the outline.

## Era 02: The 1500s

### Place names from the church calendar
- Ponce de León landed between 2 and 8 April 1513 and named the land "la Florida" for "Pascua Florida", Spain's Easter season (Wikipedia "Pascua Florida"; USF FCIT "April 2: La Florida Claimed for Spain", https://fcit.usf.edu/project/1513/) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Second source to open for the writer: Britannica "Juan Ponce de León".
- Florida Museum of Natural History, Menéndez timeline: "August 28: Sighted land of Florida on St. Augustine's feast day"; "September 8: Menéndez formally claims Florida"; "September 20: Menéndez and 500 soldiers march on Ft. Caroline, capture the fort and rename it San Mateo"; "September 29(?) – Massacre of French at Matanzas"; "October 11 – Second massacre at Matanzas." (https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/histarch/research/st-augustine/menendez/timeline/)

### Fort Caroline thanksgiving, 30 June 1564
- Fort Caroline established by René Goulaine de Laudonnière, 22 June 1564 (Wikipedia via search summary). Laudonnière's memoir *L'histoire notable de la Floride* (published 1586): "On that day on the beach, I commanded us to sing aloud (like a trumpet), to ends that we assembled render thanks to God, of our arrival felicitous and happy." (quoted from the National Huguenot Society by Heidelblog, https://heidelblog.net/2021/11/the-first-huguenot-thanksgiving-in-1564-at-ft-caroline-florida/). Jacksonville History Center describes a 30 June 1564 feast shared by Huguenots and Timucua (https://jaxhistory.org/timucua_first_thanksgiving/). The primary quote mentions the singing and thanks, not a shared meal. Outline states only the service.

### St. Augustine Mass and meal, 8 September 1565
- NPS, Castillo de San Marcos, "The First Thanksgiving" (https://home.nps.gov/casa/learn/historyculture/the-first-thanksgiving.htm): "On September 8, 1565, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and 800 Spanish settlers founded the city of St. Augustine"; celebrant Father Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, first pastor; the Seloy "occupied the site"; the Spanish likely served "cocido, a stew made from salted pork and garbanzo beans"; Seloy may have contributed turkey, venison, gopher tortoise, fish, maize, beans, squash (NPS says "may have", so not stated as fact); "the first community act of religion and thanksgiving in the first permanent European settlement in North America", "56 years before" Plymouth; it "did not become the origin of a national annual tradition"; "British observances ... became a national practice."
- Jacksonville History Center: Grajales's memoir notes that after Mass "the Adelantado [Menendez] had the Indians fed and dined himself." The "first community act" phrase is Michael Gannon's, *The Cross in the Sand*.

### Matanzas (context for the two thanksgivings; `war` leads)
- NPS Fort Matanzas, "The Massacre of the French" (https://www.nps.gov/foma/learn/historyculture/the_massacre.htm): first killing, "111 Frenchmen were killed. Only sixteen were spared - a few who professed being Catholic, some impressed Breton sailors, and four artisans needed at St. Augustine"; second, 12 October 1565, "134 were killed" when Jean Ribault's group surrendered; at Fort Caroline on 20 September "most of the men" were killed, women and children spared and shipped to Havana; "From that time, the inlet was called Matanzas -- meaning 'slaughters' in Spanish."
- Date disagreement: NPS gives 12 October for the second killing, the Florida Museum timeline gives 11 October. The first is "September 29(?)" in the Florida Museum timeline. Outline says "late September" and "October".
- `war` outline gives "About 127" surrendered in the first group (war.md line 60). Numbers differ by source; `war` owns the count.

### Oñate's thanksgiving, 30 April 1598
- Texas Almanac, "The First Thanksgiving?", adapted by Mike Kingston, *Texas Almanac 1990–1991* (https://www.texasalmanac.com/articles/the-first-thanksgiving): 30 April 1598 near present San Elizario; about 500 people "including soldiers, colonists, wives, children" and 7,000 head of livestock; about 50-day desert crossing; Oñate ordered a thanksgiving after a 10-day rest; Franciscan Mass; game hunted by Spaniards and fish from local Native people; Oñate read La Toma claiming the land for Philip II; a play by Capt. Farfán; one member wrote "We built a great bonfire and roasted the meat and fish". Competing claim: Coronado's 1541 celebration in Palo Duro Canyon, "later questioned".
- Primary chronicler: Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, *Historia de la Nueva México* (1610). The Native people who brought fish are called Manso in popular accounts (search summary, Texas Parks & Wildlife Magazine) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Outline says "local Native people".

### Secotan, 1585
- Encyclopedia Virginia, "A Festive Dance" (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/1663-9dadb0c31c73827/): watercolor by John White, about 1585, Secotan, Outer Banks region of present-day North Carolina; "a great religious ceremony, perhaps connected to the corn harvest"; Hariot: participants "attyred in the most strange fashion they can devise havinge certayne marks on the backs to declare of what place they bee"; posts "carved with heads like ... the faces of Nonnes covered with theyr vayles." British Museum holds the painting. White visited in July 1585 (search summary of the same page).

## Era 03: The 1600s

### Virginia
- John Smith, *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles* (1624), Book 3 (Project Gutenberg #56347, read in the text): "The next night being lodged at Kecoughtan; six or seaven dayes the extreame winde, rayne, frost and snow caused us to keepe Christmas among the Salvages, where we were never more merry, nor fed on more plentie of good Oysters, Fish, Flesh, Wild-foule, and good bread; nor never had better fires in England, then in the dry, smoaky houses of Kecoughtan." Colonial Williamsburg (search summary): Smith reached Kecoughtan on 31 December (old style), inside the Twelve Days of Christmas, which by the modern calendar began 10 January 1609 (unconfirmed: search summary only). Kecoughtan is now Hampton (Wikipedia via search summary).
- Berkeley Plantation, "First Thanksgiving" (https://berkeleyplantation.com/first-thanksgiving/): "Good Ship Margaret", 4 December 1619, 36 men (35 settlers plus Captain John Woodlief); "We ordaine that this day of our ships arrival, at the place assigned for plantacon, in the land of Virginia, shall be yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of Thanksgiving to Almighty God"; eleven colonists killed at Berkeley on 22 March 1622; revived 1958 (Senator John Wicker, the Jamieson family, Woodlief descendants); Virginia Thanksgiving Festival incorporated 1960, open to public 1962. The page says "Indian uprising". The 1622 attacks were by Powhatan men under Opechancanough (standard; other chapters own the attack). The outline says "Powhatan men attacked".
- Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation "Thanksgivings in America" returned 403.

### Plymouth 1621
- Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Primary Sources for 'The First Thanksgiving' at Plymouth" (https://www.pilgrimhall.org/primary-sources-for-the-first-thanksgiving-at-plymouth/): only two primary sources, Winslow in *Mourt's Relation* (London: John Bellamy, 1622) and Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation*. Winslow's text: "our harvest being gotten in, our governour sent foure men on fowling, that so we might after a speciall manner rejoyce together, after we had gathered the fruits of our labours ; they foure in one day killed as much fowle, as with a little helpe beside, served the Company almost a weeke, at which time amongst other Recreations, we exercised our Armes, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoyt, with some ninetie men, whom for three dayes we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five Deere, which they brought to the Plantation and bestowed on our Governour, and upon the Captaine and others." (Clears the seed [VERIFY] on "about 90": Winslow says "some ninetie men".)
- Plimoth Patuxet Museums, "Eye-Witness Account of the 1621 Harvest Celebration" (https://plimoth.org/yath/unit-4/eye-witness-account-of-the-1621-harvest-celebration): letter dated December 1621 to a friend in England; English did not call it a thanksgiving; 1841, Alexander Young's note "This was the first Thanksgiving, the harvest festival of New England"; the gathering may have been "a political show of force" by Ousamequin (Massasoit); Wampanoag understood alliances through "oral agreements accompanied by a ritual exchange of gifts"; survivors of 102 Mayflower passengers: 24 men, 5 women, 24 children and teenagers.
- Land: Plymouth stood on the site of Patuxet, Wampanoag town emptied by the 1616 to 1619 epidemic (research-health.md, Marr and Cathey: "The Patuxet (Plimouth) Native American village was severely depopulated"; research-food-farming.md line 41).
- Parked note from food-farming (below) is now cleared: wording confirmed at Pilgrim Hall.
- Library of Congress Law blog "The Treaty that Made Thanksgiving" (2017) returned 403. The March 1621 Plymouth-Wampanoag treaty is not stated in the outline beyond "the alliance the two sides had made that year".

### Christmas at Plymouth, 1621
- Bradford, *Of Plimoth Plantation* (Project Gutenberg #24950, end of the 1621 year, just before "Anno 1622"): "One ye day called Chrismasday, ye Govr caled them out to worke, (as was used,) but ye most of this new-company excused them selves and said it wente against their consciences to work on yt day. So ye Govr tould them that if they made it mater of conscience, he would spare them till they were better informed. So he led-away ye rest and left them; but when they came home at noone from their worke, he found them in ye streete at play, openly; some pitching ye barr, & some at stoole-ball, and shuch like sports. So he went to them, and tooke away their implements ... If they made ye keeping of it mater of devotion, let them kepe their houses, but ther should be no gameing or revelling in ye streets." The "new-company" came on the *Fortune*, November 1621 (Pilgrim Hall, Fortune arrival in November).
- 1623: Bradford records a "solemne day of humilliation" in the drought, then rain and a good harvest, "For which mercie (in time conveniente) they also sett aparte a day of thanksgiveing" (Bradford, Gutenberg text).

### Massachusetts Bay thanksgivings and the Pequot War
- *Records of the Governor and Company of the Massachusetts Bay in New England*, ed. Nathaniel B. Shurtleff, vol. 1 (Boston 1853), Internet Archive `recordsofgoverno01mass`, full text read by grep:
  - Quarter Court, Boston, "6th Day of the 4th Month, 1637" (6 June 1637): "The 5th day of the next weeke, being the 15th of this month, was appointed to bee kept a day of thanksgiveing in the severall churches." (The entry does not state the reason.)
  - General Court of 1 August 1637 (the "First of the 6th Month, called August, 1637"): "The Court did intreat the magistrats to treat with the elders about a day of thanksgiveing vpon the returne of the souldiers, & the souldiers to bee feasted by their townes."
  - A session in autumn 1637 (after the 19 September Quarter Court entries): "The 12th of the 8th m was ordered to bee kept a day of publike thanksgiving to God for his great mercies in subdewing the Pecoits, bringing the soldiers in safety, the successe of the conference, & good news from Germany." (12 October 1637. "The conference" is the church synod of that summer, standard reading, not researched.)
  - General Court, 6 September 1638 ("6th Day of the 7th Month, 1638"): intimation to the elders "of the desire of the Court to keepe the last Thursday of the 8th month a day of thanksgiving for the safe comeing of so many ships this yeare, & the seasonable weather in the spring, & now to ripen the harvest." (OCR reads "S*" for the month numeral. 8th is the reading that makes sense after a September session.)
- Winthrop's journal: "There was a day of thanksgiving kept in all the churches for the victory obtained against the Pequods, and for other mercies" (search summary, several sites) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Also July 1630, "a day of thanksgiving in all the plantations" for ships arriving (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- A search summary claimed Winthrop "proclaimed" the 12 October text on 15 June 1637. The Records show two separate entries (June for 15 June, autumn for 12 October). Use the Records.
- Mystic, 26 May 1637: John Mason, Mohegan and Narragansett allies, 400 to 700 Pequot killed, mostly old men, women and children, per the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation (research-war.md §3.3, sources there).
- **The claim tying Thanksgiving's origin to 1637.** William B. Newell, Penobscot, former chair of anthropology, University of Connecticut: "Thanksgiving Day" was first proclaimed by the Governor of Massachusetts Bay in 1637 to commemorate the massacre of 700 men, women and children "who were celebrating their annual Green Corn Dance" (quoted on many sites, for example nativeamericannetroots.net and thepeoplespaths.net, via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only; the original publication of Newell's statement was not found). Counter: Jeremy Bangs, "The Truth About Thanksgiving Is that the Debunkers Are Wrong", History News Network, 1 September 2005 (https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/15002): disputes Newell and says Plymouth did not take part in the Mystic attack. New Haven Museum, "A Thanksgiving Story": "An official proclamation of Thanksgiving was declared by the Massachusetts Bay Colony Governor John Winthrop in 1637 after ... the Battle of Mistick Fort"; colonial thanksgivings "would fluctuate around perceived crises and wins" (https://www.newhavenmuseum.org/museum-collections/online-exhibitions/micro-histories/a-thanksgiving-story/). Snopes returned 402.
- **Green Corn detail:** no source opened states the Pequot at Mystic were holding a Green Corn ceremony. The `war` bank says the fighting men were away with Sassacus. The outline reports Newell's claim as his.

### Christmas law, 1659 to 1681
- Mass Moments (Mass Humanities), "Christmas Celebration Outlawed" (https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/christmas-celebration-outlawed.html): 11 May 1659, five-shilling fine for "observing any such day as Christmas or the like, either by forbearing of labor, feasting, or any other way"; Puritans saw Christmas as "a time of seasonal excess with no Biblical authority"; Increase Mather called it "a profane and superstitious custom"; repealed 1681 "under pressure from the government in London"; by the 1750s hymnals included Christmas music; most almanacs named 25 December as Christmas by 1760; family holiday in the early 1800s; Massachusetts legal holiday 1856 (era 6 fact for T-273b).
- Full text of the 1659 law (search summary, several sites): "For preventing disorders, arising in several places within this jurisdiction by reason of some still observing such festivals as were superstitiously kept in other communities, to the great dishonor of God and offense of others..." (unconfirmed: search summary only). Vol. 4 part 1 of the Records would confirm it but did not download (Internet Archive 500 error).
- Repeal confirmed in *Records*, vol. 5 (Internet Archive `recordsofgoverno05mass`): 1681 committee item "8. The law against keeping Christmas to be left out"; and the order of 1681-2 "the law referring to Christmass, page 57, 58, ... is heereby repealled" (page 339).

### New Mexico: ceremonies banned (`religion` leads)

- Governor Bernardo López de Mendizábal (1659 to 1661) allowed the katsina dances that the friars had banned (Wikipedia via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Not used in the outline.

## Era 04: 1700 to 1750

### Governors' thanksgiving and fast proclamations (Library of Congress, Printed Ephemera Collection)

### Christmas and New Year's Day under slavery
- Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845), ch. 10 (Project Gutenberg #23, read): "My term of actual service to Mr. Edward Covey ended on Christmas day, 1833. The days between Christmas and New Year's day are allowed as holidays"; people with families at a distance "were generally allowed to spend the whole six days in their society"; some made "corn-brooms, mats, horse-collars, and baskets"; "These holidays serve as conductors, or safety-valves, to carry off the rebellious spirit of enslaved humanity"; "They do not give the slaves this time because they would not like to have their work during its continuance, but because they know it would be unsafe to deprive them of it"; "Their object seems to be, to disgust their slaves with freedom". - Solomon Northup, *Twelve Years a Slave* (1853), ch. 15 (Project Gutenberg #45631, read). Northup was held on Edwin Epps's plantation on Bayou Boeuf, Louisiana, in the 1840s: "The only respite from constant labor the slave has through the whole year, is during the Christmas holidays. Epps allowed us three--others allow four, five and six days"; planters took turns giving a "Christmas supper" for enslaved people from neighbouring plantations, "Usually from three to five hundred are assembled"; meat roasted over a trench of coals; "White people in great numbers assemble to witness"; on the other holiday days they got passes to go "within a limited distance", or could work for pay; "Such is 'southern life as it is,' three days in the year, as I found it--the other three hundred and sixty-two being days of weariness, and fear, and suffering, and unremitting labor." `slavery-freedom` tells Northup's kidnapping and life; this chapter uses only the Christmas chapter. (Movie *12 Years a Slave*, 2013, is about Northup: `slavery-freedom`'s to list.)
- Harriet Jacobs, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* (1861), ch. 3 "The Slaves' New Year's Day" and ch. 22 "Christmas Festivities" (Project Gutenberg #11030, read). Setting: Edenton, North Carolina, 1820s to 1830s (Jacobs hid 1835 to 1842). Ch. 3: "Hiring-day at the south takes place on the 1st of January. On the 2d, the slaves are expected to go to their new masters"; enslaved farm workers got four or five days at Christmas "If no heavy charges are meantime brought against them"; a person who refused a new master "is whipped, or locked up in jail, until he consents to go"; after hiring, "those for sale are called up"; "On one of these sale days, I saw a mother lead seven children to the auction-block. ### Thanksgiving becomes national, 1863
- Richmond *Whig*, 1856, editorial against the District of Columbia's thanksgiving: the holiday robbed men of a day's wages and encouraged drunkenness; New England had "crazy society" (Atlas Obscura, https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/thanksgiving-pumpkin-pie-culture-war, curl read). Governor Henry A. Wise's "theatrical national claptrap" reply to Hale came "a few years later" (Atlas Obscura, citing Melanie Kirkpatrick). Wise was governor 1856 to 1860. **CORRECTION to the era 6 bank line: the Wise letter is dated by Atlas Obscura only as after 1856.** The story block does not give a year.
- Sarah Josepha Hale to Abraham Lincoln, Philadelphia, 28 September 1863 (Library of Congress, Abraham Lincoln Papers. Transcription on Wikisource, "Letter to Abraham Lincoln from Sarah Josepha Hale", read): "This subject is to have the day of our annual Thanksgiving made a National and fixed Union Festival"; "For the last fifteen years I have set forth this idea in the 'Lady's Book', and placed the papers before the Governors of all the States and Territories"; state laws "would require years", so "a proclamation from the President of the United States would be the best, surest and most fitting method"; she had written to "my friend, Hon. Wm. H. Seward"; asked for "the last Thursday in November (which falls this year on the 26th)".
- Lincoln, Proclamation of Thanksgiving, 3 October 1863 (Abraham Lincoln Online, from *Collected Works*, ed. Basler, https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/thanks.htm, curl read): "written by Secretary of State William Seward, and the original was in his handwriting" (per Lincoln's secretaries, as the site states); "I do therefore invite my fellow citizens in every part of the United States, and also those who are at sea and those who are sojourning in foreign lands, to set apart and observe the last Thursday of November next, as a day of Thanksgiving and Praise"; asks them to "commend to His tender care all those who have become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers in the lamentable civil strife".
- Atlas Obscura: this was Lincoln's second thanksgiving proclamation of 1863. The first followed Gettysburg. In Texas, Governor Oran Milo Roberts, "a former Confederate army officer, refused to declare Thanksgiving a holiday as late as the 1880s"; some Southern governors moved the date.
- CRS, *Federal Holidays: Evolution and Current Practices*, R41990 (2014, archived PDF read via web.archive.org of fas.org): from 1863 "each President, by proclamation, established the exact date"; exceptions: Andrew Johnson chose the first Thursday of December in 1865, Grant the third Thursday of November in 1869. The 1870 act made "any day appointed or recommended by the President ... as a day of public fasting or thanksgiving" a holiday in DC.

### Christmas and the first federal holidays, 1870
- CRS R41990 (above): "On June 28, 1870, the first federal holidays were established for federal employees in the District of Columbia" (16 Stat. 168): New Year's Day, Independence Day, Christmas Day and any presidential thanksgiving or fast day. "Apparently drafted in response to a memorial drafted by local 'bankers and business men'", to "correspond with similar laws of States around the District" (Rep. Burton Cook, House, 17 June 1870) and "in every State of the Union" (Sen. Hannibal Hamlin, 24 June 1870). - Washington's Birthday: CRS: "In January 1879, Congress added George Washington's Birthday to the list of holidays observed in the District of Columbia" (20 Stat. 277, 31 January 1879); "The principal intent of the law was to make February 22 'a bank holiday.'" ### Juneteenth, 1865
- General Orders No. 3, Headquarters District of Texas, Galveston, 19 June 1865, issued by Major General Gordon Granger: "The people of Texas are informed that, in accordance with a proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all slaves are free. This involves an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves, and the connection heretofore existing between them becomes that between employer and hired labor. The freedmen are advised to remain quietly at their present homes and work for wages. They are informed that they will not be allowed to collect at military posts and that they will not be supported in idleness either there or elsewhere." (Henry Louis Gates Jr., "What Is Juneteenth?", PBS *The African Americans: Many Rivers to Cross*, originally *The Root*, https://www.pbs.org/wnet/african-americans-many-rivers-to-cross/history/what-is-juneteenth/, curl read. TSHA text agrees, minus "quietly".)
- Troops: "Granger and the 1,800 bluecoats under him" (Gates). `slavery-freedom` bank says about 2,000. **Record both.** Enslaved people in Texas: about 250,000 (Gates; TSHA "approximately 250,000"). More than 150,000 enslaved people had been moved to Texas by enslavers fleeing the Union army after 1862 (Gates, citing Leon Litwack, *Been in the Storm So Long*).
- What enslavers did (Gates, citing Litwack and Elizabeth Hayes Turner, "Juneteenth: Emancipation and Memory"): masters "had to decide when and how to announce the news ... and it was not uncommon for them to delay until after the harvest"; "Even in Galveston city, the ex-Confederate mayor flouted the Army by forcing the freed people back to work"; former slave Susan Merritt: "You could see lots of niggers hangin' to trees in Sabine bottom right after freedom, 'cause they cotch 'em swimmin' 'cross Sabine River and shoot 'em"; Katie Darling kept working for her mistress six more years: she "whip me after the war jist like she did 'fore"; the Freedmen's Bureau did not arrive in Texas until September 1865. First Juneteenth celebrations "one year later in 1866" (Gates).
- TSHA Handbook, Teresa Palomo Acosta, "Juneteenth" (https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/juneteenth, curl read): news reached people "gradually as individual plantation owners informed their slaves over the months following the end of the war"; the first broader celebrations "were used as political rallies and to teach freed African Americans about their voting rights"; "Some of the early emancipation festivities were relegated by city authorities to a town's outskirts"; Black groups "collected funds to purchase tracts of land"; "In Houston, for instance, a deed for a ten-acre site was signed in 1872"; Austin first celebrated in 1867 under the Freedmen's Bureau.
- Prairie View A&M, Texas Institute for the Preservation of History and Culture, "Juneteenth: The Emancipation Proclamation, Freedom Realized and Delayed" (https://www.pvamu.edu/tiphc/research-projects/juneteenth-the-emancipation-proclamation-freedom-realized-and-delayed/, curl read), quoting the Texas slave narratives: an enslaver who "didn't tell us we were free til a whole year after we were"; Felix Haywood: "We knew freedom was on us, but we didn't know what was to come with it"; the *Galveston Daily News*, 21 June 1865: "Their freedom can never make them the equals of the white race." ### Decoration Day and its competing origins
- National Cemetery Administration (VA), "Memorial Day history" (https://www.cem.va.gov/history/Memorial-Day-history.asp, curl read):
  - Boalsburg, Pennsylvania: "a trio of women decorated the graves of fallen soldiers in October 1864."
  - Charleston, South Carolina, 1 May 1865: "Black freedmen and White 'Northern abolitionist allies' hosted an enormous and historically significant program ... at the 'Martyrs of the Race Course' cemetery where 257 Union dead were buried"; "the Army removed the dead to nearby Beaufort National Cemetery within a few years."
  - Columbus, Georgia: a Ladies Memorial Association; secretary Mary Ann Williams's letter, published 11 March 1866, asked "to set apart a certain day ... to wreathe the graves of our martyred dead with flowers"; chosen date 26 April 1866; Columbus, Mississippi held its event 25 April 1866; "Recent scholarship ... points to the ladies of Columbus, GA". These honored Confederate dead (and, at Columbus MS, Union dead too, per the Finch poem "The Blue and the Gray").
  - General Orders No. 11, Grand Army of the Republic, 5 May 1868, issued by Commander-in-Chief John A. Logan: "The 30th day of May, 1868 is designated for the purpose of strewing with flowers or otherwise decorating the graves of comrades who died in defense of their country during the late rebellion" (text at https://www.cem.va.gov/history/memdayorder.asp, curl read). The GAR credited an "anonymous comrade" who wrote to Adjutant General Norton P. Chipman about the German custom; Mary Logan claimed the idea came from her visit to a Confederate cemetery in Virginia. Logan's July 1866 speech at Salem, Illinois, defended the right of a freed Black man to put flowers on "the grave of some poor loyal soldier".
  - First national Decoration Day, 30 May 1868, Arlington National Cemetery: about 11,250 Union soldiers (white and Black) and about 350 Confederates buried there, more than half unknown; remarks by Congressman James A. Garfield; orphans strewed flowers; federal employees got "free leave". Arlington was Robert E. Lee's estate, made a national cemetery by General Montgomery Meigs in 1864.
  - 1966: a congressional resolution, proclaimed by President Lyndon B. Johnson, named Waterloo, New York, the "birthplace" of Memorial Day (era 9).
  - The NCA quotes a scholar: "it may be less important to identify the holiday's first observation ... than to understand how profoundly the large number of claimants to its origin indicates the ubiquity of the impulse".
- College of Charleston, Amanda Kerr, "Memorial Day Uncovered: Charleston's 'Martyrs of the Race Course'" (29 May 2017, https://today.cofc.edu/2017/05/29/memorial-day-history/, curl read): the site was a Confederate prison camp at the Washington Race Course and Jockey Club, now Hampton Park; "For two weeks prior to the ceremony, former slaves and black workmen exhumed the soldiers' remains from a hastily dug mass grave behind the race track's grand stand and gave each soldier a proper burial", built a fence and an arch reading "Martyrs of the Race Course"; the parade "is believed to have included ... as many as 10,000 people, including 3,000 black schoolchildren singing ... 'John Brown's Body'"; David Blight found the account at Harvard and in the Avery Research Center, published in *Race and Reunion* (2001); historian Adam Domby: "nearly 260 Union troops who died at the site"; the dead prisoners "had suffered imprisonment and mistreatment while held captive by white southerners"; "there is some debate as to whether there are any connections between the Charleston ceremony and the 1868 Decoration Day"; "A parallel holiday, called 'Memorial Day,' was observed by Confederate sympathizers." **Counts: 257 (NCA), nearly 260 (Domby). Crowd: "as many as 10,000", a hedged figure.**
- CRS R41990: Decoration Day became a holiday for federal workers in DC in 1888 (25 Stat. 353, 1 August 1888), "likely created primarily because a sizable number of federal employees were also members of the Grand Army of the Republic" who lost a day's wages to attend.

### Labor Day
- Parked from `work-workers` (below): first parade Tuesday 5 September 1882, New York City, Central Labor Union.
