## Era 01 — Before 1500 (full)

- **Households were kin-groups; the house followed the climate and the food-way.** Children learned work by doing it beside adults; elders lived in the household. (General framing supported by the museum sources below.)
- **Longhouse (Haudenosaunee and neighbors):** bark over a post frame; one longhouse held many related families — 20 or more families in some accounts, or 30–60 people — all related through the mothers' line. The eldest/most respected woman of the clan headed it (clan mother); women ran the longhouse and owned its everyday goods; a husband moved into his wife's longhouse. (New York State Museum "Haudenosaunee Longhouse"; Haudenosaunee Confederacy "Family Structure"; Onondaga Nation "Clan Mothers.")
- **Pueblo apartment living:** Taos Pueblo's multistory adobe North and South Houses rise five stories; main buildings likely constructed between about 1000 and 1450 CE and lived in ever since — over 1,000 years of continuous habitation. UNESCO World Heritage Site 1992; also a National Historic Landmark. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre "Taos Pueblo"; taospueblo.com.)
- **Earth lodge (Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara — upper Missouri):** timber dome packed with earth. Women owned, built, and maintained the lodges (an older woman supervised the build); one lodge housed roughly 10–20 people (some sources say up to 30) and lasted about 10 years; big villages held 100+ lodges. (NPS Knife River Indian Villages — earthlodge and history pages.)
- **Wigwams (Eastern Woodlands):** sapling frame with bark or mat cover. **Tipis (Plains):** hide cover, built to pack up and move with the hunt. (NPS/standard museum descriptions; match house to life-way, keep terse.)
- **Story (candidate) — Buffalo Bird Woman (Waheenee/Maxidiwiac), Hidatsa, ca. 1839–1932:** born in an earth lodge near the mouth of the Knife River (North Dakota). From 1906, anthropologist Gilbert L. Wilson worked summers at Fort Berthold for over a decade; Buffalo Bird Woman was his chief informant. Her accounts (with drawings by her son Edward Goodbird) produced *Waheenee: An Indian Girl's Story* (1921), *Agriculture of the Hidatsa* (1917), and the posthumous *The Hidatsa Earthlodge* (1934) — the fullest documented record of earth-lodge household life. **Era-placement caution:** she described 19th-century life; using her in Before 1500 means labeling her testimony as a later window into an older house tradition. Director to confirm (native-nations will likely also want her — lead parked there). (Wikipedia/Univ. of Nebraska Press edition details; notablefolkloristsofcolor.org; Gilbert Livingston Wilson bibliography.)

## Era 02 — The 1500s (thin)

- Newcomers this century were mostly soldiers, priests, and sailors; European family households on this land were few. Native households continued, now hit by introduced diseases (epidemics are `health`'s).
- **St. Augustine, founded 1565** by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés — oldest continuously occupied European settlement in the continental U.S. (History of St. Augustine — standard; Florida Museum "The Timucua in St. Augustine.")
- **Mixed households documented:** fewer than half of Spanish men in St. Augustine had wives, fewer still Spanish wives; marriages of Spanish soldiers and Native women were common from 1565 on. (NPS Fort Matanzas "Women" history page.)
- **The parish records:** Cathedral Parish records begin January 24, 1594 (first recorded marriage February 2, first baptism June 25 that year) — the oldest surviving parish/ecclesiastical records in the United States. (Diocese of St. Augustine timeline; St. Augustine church-records guides.)
- **Story (verified) — the Camacho household, 1580:** soldier Francisco Camacho; his wife Teresa, a Native woman; Teresa's sister Catalina; and Catalina's son Juan — four people, one St. Augustine household, straight from NPS's account of the town's women. (NPS Fort Matanzas "Women.")

## Era 03 — The 1600s (full)

- **The one-room house:** the basic unit in Massachusetts Bay was the single cell — one room (the "hall") of about two structural bays plus a chimney bay with the entrance and stair; the hearth was the center of cooking, heat, light, and work. Next step up: the "hall and parlor" plan — hall for cooking and work, parlor the best room, usually holding a bed. One-room-plan houses continued to be built well into the 1700s. (Plymouth Colony Archive Project, "Vernacular House Forms in Seventeenth Century Plymouth Colony," histarch.illinois.edu; Historic Ipswich / historicmassachusetts.org "First Period Construction.")
- **Family size and child death, stated plainly with the regional split:** New England families averaged about 8 births with about 6 children surviving to adulthood; New England life spans were long for the era. In the Chesapeake, about 25% of children died in infancy and roughly another 25% before age 20; most Chesapeake children lost one or both parents before adolescence. Sources give ranges, not one number — keep the split, do not average it. (EH.net Encyclopedia "Fertility and Mortality in the United States"; American History Central "American Life in the 17th Century.")
- **Servants inside the household:** indentured servants lived and worked as household members (the arrival story is `immigration`'s — Richard Frethorne is theirs). Enslaved people were increasingly held inside households in this century; the system is `slavery-freedom`'s.
- **Apprenticeship emptied households of teenagers:** boys typically bound at about 14 (some younger) to serve to age 21 — terms up to 7 years; the master stood in loco parentis, pledged to feed, lodge, train, and educate; about two-thirds of indentures included education clauses (reading/writing); "freedom dues" (clothes, tools, or money) at the end. (EH.net "Apprenticeship in the United States"; Gilder Lehrman "Apprenticeship and Indentured Servitude.") — clears the seed's [VERIFY] on ages and terms.
- **Story (verified) — Mary Ring, Plymouth:** came to Plymouth a widow with three children (about 1629/30); died July 1631 (15th or 19th); wrote her own will — she could sign her name — and her estate inventory was taken and recorded (will proved in court October 28, 1633; recorded in Plymouth Colony wills vol. 1:4–6). The inventory lists books (a Bible, a psalmbook, and named religious titles) and money owed to her by William Bradford and Edward Winslow; her inventory is transcribed by the Plymouth Colony Archive Project. All three children — Elizabeth, Susanna, Andrew — married in the colony. Her inventory's detailed textiles/clothing are a lead for `styles` (parked). (Pilgrim Hall Museum "Mary Ring"; Plymouth Colony Archive Project "Probate Inventory for Estate of Mary Ring"; WikiTree compilation of colony records.)

## Era 04 — 1700 to 1750 (full)

- **The Georgian house gives rooms jobs:** symmetry and proportion from classical models; formal parlors and dining rooms toward the front, service spaces deeper in; central hall as the spine; four-chimney plans heating four major rooms. Linked to the wealthy — privacy was invented for the few; most families still lived in one- and two-room houses. (Metropolitan Museum of Art essay "American Georgian Interiors"; architecture style guides.)
- **The hearth as the job center; household production:** open-hearth cooking; cloth, candles, soap, dairying, preserving made at home, chiefly by women and children — documented in Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's *Good Wives* (1982) for northern New England and, for the next era, day by day in Martha Ballard's diary.
- **Story (verified) — Sarah Kemble Knight (1666–1727), Boston:** businesswoman/teacher; in October 1704 rode from Boston toward New Haven and New York on legal business — a trip considered unthinkable for a woman alone — keeping a journal of the road, the inns, and the households she lodged in. The little diary went unpublished until Theodore Dwight printed it in 1825; it is one of the few surviving pieces of American women's writing from the period. (EBSCO Research Starters "Sarah Kemble Knight"; Internet Archive edition; connecticuthistory.org.)

## Era 05 — 1750 to 1800 (full)

- **Two American houses at once:** the frontier log cabin and the settled farmhouse; both households produced most of what they used, and war pulled men away while women ran the households.
- **Log cabin, fact vs. myth:** the notched-corner log cabin was brought by Swedish and Finnish settlers of New Sweden (Delaware Valley, from 1638; the colony lasted 17 years before Dutch takeover; "Forest Finns" were skilled log builders; the Nothnagle Log House in Greenwich Township, NJ, built ca. 1638–43, still stands). English colonists at Plymouth and Jamestown did not build log cabins. By this era it had become the standard first house of the frontier. (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia "Log Cabins" and "New Sweden"; Swedish Finn Historical Society "The Log Cabin Tradition"; LOC "Finns in America" chronology.)
- **Household production at its peak:** spinning, weaving, candle- and soap-making, preserving — women's and children's work (Ulrich, *Good Wives*; Ballard diary below).
- **Story (verified, famous) — Abigail Adams, the household angle:** from 1774 John's Continental Congress and then diplomatic duties kept him away for years at a time; Abigail ran the Braintree (Quincy) farm and finances, personally educated the children, and arranged smallpox inoculation for the entire household (1776); the couple exchanged over 1,100 letters. Her "remember the ladies" letter is `rights-movements`' story — ours is the running of the household through a war. Movie: *John Adams* (2008 HBO miniseries) — about John and the family; Abigail is a lead character, not the subject. (NPS Adams NHP biography; National Women's History Museum; ouramericanrevolution.org.)
- **Story (verified, ordinary) — Martha Ballard, Hallowell, Maine:** kept a daily diary January 1785 to 1812 — 27 years, roughly 10,000 entries over 9,965 days — recording midwifery (816 deliveries, each named and numbered) and the household economy: garden, spinning, weaving, quilting, brewing, daughters' work, visitors, weather. Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's *A Midwife's Tale* (1990) won the Pulitzer Prize; the diary is fully online at DoHistory.org. Movie: *A Midwife's Tale* (1997), PBS American Experience, dir. Richard P. Rogers, prod. Laurie Kahn — a documentary/docudrama about Ballard herself. (dohistory.org; Encyclopedia.com "Ballard, Martha"; PBS American Experience film page; NEH project page.)

## Era 06 — 1800 to 1850 (full)

- **The parlor and the "cult of domesticity":** for the town and city middle class, home was redefined as a place apart from paid work — the mother's realm, the parlor the show room. Catharine Beecher's *Treatise* was the movement's handbook (story below). (Britannica "Catharine Beecher"; connecticuthistory.org.)
- **The cast-iron cookstove replaces the open hearth:** cookstoves spread from the 1830s (foundries casting step stoves, six- and ten-plate stoves, elevated-oven models); by 1850 most urban middle-class hearths had given way to stoves; the open hearth was largely gone by the Civil War. Stoves used less fuel — wood was getting scarce in the settled East — and changed the cook's posture and pace. Critics from the 1840s called them poor substitutes for the family hearth. The stove as invention is `technology`'s; the changed kitchen is ours. (Encyclopedia.com "The Iron Cookstove"; The Magazine Antiques "Hot Stove League.")
- **A child's day (school thread — `education` leads the school):** district schools before ~1900 commonly ran two terms — summer (roughly May–August, younger children, women teachers) and winter (roughly November–April, when older boys could be spared from farm work; men often hired to teach winter term). Attendance bent to weather and chores. Teachers commonly boarded with district families in turn ("boarding around"). (Morning Ag Clips "The One-Room Schoolhouse"; Colchester (NY) Historical Society; Heritage League "America's One-Room Schools of the 1890s.") — clears the seed's [VERIFY] on the chores-vs-school pattern.
- **Discipline at home:** obedience expected; the rod normal, with Proverbs' "spare the rod" the standard justification; 19th-century advice manuals began urging calm, firm correction (one manual: never punish in anger), but the real shift came with the child-development and psychology movements of the 1920s–30s (noted again era 08). (ScienceDirect: "Pediatric discourse on corporal punishment: a historical review"; faqs.org Encyclopedia of Children and Childhood, "Discipline.") — clears the seed's [VERIFY] on home discipline.
- **Home under slavery (shared — `slavery-freedom` leads):** enslaved families' households lived under the standing threat of sale; marriages had no legal standing; EJI's accounting (in `research-slavery-freedom.md`): the domestic trade separated nearly half of the people it moved from a spouse or a parent. Ours is one plain span telling it as home life; their bank carries the system and the numbers.
- **Story (verified, famous) — Catharine Beecher (Sept 6, 1800, East Hampton, NY – May 12, 1878, Elmira, NY):** founded the Hartford Female Seminary 1823; published *A Treatise on Domestic Economy* 1841 — the first American book treating all facets of domestic life, ~15 editions, reprinted nearly annually for years; argued housekeeping was a profession to be studied and taught in schools; with sister Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote *The American Woman's Home* (1869); also campaigned to make teaching a women's profession (`education` has her too, suffixed slug there). No film. (Britannica "Catharine Beecher" and "A Treatise on Domestic Economy"; CT Women's Hall of Fame; connecticuthistory.org.)

## Era 07 — 1850 to 1900 (full)

- **The tenement flat:** 97 Orchard Street, New York, built 1863; about 7,000 people from 20+ countries lived there before its residential floors closed (Tenement Museum). Family life: small rooms, boarders, shared privies in the yard, water carried. Housing-law changes that reached inside the home: NY Tenement House Act of 1879 ("Old Law") → dumbbell air shafts; Act of 1901 ("New Law") → outward-facing windows in every room, courtyards, indoor toilets, fire safeguards, with retrofits forced on old buildings. The building form and the city are `city-building`'s (their story is the Moore family, 1869 — do not reuse); the toilet and the window as family facts are ours. (Tenement Museum; Village Preservation "Tenement House Act of 1901"; NYC LPC timeline — via city-building's bank.)
- **Servants ("help") in middle-class homes:** the 1870 census — the first to systematically record women's employment — counted 867,354 women in domestic service, about 47% of all women in paid occupations: the largest women's occupation by far. (1940 Census comparative occupation statistics 1870–1930, census.gov; IPUMS occupational coding notes.)
- **Indoor plumbing arrived unevenly:** city mains and the 1901 law put toilets inside tenements; farm households waited decades longer (see era 08 electrification-era figures for the rural lag; keep the claim qualitative here).
- **Sod houses and dugouts on the plains:** the Ingalls family's Plum Creek dugout near Walnut Grove, MN (1874–76) is documented: Charles and Caroline Ingalls settled May 1874, left after three straight crop failures, bought the land July 1876 for $413 and immediately resold; the dugout site (a depression in the creek bank) is preserved on the former Gordon farm. `migration` features Laura Ingalls Wilder (their slug `laura-ingalls-wilder`) for the moves; the dwellings are ours as span material. (walnutgrove.org "Ingalls Dugout" and "Walnut Grove History"; Historical Marker Database; On the Banks of Plum Creek background.)
- **Story (verified, ordinary) — Nathalie Gumpertz, 97 Orchard Street:** East Prussian immigrants Nathalie and Julius Gumpertz lived at 97 Orchard through the Panic of 1873; one morning in 1874 Julius left for work and never returned, leaving Nathalie with four young children. She became a dressmaker working from the parlor — about three dresses and $8–9 a week — and kept the household going. In 1883 a court declared Julius legally dead so she could claim a $600 inheritance from his father's estate; she then moved the family to upper Manhattan. Museum genealogists later traced Julius to Cincinnati. (Tenement Museum materials — thirteen.org "Gumpertz Apartment," tenement.org blog; details museum-published. Her death date appears variously in secondary write-ups — omit it.)
- **Story (verified, ordinary) — Rachel Bella Calof, North Dakota:** in 1894, aged 18, traveled from Russia for an arranged marriage to homesteader Abraham Calof; the memoir she wrote in 1936 (in Yiddish; published 1995 as *Rachel Calof's Story*, Indiana University Press) records 1894–1904: 12-by-14-foot dirt-floored shanties; whole winters with Abraham's parents and brothers — and the chickens — sharing the one room to save fuel; her line that of all the privations, "the lack of privacy was the hardest to bear." (Indiana University Press; Jewish Women's Archive; Kirkus review.)

## Era 08 — 1900 to 1950 (full)

- **Electric light and power reach homes unevenly:** cities first; in 1935 fewer than 11 of 100 U.S. farms had central-station electricity; after the REA (1935): about 50% of farms electrified by 1945, about 9 in 10 by 1950, ~97% by 1960. The wires and the agency are `energy`'s; what the switch did to washday and the kerosene lamp is ours. (EH.net "Rural Electrification Administration"; Living New Deal; EBSCO.)
- **"More work for mother" — a scholar's claim, labeled:** historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan (*More Work for Mother*, 1983) argued that household machines mostly replaced the labor of servants, children, and men, while standards (cleanliness, laundry frequency, cooking) rose — so the housewife's own hours did not fall in the century after 1870. Present as her argument, not as settled fact. (Cowan 1983; UMSL teaching summary; Mokyr's "Why Was There More Work for Mother?" engages the thesis.)
- **The bungalow and the mail-order house:** the bungalow was the era's small family house; Sears sold complete kit houses by catalog 1908–1940 — commonly cited ~70,000–75,000 sold, with estimates running to 100,000 (no official count; 447 designs offered). State the range. (Sears Modern Homes literature; HUD User "Kit Homes in America"; chicagobungalow.org.)
- **The mortgage remade:** before the 1930s the standard home loan ran 5–10 years, interest-only, with the whole principal due in a balloon at the end (down payments commonly around half). Depression foreclosures broke the system. HOLC (Home Owners' Loan Act, June 1933) refinanced with self-amortizing fixed-rate loans stretched to 15 years; FHA (National Housing Act, 1934) insured lenders and pushed fully amortized 20–25-year loans with small down payments; the 30-year loan became the postwar standard. The HOLC maps and redlining → `city-building`/`rights-movements`; the family-level fact — owning a house by monthly payment — is ours. (Richmond Fed *Econ Focus* "A Short History of Long-Term Mortgages"; Living New Deal; FHFA-OIG history.)
- **Depression households:** marriage rate fell from 10.14 per 1,000 (1929) to 7.87 (1932); couples postponed marriage; the birthrate went below replacement for the first time in U.S. history; young couples moved back in with parents and elderly parents moved in with children; by 1940, 1.5 million married women were living apart from their husbands. (Encyclopedia.com "Family and Home, Impact of the Great Depression on" and "Everyday Life 1929–1941.")
- **Kitchenettes (Great Migration home life, parked from city-building — verified sources as parked):** Chicago landlords subdivided flats into one-room units; Bronzeville held 80,000+ kitchenette units; whole families in one room, bath shared down the hall; Richard Wright's *12 Million Black Voices* (1941) is the primary literary record. (Chicago Public Library "Housing and Race in Chicago"; Morrison, *A Kitchenette to Fit Your Needs*, 2026.)
- **The "teenager":** scattered print uses from 1913 ("Teen Ager" as two words); hyphenated "teen-agers" in Popular Science, April 1941, is the usually-credited first single-term print use; the word caught on in the late 1940s, pushed by advertisers selling to a new audience; the stage itself was created partly by children staying in high school instead of going to work. (Wikipedia "Teenager (word)" summarizing print history; Time "The Invention of Teenagers"; Gothamist.) State the messy word-history honestly if used.
- **WWII households:** ration books ran the kitchen — `food-farming` leads rationing and holds the ration-book household story; ours is one line (the book in the kitchen drawer, points juggled, households moving for war work).
- **Story (verified, ordinary) — the Baldizzi family, 97 Orchard Street, 1928–1935:** Adolfo Baldizzi, trained fine woodworker from Palermo (immigrated 1923), worked odd jobs through the Depression; wife Rosaria lined coats in a garment factory; children Josephine and Johnny. Evenings at the kitchen table: cards, the radio; a corner cabinet Adolfo built himself. In 1934–35 the city required costly upgrades (fireproofing, etc.); 97 Orchard's landlord instead evicted the remaining families, boarded the upper windows, and sealed the residential floors (1935) — the Baldizzis had to move. Josephine returned after the museum's founding (1988) and recorded hours of oral history — the museum's apartment recreation rests on her memories. (Tenement Museum "The Baldizzi Family Story" teacher guide, lesson plans, and blog; Tenement Museum housing-laws blog on the 1935 closure.)
- **Story (candidate, ordinary) — the first Levittown families (Oct 1, 1947):** the first 300 families moved into Levittown, Long Island on October 1, 1947; the Bladykas family moved into the first house Levitt & Sons built, at 67 Bellmore Road. Rent $60/month, with an option to buy after a year for $7,500; the 1949 ranch model sold for $7,990 on easy veteran terms; 17,447 Levitt houses built 1947–1951. Sales and leases were whites-only: Clause 25 of the standard lease barred occupancy by any "person other than members of the Caucasian race" (Caucasian domestic servants excepted); Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) made such covenants unenforceable in court, and Levitt dropped the written clause but kept the policy. Boundary rule: 1947 → this era, not 1950–2000. First names of the Bladykas family not confirmed in the sources checked — candidate until firmed. (Levittown Public Library "Facts & Trivia" history collection; EBSCO "Levittown"; Levittown Historical Society-adjacent coverage; Tougaloo sundown-town file and liherald.com on Clause 25.)

## Era 09 — 1950 to 2000 (full)

- **Suburbia becomes the standard picture:** U.S. homeownership rose from 43.6% (1940) to 61.9% (1960) (Census, widely reproduced); the baby boom put 76 million births between 1946 and 1964 (Census P25-1141; Britannica); more Levittowns followed (Pennsylvania from 1952). Sprawl and the shape of metro areas are `city-building`'s.
- **Who was shut out:** redlining and covenant practice outlived Shelley v. Kraemer; the campaign against housing discrimination (through the Fair Housing Act 1968) is `rights-movements`' — ours is the family-level fact of being unable to buy, and the family who did buy (Myers story below).
- **The TV in the living room:** households with a set: about 9% (1950) → 64.5% (1955) → 87% (1960). The evening rearranged around it. (Sage Social History of the American Family "Television, 1950s"; TVB penetration tables.)
- **The household changes shape, numbers anchored:** divorces per 1,000 married women more than doubled 1960–1980 (9.2 → 22.6); the divorce rate peaked in 1981 and declined after. Married mothers in the labor force: ~17% (1948) → 61% (1985) → ~70% (1995) (BLS Monthly Labor Review). Day care grew with two-income households. "Latchkey" children counts were disputed: a 1987 Census analysis found ~2.1 million children 5–13 regularly home alone; advocacy estimates of the era ran 6–7 million — state the disagreement, do not pick. (BLS; Encyclopedia.com "Latchkey Children"; Census 1987 analysis as cited there.)
- **School comes home — homeschooling's two roots (`education` leads the schooling and the legal fight):** John Holt (secular, school-reform side) started the newsletter *Growing Without Schooling* in August 1977 — likely the first homeschooling newsletter; Raymond Moore (religious side) argued for keeping young children home, *Home Grown Kids* (1981). Homeschooling was illegal or effectively illegal in many states into the 1980s. Ours: the kitchen-table day, a parent leaving paid work, families forming groups. (johnholtgws.com; Coalition for Responsible Home Education "A Brief History of Homeschooling"; Sutherland Institute history series.) — clears the seed's [VERIFY] on the two roots and period accounts (GWS reader letters are period documentation).
- **Story (verified, ordinary) — Daisy and William Myers, Levittown, PA, August 1957:** first Black family in Levittown, PA — William a WWII Army veteran, Daisy a teacher, three young children. For nearly three months: mobs outside, rocks through the windows, crosses burned, threatening calls, milk/bread/oil deliveries stopped, police protection required. Daisy Myers told it in her memoir *Sticks 'n Stones* (2005). Movie: *Crisis in Levittown* (1957), documentary by Lee Bobker and Lester Becker, filmed as it happened. Our angle: the family inside the house; the campaign against housing discrimination is `rights-movements`' (parked there). (Bristol Township official commemoration; Library of Congress photo records; Wikipedia/Daisy Myers; witnessingyork.com.)

## Era 10 — 2000 to Today (full; current through 2026 — state the cutoff)

- **More house, fewer people:** median new single-family house: 983 sq ft (1950) → peak 2,467 sq ft (2015), easing since; average household size: 3.7 (1940) → about 2.5 (2022). (Census AHS working paper and "Bigger Houses, Smaller Lots"; Census CPS households data; USAFacts summary.)
- **2008 at the family level:** counts differ by method — about 3.8 million foreclosures 2007–2010; estimates for the whole crisis run from about 7.7 million to 10 million homes lost; foreclosures rose from 650,000 (2007) to about 2.9 million homes in 2010 alone. State the range and label the estimates. (Federal Reserve History "Subprime Mortgage Crisis"; Chicago Fed Letter 2016; St. Louis Fed; ProPublica.)
- **Multigenerational living returns:** people in multigenerational family households quadrupled 1971–2021 to 59.7 million — from 7% to 18% of the U.S. population; growth accelerated in the Great Recession. (Pew Research Center, March 2022 report.)
- **Homelessness, counted:** HUD's January 2024 point-in-time count: 771,480 people — the highest recorded and the largest one-year jump (18%); homelessness among families with children rose 39% 2023→2024; veteran homelessness fell ~8%. (HUD 2024 AHAR Part 1; Bipartisan Policy Center summary.)
- **2020 — the home becomes office and school at once:** workers primarily working from home tripled, 5.7% (≈9M people, 2019) → 17.9% (27.6M, 2021) (Census ACS 1-year, Sept 2022 release). Households homeschooling (not remote schooling): 5.4% (spring 2020) → 11.1% (fall 2020) of households with school-age children; Black households 3.3% → 16.1% (Census Household Pulse Survey; pre-pandemic norm ~3.3%). The homework gap: 35% of households with children 6–17 and incomes under $30,000 lacked home broadband vs 6% of those over $75,000; in April 2020, 59% of lower-income parents said their child would likely face at least one tech obstacle to schoolwork (Pew Research Center). How much of the homeschooling rise stayed is not yet settled — left open (also flagged to `education`).
