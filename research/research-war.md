## Parked from `native-nations` (2026-08-07) — the campaigns' military side; native-nations tells the nations' side
- **St. Clair's Defeat (Battle of the Wabash), November 4, 1791:** the worst defeat in U.S. Army history by casualty rate (~97%) — over 650 soldiers killed, 270+ wounded, of ~1,000 engaged, by ~1,000 Northwest Confederacy fighters under Little Turtle (Miami), Blue Jacket (Shawnee), and Buckongahelas (Delaware); ~100 Native casualties. Triggered the first congressional investigation ever. Led to the creation of Wayne's Legion of the United States → Fallen Timbers (Aug 20, 1794) → Treaty of Greenville (Aug 3, 1795). (Sources: Britannica "Saint Clair's Defeat"; U.S. Army article "St. Clair's Campaign of 1791"; Levin Center.)
- **Choctaw code talkers, WWI:** Meuse-Argonne; Germans tapped lines and captured 1 in 4 runners; Choctaw soldiers of the 36th Division began secure transmissions October 26, 1918; credited at St. Etienne and Forest Ferme; code words devised ("little gun shoot fast" = machine gun). (Sources: Oklahoma Historical Society; National WWI Museum; Choctaw Nation.)
- **Navajo code talkers, WWII:** original 29 recruits to San Diego May 5, 1942; 350–420 served across all six Marine divisions in the Pacific; 5th MarDiv signal officer Maj. Howard Connor on Iwo Jima: "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima." Code never broken; classified until 1968; Congressional Gold Medals July 26, 2001 (gold to the 29, silver to the rest). Chester Nez (last original, d. June 4, 2014), memoir *Code Talker* (2011). *Windtalkers* (2002) is fiction about the program. (Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command; National WWII Museum; U.S. Mint; LOC Veterans History Project.)
- **Little Bighorn, June 25–26, 1876:** five companies of the 7th Cavalry destroyed (~200 with Custer died within about an hour) by allied Lakota/Cheyenne/Arapaho from the Sitting Bull camp; campaign context: Dec 1875 ultimatum to enter the reservation by Jan 31, 1876. (Sources: Britannica; History.com; NPS "Fighting for the Black Hills.")
- **Nez Perce War, 1877:** ~750 people (about 200 fighters) covered roughly 1,170 miles (NPS trail figure; some accounts up to ~1,700) June–October 1877 with repeated rearguard actions; surrender at Bear Paw Oct 5, 1877, 40 miles from Canada. (Sources: U.S. Army "The Nez Perce War of 1877"; LOC.)

## 1. BEFORE 1500

### 1.1 How we know anything at all
Nobody in North America north of Mexico wrote war down before Europeans arrived. What is known
comes from the ground: skeletons with wounds on them, houses burned where they stood, and the
ditches and walls people dug and built around their towns. That limit is a fact to state, not to
hide — archaeology can show that a town was attacked and roughly when, and it can show what a
weapon did to a bone, but it cannot give a name, a reason, or a speech.

### 1.2 The Plains picture — Douglas B. Bamforth, University of Colorado, for the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains (University of Nebraska–Lincoln)
- Archaeological evidence of violence on the Plains goes back to the **Woodland Period (250 B.C.–A.D. 900)**.
- **Conflict intensified after the thirteenth century (after about 1250).** From then on, villages
  were often destroyed by fire and human skeletons regularly show marks of violence — **scalping,
  dismemberment, and other mutilations**.
- Farming villages, especially along the Missouri River in the present-day Dakotas, were **"often
  fortified with ditch and palisade defenses."** Some held **up to 1,000 people**.
- Bamforth attributes the Crow Creek killings to **"competition for food, probably due to local
  overpopulation and climatic deterioration."**
- Horses and guns later changed how fighting worked: shield-line tactics became useless against
  mounted men with firearms, **horse-raiding became the main form of conflict from about 1750**, and
  warfare became more destructive.
- Source: Douglas B. Bamforth, "Intertribal Warfare," *Encyclopedia of the Great Plains*, University
  of Nebraska–Lincoln, https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.war.023.html

### 1.3 Crow Creek, about 1325 — the one pre-1500 attack that can be counted
Site 39BF11, on the Missouri River in south-central South Dakota; an ancestral Arikara town.
- **At least 486 people** — men, women and children — were recovered in 1978 from a mass grave in
  the town's fortification ditch. Bamforth: "essentially the town's entire population."
  (Bamforth, above; corroborated by P. Willey and Thomas E. Emerson, "The Osteology and Archaeology
  of the Crow Creek Massacre," *Plains Anthropologist* 38(145), 1993,
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2052546.1993.11931655)
- **Date: about A.D. 1325**, from radiocarbon dating (one report gives 1325 ± 55 years).
- The town covered **7.3 hectares (18 acres)** and was enclosed by a **large bastioned fortification
  ditch** — a ditch with towers projecting from it. (Willey & Emerson 1993 abstract, above; and
  "Reconsidering the Occupational History of the Crow Creek Site (39BF11)," *Plains Anthropologist*
  52(202), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/pan.2007.012)
- What was done to the bodies: **many were scalped, decapitated, and dismembered.** Scalping means
  the skin and hair were cut away from the top of the skull; it leaves cut marks on the bone, which
  is how it is known. **Their bones also showed clear evidence of severe malnutrition** — these were
  hungry people before they were killed. (Bamforth, above.)
- **Chewing marks on many of the bones show the bodies lay in the open for some time before anyone
  buried them.** (Willey & Emerson 1993.)
- Excavated by the University of South Dakota in 1978: project director **Larry J. Zimmerman**, field
  director **Thomas Emerson**, osteologist **P. Willey**. The original report: *The Crow Creek Site
  (39BF11) Massacre: A Preliminary Report*, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Omaha District, 1981
  (NTIS ADA098882, https://ntrl.ntis.gov/NTRL/dashboard/searchResults/titleDetail/ADA098882.xhtml).
- **CAUTION — figures found in circulating summaries that this bank does NOT adopt:** "a quarter of
  the skeletons showed knife wounds on the first neck vertebra" and "40 percent of complete skulls
  showed depression fractures" appear in popular retellings (e.g. Washington Times, 9 Feb 2015).
  They are plausible but were not confirmed against Willey & Emerson here. Do not put them in the
  outline without checking the 1993 paper directly.

### 1.4 Norris Farms #36, central Illinois, about A.D. 1300 — a second countable case
- An Oneota village cemetery in Fulton County, Illinois. The remains of **264 people** were studied.
- **At least 43 of them — 16 percent — died violently.** Many had been struck front, side and back
  with heavy weapons such as stone axes (celts), or shot with arrows.
- Source: the Norris Farms 36 excavation report, Sharron K. Santure et al., *Archaeological
  Investigations at the Morton Village and Norris Farms 36 Cemetery*, indexed at tDAR,
  https://core.tdar.org/document/118029/archaeological-investigations-at-the-morton-village-and-norris-farms-36-cemetery
  ; figure of 16 percent as summarized in the warfare literature (see also Steadman, "Warfare
  related trauma at Orendorf," *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*, 2008,
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajpa.20778).

### 1.5 Cahokia's stockade — and the dispute about what it was for
- Cahokia (near present-day St. Louis) was the largest town north of Mexico. Peak roughly
  **A.D. 1050–1150**, with perhaps **15,000–20,000 people** in the central area and another
  30,000–40,000 in the surrounding countryside. (Mythic Mississippi Project, University of Illinois
  at Urbana-Champaign, https://mythicmississippi.illinois.edu/lesson-plans/ancient-cahokia/)
- A **stockade of upright logs about two miles long** was built around the central part of the town
  and **rebuilt several times**. Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site material gives **four builds and
  an estimated 20,000 logs each time**; other summaries say it was begun about A.D. 1100 and rebuilt
  three times over 200 years. **State the range: three or four rebuilds, sources differ.**
  **Bastions — towers projecting from the wall — stood at regular intervals.**
  (Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, https://cahokiamounds.org/explore/ ; site brochure PDF
  https://cahokiamounds.org/wpress/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Site-Brochure-8-13.pdf — the PDF host
  refused automated fetches on 2026-09-10; figures taken from the site's indexed text.)
- **The purpose is disputed.** The University of Illinois lesson material says archaeologists read
  the wall as "an imposing physical symbol of difference between Cahokia elites and Cahokia
  commoners, or between insiders and outsiders" — i.e. possibly a boundary rather than only a
  defence. Bastions are a military feature, so both readings are live. **Say the dispute.**

- No named person. There is no documented named individual in North American warfare before 1500

## 2. THE 1500s

### 2.0 CORRECTION to the seed outline
The seed outline's 1500s span read: **"the burning of St. Augustine in 1566."** That date is wrong.
St. Augustine was founded 8 September 1565 and was **burned by the English privateer Francis Drake
between 28 and 30 May 1586** — twenty years later. Drake arrived with **21 vessels**; the town had
only about **150 soldiers**; he burned the town and the wooden fort (San Juan de Pinos) and took
about **2,000 pounds** from the treasury chest. (Library of Congress, Kraus Collection, "The Caribbean
Raid," https://www.loc.gov/collections/sir-francis-drake/articles-and-essays/drake-biography/the-caribbean-raid/
; Florida Museum of Natural History, University of Florida, "Disasters and Rebuilding,"
https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/staugustine/timeline/disasters-and-rebuilding/ ; Florida Center
for Instructional Technology, USF, https://fcit.usf.edu/florida/lessons/drake/drake1.htm)
**Fixed in the outline. No source found gives a 1566 burning.**

### 2.1 The de Soto entrada, 1539-1543
- Landed in Florida **May 1539 with 620 men and 220 horses**; the expedition crossed the Southeast
  until **1542**. De Soto **took hostages to force safe passage** — he held the Coosa leader hostage
  to get through the Coosa towns, and his men ate through the towns' stored food.
  (NPS, "Hernando De Soto," https://home.nps.gov/people/hernando-de-soto.htm)
- **De Soto died of fever on 21 May 1542.** His men hid the body and sank it in the Mississippi so
  the nations they had been fighting would not learn he could die. (NPS, same page.)

### 2.2 Mabila, 18 October 1540 — the largest battle of the century in North America
- De Soto's force fought the Mississippian town of **Mabila**, a **palisaded town** whose leader the
  Spanish called **Tascalusa**. The fight lasted about **nine hours** and the Spanish burned the town
  with the people inside it.
- **Casualty figures are in genuine dispute and the range is the finding:**
  - NPS: **"over 200 Spanish lives and approximately 2,000 of the Mabila Indians' lives."**
    (https://home.nps.gov/people/hernando-de-soto.htm)
  - University of Alabama coverage of the archaeological search: **about 20 Spanish killed and some
    250 wounded**, with **thousands** of Mabila dead.
    (University of Alabama News, "A Search Renewed: De Soto, Tascalusa Battle Site Remains Elusive,"
    https://news.ua.edu/2009/08/a-search-renewed-de-soto-tascalusa-battle-site-remains-elusive/)
  - **The town has never been found.** Its location is still unknown; see *The Search for Mabila*,
    University of Alabama Press, https://www.uapress.ua.edu/9780817355425/the-search-for-mabila/
- The expedition's own record-keeper on the outcome: **"We killed them all either with fire or the
  sword."** (Encyclopedia of Alabama, "European Exploration and Colonial Period,"
  https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/european-exploration-and-colonial-period/)
- What set it off, per the same source: a Spanish officer decided a Mabila man had not shown him
  proper respect, and the argument **ended with the man's arm cut off.**

### 2.3 The Tiguex War, August 1540 - March 1541
- Coronado's expedition **took the village of Coofor (near modern Bernalillo, New Mexico) and made
  its people leave.** The expedition's livestock ate the corn stalks the Tiguex needed for winter
  fuel. The immediate trigger, in the NPS's words: **"a Spanish soldier assaulted a Pueblo woman,
  wife to a Tiguex man."**
- The fighting ran about **three months**, winter permitting. The Tiguex **barricaded themselves in
  their multi-storey villages and shot arrows from loopholes and rooftops**; the Spanish used
  **cavalry charges and set fires to drive them into the open.**
- Outcome per NPS: **"hundreds of Tiguex had been killed, along with a smaller number of Spanish and
  Mexican soldiers"**; many villages burned or abandoned; **women and children enslaved.**
  (NPS, Coronado National Memorial, "The Tiguex War,"
  https://www.nps.gov/coro/learn/historyculture/the-tiguex-war.htm)
- At **Arenal**, the pueblo across the river from Coofor, the expedition took the town and
  **executed dozens of Pueblo captives**; after that the rest of the Tiguex abandoned all but two of
  their pueblos, and the expedition besieged those two (one called **Moho**) in the deep cold of
  early 1541. (New Mexico Office of the State Historian, "Coofor and Juan Aleman,"
  http://newmexicohistory.org/places/coofor-and-juan-aleman)
- **NOT ADOPTED:** popular accounts say the Arenal captives were burned alive at the stake. Neither
  the NPS page nor the New Mexico State Historian page gives the method. **The outline says
  "put to death" and says these sources do not give the method.** Do not upgrade this without a
  citation to the Castaneda narrative.

### 2.4 Fort Caroline and Matanzas, September-October 1565
- The French Huguenot colony at Fort Caroline (near present-day Jacksonville) began with **200
  colonists** under Rene de Goulaine de Laudonniere in 1564. (NPS, "Florida: Fort Caroline National
  Memorial," https://www.nps.gov/articles/ftcaroline.htm)
- **20 September 1565: Pedro Menendez de Aviles and 500 soldiers marched overland and took Fort
  Caroline**, renaming it San Mateo. (Florida Museum of Natural History, University of Florida,
  Menendez timeline, https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/histarch/research/st-augustine/menendez/timeline/)
  NPS: it was a dawn attack; his troops **killed most of the settlers they found**; **Menendez
  spared the women and children and shipped them to Havana** (about **50** women and children).
  (NPS, "The Massacre of the French,"
  https://www.nps.gov/places/000/the-massacre-of-the-french.htm)
- **Matanzas Inlet, first killing:** about **127 shipwrecked Frenchmen**, who had lost their food and
  weapons in a hurricane, **surrendered**. Menendez demanded they give up their Protestant faith;
  they refused. **111 were killed. 16 were spared** — a few who said they were Catholic, some
  Breton sailors who had been forced aboard, and four craftsmen St. Augustine needed.
  Date: NPS says after 8 September; the Florida Museum timeline dates it **29 September 1565**.
- **Matanzas Inlet, second killing:** **Jean Ribault and his men surrendered**, refused the same
  demand, and **134 were killed**. NPS dates it **12 October**; the Florida Museum timeline says
  **11 October 1565**. **State both dates.**
- **Both groups had surrendered before they were killed.** (NPS, same page.)
- The inlet has been called **Matanzas** ever since — **Spanish for "slaughters."** (NPS, Fort
  Matanzas National Monument, https://www.nps.gov/foma/learn/historyculture/the_massacre.htm)

### 2.5 The Pardo forts, 1566-1568 — every Spanish fort inland destroyed
- Captain **Juan Pardo** built **Fort San Juan at the Native town of Joara** (upper Catawba Valley,
  present-day Burke County, North Carolina) in **January 1567** and garrisoned it with **30
  soldiers**; in all he left **about 120 men in a chain of small forts** — one in east Tennessee, two
  more in North Carolina, two in South Carolina.
- **By May 1568 word reached Santa Elena that Native people had attacked and destroyed every one of
  them, killing nearly all the soldiers in them.** The cause given: the soldiers' demands for food
  were more than the host towns could carry, and Pardo's men were violent toward the people they
  lived among.
- Fort San Juan has been excavated: it is the **Berry site** in Burke County, North Carolina.
- Sources: NCpedia, "Fort San Juan," https://www.ncpedia.org/history/colonial/fort-san-juan ;
  NCpedia, "Pardo Expeditions," https://www.ncpedia.org/pardo-expeditions ; North Carolina
  Department of Natural and Cultural Resources, https://ncdcr.gov/blog/2012/12/01/the-spanish-begin-to-explore-the-mountains ;
  UNC Research Laboratories of Archaeology, "Fort San Juan (Burke County),"
  https://ancientnc.web.unc.edu/colonial-heritage/by-time/early-colonial-period/fort-san-juan-burke-county/

### 3.1 The militia — the colonial military system
- **13 December 1636:** the Massachusetts Bay General Court ordered the colony's militia companies
  into **three regiments (North, South, East)** and required **all able-bodied men aged 16 to 60**
  to join. The Army National Guard dates its own founding to that order.
  (National Guard Bureau, "How We Began," https://www.nationalguard.mil/About-the-Guard/How-We-Began/
  — **the page returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches on 2026-09-10**; figures taken from the search
  index of that page and corroborated by the U.S. Army Center of Military History reference page
  "The Massachusetts Bay Colony Militia," https://www.history.army.mil/Reference/mamil/Mamil.htm,
  which was also 403. **Re-verify by hand before the prose stage.**)
- The point for this chapter: there was **no standing army in the colonies.** War was fought by
  farmers and tradesmen called out of their fields, who went home when the campaign ended. Every
  colonial war in this era was fought that way, alongside Native allies.

### 3.2 Virginia: the Second Anglo-Powhatan War, 1622-1632
- **22 March 1622:** Opechancanough's coordinated attack on the English plantations along the James
  River. Warriors came to settlers' houses and fields as if to trade or eat, then attacked.
  **Perhaps as many as 347 colonists killed** out of a colony of about **1,200**.
  (Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation, "Why did the English and Powhatan go to war in 1622?",
  https://www.jyfmuseums.org/learn/research-and-collections/essays/why-did-the-english-and-powhatan-go-to-war-in-1622
  ; Encyclopedia Virginia, "Anglo-Powhatan War, Second (1622-1632),"
  https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/anglo-powhatan-war-second-1622-1632/)
- **Jamestown itself was warned by a Powhatan man whose name is not recorded** — he told the settler
  he worked for, Richard Pace, and Pace warned the town. **DOCUMENTED BUT UNNAMED. Tell it unnamed.**
  (Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation, same essay.)
- **How the English then fought: "feedfights" — burning the food.** Encyclopedia Virginia: the
  English "sought no revenge till their corne was ripe," then raided through the fall and winter of
  1622-23 to "surprize their corne." They agreed truces timed to let both sides plant, then attacked
  again after harvest. In July 1624, while sixty Englishmen fought a two-day stalemate near a
  Pamunkey town, English soldiers burned fields holding **enough corn "to have sustained four
  thousand men for a twelve-month."** Raids were still doing that much damage in 1629.
- **May 1623, the poisoned toast.** Opechancanough agreed to meet English delegates on the Pamunkey
  River. Encyclopedia Virginia: "the English offered poisoned drinks to toast the accord, then fired
  on the deathly ill Powhatan delegates." **Some English took scalps.** The entry gives **no
  casualty figure and does not name the poison** — say so; do not supply either.
- The war ran into **1632**, when a new governor signed an agreement ending it.
- **18 April 1644:** Opechancanough's second general attack. **About 400 colonists killed** — more
  than in 1622, but a much smaller share of a much larger English population. Fighting continued
  until **Opechancanough was captured and killed in 1646.**
  (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Opechancanough (d. 1646),"
  https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/opechancanough-d-1646/)

### 3.3 The Pequot War, 1636-1638, and the Mystic attack of 26 May 1637
- English colonists from Connecticut under **Captain John Mason**, with **Mohegan and Narragansett
  allies**, attacked the fortified Pequot town at Mystic **before dawn on 26 May 1637**. They set
  fire to the palisaded town and **shot the people who tried to get out.**
- **Between 400 and 700 Pequot were killed** — the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation's own history
  says "hundreds of elderly men, women, and children," killed **in less than an hour**. The Pequot
  fighting men were away with their sachem **Sassacus**.
  (Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation, "History," https://www.mptn-nsn.gov/history/ ; Connecticut
  State Department of Education Mashantucket Pequot curriculum, "The Mystic Massacre,"
  https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/SDE/Publications/Native-Americans/Mashantucket/MashantucketPequot_Cycle3_Lesson06_TreatyofHartford_S2_CloseReading_Classwork_LessonPlan.pdf
  ; Connecticut History, https://connecticuthistory.org/topics-page/pequot-war/)
- **Treaty of Hartford, 1638:** signed by the Hartford colony's leaders and the Mohegan and
  Narragansett. It **divided the surviving Pequot among the Mohegan and Narragansett**, **forbade
  them ever to be called Pequot again**, and **barred them from their homeland**. Survivors were
  handed to the victors or sold into slavery.
- **It failed.** By **1651** some Mashantucket Pequot had permission to resettle **500 acres** under
  **Robin Cassacinamon**, and a reservation was established at **Mashantucket in 1666**. The
  Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation exists in Connecticut today. (Mashantucket Pequot Tribal
  Nation, "History," above.)

### 3.4 King Philip's War, 1675-1676 (Metacom's War)
- Started in **June 1675** at Swansea, after Plymouth Colony executed three Wampanoag men.
  (NPS Nockum Hill article, above.)
- **The Great Swamp Fight, 19 December 1675, Rhode Island.** About **1,000 Narragansett men, women
  and children** were in a fortified winter village of about five acres in a frozen swamp. An
  English force — roughly **1,000 soldiers** in the NPS account, drawn from Plymouth under Governor
  Josiah Winslow, Massachusetts under Major Samuel Appleton, and Connecticut under Major Robert
  Treat — took the fort and **burned every wigwam in it**. NPS: **300 Native men killed and almost
  400 women and children killed.** (NPS, Roger Williams National Memorial, "Roger Williams: King
  Philip's War," https://www.nps.gov/rowi/learn/historyculture/philipswar.htm)
- Towns burned on the Rhode Island mainland included **Wickford, Warwick and Pawtuxet**, and
  **Providence was burned in late March 1676.** (NPS, same page.)
- **Captives were sold overseas as slaves.** New England colonies shipped Native captives to
  **Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier in North Africa.** Certificates
  signed by **Plymouth Governor Josiah Winslow and Massachusetts Bay Governor John Leverett**
  authorised transport and sale into perpetual slavery of Native men, women and children convicted
  of helping Metacom. Descendants of captives sent from Rhode Island to Bermuda have since been
  reconnected with Indigenous nations in New England and Canada.
  (Brown University, "Colonial enslavement of Native Americans included those who surrendered, too,"
  15 February 2017, https://www.brown.edu/news/2017-02-15/enslavement ; Brown University Slavery and
  Justice Report, https://slaveryandjusticereport.brown.edu/sections/slavery-the-slave-trade-and-brown/)
- **Metacom was shot dead at Mount Hope on 12 August 1676** by a Native man serving with the
  colonial force; his wife and nine-year-old son were sold into slavery; his head was displayed at
  Plymouth. **`native-nations` leads on Metacom himself** (their story slug: `metacom-native-nations`)
  — this chapter must not retell his biography. (Sourced in `research/research-native-nations.md`,
  era 3.)
- **Shared with `native-nations`** (their angle: the nations' war and Metacom) and `slavery-freedom`
  (their angle: Native enslavement as part of the slavery system). **Our angle: how the war was
  fought and what it cost — the burned fort, the burned towns, the per-capita death rate, and the
  disposal of the defeated.**

### 3.5 The Beaver Wars
- Haudenosaunee raids on Wendat (Huron) villages ran through the 1600s to control the fur trade.
  **Between 1642 and 1649 those raids dispersed the Wendat**; the Wendat homeland, Wendake, was
  destroyed in **1649-1650**. **Many Wendat were killed; many others were adopted into Haudenosaunee
  nations.** About **500 Wendat** left Georgian Bay for the Quebec City region.
- French and Dutch traders **armed their Native trading partners with guns**, which is what made
  these wars what they were.
  (The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Wendat (Huron)," https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/huron
  ; "Iroquois Wars," https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/iroquois-wars)

### 3.6 The Pueblo Revolt, 10 August 1680 — the military side only
- The Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande rose together, besieged and took Santa Fe, and drove Spain
  out of New Mexico. **401 settlers and 21 Franciscan friars were killed**; most Spaniards were
  allowed to leave. The Pueblos governed themselves **12 years**; Diego de Vargas began the
  reconquest in **1692**, and armed resistance went on for years after.
- **`native-nations` leads and has the story block (`pope`).** This chapter gives only the military
  fact: a coordinated rising across many towns and languages that beat a European army and held the
  ground for twelve years. Sourced in `research/research-native-nations.md`, era 3 (Britannica;
  EBSCO; Indian Pueblo Cultural Center; Gilder Lehrman).

### 4.1 The shape of the era
European wars were fought here under other names, by colonial militia and by Native nations that had
their own reasons for fighting. The characteristic act of these wars in the north was **the raid on a
sleeping town in winter, followed by a forced march of captives hundreds of miles to Canada.**
Captives were currency: they were adopted, ransomed, or died on the road.

### 4.2 Deerfield, 29 February 1704 — the raid, and the numbers
- **Attacking force:** a French lieutenant leading **47 French Canadians and 200 Abenaki,
  Kanien'kehaka (Mohawk) and Wendat (Huron) fighters.** They struck before dawn.
- **The town held about 300 people. Over half were killed or captured. PVMA gives 48 killed and 111
  captured.** The Library of Congress "Today in History" entry for 29 February gives **57 killed and
  112 captured**, and adds that of the more than 100 captives **only about half ever returned to New
  England.** **Give the range: 48-57 killed, 111-112 taken.**
  (Pocumtuck Valley Memorial Association, "The Deerfield Raid,"
  https://americancenturies.org/essays/essay-deerfield-raid/ ; Library of Congress, "Today in
  History - February 29," https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/february-29/)
- **The march:** captives were herded across the frozen Deerfield River (Pocumpetook) and marched
  **300 miles through Abenaki country to New France in winter. Captives who could not keep up were
  killed on the road.** (PVMA, same essay.)
- Context: the raid was part of **Queen Anne's War**; France sent soldiers from New France to attack
  English settlements, and Native nations fought alongside them to keep the English out of their
  homelands. (PVMA, same essay.)

### 4.4 STORY — Eunice Kanenstenhawi Williams (1696-1785)
All from PVMA, "Eunice Kanenstenhawi Williams,"
https://americancenturies.org/lookitup/williams-eunice-kanenstenhawi/ unless marked.
- **Born Deerfield, Massachusetts, 17 September 1696** (birth date from
  https://deerfieldraid1704.org/popups/people/EuniceKWilliams.html). **Seven years old when she was
  taken in the 1704 raid** and carried to New France.
- **Ritually adopted into a Kanien'kehaka (Mohawk) family at Kahnawake.** First given the name
  **A'ongote**, "she was taken and placed" as a new member of the nation; later **Kanenstenhawi**,
  "she brings in the corn," and **accepted as a full member of the nation.** She was baptised
  Catholic as **Marguerite**.
- **Her father and English officials tried repeatedly to get her back. She refused.**
- **At 16 she married François-Xavier Arosen**, a Native man of her village. **At least three
  children.** Known daughters: **Catherine (Gassinontie, "Flying Leg"), born 1736**, and **Marie
  (Skentsiese, "New Fish"), born 1739**; a son, **John**, who apparently died at Lake George, New
  York. **One grandson and 12 great-grandchildren**, two of whom were sent to school in Longmeadow,
  Massachusetts, where her brother **Stephen Williams** was minister.
- **She made four trips back to New England — 1740, 1741, 1743 and 1761 — to visit her Williams
  brothers and sisters**, sometimes with Arosen. **Staying at her brother's house in Longmeadow she
  slept outside in the orchard rather than inside an English house.** Every time, she went home to
  Kahnawake.
- **Died 1785, aged 89, at Kahnawake.** English New England called her the famous "unredeemed
  captive."

### 4.5 The Yamasee War, 1715-1717 (South Carolina)
- Began **Good Friday, 15 April 1715.** Cause given by the South Carolina Encyclopedia: **abusive
  trade practices by white traders** — and specifically **trade debt, the enslavement of Native
  people, and the whipping of Native people**, plus settlement on their land. The Yamasee, having
  run out of deer and of captives to sell, **killed their creditors** and attacked settlements along
  the colony's southern frontier.
- **About 100 settlers were killed in 1715.** The war spread far beyond the Yamasee — Cherokee,
  Creek (Muscogee), Choctaw, Catawba, Pee Dee and Santee were all involved, from the Savannah River
  to Charleston, in an attempt to force the colonists out. **By spring 1716 most of the Yamasee had
  moved to Spanish Florida**; the Catawba and Santee made peace by that autumn.
- Sources: South Carolina Encyclopedia, "Yamassee War,"
  https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/yamassee-war/ ; NCpedia, "Yamassee War,"
  https://www.ncpedia.org/yamassee-war ; SCIWAY, https://www.sciway.net/hist/indians/yemassee.html
- **Shared with `slavery-freedom`** (their angle: the Indian slave trade as part of the slavery
  system). **Our angle: a colonial war whose cause was the slave trade in Native people, and which
  nearly ended the colony that ran it.**
- **NOT ADOPTED:** the frequently seen figure of "400 settlers killed" was not confirmed in these
  sources, which give about 100 for 1715. **Do not write 400.**
