### 6. 1800 to 1850
- **The first long-distance telegraph message, May 24, 1844:** Morse sent "What hath God wrought" (Numbers 23:23, chosen by Annie G. Ellsworth, the Patent Commissioner's daughter) from the U.S. Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore; Vail transcribed and repeated it back. The paper tape survives at the Smithsonian. News no longer traveled at the speed of a horse or a train — this chapter's outline already carries "the telegraph breaking the link between news and travel." (Sources: U.S. Senate historical office; Smithsonian NMAH object record.)

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **The Audion, 1906:** Lee de Forest's triode vacuum tube — the first practical amplifier of weak radio signals — is the device that later made home radio receivers (and KDKA-era broadcasting, 1920, already in this chapter's outline) possible. (Sources: Britannica "Lee de Forest"; National MagLab "Audion – 1906.")
- **Electronic television, September 7, 1927:** Philo Farnsworth, age 21, transmitted the first all-electronic TV image (a straight line) at his Green Street lab in San Francisco using his image-dissector camera tube. The device is `technology`'s; TV news is this chapter's. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters "Philo T. Farnsworth"; FoundSF.)

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **Mosaic, April 22, 1993:** the first widely used graphical web browser, from Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina, and colleagues at the University of Illinois' NCSA — the moment the web (opened to the public by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN, August 6, 1991; Berners-Lee is British) became usable by ordinary readers, setting up this chapter's "news on a screen" turn. (Sources: NCSA, University of Illinois; NSF; CERN "A short history of the Web.")

## Parked from `marketplace` research (2026-08-08) — advertising as the money behind the medium

### 4. 1700 to 1750
- Benjamin Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from Samuel Keimer on **October 2, 1729**. Under him advertising became the paper's main revenue source; he used more white space and varied type sizes on the notices, and the paper grew **from two pages to four** partly to hold them. Historians attribute much of his wealth and his 1748 retirement from printing to advertising income. (American Antiquarian Society, *The News Media and the Making of America, 1730–1865*; Library of Congress item record; Benjamin Franklin Historical Society.)
### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **First paid radio commercial: August 28, 1922, WEAF, New York.** The Queensboro Corporation paid **$50** for about ten minutes promoting apartments in Jackson Heights, Queens. (*Saturday Evening Post*, "Making Radio Pay"; NPR; broadcast histories.)
- **First legal television commercial: July 1, 1941, WNBT, New York**, before a Brooklyn Dodgers–Philadelphia Phillies game. A Bulova watch face over a U.S. map, about ten seconds, costing **$9** ($4 air charges, $5 station charges). (Television Academy; Slate; Guinness World Records; Adweek.)
- **N. W. Ayer & Son**, founded in Philadelphia in **1869** by Francis Wayland Ayer with $250 from his father, is generally called the first U.S. advertising agency; its "open contract" disclosed what publishers charged. Records at the Smithsonian NMAH (NMAH.AC.0059). (Smithsonian SOVA; Historical Society of Pennsylvania.)
### 10. 2000 to Today
- **FTC, *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability*, May 2014:** data brokers hold "billions of data elements" on nearly every U.S. consumer, combining online and offline records, largely without consumers knowing; "onboarding" attaches offline data to browser cookies so a person can be followed site to site and shown targeted advertisements. The FTC asked Congress to legislate transparency and consumer access. (FTC report, May 2014; Senate HSGAC testimony of Maneesha Mithal, May 15, 2014.) `marketplace` explains it as shopping; the advertising money behind the feed is yours.

- **Wells's own account, printed the same year in *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* (1892), Project Gutenberg ebook 14975, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/14975/14975-h/14975-h.htm :**
  > "Wednesday evening May 24, 1892, the city of Memphis was filled with excitement. Editorials in the daily papers of that date caused a meeting to be held in the Cotton Exchange Building; a committee was sent for the editors of the Free Speech an Afro-American journal published in that city, and the only reason the open threats of lynching that were not carried out was because they could not be found."
  > "Mr. Fleming, the business manager and owning a half interest the Free Speech, had to leave town to escape the mob, and was afterwards ordered not to return; letters and telegrams sent me in New York where I was spending my vacation advised me that bodily harm awaited my return. Creditors took possession of the office and sold the outfit, and the Free Speech was as if it had never been."
- So: a committee went looking for the editors and would have lynched them if they had been found; the business partner fled and was ordered never to return; Wells was in New York and was warned not to come back; and the paper ended because **creditors seized the office and sold the equipment.** No fire is described by the woman who owned the paper.

## 1. Before 1500

### Runners and wampum (Haudenosaunee)
- Six Nations runners "summoned councils, conveyed intelligence from nation to nation and warned of impending danger. Significantly, runners brought messages and carried stringed wampum to signify their official role, diplomatic protocol and the weight of their words." They carried a pouch of pounded parched corn mixed with maple sugar. (Donald Quigley and Laurence M. Hauptman, "Great Iroquois Runners: Lewis 'Deerfoot' Bennett and Tom Longboat," *American Indian* magazine, NMAI, Summer 2016, vol. 17 no. 2, https://www.americanindianmagazine.org/story/great-iroquois-runners-lewis-deerfoot-bennett-and-tom-longboat )
- Onondaga Nation: "Wampum is created from the shell of a clam. The beads are cut from the white and purple parts of the shell." Wampum strings "invite the other nations to council meetings. These wampum strings are given the topic that all of the nations are to meet and discuss about." "At the end of the wampum string is a wooden stick. The wooden stick tells the people of the nation when the meeting is to take place. As each day passes, a notch is cut off the stick." Onondaga, as Keepers of the Central Fire, keep the wampum records. Some belts came back to the Onondaga in the fall of 1989 after the chiefs worked to recover them. (Onondaga Nation, "Wampum," https://www.onondaganation.org/culture/wampum/ ) The page gives no date for when the notched-stick practice began. Do not place it before 1500 as a dated fact. Tell it as the Onondaga's own account of how a council is called.
- Ganondagan and the Haudenosaunee Confederacy site (search summary only, not opened): a runner without wampum would not be taken seriously. (unconfirmed: search summary only) The NMAI magazine line above says the same thing in weaker words, so the outline uses that one.
- Shell beads: "shell beads in the Northeast have been found which are 4500 years old." Earlier beads "were larger and relatively uncommon because drilling the material was difficult with stone drill bits." (Tara Prindle, NativeTech, "Wampum: History and Background," https://www.nativetech.org/wampum/wamphist.htm )
- Treaty belts: "Important matters such as treaty agreements were likely to be marked by an exchange of Wampum belts, with designs in two colors, which thereafter served as visual reminders of the event itself." (NativeTech, same page)
- **Dispute to respect.** Older scholarship held that narrow tubular wampum could not be made with stone drills and so dates from after European contact. Marge Bruchac (On the Wampum Trail project) challenges that assumption and notes that "by the 1610s, Native people living along the Saint Lawrence seaway were already familiar with (and specifically requesting) awls as trade goods," which they used alongside nails as drill bits. (Bruchac, "Of Shells and Ship's Nails," On the Wampum Trail, 21 June 2014, https://wampumtrail.wordpress.com/2014/06/21/of-shells-and-ships-nails/ ) So the outline does NOT say that belts date mostly from after 1600. It says only that stone-drilled beads were larger and less common (NativeTech) and that bead makers later used iron awls and nails.

### Publick Occurrences, 1690
- David A. Copeland, "Publick Occurrences, Both Forreign and Domestick," Britannica (from *The SAGE Encyclopedia of Journalism*), https://www.britannica.com/topic/Publick-Occurrences-Both-Foreign-and-Domestick , read via curl: appeared once, 25 September 1690, Boston; three pages about 7.5 by 11.5 inches and a blank fourth, which the prospectus "intimated was left blank so that people who had more news or more accurate information could add it to the paper and then pass it on." "Beginning in 1667 a series of one-time broadsheets appeared in Massachusetts." "Numb. 1" on the front page. Prospectus: "It is designed, that the Countrey shall be furnished once a moneth (or if any Glut of Occurrences happen, oftener,) with an account of such considerable things as have arrived unto our Notice." Harris wanted to stop the "many False Reports, maliciously made, and spread among us." Reported a fire that destroyed about 20 houses and a smallpox epidemic. "He complained that colonial officials provided the Mohawks with too much information about colonial military activities." Rumour that Louis XIV was having sexual relations with his "Sons Wife." "On September 29 it [the Governor's Council] ordered Harris never again to publish Publick Occurrences or any other newspaper in the colony," citing "Reflections of a very high nature," and forbidding printing any newspaper "without License first obtained from those that are or shall be appointed by the Government to grant the same." The council followed a 1686 English instruction requiring "especial leave and license." Harris "printed no more newspapers, but he did print a news pamphlet in 1692, the same year he was named the colony's official printer. Harris returned to England in 1695 and resumed newspaper publication in 1699 with the London Post." "All copies of Publick Occurrences were ordered called in. A single copy is known to exist in London."
- Harris background (same Britannica article): born about 1647, died 1720; printed pamphlets for religious dissenters in the 1670s and 1680s; a Baptist opposed to Charles II; in 1679 printed the unlicensed *The Domestick Intelligence; or, News both from City and Country*; "jailed twice"; arrested a third time in 1685, fled to the Netherlands, came to America a year later and set up a printing business.
- EBSCO Research Starters, "Publick Occurrences (newspaper)," 2023, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/publick-occurrences-newspaper : suppressed by "Massachusetts Governor Simon Bradstreet and the colony's governing council" four days after release; the council announced its "high resentment and Disallowance of said Pamphlet, and order that the same be Suppressed and called in"; Harris had published "Reflections of a very high nature: As also sundry doubtful and uncertain Reports"; the paper "charged Indian allies of the British with 'barbarous' atrocities against French prisoners." EBSCO says London authorities had jailed Harris for seditious libel ten years earlier.
- American Antiquarian Society, *The News Media and the Making of America*, item 31, the broadside "By the Governour & Council. Whereas some have lately presumed to Print and Disperse a Pamphlet, Entituled, Publick Occurrences..." https://collections.americanantiquarian.org/earlyamericannewsmedia/items/show/31 : "It is not entirely clear what the governor and council found objectionable, but certainly the most important objection was that the paper was published without their approval." The broadside is held in the Public Record Office, London.
- "First newspaper": Britannica says "considered the first newspaper published in the British-American colonies." The outline says "the first newspaper in the English colonies," which matches. ### The Pueblo Revolt runners, 1680
- Dennis Zotigh, "In 1680, Pueblo Natives in the Southwest Launched the First Successful, Although Short-Lived, American Revolution," Smithsonian Voices, National Museum of the American Indian, 2 September 2026, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-american-indian/2026/09/02/pueblo-natives-southwest-launched-first-successful-shortlived-american-revolution/ (read via curl): 1598, Juan de Oñate came with 129 soldiers, 10 priests and colonizers; the occupiers "sought land, labor and food"; 1675, Governor Juan Francisco Treviño "ordered the arrest of 47 medicine men for practicing sorcery. Pueblo people went to Santa Fe to demand the prisoners' release and the Spanish complied. "Among those released was a leader from Ohkay Owingeh ... His name was Po'pay." Towa, Tiwa, Tewa, Tano and Keres-speaking pueblos, Zuni, Hopi and some Apache pledged support. "Po'pay sent runners carrying knotted ropes to the distant villages. A knot was unraveled each day until all the knots were gone ... set for August 11, 1680. On the morning of August 8, Pedro Omtua and Nicolas Catua, two young runners from Tesuque Pueblo, meaning 'village of the narrow place of the cottonwood trees' in the Tewa language, set out for Tanogeh (Tano villages) with the ropes. The first pueblo they reached was Pecos. Upon their departure from Pecos, Christian Indians informed Franciscan missionary leader Fernando De Velasco that two Tewa messengers had visited the war chief's house." "On August 9, the Spanish captured Pedro Omtua and Nicolas Catua and tortured them to make them reveal the significance of the knotted cord. Learning of their capture, Po'pay ordered the revolt to begin a day early." Hopi villages did not get the advance notice. 10 August the revolt began; "approximately 400 people were killed, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in Nuevo México"; by 13 August almost all Spanish settlements destroyed; 21 August Governor Antonio de Otermín led the remaining 2,000 settlers out to El Paso del Norte.
- Indian Pueblo Cultural Center, "Pueblo Revolt," https://indianpueblo.org/pueblo-revolt-2/ : "Runners carried knotted cords as they ran to notify the distant Pueblos of the plan." "Each day a knot was to be undone." Uprising "initially set for August 13, 1680." "Catua and Omtua (Taytsugeh Oweengeh/Tesuque Pueblo) were caught, questioned, and hanged." The revolt "started sooner than planned on August 10th." Spanish reconquest in 1692.

The outline gives both warnings. The hanging is from IPCC. NMAI says captured and tortured, and does not mention the hanging.

### News pooled for a war: the Associated Press, 1846
- AAS item 71 (above): "In 1846, with the start of the Mexican-American War and the early development of the telegraph, timely information became significantly more costly." Before that, New York papers "used fast harbor sloops, special pony expresses, and even carrier pigeons" to beat rivals. "The proprietor of the Sun, Moses Yale Beach (1800-1868), organized the leading newspaper publishers of New York to pool their resources to get the war news. ### The telegraph and who owned it, 1843 to 1851

### The Cherokee Phoenix, 1828 to 1835
- Angela F. Pulley, "Cherokee Phoenix," New Georgia Encyclopedia, 2002, last edited 17 July 2020, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/cherokee-phoenix/ (read via curl): "the first Native American newspaper in the United States," first printed 1828 at New Echota, Georgia, capital of the Cherokee Nation; weekly, "adjacent columns of English and Cherokee text"; the General Council of the Cherokee Nation with Rev. Samuel Worcester (American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions) set up the printing office; editor **Elias Boudinot**; first issue **21 February 1828**; renamed *Cherokee Phoenix and Indians' Advocate* in 1829; "The paper was never intended to be a vehicle of free speech but an instrument of the official leadership of the Cherokee Nation, which vehemently opposed Cherokee removal on any terms"; Boudinot "forced to resign" August 1832; Elijah Hicks editor; "published weekly until May 1834, when the Cherokee annuity was not paid"; "In 1835 the Georgia Guard, a militia unit organized to police the Cherokee territory that the state claimed, confiscated the printing press to prevent anti-removal sentiments from being voiced."
- AAS item 69, *Cherokee Phoenix*, 21 February 1828, https://collections.americanantiquarian.org/earlyamericannewsmedia/items/show/69 : "In 1825 the Cherokee Council pledged $1,500 for the purchase of a printing press and type"; "a cast iron press shipped from Boston"; Boudinot (1802-39) editor; half the content in "the eighty-five-character Cherokee syllabary devised by Sequoyah"; "The paper ceased publication on May 31, 1834. There were thoughts of reviving it the next year, but the Georgia Guard confiscated the press and threw out the type."
- George Lamplugh (retired historian), "The News from Indian Country: The Cherokee Phoenix, 1828-1834, Part II," 8 January 2013, https://georgelamplugh.com/2013/01/08/in-pursuit-of-dead-georgians-9-the-news-from-indian-country-the-cherokee-phoenix-1828-1834-part-ii/ (read via curl): Boudinot printed the Georgia Guard's campaign against missionaries, and Guard commander **Col. Charles Nelson** "threatened to shut down the paper and to chastise Boudinot physically"; President Jackson ordered the annuity paid to individuals, not the tribal treasury, cutting the paper's money; Stand Watie edited while Boudinot toured (December 1831 to June 1832); by early 1834 "the paper's white printer had been ordered by Georgia authorities to leave the Nation; mail service was frequently disrupted; Hicks was being sued for libel by a local white sheriff"; last issue **17 May 1834**; in 1835, when Principal Chief John Ross planned to revive it, "The Georgia Guard heard rumors of Ross's plan and, aided by Elias Boudinot's brother Stand Watie, went to New Echota and raided the newspaper office, scattering type, removing the press, and burning the building."
- Cherokee Phoenix (the Cherokee Nation's paper today), Staff Reports, "History of the Cherokee Phoenix," 13 January 2015, https://www.cherokeephoenix.org/archives/history-of-the-cherokee-phoenix/article_30c25bf9-bc26-5628-9687-75e1be8581ba.html : Worcester and the ABCFM "helped build the printing office, cast type in the Cherokee syllabary"; Boudinot, Stand Watie, John Ridge and Elijah Hicks raised money; "When word leaked that Chief Ross intended to move the printing press from New Echota to nearby Red Clay, Tenn., the Georgia Guard ... moved in and destroyed the press and burned the Cherokee Phoenix office with the help of Stand Watie who was a member of the Treaty Party." The Cherokee Nation published again from September 1844 as the *Cherokee Advocate* in Tahlequah (editor William Potter Ross). The Cherokee Independent Press Act passed in 2000.
- **Disputes recorded:** (1) Last issue: 17 May 1834 (Lamplugh) or 31 May 1834 (AAS). NGE says "May 1834." The outline says May 1834. (2) What the Guard did: "confiscated the press" (NGE), "confiscated the press and threw out the type" (AAS), raided, scattered type, removed the press and burned the building (Lamplugh), destroyed the press and burned the office (Cherokee Phoenix). All agree the Georgia Guard took or destroyed the press in 1835. Two sources (Lamplugh, Cherokee Phoenix) say Stand Watie helped. The outline gives the press taken and the type thrown out as agreed, and the burning as what two accounts add. (3) Why: NGE says "to prevent anti-removal sentiments from being voiced"; Lamplugh and the Cherokee Phoenix say Ross planned to revive or move the paper. The outline gives the Ross plan.
- **Who gave the order to seize the press:** not named in any source opened. See SEARCHED, NOT FOUND below.
- **Land:** New Echota was the capital of the Cherokee Nation, in land the state of Georgia claimed (NGE). The Treaty of New Echota (December 1835), signed by Boudinot and others of the Treaty Party, gave up all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi (NGE "Elias Boudinot"). The forced removal is `native-nations`' to tell.

### Elias Boudinot (story)

  - "In the vast majority of instances, the government restrained the free press without any legal process. The military routinely arrested newspaper editors and closed their presses; military tribunals banished some of them to the Confederacy."
  - Canton, Ohio: "a mob destroyed the offices of the Stark County Democrat after its editor, Archibald McGregor, published allegedly treasonous statements." A year later "the army arrested him on unspecified charges," with the city's Republican postmaster present.
  - Secretary of State William Seward ordered the arrest of an editor of the *Freeman's Journal*; held 11 weeks and released without trial. (Editor not named on the page.)
  - Secretary of War Edwin Stanton "authorized a military governor to destroy the office of the Sunday Chronicle in Washington."
  - May 1864: Lincoln "directly ordered Gen. John A. Dix to arrest the editors of the New York World and seize the paper's offices after two reporters ran a bogus proclamation," purportedly Lincoln's, calling for a day of fasting and a much larger draft; Lincoln rescinded the order once it was clear the two had forged it "to influence the price of gold."
  - Telegraph: "As the Civil War began in April 1861, the Lincoln administration censored telegraph dispatches to and from Washington." Gen. George McClellan reached an agreement with Washington correspondents. The House Judiciary Committee reported in December 1861.
  - 1863: Lincoln revoked Gen. Ambrose Burnside's order suspending the Democratic *Chicago Times*, saying he was "embarrassed" by the failure to balance the Union's needs and "the Liberty of the Press."
  - Gen. Burnside's General Order No. 38 (1863): treason "express or implied" would not be tolerated. Clement Vallandigham, former congressman, arrested at home by soldiers on 5 May 1863 after a speech of 1 May; military tribunal; prison for the war; Lincoln changed it to banishment to the Confederacy; *Ex parte Vallandigham* (1863), the Supreme Court denied review.
  - Military information in papers: Robert E. Lee read Northern papers for McClellan's movements; a *New York Tribune* report of Sherman's supply ships at Morehead City reached Gen. William Hardee.

### Western Union's monopoly, 1866 to 1900
- EH.Net, Nonnenmacher (era 6 above): "By 1864 only Western Union and the American Telegraph Company remained of the 'Six Nations.'" "By 1866 Western Union absorbed its last two competitors and reached its position of market dominance." "Western Union was the first nationwide industrial monopoly, with over 90% of the market share and dominance in every state." Messages "increased from 5.8 million in 1867 to 63.2 million in 1900"; average price fell "from an average of $1.09 to 30 cents per message"; "roughly 30 to 40 cents of every dollar of revenue were net profit." "Bills were introduced in almost every session of Congress calling for either regulation of or government entry into the industry. Western Union's lobby was able to block almost any legislation." Rivals: Jay Gould's Atlantic and Pacific (1874), merged with Western Union in 1881 when Gould took control; John Mackay's Postal Telegraph Company from the 1880s, "10-20% of the market at various times." Bell "offered Western Union the patent for the telephone for $100,000, but the company declined." "In 1900, local calls accounted for 97% of the telephone's business."
- Joshua Wolff, "The Telegraph Act of 1866: An 'Entering Wedge' against the Western Union Monopoly," Business History Conference, 2007 meeting abstract, https://thebhc.org/telegraph-act-1866-entering-wedge-against-western-union-monopoly : in 1866 Western Union "consolidated with its major rivals, giving it a nearly total monopoly"; members of the 39th Congress proposed competition, rate rules or buying the lines; Senator B. Gratz Brown called the resulting act an "entering wedge."
- AAS item 71 (era 6): the New York Associated Press "became a formidable news monopoly in the late nineteenth century." Its power "was eventually broken by a rival group of Chicago newspaper publishers known as the Western Associated Press," and a new national cooperative, the Associated Press, formed in 1892.

### The Espionage Act, 1917, the Sedition Act, 1918, and who was jailed
- David Asp, "Espionage Act of 1917," First Amendment Encyclopedia, 8 August 2023, updated 6 August 2024, https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/espionage-act-of-1917/ : enacted 15 June 1917; criminal penalties for obstructing enlistment or causing disloyalty in the forces; materials "urging treason" declared "nonmailable matter," and "Postmaster General Albert S. ### The Chicago Defender and Robert S. Abbott (story)
- Britannica, "Chicago Defender," https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chicago-Defender (read via curl): founded in Chicago in 1905 by Robert Sengstacke Abbott; "the most influential African American newspaper during the early and mid-20th century"; four-page weekly at first; "Like the white-owned Hearst and Pulitzer newspapers, the Defender under Abbott used sensationalism"; editorials against white supremacy and lynching; "By 1929 the Defender was selling more than 250,000 copies each week"; in World War II Black papers protested the treatment of Black servicemen and "the U.S. government threatened to indict African American publishers for sedition, but the Defender's publisher, John H. ### Spanish-language broadcasting, 1946 and 1955
- Texas State Historical Association, Handbook of Texas, "Cortez, Raoul Alfonso" (https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/cortez-raoul-alfonso): Raoul A. Cortez, born Xalapa, Veracruz, Mexico, 17 October 1905, former reporter for *La Prensa* in San Antonio. Opened KCOR-AM in San Antonio in 1946, "the first all Spanish-language radio station owned and operated by a Hispanic." ### The broadcast blacklist, 1950
- PBS American Masters, "How the Red Scare shaped American television" (https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/huac-red-scare-shaped-television/35229/): American Business Consultants, owned by three former FBI agents, published the newsletter *Counterattack* and in June 1950 the book *Red Channels: The Report of Communist Influence in Radio and Television*, which named 151 people in media. The owners visited networks, advertisers and sponsors and offered, for a fee, to "clear" people they had named. Clearance usually meant cooperating with the FBI or the House Un-American Activities Committee by naming others. The FBI and anti-communist groups pressed employers with letter campaigns and boycott threats, and networks and advertisers fired people. In 1946 liberal commentators at a leading Los Angeles radio station were fired. Publication date 22 June 1950 and the three agents' names (the summary spells them Kenneth Brierly, Theodore Kirkpatrick, John Keenan; spelling of the first unchecked) (unconfirmed: search summary only, Wikipedia).

### WLBT in Jackson, Mississippi: a station that cut the civil rights news, 1955 to 1971
- Mississippi Encyclopedia, Bryan Hardin Thrift, "WLBT-TV and Civil Rights" (https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/wlbt-tv-and-civil-rights/, published 2017): WLBT "was the first television station ever to lose its Federal Communication Commission (FCC) license, primarily for its racist defense of segregation." In fall 1955 Thurgood Marshall appeared on an NBC news program about the Brown v. Board of Education decisions. WLBT cut in with a slide reading "Sorry, Cable Trouble from New York." General manager Fred Beard said he broke off the program "because the TV networks were overloading the circuits with Negro propaganda." WLBT reporters used racial slurs on air. The station cut away from NBC's Huntley-Brinkley Report when it turned to civil rights, ran ads from the Citizens' Councils, and carried the *Citizens' Council Forum*. It refused Black Mississippians' requests for time to reply. In May 1963 Medgar Evers, NAACP field secretary, spoke on WLBT in reply to Mayor Allen Thompson. Three weeks later Byron De La Beckwith assassinated Evers. In 1963 WLBT ran "Sorry, Cable Trouble" over NBC's coverage of white violence against protesters at a Jackson lunch counter, an incident WLBT had itself filmed. About half of WLBT's viewing area was Black. In 1963 the Office of Communication of the United Church of Christ, led by Rev. Everett Parker, joined Aaron Henry (state NAACP president) and A. D. Beittel (president of Tougaloo College) to gather complaints, "with the threat of violent reprisal hanging over their heads." They petitioned against renewal in 1964. The FCC gave a one-year renewal with no hearing. The Court of Appeals ruled that viewers had "standing." At the hearing (encyclopedia: 1968) the FCC renewed the license for three years. "In 1971 the court reversed the FCC's license renewal, forcing the sale of the station."
- Mississippi Development Authority, Mississippi Freedom Trail marker "WLBT-TV," erected 2018, transcribed at HMdb (https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=178458): WLBT founded 1953 by the Lamar Life Insurance Company (call letters for Lamar Broadcasting Television). The White Citizens' Council ran a bookstore in the lobby of its studios. The marker says Marshall appeared on the *Today* show (the encyclopedia says "an NBC news program"). The 1966 appeals court opinion was by Warren Burger. New FCC hearing in 1967 (the encyclopedia says 1968). The court "ordered the FCC to revoke Lamar's license in 1969. Lamar appealed, but lost in 1971." In 1971 the FCC let Communications Improvement, Inc., a biracial nonprofit board, run the station, and it hired some of the first Black television executives in the South.
- **Dates, recorded as the sources give them:** 1969 appeals court order to revoke (marker), 1971 final loss (marker and encyclopedia). Hearing year 1967 (marker) or 1968 (encyclopedia).

### Vietnam on television, 1965 to 1973
- Britannica, "Vietnam War and the media" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Vietnam-War-and-the-media-2051426): fewer than two dozen American newsmen in Indochina as late as 1964. Reporting from Vietnam was uncensored, and the military found only a handful of journalists guilty of breaking security rules. Many experts found that before 1968 most reporting supported the war. William Calley, the only soldier convicted, was found guilty in March 1971 of murdering 22 civilians. In 1969, acting on a tip, the freelance reporter Seymour Hersh found and interviewed Calley. His syndicated account was the first public report. Hersh won the 1970 Pulitzer Prize. (The killings are `war`'s. This chapter tells how the public learned.)

### Walter Cronkite (story)
- Britannica, "Walter Cronkite" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Walter-Cronkite): born 4 November 1916, St. Joseph, Missouri. Died 17 July 2009, New York. Moved to Houston 1927, school newspapers. University of Texas 1933 to 1935, left to work full time for a Houston paper. United Press news editor 1939. War correspondent in World War II: North Atlantic, German bombing of London, North Africa, bombing raids over Germany, D-Day 1944, the Nuremberg trials. UP bureau chief in Moscow 1946 to 1948. Hired by Edward R. Murrow in 1950 for the CBS station in Washington. Anchor of the *CBS Evening News* 1962 to 1981. Soon after he took over from Douglas Edwards the program grew from 15 to 30 minutes, "the first half-hour nightly news show on American network television." Reported the assassination of John F. Kennedy in 1963 and the Apollo 11 Moon landing in 1969. In 1968 he went to Vietnam after the Tet Offensive and on his return said the war could end only in a stalemate. Johnson told his staff, "If I've lost Cronkite, I've lost Middle America," and "some held" that Johnson's decision not to run again was a result. (Britannica gives it as a claim some held, not a fact.) Covered Watergate, Nixon's resignation, the Sadat-Begin talks. A 1972 poll named him "the most trusted man in America." Sign-off: "And that's the way it is." Covered nearly every American crewed spaceflight 1961 to 1981. Presidential Medal of Freedom 1981.
- Date of the Vietnam broadcast: Britannica titles its timeline entry "Vietnam War Timeline: February 27, 1968."
- **Movie:** *Walter Cronkite: Witness to History*, PBS *American Masters*, 2006, directed by Catherine Tatge, narrated by Katie Couric; a documentary about him (PBS American Masters episode page, https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/archive/episode/walter-cronkite-witness-to-history ; Variety review 2006; search results).

### The Pentagon Papers, 1971
- Britannica, "Pentagon Papers" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pentagon-Papers): a 47-volume secret history of the US role in Indochina from World War II to May 1968, ordered in 1967 by Secretary of Defense Robert S. McNamara. About 3,000 pages of narrative and 4,000 pages of documents. Daniel Ellsberg, who had worked on it, gave it to *The New York Times* without permission. The *Times* began publishing on 13 June 1971. After the third day the Justice Department got a temporary court order stopping further publication. The *Washington Post* also had the papers. On 30 June 1971 the Supreme Court, 6 to 3, freed the papers to publish. What the papers showed: Truman gave military aid to France in its colonial war; Johnson planned open war in 1964, a year before the public knew; Johnson ordered bombing of North Vietnam in 1965 despite intelligence officials' judgment that it would not work. Nixon's "plumbers," led in this case by former CIA officer E. Howard Hunt, broke into the Beverly Hills office of Ellsberg's psychiatrist to find damaging information. Some parts stayed classified until 2011.
- Britannica, "Daniel Ellsberg": born 7 April 1931, Chicago, died 16 June 2023. Charged under the Espionage Act, facing up to 115 years in prison. Trial began January 1973, lasted four months, and ended with all charges dismissed because of "gross governmental misconduct," including the break-in ordered through John D. Ehrlichman's "plumbers."
- Prior restraint, defined: a government order stopping something from being published before it is published. (Britannica calls the case "one of the most significant prior-restraint cases in history.")

### Watergate and the reporting, 1972 to 1974 (story: Woodward and Bernstein)
- Britannica, "Watergate scandal" (https://www.britannica.com/event/Watergate-Scandal): early on 17 June 1972 police arrested five burglars at the Democratic National Committee office in the Watergate complex. One, James W. McCord Jr., was security chief of the Committee to Re-elect the President. The next morning's *Washington Post* story was written by Alfred E. Lewis, Carl Bernstein and Bob Woodward. Bernstein covered Virginia politics and Woodward, new at the *Post*, covered minor crimes. Woodward, Bernstein and FBI investigators soon identified E. Howard Hunt (White House staff) and G. Gordon Liddy (counsel to the re-election committee). Press secretary Ron Ziegler called it a "third-rate burglary attempt." Most early news reports said the administration was not involved. On 23 June 1972 Nixon ordered the FBI to hold back its investigation. The tape of that order became the "smoking gun." A source the reporters called "Deep Throat" gave them leaks. In a front-page story on 10 October 1972 they reported that the bugging "stemmed from a massive campaign of political spying and sabotage conducted on behalf of President Nixon's re-election and directed by officials of the White House." The White House cast their work as one "liberal" paper's vendetta. CBS News prepared a two-part report. After the first part aired on 27 October 1972, Nixon aide Charles Colson threatened CBS's William Paley, and the second part was cut short. Newspapers friendly to Nixon hardly mentioned Watergate. Nixon won re-election in November 1972, carrying every state except Massachusetts (and losing the District of Columbia). On 9 August 1974, facing likely impeachment, Nixon resigned.
- Britannica, "W. Mark Felt": Deep Throat was W. Mark Felt, associate director of the FBI, who revealed it in a *Vanity Fair* interview in 2005. Born 17 August 1913, died 18 December 2008.
- Britannica, "Bob Woodward": born 26 March 1943, Geneva, Illinois. Police beat for nine months before the break-in. The *Post* won the 1973 Pulitzer Prize for public service for the reporting. Book *All the President's Men* (1974). Film 1976, Robert Redford as Woodward.
- Britannica, "Katharine Graham": born 16 June 1917, died 17 July 2001. Owner and publisher of the *Washington Post*, "especially known for supporting the Post's investigation into the Watergate scandal."
- **Clearing the [VERIFY] "brought down a president":** the sources do not support the reporters alone forcing Nixon out. Britannica's Woodward page says the reporting "led to" the resignation. Britannica's Watergate page and `government-politics` era 9 (bank and outline) show the other steps: the Senate hearings, the special prosecutor, *United States v. Nixon* (24 July 1974, 8 to 0) ordering the tapes released, the House Judiciary Committee's three articles of impeachment (27 to 30 July 1974), and the 23 June 1972 tape. Say that their reporting tied the break-in to the White House, and that the tapes, the courts and Congress followed. Recorded in the outline that way.
- **Movie:** *All the President's Men* (1976), about Woodward and Bernstein (Britannica).

### The fairness doctrine, 1949 to 1987
- Britannica, "Fairness doctrine" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Fairness-Doctrine): an FCC policy of 1949 to 1987. In 1949 the FCC report *In the Matter of Editorializing by Broadcast Licensees* required stations holding licenses to give time to fair and balanced coverage of controversial issues important to their communities, and to give people attacked on air a chance to reply. The "equal time" rule for candidates comes from the Communications Act, not the doctrine. 1969, *Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC*: the Supreme Court upheld the doctrine. In 1985 the FCC said the doctrine had a "chilling effect" on speech. "In 1987 the FCC formally repealed the fairness doctrine." The personal-attack and editorial rules lasted until 2000. More than 80 rules with language from it were repealed in 2011.
- Reagan Library topic guide, "Fairness Doctrine" (https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/topic-guide/fairness-doctrine): Congress passed the Fairness in Broadcasting Act of 1987 (S. 742) in June 1987 to write the doctrine into law. Reagan vetoed it, and Congress could not override. The FCC under new chairman Dennis Patrick then repealed the doctrine 4 to 0. Members of Congress called the vote "wrongheaded, misguided and illogical."
- Reagan veto message, Senate Document 100-10, printed 23 June 1987 (https://www.senate.gov/legislative/vetoes/messages/ReaganR/S742-Sdoc-100-10.pdf): Reagan wrote that the doctrine "requires Federal officials to supervise the editorial practices of broadcasters" and that it was "antagonistic to the freedom of expression guaranteed by the First Amendment." Veto date 19 June 1987 and FCC vote date 4 August 1987 (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Mississippi Encyclopedia (above): the WLBT challengers used the fairness doctrine.

### The Watergate hearings on television, 1973
- US Senate Historical Office, "Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities" (https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/watergate.htm): committee set up 7 February 1973, chairman Sam J. ### Section 230, 1996
- 47 U.S. Code section 230(c)(1), Cornell LII (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230): "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." Plain meaning: a website is not legally responsible, as a publisher, for what its users post. Part of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 (era 9 entry).

### Measuring the spread of false news, 2018
- MIT News, "Study: On Twitter, false news travels faster than true stories," 8 March 2018 (https://news.mit.edu/2018/study-twitter-false-news-travels-faster-true-stories-0308): Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, "The Spread of True and False News Online," *Science*, 2018. They tracked about 126,000 chains of retweets, tweeted more than 4.5 million times by about 3 million people, 2006 to 2017. Six fact-checking groups judged the stories and agreed more than 95 percent of the time. False stories were 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true ones. True stories took about six times as long to reach 1,500 people. Political false news spread most. With bots removed the difference remained: people, not automated accounts, spread the false stories.
