**Honest-credit framing for the superheavies (settled this pass):** every element from 114 up was *made in Russia* (the atoms formed at Dubna’s accelerators) by *joint Russian–American teams*, with U.S. labs contributing co-authors, detectors, analysis, and — decisively for 117 — target material only American reactors could make. IUPAC credits the collaborations, not one country. Say “made together,” never “discovered by the U.S.” (Sources: IUPAC Dec 2015/Nov 2016 releases; LLNL; ORNL.)

## Pre-discovery America (use of elements, not isolation of new Z)

*(Owner’s original notes — kept as written; expanded into the full “By time section” layer below.)*

### Before 1500
- Native peoples used **native copper** (Great Lakes Old Copper), **gold**, **silver**, **meteoritic iron** in some regions, pigments (iron oxides, etc.), salt, flint/chert (silicon compounds)—materials science without modern atomic theory.
- No isolation of a *new* chemical element in modern sense.

### 1500s–1700s
- Colonial mining: gold, silver (Southwest Spanish), ironworks (Saugus), lead, copper.
- European element discoveries (phosphorus, etc.) happen abroad; colonies export ores and use metals.

### 1800s
- American chemists analyze minerals; few/no new elements first isolated in U.S. compared to Europe (Davy, Berzelius, etc.).
- **Helium story (careful):** 1868 solar helium (Janssen, Lockyer). 1895 terrestrial helium Ramsay (UK) from cleveite. William F. Hillebrand (USGS) had seen inert gas from uraninite earlier without full identification. **1905** H.P. Cady & D.F. McFarland (University of Kansas): helium in natural gas from Dexter, Kansas—industrial helium path is American.
- Petroleum and industrial chemistry expand known *uses*, not always new Z.

### 1900–1940
- Ernest O. Lawrence invents **cyclotron** (1930s Berkeley)—machine that makes artificial elements possible.
- Segrè visits Berkeley; later Tc and At work.

## By time section (deep pass, 2026-07-23 — all ten eras, verified and sourced)

### 1. Before 1500
- **The Old Copper Complex of the Great Lakes — among the oldest metalworking on Earth.** Native peoples of the Lake Superior region (present-day Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota) mined veins of pure “native” copper and hammered it into spearpoints, knives, awls, fishhooks, and ornaments. A 2021 re-dating study (53 radiocarbon dates, led by geologist David Pompeani, published in *Radiocarbon*) found the copper work began **at least 9,500 years ago** — about 3,500 years earlier than once thought — and peaked roughly 7,000–5,000 years ago. That makes Great Lakes people among the world’s **first coppersmiths**, as old as or older than the earliest documented copper work in the Middle East (a copper pendant there is dated about 8,700 years old). (Sources: *Science* news, Mar 2021, “Ancient Native Americans were among the world’s first coppersmiths”; Archaeology Magazine; *Radiocarbon* 2021.)
- **Copper traveled the trade routes.** Lake Superior copper moved hundreds of miles along Native trade networks — the same continental routes that carried turquoise, shells, and obsidian (routes → Ch1/Ch3, already noted in those files). (Sources: ch03 research bank, sourced there; Smithsonian.)
- **Iron from the sky.** Some Native peoples worked **meteoritic iron** — iron that fell as meteorites, the only workable iron metal on the continent before smelting. Documented case: 22 iron beads from Hopewell burial mounds near **Havana, Illinois** (excavated 1945), made about 2,000 years ago from the **Anoka, Minnesota** iron meteorite — the metal was carried or traded several hundred miles and worked with the same hammering skills used on copper. (Sources: McCoy et al., *Journal of Archaeological Science* 2017; Smithsonian repository; *Elements* magazine, “Hopewell Meteoritic Metal Beads,” 2018.)
- Also in use: gold and silver ornaments (in some regions), red and yellow **ochre pigments** (iron oxides), **salt** (sodium chloride) gathered and traded, flint/chert tools. Materials science without atomic theory — nobody anywhere on Earth yet knew what a chemical element was.
- **State of the land:** entirely Native land. The very idea of “an element” in the modern sense would not exist anywhere until Europe’s 1600s–1700s.
- **Featured person:** none — no named individuals documented this far back.

### 2. The 1500s
- **Spaniards came hunting two elements: gold and silver.** Hernando de Soto’s expedition (1539–43) and Francisco Vázquez de Coronado’s (1540–42) crossed the Southeast and Southwest chasing stories of golden cities and found none (the journeys themselves → Ch1). The great Spanish silver strikes of the century were in Mexico and South America, not in the future United States.
- **No element science here or anywhere yet.** In Europe this is still the age of alchemy; the modern idea of an element had not been stated.
- **State of the land:** still Native land coast to coast; the metals in use were the same native copper, gold, and meteoritic iron as before, plus whatever the few Spanish outposts carried in.
- **Featured person:** none.

### 3. The 1600s
- **America’s first industry that made a metal: iron.**
  - **Falling Creek, Virginia — the first try.** The Virginia Company began building an ironworks on Falling Creek (near present-day Richmond) in 1619; the furnace was near or at production when, on **March 22, 1622**, forces coordinated by the Powhatan leader **Opechancanough** destroyed the works and killed twenty-seven people there, part of the wide 1622 attacks on the colony. The ironworks was never successfully rebuilt. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; Virginia DHR; Falling Creek Ironworks Park.)
  - **Saugus, Massachusetts — the first success.** On the Saugus River near Boston, the Company of Undertakers of the Iron Works in New England began building in **1646** under Richard Leader; the blast furnace of the works called **Hammersmith** ran the next year. It was the **first integrated ironworks in North America** — blast furnace (pig iron), forge (wrought iron), and a rolling and slitting mill (flat stock for nails, tools, horseshoes) all on one site, powered by seven waterwheels. It ran until about 1668–1670 and is now Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site. (Sources: NPS; ASME “The Saugus Ironworks Launches America’s Iron Industry”; National Park Foundation.)
- The ore was **bog iron** — iron collected from swamps and ponds — and the story of iron here is use and industry, not discovery: iron had been smelted in the Old World for thousands of years.
- **Meanwhile in Europe,** chemistry began separating from alchemy (Robert Boyle’s *The Sceptical Chymist*, 1661). Every element discovery of this century (phosphorus, 1669, Hamburg) happened abroad.
- **Featured person:** none singled out (connective history).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241e): why the Powhatan attacked in 1622, and who captured, shipped and held the Saugus Scots (era 3)
- **Why Opechancanough attacked (Falling Creek gap).** Virtual Jamestown (Virginia Center for Digital History, Crandall Shifflett, https://www.virtualjamestown.org/phatmass.html): "By 1622 it was apparent to the Indians that the colonists intended to expand their holdings in Virginia. This physical expansion threatened the Indian way of life. Of even greater concern, perhaps, were the renewed colonial efforts to convert and educate the 'savages.' Opechancanough's response ... was to plan and stage a massive attack on the English settlement as a demonstration of Indian power and in an attempt to drive off the English for good." Encyclopedia Virginia, "Anglo-Powhatan War, Second" (via search summary; the page returns a captcha here): the headright system begun in **1618** granted land to new colonists who wanted to grow **tobacco**, and "English settlements pressed up the James River and toward the fall line"; Virginia Company officials, especially **George Thorpe**, pressed the Powhatan from **1620** to send children to be educated among the colonists. Historian Betty Wood, *Origins of American Slavery* (1997), p. 72, as quoted in Wikipedia "Indian massacre of 1622": "By 1620 the colonists were simply taking the acres they required for their expanding tobacco economy without even the pretense of negotiation or payment." About two weeks before the attack, colonists shot **Nemattanew**, a Powhatan war leader, after they accused him of killing a colonist named Morgan (Encyclopedia Virginia via search summary; Wikipedia). Encyclopedia Virginia says historians now doubt that this killing set off the attack; record it as the event just before, not as the cause.
- **Falling Creek itself.** Falling Creek lay near the falls of the James (present-day Richmond), at the upriver edge of English settlement (bank era 3; Encyclopedia Virginia via search summary). Virginia DHR (https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/historic-registers/020-0063/): "During the Indian uprising of 1622, twenty-seven persons were slain at the ironworks, and the facility was destroyed." Among the dead was **John Berkeley**, the works' overseer and a member of the governor's Council (DHR/Wikipedia via search summary). No source fetched names the Powhatan town nearest the works as its landholder; do not supply one. Also record: the NPS count for the whole colony is about 350 dead, a quarter of the colonists (NPS, "Settling the James River," https://www.nps.gov/articles/settlejames.htm); Virtual Jamestown gives 347.
- **Who captured, shipped and held the Scots (Saugus gap).** Durham University Archaeology, "Scottish Soldiers" project (https://www.durham.ac.uk/departments/academic/archaeology/research/archaeology-research-projects/scottish-soldiers/): the English army "led by Oliver Cromwell" won at Dunbar (September 1650); Cromwell freed over 5,000 prisoners and marched about **3,900** of the fittest south to Durham; on the march "the Scots were denied food and shelter and some were executed for refusing to continue"; at Durham they were locked in the unused cathedral. Scottish Prisoners of War Society, quoting historian Marsha L. Hamilton, *Social and Economic Networks in Early Massachusetts* (Penn State University Press, 2009), p. 42 (https://spows.org/battle-of-dunbar/battle-of-dunbar-prisoners-of-war/): England's **Council of State** decided to send most prisoners to the colonies; in early **November 1650** it ordered **Sir Arthur Haselrig**, governor at Newcastle, "to deliver 150 prisoners from Dunbar to the agents of **John Becx and Joshua Foote**, two of the principal investors in the Company of Undertakers, bound for the Saugus ironworks." They sailed on the ship ***Unity*** (master **Augustine Walker** of Charlestown) from Gravesend on **November 11, 1650**, arriving in Boston in mid-December. "The number of Scots who survived the voyage is unknown." The **Company of Undertakers** kept **62** for the ironworks: **36** went to Saugus and **17** to manager **William Aubrey**'s Boston warehouse; the rest were sold to farmers, merchants and other mills. NPS (bank): they worked to repay the cost of their voyage, food, shelter, care and clothing (NPS, "Scottish Prisoners at the Iron Works"). So the holders of the Saugus indentures were the Company of Undertakers, whose investors Becx and Foote received the prisoners.

### 4. 1700 to 1750
- **Colonial iron grew from a few furnaces into a real industry.** Ironworks spread through New England, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, and New Jersey. By mid-century the colonies were exporting raw iron to Britain.
- **Britain tried to keep the finished-goods business for itself:** Parliament’s **Iron Act of 1750** encouraged colonial raw iron (pig and bar) but banned new colonial mills for finished ironware. Colonists widely ignored it — more than sixty new works were built in defiance of the law, about twenty of them in Pennsylvania. By the Revolution the colonies ranked among the **world’s largest iron producers** (commonly ranked third, ahead of England and Wales). (Sources: Britannica “Iron Act”; Encyclopedia.com; Penn Libraries “Iron and Labor in the Revolutionary Era.”)
- Other elements in colonial life: lead (shot, pewter), copper and brass (kettles, buttons), silver and gold (coin and plate — the metals arrived as Spanish coin; the money side → Ch10).
- **Element science was still entirely European** — no element was yet discovered anywhere in the Americas.
- **Featured person:** none.

### 5. 1750 to 1800
- **Chemistry became a science in Europe — and one of its founders moved to Pennsylvania.** See featured-person candidate **Joseph Priestley** below: discovered oxygen in England (Aug 1, 1774), emigrated to America in 1794, built the best chemistry laboratory in the young country at Northumberland, Pennsylvania, and died there in 1804. The man who found the element in the air ended his life an American immigrant (immigration angle ties to Ch2).
- **The new nation put three elements in every pocket:** the **Coinage Act of April 2, 1792** created the U.S. Mint and a coinage of **gold** (eagles), **silver** (dollars and smaller), and **copper** (cents) — element chemistry as daily money (money system → Ch10). (Sources: U.S. Mint history; Coinage Act references.)
- **America’s first documented gold find (1799).** **Conrad Reed**, the 12-year-old son of farmer John Reed, pulled a heavy yellow rock — a **17-pound gold nugget** — out of Little Meadow Creek in Cabarrus County, North Carolina. The family did not know what it was and **used it as a doorstop for about three years**, until a jeweler identified it in 1802 (and bought it from John Reed for $3.50 — roughly a week’s wages — when it was worth about $3,600). It was the first authenticated gold discovery in the United States and set off the first American gold mining (the rush itself unfolds in the next era). (Sources: NCpedia; Reed Gold Mine state historic site; NC ANCHOR.)
- In this half-century European chemists (Lavoisier, Scheele, and others) defined the modern element and found new ones; the U.S. contribution was still use, ores, and one immigrant discoverer.
- **Featured person:** Joseph Priestley (verified below).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241e): the Maryland ironworks confirmed, their owners, the work and the treatment (eras 4–5)

### 6. 1800 to 1850
- **Gold: the first U.S. gold rushes.** Mining at Reed’s farm and neighboring North Carolina creeks grew from 1803; the **Georgia gold rush** followed in 1829. North Carolina was the leading U.S. gold producer until California (the 1849 rush and its crowds → Ch3; the money → Ch9). (Sources: NCpedia; Reed Gold Mine.)
- **Copper: the nation’s first mineral rush (1843).** After Michigan state geologist **Douglass Houghton** reported on the Keweenaw Peninsula’s pure native copper — the same veins Native miners had worked for thousands of years — thousands of prospectors poured into Michigan’s Upper Peninsula from 1843. By 1849 the district produced **96 percent of U.S. copper**, and it stayed the country’s biggest copper source into the 1880s. This predates the California gold rush by five years. (Sources: NPS Keweenaw National Historical Park; MSU geography materials; Cliff Mine archaeology project.)
- **The Ontonagon Boulder.** A **3,708-pound** boulder of pure native copper from the Ontonagon River country, long known and held sacred by the Ojibwe (used for offerings, per the Keweenaw Bay Indian Community), was hauled away in 1843 by Detroit merchant Julius Eldred, seized by the War Department, and ended up at the Smithsonian in 1860. The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community formally asked for its return in 1991; the Smithsonian refused. State the taking and the still-open request plainly (injustice angle → Ch17 if wanted). (Sources: Wikipedia/Smithsonian records; *Mining Gazette* 2025; EAS Miscellany, “Native Copper.”)
- **Lead** mining grew in the Galena district (Illinois–Wisconsin) — the town of Galena, Illinois is named for the lead ore galena — and in Missouri (economy → Ch9).
- **Element discovery stayed European:** in these decades Humphry Davy, Jöns Jacob Berzelius, and others isolated element after element in Europe. **No chemical element had yet been discovered in the United States.**

### 7. 1850 to 1900
- **Silver: the Comstock Lode (1859).** The richest silver deposit ever found in North America, at Virginia City, Nevada — a silver-and-gold ore body that drew its own rush (crowds → Ch3; money and the silver question → Ch9/Ch10). (Sources: Britannica; Online Nevada Encyclopedia.)
- **The periodic table arrives — in Russia.** Dmitri **Mendeleev** published the periodic system in 1869 and used it to predict undiscovered elements. Context only: the table every later American discovery would be slotted into was a Russian invention — a fact that pays off in 1955 when Berkeley names element 101 for him.
- **Aluminum: America’s process story (no false first — the element was known abroad).**
  - **The Washington Monument’s aluminum tip (1884).** When the monument was finished, the government wanted a precious-metal cap; it chose **aluminum**, then a semiprecious rarity priced about **$1 an ounce — the same as silver**. Philadelphia foundryman **William Frishmuth** cast a 100-ounce aluminum pyramid (the largest aluminum casting yet made) for $225. Before installation it was shown at **Tiffany’s in New York**, set on the floor so visitors could **step over the top of the Washington Monument**. Set in place Dec 6, 1884. (Sources: NPS “The Point of the Matter”; TMS/JOM “A History of the Aluminum Cap”; National Archives *Prologue*.)
  - **Charles Martin Hall, Oberlin, Ohio (Feb 23, 1886).** A 22-year-old fresh out of Oberlin College, working in the family **woodshed** with homemade batteries, dissolved aluminum oxide in molten cryolite and ran electricity through it — and out came beads of aluminum metal. His older sister **Julia Brainerd Hall** (Oberlin chemistry graduate) assisted the work and witnessed and documented the experiments — her notes later helped win the patent fight. The Frenchman **Paul Héroult** hit the same process independently the same year (both men born 1863, both discovered it 1886, both died 1914), so it is fairly called the **Hall–Héroult process** — it still makes the world’s aluminum. (Sources: ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark “Hall Process,” dedicated at Oberlin 1997; Science History Institute; Wikipedia Julia Brainerd Hall, cross-checked.)
  - **The price collapse.** Aluminum fell from about **$12 a pound** in the early 1880s to about **$2** after Hall’s process reached industry, then to well under **$1 by the mid-1890s** (about 78 cents by 1893, later tens of cents). Hall’s backers formed the **Pittsburgh Reduction Company** (1888), renamed the **Aluminum Company of America — Alcoa** — in 1907. A metal once displayed at Tiffany’s became foil and pots-and-pans cheap. (Sources: Science History Institute; ACS landmark; abc27 “aluminum becomes cheap.”)
- **Helium: the American near-miss.** USGS chemist **William Hillebrand** in about 1890 extracted an unreactive gas from uranium ore and recorded it as nitrogen. In 1895 **William Ramsay** in London, following up that very paper, ran the gas through a spectroscope and saw the yellow line of **helium** — known in the sun since 1868 but never before found on Earth. The element credit is British; the ore-gas trail ran through American rock, and the American helium story proper starts in the next era. (Sources: Chemistry World “History of noble gases”; Ramsay biographies; owner’s note verified.)
- **Copper for wires.** Electric light and telephone turned copper from kettle metal into the nerve metal of cities (technology → Ch5; economy → Ch9). Michigan, then Butte, Montana, supplied it.

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Helium: the well that would not burn (Dexter, Kansas, 1903).** In May 1903 a drilling company hit a roaring gas well on William Greenwell’s farm near **Dexter, Kansas**. The town planned a celebration and pushed a burning hay bale toward the gusher to light a great flame — and the gas **put the fire out**. Locals scoffed at the “wind gas.” Kansas geologist Erasmus Haworth carried samples to the University of Kansas, where chemists **Hamilton P. Cady and David F. McFarland** showed on **Dec 7, 1905** that the gas was almost 2 percent (1.84%) **helium** — an element thought vanishingly rare on Earth, sitting in quantity under the Great Plains. An ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark (2000). (Sources: ACS landmark “Discovery of Helium in Natural Gas”; KU History “High on Helium”; American Oil & Gas Historical Society.)
- **Helium becomes strategic.** Airships in World War I made nonflammable helium a military prize; Congress passed the **Helium Act of 1925**, creating federal helium production and the **Federal Helium Reserve**, stored underground in the Bush Dome of the Cliffside field near **Amarillo, Texas** — for decades nearly all the world’s helium supply. A 1927 law barred exports; that is why Germany’s **Hindenburg** flew on flammable **hydrogen** — the U.S. would not sell helium — and burned at Lakehurst, New Jersey, May 6, 1937. After the disaster FDR considered helium for Germany, but Interior Secretary Harold Ickes refused after Hitler annexed Austria in 1938. (Economy thread → Ch9; disaster itself → Ch19.) (Sources: Texas State Historical Association “Bush Dome”; U.S. State Dept. FRUS 1938; History.com Hindenburg facts.)
- **The radium craze — an element as a glow-in-the-dark fad.** Radium (discovered by the Curies in France, 1898 — no false first) was painted onto watch dials, sold in quack tonics, and marketed as health itself.
  - **The Radium Girls.** From 1917, young women — many teenagers — painted glowing radium numbers on watch and instrument dials at the U.S. Radium Corporation in **Orange, New Jersey** (and later at Radium Dial in **Ottawa, Illinois**). They were taught to **point the brushes with their lips** (“lip, dip, paint”) and went home glowing — some called them ghost girls. The radium settled in their bones; jaws crumbled (“radium jaw”); women in their twenties began dying. **Grace Fryer**, who started at the Orange plant Apr 10, 1917, led four other dying workers into court in 1927; the company settled in June 1928 ($10,000 each, a $600-a-year pension, and medical bills). In Illinois, **Catherine Wolfe Donohue**, so ill she collapsed testifying, won her case in 1938 and died within months. The court fight and what it changed for workers → **Ch17**; the radiation-health science → **Ch18**. This chapter’s angle is the element itself: what radium is, why it glows, how a wonder-element turned deadly. (Sources: History.com “How the Radium Girls Forced Workplace Safety Reforms”; Library of Congress “Radium Girls: Living Dead Women”; NJ state historic-register documentation.)
  - **Radithor and Eben Byers.** Wealthy Pittsburgh industrialist Eben Byers drank about **1,400 bottles** of Radithor, a patent medicine of radium dissolved in water, from 1927–30; the radium destroyed his bones and killed him in 1932, and his death pushed the government to act against radioactive cures. (The famous line “The Radium Water Worked Fine Until His Jaw Came Off” is a later *Wall Street Journal* retrospective headline, not from 1932 — do not date it to his death.) (Sources: Wikipedia Eben Byers/Radithor, cross-checked; A Medical Education blog on the headline’s true date.)
- **The false American first: “alabamine” (1931).** Physicist **Fred Allison** of Alabama Polytechnic (now Auburn) announced element 85 (“alabamine”) and element 87 (“virginium”), found with his “magneto-optical” apparatus. Other labs could not reproduce it; the method was shown to be an illusion of the equipment, and the claims died — a cautionary tale later cited in Irving Langmuir’s famous lecture on “pathological science.” Element 85 was really made at Berkeley in 1940 (astatine). (Sources: Encyclopedia of Alabama; *Time* 1932 “Alabamine & Virginium”; Scientific American “A Tale of 7 Elements” excerpt.)
- **The machine that opened the door: Lawrence’s cyclotron.** **Ernest O. Lawrence** built his first cyclotron at Berkeley in 1930–31 — a device of glass, sealing wax, and bronze **small enough to hold in one hand** — that whirled charged particles to high speed in a spiral. Bigger and bigger versions (11-inch, 27-inch, 37-inch, the 60-inch of 1939 with its 220-ton magnet) turned Berkeley’s Radiation Laboratory (founded as an independent lab 1936) into an element factory. Nobel Prize in Physics, **1939**. The machine story → Ch5; the lab as institution → Ch6. (Sources: LBL “Ernest Lawrence’s Cyclotron: Invention for the Ages”; APS News “Lawrence and the First Cyclotron.”)
- **The first artificial element — made in America, identified in Italy (1937).** Lawrence mailed a molybdenum **deflector foil** from the 37-inch cyclotron to **Emilio Segrè** in Palermo, Sicily. Segrè and mineralogist **Carlo Perrier** dug out of it a few billionths of a gram of **element 43** — the gap in Mendeleev’s table below manganese — and named it **technetium**, “the artificial one”: the first element made by people. Credit is honestly shared: Italian chemistry, American machine. (Technetium-99m later became a workhorse tracer of hospital imaging → Ch18.) (Sources: LANL periodic table; Guinness; SIF Prima Pagina “1937, Palermo.”)

He stayed, became an American, and helped find **astatine (1940)** — element 85, made by bombarding bismuth in the 60-inch cyclotron with Corson and MacKenzie, the element “alabamine” was supposed to be — and showed with Seaborg that plutonium-239 was fissile. ### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **Californium (early 1950, announced Mar 17, 1950)** — Thompson, Street, Ghiorso, Seaborg; named for California (grid boundary note: era 9).
- **Elements from a hydrogen bomb (1952, secret until 1955).** The **Ivy Mike** test (Nov 1, 1952, Enewetak Atoll) vaporized an island; drone planes flew **through the mushroom cloud** trailing filter papers, and tons of blasted coral were shipped to Berkeley. In the debris, Ghiorso and teams from Berkeley, Argonne, and Los Alamos found two new elements — 99 and 100 — where uranium had swallowed floods of neutrons. The discoveries were **classified**; when finally published (Aug 1, 1955), they were named **einsteinium** and **fermium**. Both namesakes had died in the meantime (Einstein Apr 1955, Fermi Nov 1954) without being told an element bore their name. (Sources: Science History Institute “Element Hunting in a Nuclear Storm”; LANL; Wordorigins.)
- **Mendelevium (1955) — 17 atoms, one at a time.** The Berkeley team (Ghiorso, Harvey, Choppin, Thompson, Seaborg) bombarded a target of about a **billion einsteinium atoms** (all that existed) and caught new atoms by Ghiorso’s recoil trick — a gold catcher foil, dissolved and rushed through chemistry. They made **17 atoms** of element 101, the first element identified atom by atom. Ghiorso wired the detector to the **building fire alarm** as a celebratory bell for each atom — and after forgetting to disconnect it, set off a late-night evacuation. Naming it **mendelevium**, for the Russian Mendeleev, in the depths of the Cold War took government sign-off; a French chemist later told Ghiorso the gesture did more for U.S.–Soviet relations than most diplomacy. (Sources: LANL; Britannica; Atomic Heritage; BBC Science Focus for the fire-alarm/VW anecdotes.)
- **Nobelium (1957–66): a three-country tangle.** A Stockholm team claimed element 102 in 1957 and named it for Alfred Nobel — then retracted. Berkeley claimed it in 1958. The decisive work came at **Dubna in 1966**, and the IUPAC/IUPAP Transfermium Working Group later credited Dubna — but kept the Swedish name. An honest miss for Berkeley: state it plainly. (Sources: IUPAC TWG *Pure Appl. Chem.* 1993; EBSCO; sciencenotes.)
- **Lawrencium (1961, shared credit).** Ghiorso, Sikkeland, Larsh, and Latimer at Berkeley; Dubna did key work 1965–67; the Working Group split the credit. Named for **Ernest Lawrence** — the cyclotron man got element 103. (Sources: IUPAC TWG; RSC; Britannica.)
- **The Transfermium Wars (elements 104–106).** Berkeley and Dubna both claimed 104 (1964/1969) and 105 (1968/1970), and the naming fight ran for decades — American names, Soviet names, dueling journals. The Working Group (1992–93) assigned **shared credit** for 104 and 105 and credited Berkeley for **106 (1974)**. IUPAC’s 1994 compromise even proposed banning names for living people — aimed at **seaborgium**. The final package (**1997**): rutherfordium (104), dubnium (105) — putting the Russian lab’s town on the table — seaborgium (106), bohrium (107), hassium (108), meitnerium (109) (107–109 were German discoveries, GSI Darmstadt). **Seaborgium made Glenn Seaborg the first person to see an element named for him in his lifetime** (he died 1999). (Sources: IUPAC 1997 recommendation; Science History Institute “The Transfermium Wars”; LANL naming page.)
- **Seaborg could receive mail written entirely in elements:** Seaborgium, Lawrencium (lab), Berkelium (city), Californium (state), Americium (country) — a real quip from the Berkeley years. (Sources: Berkeley alumni magazine; Make magazine.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241a): uranium mining on Navajo land — the miners, the study that did not warn them, Church Rock (eras 8–9)
*Pre-write bank check. The bank had no uranium-mining harm at all. Uranium as a mined element is this chapter's; the bomb is `war`'s; the nation's own government, land and organizing lead in `native-nations`; the daily job in `work-workers`; the disease course in `health`. Shared with all four.*
- **Start and scale.** The **Vanadium Corporation of America** began secretly mining uranium on Navajo land in **1943** (Linda M. Richards, "On Poisoned Ground," Science History Institute *Distillations*, 2013-04-22, https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/on-poisoned-ground/). Native American miners (Navajo, Pueblo and Hopi) worked uranium and vanadium mines from **1941** (same). From **1944 to 1986**, "nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands" under leases with the Navajo Nation (EPA, "Navajo Nation: Cleaning Up Abandoned Uranium Mines," https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup, page updated 2026-09-03). The federal **Atomic Energy Commission (AEC)** was the only buyer of uranium until **1966** and kept buying ore until **1970** (EPA, search summary of its AUM pages). Brugge and Goble: the government was the sole purchaser from World War II until **1971**. Record both end dates.
- **How many worked.** About **10,000** uranium workers in all, "about a quarter of them" Navajo; mining peaked in the mid-1950s with "about 750 mines in operation" (Doug Brugge and Rob Goble, "The History of Uranium Mining and the Navajo People," *American Journal of Public Health* 92(9), 2002, PMC3222290, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3222290/). Companies named in the sources: Vanadium Corporation of America, **Kerr-McGee**, **United Nuclear Corporation**, Hydro Resources Inc. (Richards 2013).
- **What the miners were not given.** Navajo miners "report that they were not educated about the hazards of uranium mining and were not provided with protective equipment or ventilation" (Brugge and Goble 2002). The mines lacked ventilation systems to thin out the radon (Richards 2013). The danger was known from Europe: "few protections were provided for US miners before 1962 and adoption after that time was slow and incomplete" (Brugge and Goble abstract, PubMed 12197966).
- **What radon does (define it for the reader).** Uranium breaks down into radon, a radioactive gas, and radon breaks down into radioactive particles called "radon daughters." Unlike the gas, the daughters "can be retained in the lung adjacent to sensitive cells," "delivering high doses of radiation" (Brugge and Goble 2002). The disease named in the record is **lung cancer**, plus lung fibrosis (scarring that stiffens the lungs) (Richards 2013, on Harris Charley).
- **Who knew, and when.** At a **January 25, 1951** internal meeting, AEC and U.S. Public Health Service (PHS) staff "believed that radon was present in levels that would cause cancer." **Wilhelm Hueper**, a government cancer researcher who warned about radon, was reportedly "forbidden to speak in public" and "forbidden to travel west of the Mississippi" (Brugge and Goble 2002). From **1950**, PHS workers led by **Duncan Holaday** and **Victor Archer** followed the miners to measure their cancer risk (search summary of Brugge/Goble and *EHP* "Once Upon a Mine," PMC3915248). The PHS study "failed to inform miners of the risks being studied" and at first left minority miners out of the analysis, citing the wish for "a homogeneous population" (Brugge and Goble 2002). The study tracked **3,415** miners, **779** of them Indigenous (Richards 2013). The researchers agreed with the mining companies not to tell the miners of the hazard, as the only way to get into the mines (EHP 2014, PMC3915248, via search summary; confirm the wording before quoting).
- **The dead — counts, each with its counter.** Richards 2013: by the early 1980s "hundreds of Indian miners had died from lung diseases and cancers." Brugge and Goble cite a 2000 study: **94** lung-cancer deaths among Navajo people documented **1969–1993**, **63** of them former uranium miners. No source fetched gives a complete count of Navajo miners dead of mining disease; state the two counts with their sources and say no full count exists in them.
- **A named miner.** **Harris Charley**, a Navajo uranium miner, died in **1986** of lung fibrosis, with a recorded exposure of **1,192 "working level months"** (a unit for how much radon a miner breathed over time). His son **Perry H. Charley** became an environmental scientist, worked with former Interior Secretary **Stewart Udall** investigating miners' illnesses, and co-founded the Uranium Education Program at Diné College in **1996** (Richards 2013). Navajo organizers named in Brugge and Goble: **Harry Tome, Perry Charley, Phil Harrison**, organizing from the 1960s. Closes on what people did: they organized, and in 1990 Congress passed compensation (below).
- **Compensation.** Congress passed the **Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA)** on October 5, 1990; President **George H. W. Bush** signed it **October 15, 1990** (search summary, CRS R43956). By **March 2001** about **3,975** workers had applied (Brugge and Goble 2002). The award for a qualifying uranium worker is a one-time **$100,000** (DOJ Civil Division RECA page, updated 2026-09-25, https://www.justice.gov/civil/reca). Era-10 status: see the era-10 patch.
- **Church Rock, July 16, 1979.** A **United Nuclear Corporation** earthen dam holding uranium mill waste broke near Church Rock, New Mexico. Figures differ by source: Richards 2013 gives **1,000 tons** of radioactive mill tailings (the ground-up rock left after uranium is taken out) and **93 million gallons** of acidic, radioactive wastewater; other accounts give **1,100 tons** and **94 million gallons** (Wikipedia "Church Rock uranium mill spill," journalism-grade). The waste ran at least **80 miles** down the Rio Puerco past about **1,700** Navajo people. It released **46 curies** of radiation against **13** at Three Mile Island four months earlier; the U.S. Geological Survey called it "the largest accidental radioactive release in the United States." Officials declared no state of emergency and ordered no evacuation (Richards 2013). The mill was put on EPA's National Priorities List in **1983** (search summary). No source fetched names the company official responsible for the dam; do not supply one.
- **Homes built of mine waste.** **Elsie Mae Begay**, a Navajo woman, lost two sons to rare cancers; her family's hogan (a traditional Navajo house) was taken apart in **2001** because it was radioactive (Richards 2013).
- **The land.** The mines sat on Navajo land, worked under leases with the Navajo Nation (EPA). The Navajo Nation Council banned uranium mining on its land in the **Diné Natural Resources Protection Act of 2005** (Richards 2013).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241a): lead — the scientists who proved it and the bans (era 9)
- **Clair Patterson**, a Caltech geochemist, found lead in the air at about **1,000 times** natural levels; his **1965** paper called Kehoe's safe-threshold idea "an ill-defined opinion unsupported by any evidence" (Kovarik 2005). **Herbert Needleman**, a Pittsburgh pediatrician, measured lead in the baby teeth of about **2,500** schoolchildren and linked higher lead to worse school performance (Kovarik gives **1974** for the study; the major paper is usually dated 1979 — confirm the year before stating). Industry-funded scientists challenged his work; it was later upheld (Kovarik 2005).
- **Paint.** The **Lead-Based Paint Poisoning Prevention Act (1971)** limited lead paint in housing built or repaired with federal money. In **1978** the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission banned lead paint in all homes: paint could hold no more than **0.06 percent** lead (it had been limited to 0.5 percent in 1972). Homes built before 1978 are still likely to have some lead paint (CDC "About Lead in Paint"; CDC CLPPP timeline, https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/php/about-clppp/timeline.html; NIOSH 2021).
- **Gasoline.** EPA began a health-based rule to cut lead in gasoline in **1972–73** (CDC gives 1972; Kovarik gives the 1973 announcement). Unleaded pumps and cars built for unleaded fuel spread in **1975–76**; the Clean Air Act ban on leaded fuel for road vehicles took effect **January 1, 1996**. On **January 29, 1996**, EPA Administrator **Carol M. Browner** announced the last step of the 25-year phase-out and said children's blood lead levels had fallen **70 percent** (EPA archive, "EPA Takes Final Step in Phaseout of Leaded Gasoline," https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/aboutepa/epa-takes-final-step-phaseout-leaded-gasoline.html, via search summary). Lanphear 2007: blood lead in children and adults "plummeted as a result of bans on lead in gasoline, paint, and solder."

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241e): uranium — who decided, from the federal report; Church Rock; the 1960s lead deaths (era 9)
- **Source.** Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments (ACHRE), *Final Report* (1995), chapter 12, "The Uranium Miners," fetched in full (Georgetown bioethics archive, https://bioethicsarchive.georgetown.edu/achre/final/chap12_2.html). A federal advisory committee's final report; this is the government report the dispatch names. (Who appointed the committee is not stated on the fetched page; do not state it.)
- **Who refused to make the mines safe.** In **1948** the AEC's New York Operations Office, whose industrial hygienist **Merril Eisenbud** reported on the mines, "recommended that the AEC write requirements for health protection into its contracts with the mine operators," as it had done for beryllium. "The **DBM** [AEC Division of Biology and Medicine] and the AEC **Raw Materials Division** rejected Dr. Eisenbud's recommendation for health protection, arguing that the Atomic Energy Act did not give the AEC authority over uranium mine health and safety." The AEC then moved uranium buying to a new section in Washington; the New York office's director and many staff quit. In May **1949**, **Dr. John Z. Bowers**, DBM deputy director, said conditions "were not satisfactory" but did not want "drastic steps." AEC sanitary engineer **A. E. Gorman** argued the AEC "had a moral responsibility" and could add a contract clause; "Gorman's perspective did not win out." At a January **1956** AEC advisory meeting DBM's **Dr. Roy Albert** gave the decision to "sit tight"; Eisenbud answered that the government should pay to ventilate the mines. On **March 11, 1959** AEC General Counsel **L. K. Olson** wrote that Congress had not meant the AEC to regulate mining. Surgeon General **LeRoy Burney** had written in October 1958 that about **1,500** men in some **300** mines worked in "uncontrolled or poorly controlled" air. The first federally enforceable radon standard came only in **1967**, from Secretary of Labor **Willard Wirtz**, who told Congress that after 17 years "there are today ... no adequate health and safety standards." ACHRE's conclusion: legal responsibility "appears to have rested largely, but not exclusively, with the states," but "the mine owners, the state governments, and the federal government each had a responsibility to take action leading to ventilation of all mines" after 1951; the AEC "interpreted the Atomic Energy Act as not providing it with authority"; "the product it purchased was at the price of hundreds of deaths."
- **Ventilation, who did and did not.** January 25, 1951: AEC and PHS staff said the radon "could be abated by proper ventilation" but found it "unrealistic" to set a level the mine operators must meet. The PHS "met with the mining companies ... and urged them to undertake ventilation"; "it appears the mining industry lacked the commitment." "While some large mines were ventilated during the 1950s and 1960s, most of the small mines were not ventilated until the 1960s or later." A Colorado deputy commissioner of mines said in 1960 that 98 percent of mines would have to stop work under a 1955 proposed standard (ACHRE).
- **Who silenced Hueper.** ACHRE: in **1952** Wilhelm Hueper, founding director of the environmental cancer section of the National Cancer Institute, was invited to speak to the Colorado Medical Society "but declined to attend when ordered by the director of the NCI, at the request of the AEC's **Shields Warren**, to delete references 'to the observation of lung cancer in from 40 to 75 percent of the radioactive ore miners in ... [Europe].'" An AEC representative called his warnings "not in the public interest." Warren then wrote to the NCI director "asking for Dr. Hueper's dismissal for 'bad judgment.' Dr. Hueper kept his job, but was, according to **Victor Archer** ..., forbidden to travel west of the Mississippi for research purposes." **Correction note beside the T-241a uranium patch:** "reportedly forbidden to speak in public" is sharpened by ACHRE: the NCI director, at Warren's request, ordered him to cut his lung-cancer figures from a 1952 speech; the travel ban is Archer's account.
- **Who made the agreement not to warn the miners.** ACHRE: "Duncan Holaday testified in court proceedings that in order to gain access to the mines, an oral agreement was made with mine owners not to directly inform those most affected by their findings, the miners"; the researchers agreed the PHS would not "alarm the miners." The *Begay v. United States* decision (D. Ariz., **July 10, 1984**), as quoted by ACHRE: "it was decided by PHS **under the surgeon general** that the individual miners would not be told of possible potential hazards from radiation ... for fear that many miners would quit." A PHS consent form of May 1960 "says nothing about the risk of lung cancer." The first federal warning to miners was a PHS pamphlet of late **1959**, which "gave no indication of the level of risk." The *Begay* court held the United States immune from suit but wrote that the miners' situation "cries for redress" and called it a "tragedy of the nuclear age."
- **A death count with its counter.** ACHRE: "By 1990, **410** lung cancer deaths had occurred among the **4,100** miners in the Colorado Plateau study group; about **75** lung cancer deaths would normally have been expected." - **Church Rock: government record.** New Mexico Environmental Improvement Division, *The Church Rock Uranium Mill Tailings Spill: A Health and Environmental Assessment* (in EPA Region 6 records, https://semspub.epa.gov/work/06/1000720.pdf, fetched and text-extracted): the dam of a UNC tailings pond failed "early on the morning of July 16"; about **94 million gallons** of liquid and **1,100 tons** of solids; "the largest single release of liquid radioactive waste recorded in the United States." "Within a few hours of the dam failure the New Mexico Environmental Improvement Division initiated stream monitoring and ordered UNC to cease the discharge of tailings to the breached pond"; UNC stopped milling for about three months. The EID report concluded that the spill's impacts "on people and the environment were quite limited," while dewatering water pumped from the mines into the Puerco River "may represent a greater long-term hazard." Record this as the state agency's own finding. **Who declined an emergency:** New Mexico Governor **Bruce King** turned down the Navajo Nation's request that the area be declared a federal disaster area (Wikipedia "Church Rock uranium mill spill" and Vice 2019, journalism-grade, via search summaries; the Vice summary: requests from the Navajo Tribal Council's Emergency Services Coordinating Committee). Cracks in the dam were reportedly seen from **1977** (same, journalism-grade; not confirmed from a government source, do not state). **No source fetched names a UNC official responsible for the dam.** Sources checked: NMEID/EPA report, Richards 2013, NM In Depth (blocked), Wikipedia, Vice, Stanford course page.

### 10. 2000 to Today
- **Finishing the seventh row — a Russian–American (and Japanese) story.** Between 1998 and 2010, joint teams at **Dubna (JINR)** with **Livermore (LLNL)** — and for 115/117 also **Oak Ridge (ORNL)** and **Vanderbilt** — made elements 114–118. On **Dec 30, 2015**, IUPAC officially recognized 113, 115, 117, and 118, completing period 7 of the table; the names arrived **Nov 28, 2016**: **nihonium** (113 — Japan’s RIKEN, the first element discovered in Asia), **moscovium** (115), **tennessine** (117), **oganesson** (118). Frame all of it as collaboration — the atoms formed in Russia; the science, people, and materials crossed oceans. (Sources: IUPAC press releases Dec 30, 2015 and Nov 28, 2016; LLNL; ORNL.)
- **Flerovium and livermorium (named May 2012):** element 114 (first atom Dec 1998) honors Russia’s Flerov Laboratory; element 116 (2000) honors **Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory** — putting the California city of Livermore on the periodic table. (Sources: IUPAC; LLNL; City of Livermore.)
- **Tennessine: Tennessee on the periodic table.** The full story is in the master table (row 117): ORNL’s reactor made 22 mg of berkelium-249 — the only usable target material on Earth — it crossed the Atlantic **five times** because of customs paperwork, and six months of beam time at Dubna yielded **six atoms**. Tennessee became the second U.S. state named on the table (after californium). **Promethium (Oak Ridge, 1945) remains the only element actually discovered in Tennessee — tennessine honors Tennessee but its atoms were made in Russia.** (Sources: ORNL element-117 timeline; “Charmed half-life” ORNL Review.)
- **Clarice Phelps.** An ORNL nuclear chemist (U.S. Navy veteran, Tennessee State University graduate) on the team that purified the berkelium-249 target — recognized as the **first Black woman to help discover a chemical element**. (Sources: ORNL staff profile; CNN 2023; BlackPast.)
- **Oganesson: the second living namesake.** Element 118, credited by IUPAC to the JINR–LLNL collaboration, was named for **Yuri Oganessian**, the Dubna physicist who led the superheavy hunt — only the second element named for a living person, after seaborgium. His colleagues voted on the name after asking him to leave the conference call. (Sources: IUPAC; sciencenotes; Armenian Weekly/ANCA coverage of the naming.)
- **The hunt for element 120 — back in America.** In 2024, Berkeley Lab’s Heavy Element Group made **two atoms of livermorium a new way** — a titanium-50 beam on plutonium-244 at the 88-Inch Cyclotron — the proof-of-principle needed to attempt **element 120**, which would open row 8 of the table and would be the heaviest atom ever made. (Sources: Berkeley Lab News Center, July 23, 2024; DOE Office of Science highlight; ANS Nuclear Newswire.) **Status check 2026-08-08:** the element-120 attempt (californium-249 + titanium-50) has synthesis runs from 2025 at Berkeley; **no discovery announced as of August 2026** — the outline says "under way, no discovery announced" and era 10 is marked current through 2026. (Sources: DOE Office of Science "New Progress Toward the Discovery of New Elements"; 2025 conference report "Progress Toward the Discovery of the New Element E120 at LBNL" — lab-news grade.)
- **Helium worries (light).** The Federal Helium System (reserve, Cliffside field, 423-mile pipeline) was sold to the industrial-gas company Messer, sale completed June 2024, ending nearly a century of federal helium. **Precision (spot-checked 2026-08-08):** the accepted Messer bids totaled **$423,350,000** (GSA sealed-bid opening Jan 25, 2024); the sale ultimately provided **about $460 million to the U.S. Treasury** (BLM press release "BLM Helium System sale provides $460 million to U.S. Treasury"). Say "the sale sent about $460 million to the Treasury," not "sold for $460 million." Helium remains scarce and strategic (MRI machines, rockets, chips) — supply story → **Ch9**. (Sources: BLM press releases 2024; gasworld; Messer press releases.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-241a): lead — Flint, Michigan, 2014–2023 (era 10; every figure dated)
*Shared with: `health` (lead) · `city-building` (the water system) · `government-politics` (emergency managers and the prosecutions) · `rights-movements` (a majority-Black city). This chapter's slice: the element in the pipes and in the children's blood.*
- **Who switched the water.** In **April 2014** (the switch date usually given is April 25), Flint's water source was changed from Detroit's Lake Huron water to the **Flint River** "without necessary corrosion control treatment" (CDC CLPPP timeline). The decision was made under state-appointed emergency manager **Darnell Earley**, appointed under Governor **Rick Snyder** (NPR via WVXU, 2023-10-31, https://www.wvxu.org/news-from-npr/2023-10-31/prosecutors-say-court-decision-forces-the-end-of-criminal-cases-in-flint-water-scandal: "Managers appointed by Snyder switched Flint's water source"; Earley's name from news timelines, KSL/ABC12, journalism-grade).
- **How the lead got in.** The river water lacked the orthophosphate corrosion inhibitor that Detroit's water carried and was high in chloride. It ate into old pipes; an estimated **10 to 80 percent** of Flint's service lines were lead (Hanna-Attisha et al., *American Journal of Public Health* 2016, PMC4985856). Lead was in the system for about **18 months** (NPR 2023).
- **The children, counted.** Pediatrician **Mona Hanna-Attisha** of Hurley Medical Center and colleagues compared blood tests of **1,473** Flint children with **2,202** outside the city. Children under 5 with elevated blood lead went from **2.4 percent** (2013) to **4.9 percent** (2015); in the areas with the most lead in the water, from **4.0 to 10.6 percent**. Children absorb **40 to 50 percent** of lead they swallow in water, against 3 to 10 percent for adults; babies fed formula mixed with tap water were at highest risk (PMC4985856). In **September 2015** she and Hurley told pregnant women and children to stop drinking the water (news timelines). EPA official **Miguel Del Toral** had warned the Michigan Department of Environmental Quality of high lead in **February 2015** (news timelines, journalism-grade).
- **The deaths.** A Legionnaires' disease outbreak (a pneumonia caused by bacteria that grow in poorly treated water) in Genesee County, **June 2014–October 2015**: **90** cases; state health officials counted **10** or **12** dead depending on the report; NPR (2018-02-05) linked it to low chlorine in Flint's water; PBS *Frontline* found further uncounted pneumonia deaths (search summaries of PMC6957290, NPR, PBS). Record both counts and say the official count is disputed.
- **Charges, dated.** In **January 2021** prosecutors charged Snyder (willful neglect of duty) and eight other officials; earlier charges (2016–2017) had named Earley, emergency manager **Gerald Ambrose**, state health director **Nick Lyon** and chief medical executive **Eden Wells** (news timelines). In **2022** the Michigan Supreme Court ruled the one-judge grand jury used to indict them unconstitutional; the prosecutions ended **October 31, 2023** with **no convictions** (NPR 2023).
- **What people did.** Flint was reconnected to a regional water system in **2015** (NPR 2023). On **November 10, 2021**, U.S. District Judge **Judith Levy** approved a **$626.25 million** settlement for more than **90,000** Flint residents and businesses (Michigan Public, 2021-11-10). A federal emergency was declared in **January 2016** (CDC timeline).

## Featured-person candidates (verified; book format — Who / When and where / Movie)

**Joseph Priestley — the oxygen discoverer who became an American (Section 5). VERIFIED.**
> **Who:** the English chemist credited with discovering oxygen, who spent his last decade as an immigrant in Pennsylvania.
> **When and where:** discovered oxygen Aug 1, 1774, in England; emigrated 1794; Northumberland, Pennsylvania, until his death Feb 6, 1804.
> **Movie:** none found. Leave the Movie line out.
- Priestley heated mercuric oxide with a burning lens on **Aug 1, 1774**, and isolated the gas later named oxygen. (Carl Wilhelm Scheele in Sweden had found it earlier but published later; Lavoisier named it — state the shared history honestly.) He also invented **carbonated water** (Leeds, 1767) and in 1770 gave **rubber its English name** — he noted the stuff was good for *rubbing* out pencil marks. A dissenting minister whose views made him a target, he saw his Birmingham home and laboratory burned by a mob in 1791 and left England in **1794** for Pennsylvania. He settled in **Northumberland**, built what was then the best chemical laboratory in America (house finished 1798), identified carbon monoxide there, declined a University of Pennsylvania professorship, and died in 1804. American chemists met at his house in 1874, on the oxygen centennial — a gathering that led toward founding the American Chemical Society (1876); the house is a National Historic Landmark and ACS landmark site. (Sources: ACS landmark materials; Science History Institute biography; Joseph Priestley House.)

**Charles Martin Hall (with Julia Brainerd Hall) — the woodshed aluminum process (Section 7). VERIFIED.**
> **Who:** a 22-year-old Ohio college graduate who invented the cheap way to make aluminum, with his chemist sister assisting.
> **When and where:** Feb 23, 1886, in the family woodshed, Oberlin, Ohio.
> **Movie:** none found. Leave the Movie line out.
- Facts in era 7 above: the woodshed electrolysis of Feb 23, 1886; Julia’s documented assisting/witnessing role; Héroult’s independent same-year discovery in France (born 1863 / discovery 1886 / died 1914 for both men); the Pittsburgh Reduction Company → Alcoa; aluminum from ~$12/lb to under $1.
